smoke. She was in her mother’s kitchen when the first explosion hit two blocks away. The windows
shattered. She dove under the table, pulling her mother down with her. The air turned to dust and
glass.

For the next forty-two days, Lina and her mother lived in the basement of the family home. There was
no electricity, little food, and no way to leave. The shelling was constant, a rhythm she learned to
read: a deep thud meant a hit nearby, a higher crack meant a missile passing overhead. She stopped
sleeping. She stopped feeling hunger. She spent hours cataloging the sounds, mapping them in her
mind, trying to predict the next one.

The worst moment came on day seventeen. A blast so close it knocked her off her feet. She remembers
the ringing in her ears, the smell of cordite and something else—blood. She crawled to her mother,
who was bleeding from a cut on her forehead. For ten minutes, Lina thought her mother was dead. She
held her, whispering prayers she hadn’t said since childhood. Her mother survived, but Lina did not
recover from that ten minutes.

Now, back in her life as a prosecutor, Lina functions at a high level in the courtroom. She is known
for her relentless cross-examinations and her ability to remain calm under pressure. But that calm
