**Case Vignette: A.M.**

A.M. is a 32-year-old South Asian woman, a chaplain by profession, who presents for therapy at the
urging of her supervisor. She is recently divorced and lives alone. She is soft-spoken, often
pausing mid-sentence as if listening for a sound no one else can hear. She holds her hands clasped
tightly in her lap, knuckles white.

The trauma began three years ago, during a humanitarian mission in a conflict zone. A.M. was serving
as a spiritual caregiver at a field hospital. She believed her faith—and her role—made her neutral,
protected. She was wrong. One evening, armed men entered the compound. They separated the foreign
aid workers from the local staff. A.M. was taken to a small, windowless room. She describes the air

subjected to what she calls "the water." She does not elaborate further, but her body does: she
begins to tremble, her breath shallow, her eyes fixed on a point in the middle of the floor. She was
eventually released, exchanged in a prisoner swap, and flown home.

Now, A.M. works at a large urban hospital. She is respected by the medical staff for her calm
presence in the trauma bay. But inside, she is no longer calm. The work that once gave her meaning
now feels like a slow drowning. She avoids the emergency department whenever possible. If she hears
a code blue called overhead, her heart slams against her ribs, and she feels a phantom pressure on
her wrists, as if bound. She has started taking the stairs instead of the elevator, because the
enclosed space triggers a feeling of suffocation. She tells me she spends her shifts in the chapel,
praying for the strength to walk back onto the unit.

Her marriage ended six months ago. Her husband, a kind man who never understood why she went
overseas in the first place, could not reach her when she returned. She describes their last
conversation: he said she was "hollow," that she looked through him. She did not argue. She felt it
was true. She has not been intimate with anyone since the assault. The thought of being touched—even
a hand on her shoulder—makes her skin crawl. She flinched when a barista accidentally brushed her

doesn't smile anymore. Not her colleagues, who see her as a pillar of strength. She carries the

but she admits she hasn't prayed in months. She is afraid that God was in that room with her, and
