**Case Vignette: "Layla"**

Layla is a 19-year-old Middle Eastern woman who presents with a heavy, watchful stillness. She works
the night shift at a regional shipping warehouse, where she operates a pallet jack and scans
inventory. She has been in a relationship with her boyfriend, Amir, for two years. She requested
therapy at the urging of her mother, who noticed Layla “hasn’t been herself” since her younger
brother’s recovery from a severe illness.

The trauma began eighteen months ago. Layla’s brother, then seven years old, was diagnosed with a
rare, aggressive form of leukemia. Layla was the one who found him listless on the bathroom floor,
his skin pale and his lips tinged blue. She remembers the exact sound of her own scream, a thin,
reedy noise she did not recognize. For the next ten months, she lived in the pediatric oncology
ward. She slept in a vinyl recliner beside his bed. She learned to read the beeping machines, the
meaning of each alarm, the exact pitch of the IV pump’s warning. She learned to translate for her
parents, who spoke limited English, the doctor’s careful, terrible words. She became a translator of
dread.

The worst moment was not the diagnosis. It was the night of the bone marrow transplant. The doctors
had warned of the risk of graft-versus-host disease. At 2:00 AM, her brother’s temperature spiked to
104. Layla watched his small body shake violently on the bed. She held his hand, but he didn’t seem
to know she was there. She felt a complete, hollow certainty that he was going to die. She did not
cry. She just watched. She remembers the fluorescent lights buzzing, the smell of antiseptic, the
way the heart monitor line looked like a frantic scribble. She remembers thinking, *This is the
moment. This is when it happens.*

He survived. The fevers broke. The blood counts slowly climbed. He is now in remission, a quiet,
thin boy who plays video games and goes to school. Layla is grateful. She says this often. “I’m so
lucky. He’s fine. I’m fine.”

But she is not fine.

At work, the warehouse is a trigger she cannot escape. The high, humming sound of the conveyor belts
sounds exactly like the hospital’s HVAC system. The flashing amber lights on the forklifts remind
her of the blinking monitors. When a box falls from a shelf with a loud, sudden thud, she freezes.
Her vision narrows. Her heart pounds so hard she can feel it in her throat. She has to walk to the
break room and sit with her back against a wall until the feeling passes. Her supervisor has written
her up twice for “standing around.”

At home, she cannot sleep. She lies awake in the dark, listening to the silence. She is listening
for a beep, a gasp, a change in the rhythm. She checks on her brother three or four times a night.
She stands in his doorway and watches his chest rise and fall. If she cannot see the movement, she
steps closer. She has woken him up twice by touching his shoulder to feel if he is warm.

She avoids all hospitals. She cannot drive past the one where he was treated. She has not been to a
doctor herself in over a year. When Amir suggests they watch a medical drama on TV, she leaves the
room without a word. She cannot explain why. The words feel stuck.

She is irritable, easily startled. When Amir sneezes, she flinches. When her mother calls her name
too loudly from the other room, Layla’s hands begin to shake. She feels a constant, low-level dread,
as if something terrible is about to happen, as if the good news was a mistake. She feels guilty for
feeling this way. Her brother is alive. Why can’t she just be normal?

She has not told anyone the full story of that night in the ICU. She does not know how. She only
knows that when she closes her eyes, she is still there, sitting in that vinyl chair, watching the
line on the monitor, waiting for it to flatline.
