**Patient Vignette: Tane**

Tane is a 55-year-old man of Māori descent, a tall and broad-shouldered figure whose hands, once
accustomed to the heft of fishing nets and building materials, now move with surprising gentleness
as he fastens the tiny buttons on a toddler’s cardigan. He has been a nanny for the past eight
years, a second career that began after his divorce left him adrift. He presents as quietly
watchful, his dark eyes often scanning a room not for exits, but for small, vulnerable beings—a
child about to trip, a cup about to spill. He is here because his sleep has become a battlefield.

The traumatic event did not happen to Tane. It happened to his youngest granddaughter, Aroha. Two
years ago, she was three. They were at a crowded community market; Tane had knelt to tie his shoe,
and for a moment, his hand had released her stroller. A car, backing out of a tight space, did not
see the stroller. The impact was slow, almost gentle, but the result was catastrophic. Tane
remembers the sound of the plastic crumpling, a sound he now hears in the hum of a vacuum cleaner,
the crackle of a plastic water bottle. He remembers the silence that followed, a silence that was
worse than any scream. Aroha survived, but with a traumatic brain injury that has left her with a
permanent limp and a speech delay.

Tane’s life now is a careful, rigid architecture designed to prevent a repetition. He works for a
family with two boys, ages four and six. He is obsessively vigilant. He checks the latch on the
garden gate seven times before letting the boys play outside. He walks them to school via a route
that avoids all driveways, even if it adds fifteen minutes. He has memorized the license plates of
every car on the street. His employers praise his dedication, but they do not see what happens
inside him.

At night, the architecture crumbles. He avoids sleep. He will stay up watching old rugby matches,
the familiar commentary a blanket against the dark. When he finally succumbs, the dreams are not
replays of the accident, but distortions. In one recurring dream, he is holding a child’s hand, but
the hand is a piece of driftwood, smooth and cold. In another, he is in a car, driving in circles
around a silent, empty market. He wakes with a start, his heart hammering, his hands clutching the
sheets, drenched in a cold sweat. He often does not know where he is for a long, terrifying moment.

His days are a performance of competence. He is patient with the children, but his patience is a
brittle shell. The sound of a car horn on the street makes him flinch, his body going rigid. He has

finds himself irritable with the parents, silently furious when they are late, because every minute
they are late is a minute he is responsible, a minute something could go wrong. He has not told
anyone about the nightmares. He feels a deep, unshakeable shame. In his culture, he was the *matua*,
the elder, the protector. He failed. He replays the moment he let go of the stroller, a moment of

now, but as it was in the hospital, pale and still. He carries this image with him, a photograph he
