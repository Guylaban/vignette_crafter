**Patient Vignette: Mr. L., Age 51**

Mr. L. is a 51-year-old married man of South Eastern Asian descent, who works as a policy analyst
for a state environmental agency. He presents as a thoughtful, articulate man, though his hands
tremble slightly when he speaks of the past. He has been married for twenty-three years and has two
adult children.

The event that changed everything happened three years ago. Mr. L. was on a research trip in a
coastal region, collecting data for a report on floodplain management. He remembers the sky turning
a strange, greenish-yellow in the late afternoon. The storm surge came without warning, a wall of
brown water that lifted his rental car and spun it like a toy. He managed to climb onto the roof of
a small, abandoned gas station, clinging to a rusted sign as the water rose to his chest. For eight
hours, he watched debris—pieces of homes, dead livestock, a child’s doll—float past in the black,
churning water. He did not know if anyone was coming for him. He thought of his wife, his children,
and felt a cold certainty that he would never see them again.

In the months that followed, Mr. L. returned to work, but he is not the same person. He is a policy
analyst, a man who once found comfort in data and structured arguments. Now, he cannot look at a
weather map without his heart racing. When a colleague mentions an approaching storm, even a minor
one, Mr. L. feels a wave of nausea and a pressure in his chest. He has stopped checking the weather
at all. He avoids the breakroom if the television is on, and he has asked his supervisor to reassign
him from any project involving coastal regions or flood risk. He knows this is irrational—he works
in an inland state, three hundred miles from the ocean—but the feeling of the water rising around
him is as real as the desk in front of him.

At home, he is distant. He cannot sleep in the dark; he keeps a small lamp on in the bedroom, which
his wife tolerates but does not understand. He startles at the sound of rain on the roof, sometimes
getting up in the middle of the night to check the basement for water, even when it is not raining.
He has become hypervigilant about drains, gutters, and the sump pump, checking them obsessively. His
wife has asked him why he is so anxious, and he has told her the bare facts of the storm, but he
cannot explain the terror that lives inside him. He feels ashamed of his fear, of his inability to

vanished. He was back on the gas station roof, the sound of breaking wood and glass all around him.
He felt the cold water on his legs, the sting of salt in his eyes. He stood frozen in the aisle,
gripping his shopping cart, until a store employee asked if he was alright. He left his groceries
and walked out into the parking lot, shaking.

of his car, filled with water, a first-aid kit, and a change of clothes. He has memorized the
evacuation routes from his office and his home. He knows this behavior is excessive, but the thought
of being unprepared is worse than the ridicule he fears. He has come to therapy because he is
exhausted. He is tired of the nightmares, the constant vigilance, and the feeling that he is living
in a world that is one heavy rain away from collapsing. He does not know how to quiet the alarm that
has been ringing inside him for three years.
