**Patient Vignette: Fatima**

Fatima is a 59-year-old married woman, a police officer for over three decades, who presents for
therapy at the urging of her department’s wellness unit. She sits upright, her posture rigid, her

The story begins seven months ago. Fatima was working a late shift in a quiet residential sector of
the city, a route she had driven hundreds of times. She responded to a domestic disturbance

Standard protocol. Fatima approached, her hand resting on her duty belt, her voice calm. She did not
see the second man—the brother—who came from the side of the house. He struck her from behind with a
length of pipe. She remembers the sound more than the pain: a wet, hollow thud against her skull.
She remembers the dirt in her mouth as she hit the ground. She remembers the weight of two men on
her back, the smell of sweat and cheap beer, the feeling of her own service weapon being wrenched
from its holster. She thought, *This is how I die. A cop, killed with her own gun.* A neighbor’s
floodlight clicked on, and the men fled. She lay there for what felt like hours, though it was only
minutes, before backup arrived.

In the weeks that followed, Fatima returned to work. She insisted. She is a sergeant. She has
mentored dozens of young officers. She does not "sit on the bench." But the world has changed. The
station’s fluorescent lights now feel too bright, too harsh. The crackle of the radio makes her
flinch. She catches herself scanning every doorway, every shadow, every car that slows near her
patrol unit. She no longer sits with her back to the room in the breakroom. She eats alone in her
squad car, engine running, doors locked.
