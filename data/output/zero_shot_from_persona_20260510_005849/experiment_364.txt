**Patient Vignette: Mr. K. (Age 63, Airline Pilot)**

Mr. K. is a 63-year-old married man of Oceania descent, a retired commercial airline captain who now
works part-time as a check pilot for a regional carrier. He presents for therapy at the urging of
his wife of 38 years, who has grown increasingly concerned about his withdrawal, irritability, and
what she calls "the night episodes." He agrees to come, but his posture is guarded, his arms
crossed, his eyes fixed on a point just above my left shoulder.

The story begins not in a cockpit, but in a jungle clearing. Mr. K. was 19 years old, a conscript in
a peacekeeping force that became embroiled in a brutal counter-insurgency. He was a door gunner on a
transport helicopter. One afternoon, their bird took fire and went down hard. He remembers the rotor
blades screaming, the smell of hydraulic fluid and burnt earth, and the sudden, absolute silence
after the crash. He was the only survivor. He spent the next 36 hours hiding in the mud under the
wreckage, listening to the patrols searching for him. He could hear them speaking, less than ten
feet away. He remembers the feeling of a leech on his neck, the taste of copper and soil in his
mouth, and the profound, animal certainty that he was going to die. He was not rescued; the patrol
simply left.

He does not speak of this for the first two sessions. When he finally does, his voice is flat, a
monotone that seems to come from a great distance. He describes it as if watching a movie. "I don't
feel it," he says. "It's just a story I know."

But his body tells a different story. As a check pilot, he is required to sit in the jump seat
behind the first officer, watching their every move. He says the job is "fine," but his wife reports
that he has not slept in the same bed with her for five years. He sleeps in a recliner in the den,
with a small fan running for white noise. He cannot tolerate the sound of a helicopter, even on
television. When a news report shows one, he leaves the room without a word. He has not flown in a
helicopter since the day of the crash, a fact that complicates his otherwise stellar career.

His current life is a carefully constructed fortress. His daily routine is rigid: up at 5:00 AM, a
specific breakfast, a specific route to the airport. He avoids crowds, restaurants, and any social
gathering where he cannot see the exits. He is hypervigilant in the cockpit, scanning the sky not
just for other aircraft, but for threats. He has a pre-flight checklist that is twice as long as the
manual requires. He checks and rechecks the door locks on the aircraft, a habit his colleagues find
eccentric. He is short-tempered with junior pilots who are "too casual." He feels a constant, low-
grade pressure in his chest, a tightness he describes as "the strap," as if he is still buckled into
that shattered helicopter seat.

The worst moments come at night. He has a recurring dream, not of the crash, but of the silence
afterward. He wakes up drenched in sweat, his heart hammering, convinced he can smell the mud and
the hydraulic fluid. He will not describe the dream in detail, only that it ends with the sound of
the patrol's footsteps fading away. He feels a profound, corrosive guilt. He believes he should have
died with his crew. He is a "survivor," and he says the word with a quiet, bitter contempt.

He is a man who has spent 44 years building a life on the principle of absolute control. He commands
a machine that defies gravity. He manages complex systems, weather, and human error. But he cannot
control his own nervous system. He cannot make the smell of hydraulic fluid go away. He cannot make
his wife understand why he must sleep in the recliner. He is a pilot who has lost his compass,
flying on instruments that are all flashing red. He sits in my office, a man of quiet competence and
profound grief, a ghost in his own life, still waiting for a rescue that never came.
