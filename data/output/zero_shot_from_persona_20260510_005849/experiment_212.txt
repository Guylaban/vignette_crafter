**Patient Vignette: "Fatima"**

Fatima is a 69-year-old married Middle Eastern woman who works the late shift at a fast-food
restaurant. She presents with a haunted, vigilant quality that has deepened over the past two years,
though she traces the beginning of her current suffering to a specific evening in late autumn.

It started with a customer. A man, maybe mid-thirties, began coming through the drive-thru three,
then four times a night. He would order a single coffee, always the same, and then linger at the
window. At first, Fatima dismissed it as loneliness. But he started saying things—softly, so her
manager couldn’t hear. “You have kind eyes.” “I know where you live.” She laughed nervously the
first time, a reflex born of decades of deflecting discomfort. But he didn’t laugh back.

Over the next few weeks, the comments escalated. He began appearing inside the restaurant, sitting
in the corner booth for hours, staring. He never ate. He just watched her wipe tables, refill
napkins, clock in and out. Fatima stopped taking her breaks. She started going to the restroom only
when a coworker could stand watch. She told her husband, a retired mechanic, that a “rude customer”
was bothering her. She downplayed it, because she saw the worry in his eyes and because she was
ashamed—ashamed that a woman her age, a grandmother, could feel so small and powerless.

Then the car began appearing on her street. A dark sedan, idling at the end of the block. She would
see it from her kitchen window at 2:00 a.m., after her shift ended, while she made tea to calm her
nerves before bed. She started taking different routes home, but he was always there. She began
sleeping on the couch, facing the front door, a pair of scissors tucked under the cushion. Her
husband found her there one morning, fully dressed, her eyes wide and dry. “Just heartburn,” she
said.

The stalking stopped as abruptly as it began—six months later, the man simply vanished. Perhaps he
found another victim, or perhaps he grew bored. Fatima doesn’t know. She never filed a police
report. She didn’t want to be “that woman,” the one who makes a fuss, the one who invites trouble by
talking.

Now, two years later, Fatima’s world has shrunk. She still works the late shift, but she no longer
walks to her car alone. A teenage coworker escorts her, and Fatima tips him ten dollars every night,
a small price for a small safety. She checks her rearview mirror obsessively, sometimes missing her
turn because she is so focused on the headlights behind her. She has memorized every license plate
on her block. She no longer sits with her back to the door in the break room. She eats standing up,
facing the window.

Her sleep is fragmented. She wakes three or four times a night, her heart pounding, convinced she
heard the soft crunch of tires on gravel. She has not sat on her front porch in eighteen months. She
tells her husband she has allergies.

At work, she is efficient but brittle. A sudden laugh from a customer makes her flinch. A man in a
dark coat ordering coffee sends a bolt of ice through her chest. She has snapped at coworkers twice
for standing too close behind her, then apologized profusely, blaming her “nerves.” She feels
constantly on the verge of tears, but she cannot cry. She feels constantly on the verge of
screaming, but she cannot make a sound.

Fatima does not say she is afraid. She says she is “careful.” She says she is “tired.” But the
carefulness is a cage, and the tiredness is a deep, bone-level exhaustion that no amount of sleep
can touch. She has stopped visiting her grandchildren because the drive goes past the old coffee
shop. She has stopped going to the mosque because the parking lot is too dark.

She is a woman who survived being hunted, and now she lives inside the hunting. The predator is
gone, but the vigilance remains, a permanent, invisible sentry standing guard over a body that no
longer knows how to rest.
