**Case Vignette: Priya (Age 44, Retail Worker)**

Priya is a 44-year-old South Asian woman who has been married for 19 years and works as a sales
associate at a large home goods store. She presents as soft-spoken and meticulously dressed, but her
hands tremble slightly as she adjusts the edge of her dupatta. She has been referred by her primary
care physician after repeated visits for headaches, chest tightness, and insomnia, with no clear
physical cause.

The event that changed everything happened ten months ago. Priya was working her usual shift in the
bedding section when a man in his late twenties entered the store, walked calmly to the customer
service desk, and pulled out a handgun. Priya was thirty feet away, folding duvet covers. She
remembers the sound first—not the gunshot, which never came—but the click of the safety being
released. She watched the man point the gun at her coworker, Anika, a 19-year-old college student
who had just started the week before. Priya’s body locked. She could not scream, run, or even drop
to the floor. She watched Anika’s face go white, watched her hands rise in the air, watched the
man’s finger curl around the trigger. Another employee triggered the silent alarm. The man fled
before police arrived. No one was physically hurt.

But Priya was not the same after that shift.

In the weeks following, she began to avoid the store entirely. She took unpaid leave, then short-
term disability. Her manager called, concerned. Her husband, Raj, grew frustrated. “You weren’t even
the one he pointed the gun at,” he said once, not unkindly, but with the blunt logic of a man who
does not understand trauma. Priya tried to go back twice. Both times, she made it to the parking
lot, saw the automatic doors slide open, and felt her throat close. She drove home in a cold sweat,
her hands numb on the steering wheel.

Now, she works part-time at a small pharmacy two towns over, a job she took out of desperation. The
pay is less. The commute is longer. But there are no large glass windows, no open floor plans, no
customer service desks where someone could stand and aim. She still scans the aisles constantly. She
notices every man in a hoodie. She has memorized the location of every exit.

At home, Priya has become a ghost in her own life. She cannot watch television—any loud noise, any
sudden movement on screen, sends her heart racing. She stopped cooking because the sizzle of oil in
a pan sounds too much like a gunshot’s echo. Raj eats takeout most nights. Their conversations have
dwindled to logistics: who will pick up the dry cleaning, whether the water bill was paid. She has
not let him touch her in months. When he reaches for her in bed, she flinches. She does not know how
to explain that her body lives in a permanent state of alarm, that every touch feels like a threat.

Sleep is a battlefield. She lies awake until three or four in the morning, her mind replaying the
scene on a loop: the click, Anika’s face, the gun. She has started sleeping in the guest room
because she cannot bear Raj’s breathing, the vulnerability of it. When she does sleep, she dreams of
running through the store, but the aisles stretch infinitely, and she can never reach Anika. She
wakes gasping, her pillow soaked.

Priya has also begun to avoid the South Asian community center where she used to volunteer. She
tells herself she is too tired. But the truth is that she feels disconnected from everyone. When her
sister calls and talks about normal things—a cousin’s wedding, a new recipe—Priya feels a vast, cold
distance, as if she is watching her own life from behind a pane of glass. She cannot find the words
to explain what happened, so she says nothing.

She came to therapy because her doctor said her blood pressure is dangerously high. She does not
believe talking will help. But she is also tired of being afraid. She sits on the edge of the chair,
her purse clutched in her lap, and she waits.
