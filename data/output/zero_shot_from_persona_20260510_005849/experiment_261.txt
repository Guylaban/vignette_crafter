The trauma happened three years ago, in late autumn. A storm surge, unprecedented in the town’s

sound first: a deep, groaning roar that was not the wind but the earth itself shifting under water.
She and Arthur had only minutes to gather their dog and climb to the attic. From the small window,
she watched their garden shed lift off the ground and float away like a toy. The water rose to the
second step of the attic stairs before it stopped.

They were rescued by a neighbor in a small boat six hours later. The house was still standing, but
the first floor was gutted. Martha’s design studio—a converted sunroom where she had worked for
thirty years—was destroyed. Her drafting table, her Pantone swatches, her carefully organized flat
files of client work, all of it was soaked, swollen, and coated in silt.

In the months that followed, Martha threw herself into the rebuilding. She was practical, efficient,
and grateful. She told friends she was lucky. She told herself the same thing. But she began to
notice small changes.

She cannot look at the weather forecast anymore. If she hears the word “storm” on the radio, her
chest tightens and her hands go cold. She stopped checking the tide tables, which she used to do
every week to plan her beach walks. Now, if she accidentally sees a photograph of the ocean, she
feels a jolt of fear, as if the image itself might spill water into the room.

Her work has become difficult in ways she cannot explain. She used to love designing logos with
organic, flowing lines—curves that suggested waves or wind. Now, she finds herself avoiding those
shapes. She catches herself drawing hard angles, sharp corners, straight lines. She tells clients
she is moving toward a “more modern aesthetic,” but she knows the truth. Curves feel dangerous. They
feel like the shape of water rising.
