**Patient Vignette: Captivity and Its Aftermath**

The patient is a 59-year-old Latin American woman, divorced, who works as a project manager for a
mid-sized construction firm. She presents for therapy at the urging of her adult daughter, who has
grown increasingly concerned about her mother’s withdrawal and irritability. The patient agrees that
she has not been herself for the past three years, but she struggles to articulate what happened to
her.

She begins her story in the spring of 2021, when she was living in a suburb of a large city in her
home country. She had recently separated from her husband after a long and difficult marriage, and
was proud of her independence. One evening, after a late meeting, she was walking to her car in a
parking garage when two men approached her. They forced her into the back of a van at gunpoint. For
the next four days, she was held in a small, windowless room. She was blindfolded and bound to a
chair for most of that time. The men spoke to her in Spanish, demanding information about her
employer’s financial accounts. She did not have the information they wanted. She recalls the sound
of their voices, the smell of stale cigarette smoke, and the constant, low hum of a fluorescent
light above her head. She was not physically assaulted, but she was threatened repeatedly. On the
fourth day, she was released on a rural road, blindfolded and left to find her way home.

She tells this story in a flat, almost detached monotone. She does not cry. She says she was “lucky”
because she was not raped or killed. She says she does not think about it anymore. But her body
tells a different story. She has lost fifteen pounds since the event, despite no change in diet. She
sleeps only three to four hours a night, waking from nightmares in which she is back in that room,
the fluorescent light buzzing, the blindfold tight against her eyes. She has not entered a parking
garage since the incident. She parks on the street, often walking several blocks to her office, even
in the rain. Her daughter has noticed that she flinches at the sound of a car horn or a man’s raised
voice.

As a project manager, she was once known for her calm, decisive leadership. She could juggle
multiple subcontractors, deadlines, and budget constraints with ease. Now, she finds herself unable
to concentrate on simple spreadsheets. She has missed three major project milestones in the past six
months. She has stopped attending team meetings, claiming she prefers to work from home. In truth,
she cannot bear the feeling of being in a room full of people, especially men, especially if the
door is closed. She has become hypervigilant, scanning the faces of strangers in the grocery store,
checking her rearview mirror obsessively while driving.

She is estranged from her ex-husband, who told her she was “overreacting” when she tried to talk
about the experience. She has not dated since the captivity. She says she does not trust anyone,
especially men. She has a small group of female friends from work, but she has cancelled on them
repeatedly, making excuses about being tired or busy. The truth is that she feels disconnected from
them, as though she is living behind a pane of glass. She cannot share the details of what happened,
and she feels that no one would understand anyway.

Her daughter, a nurse, is the only person who knows the full story. The patient speaks of her
daughter with a mix of love and guilt. She knows she is worrying her, but she feels powerless to
change. She describes a profound sense of numbness, a feeling that the world is not real, that she
is simply going through the motions of her life. She says, “I used to be someone who planned for the
future. Now, I don’t even plan for next week. What’s the point?”

She denies any suicidal ideation, but her eyes are flat and tired. She says she came to therapy
because she is “tired of being tired.” She does not want medication. She does not know if talking
will help. She sits on the edge of the chair, her hands clasped tightly in her lap, ready to leave.
