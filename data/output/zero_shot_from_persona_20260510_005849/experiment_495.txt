**Patient Vignette: "Priya" (Age 43, Plumber)**

Priya arrives for her first session in worn work boots, her hands calloused and stained with what
looks like dried copper residue. She speaks with a steady, almost clinical tone, as if she is
reading a report. "I used to love the smell of PVC cement," she says, then stops. "Not anymore."

The event happened eighteen months ago, on a Tuesday. Priya was working a residential repipe in an
old building downtown. The homeowner had stored industrial-strength drain cleaner under the sink—a
concentrated sodium hydroxide solution, unlabeled. When Priya cut into the old galvanized pipe, the
chemical mixed with standing water and released a cloud of gas. She inhaled before she could turn
away.

For the next three days, she felt like she was drowning. Her throat closed, her lungs burned, and
she coughed up something that looked like rust. The ER doctor called it chemical pneumonitis. They
gave her oxygen and sent her home with an inhaler. The physical symptoms faded within two weeks, but
something else took root.

Now, Priya cannot be near a drain without her heart hammering. She stopped taking jobs that involve
old pipes, which is most of her work. She tells her dispatcher she's only available for new
construction, but even then, she checks every cabinet before she starts. She carries a small
flashlight and peers into every sink trap, every floor drain, as if the acid might be hiding there.
"I know it's irrational," she says, pressing her palms flat against her thighs. "But I can't stop."

Her husband, Ravi, noticed the changes first. She used to come home and tell him about her day—the
crooked fittings, the funny things customers said. Now she comes home silent. She showers
immediately, scrubbing her arms and face until they are raw. She changed brands of dish soap because
the old one "smelled like the hospital." She cannot eat at restaurants anymore. "What if they use
the wrong cleaner in the kitchen sink?" she asks, and she knows how it sounds.

Sleep is a battleground. Priya wakes at 3:17 AM almost every night—the exact time she was in the
ER—gasping, convinced she is breathing in poison. She has learned to sleep with the window open,
even in winter, even when the snow drifts onto her nightstand. Ravi has offered to sleep on the
couch, but she refuses. "I need to know you're there," she says, though she cannot explain why that
matters.

At work, her hands shake when she opens a toolbox. She wears a respirator now for every job, even
when she is just changing a faucet washer. Her coworkers tease her about it. "You look like you're
going into a hazmat zone," they say. She laughs along, but inside, she is calculating: *How many
seconds until I can get out of this room? Where is the nearest exit? Is the ventilation on?*

The worst part, she says, is the guilt. "I'm a good plumber," she tells me, her voice breaking for
the first time. "I can sweat a joint in under a minute. I can diagnose a clogged vent stack by the
sound of the gurgle. And now I can't walk into a basement without feeling like I'm going to die."

She has started taking the long way to job sites, avoiding streets with plumbing supply stores. She
deleted the app that shows her job assignments, so she won't see the address and panic. Last month,
she turned down a $3,000 job because the building had a boiler room. "I used to love boiler rooms,"
she whispers. "Warm. Quiet. Now they feel like a tomb."

Priya sits forward in her chair, her rough hands clasped. "I don't want to be afraid of my own
trade," she says. "But I don't know how to go back."
