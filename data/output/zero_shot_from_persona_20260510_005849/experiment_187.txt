**Patient Vignette: Tama (Age 63, Male, Oceania)**

Tama is a 63-year-old man of Oceania descent, a community organizer by profession, who presents for
therapy at the urging of his adult daughter. He has been divorced for over a decade and lives alone
in a modest apartment near the community center where he has worked for twenty years. He arrives for

tremble slightly as he adjusts the collar. He speaks in a low, measured voice, frequently pausing to
look at the floor.

The event that changed everything happened eighteen months ago, on a rainy Tuesday night. Tama was
driving home from a late community meeting about affordable housing. He recalls the headlights of an
oncoming truck swerving into his lane. He remembers the sound of metal screaming against metal, the
shatter of glass, and the sensation of his car spinning into a drainage ditch. He was trapped for
nearly an hour before emergency services arrived. He suffered a fractured pelvis, three broken ribs,
and a deep laceration on his forearm. Physically, he has healed, but the accident has not left him.

In the months since, Tama has stopped driving. He takes the bus or relies on his daughter for rides,
but even as a passenger, he grips the door handle when a car approaches too closely. He avoids the
intersection where the accident occurred, taking a longer route to work. At the community center, he
has become quieter, less willing to chair meetings that run late. His colleagues have noticed he no
longer volunteers for evening events, and he has stopped attending the weekly potlucks that were
once his anchor of connection.

Tama describes a constant, low-level vigilance. When he walks through a parking lot, he scans for
moving vehicles, his shoulders tensing at the sound of a revving engine. He has trouble sleeping,
waking three or four times a night from dreams where he is pinned in the car, unable to move, the
rain tapping on the roof. He does not remember these dreams fully, only the sensation of suffocating
and a cold dread that lingers into the morning. He has started drinking two beers before bed to
quiet his mind, but it only helps for an hour.

The most difficult part for Tama is the shame. He was a man who prided himself on his strength—the
one people called when a family needed food, a teenager needed a job, or a landlord needed to be
confronted. Now, he feels fragile. He tells me he is "not the same man." He avoids talking about the
accident with his friends, because he does not want them to see him as weak. He has stopped
attending the men's group at his church, the same group he helped found, because he cannot bear to
hear them discuss their own lives while his feels frozen.

Tama’s work has suffered. He is still effective in one-on-one conversations, but he has lost the
ability to speak in front of large crowds. At a recent town hall, he felt his heart pound so hard he
thought he was having a heart attack. He excused himself to the bathroom and stood over the sink,
staring at his reflection, his breath shallow. He has not spoken at a public meeting since.

His daughter, who is a nurse, noticed the changes. She found him one evening sitting in the dark,
staring at the television that was not turned on. She told him, gently, that he needed help. Tama
agreed, but he does not know what to say. He sits in my office and tells me, "I used to know who I
was. Now I just feel like a man waiting for the next bad thing to happen." He does not know how to
rebuild the life he had, or if he even can.
