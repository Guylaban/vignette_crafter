**Clinical Vignette: Mr. L.**

Mr. L. is a 34-year-old South Eastern Asian man, a widower, who presents as thoughtful and measured,
though his hands tremble slightly when he describes the events of the past year. He works as a
chaplain at a large urban hospital, a role he has held for nearly a decade. He sought therapy after
a colleague noticed he was avoiding the hospital’s main entrance and taking three flights of stairs
to reach the oncology unit, even when his knees ached.

The traumatic event occurred fourteen months ago, on a Tuesday evening in late autumn. Mr. L. had
just finished a twelve-hour shift. He was walking to his car in the hospital’s designated staff
parking lot, a route he had taken without incident for years. He was tired, thinking about a young
mother he had sat with who had just received a terminal diagnosis. He did not see the two men until
they were already on him. One pressed a cold, hard object—he believes it was a gun—against the back
of his neck. The other demanded his wallet and phone. Mr. L. complied immediately, speaking in a
low, steady voice he recognized from a thousand bedside prayers. He told them, “It’s okay. Take it.
Just don’t hurt me.” They took his belongings and ran. The entire interaction lasted perhaps forty-
five seconds. No one was physically injured.

In the weeks that followed, Mr. L. told himself he was fine. He had counseled countless victims of
violence; he knew the statistics, the coping skills. He repeated to himself, “It was just a robbery.
I wasn’t hurt. I am blessed.” He refused to file a police report, telling his supervisor it was a
waste of resources. But internally, something had shifted. He began to feel a constant, low-grade
static in his chest. He stopped walking to his car after dark, asking security for an escort. Then
he stopped walking to his car at all, instead taking the bus and getting off two stops early so he
could approach the hospital from a different direction. He began to avoid the staff parking lot
entirely, a place he had once considered a safe, mundane part of his day.

The avoidance has since metastasized. He no longer goes to the grocery store after 5 p.m. He will
not stand in line at a bank. He scans every room he enters for exits, a habit he finds exhausting.
The worst moments come when he is ministering to a patient. Last month, while praying with a man

in a cold sweat, convinced for a moment that he is back on that asphalt, that the gun is still
