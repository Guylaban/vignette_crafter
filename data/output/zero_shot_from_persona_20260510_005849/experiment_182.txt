**Patient Vignette: Mr. T.**

Mr. T. is a 42-year-old farmer of South Eastern Asian descent, married with three children. He
presents with a calm, weathered demeanor, his hands calloused and stained from years of working the
rice paddies. He speaks in a measured, deliberate manner, often pausing to look at the floor before
answering a question. He came to therapy at the persistent urging of his wife, who is “worried I am
not the same man.”

The trauma began three years ago, when his youngest son, then four years old, was diagnosed with a
severe bacterial infection that led to septic shock. Mr. T. describes the day as “normal” until his
son, usually running and laughing, became limp and unresponsive. He remembers the frantic drive to
the district hospital, the fluorescent lights of the emergency room, and the doctor’s face as he
said, “We need to act fast.” He stayed by his son’s side for seventeen days. He did not sleep. He
did not eat. He prayed to his ancestors and made bargains with God. He remembers the exact sound of
the heart monitor’s alarm, the way the IV tubes looked like thin, clear snakes, and the smell of
antiseptic that still makes his stomach turn.

His son survived. He is now a healthy, active seven-year-old. But for Mr. T., the ordeal never
ended.

He now works the land with a vigilance that borders on obsession. Every morning, before the sun
rises, he checks his son’s breathing, pressing a hand to his small chest until he feels the steady
rise and fall. He has installed a thermometer in every room of the house. He takes his son’s
temperature three times a day, logging it in a notebook. When the boy has a common cold, Mr. T.
cannot function. He becomes rigid, his jaw tight, his eyes scanning for signs of the same bluish
tint he saw in the hospital. He drives his son to the clinic for any cough, any sniffle, despite the
doctor assuring him it is nothing.

At work, in the fields, he cannot focus. The repetitive motion of planting rice, which once brought
him a sense of peace, now feels hollow. He is constantly startled by the sound of a bird taking
flight or a tractor backfiring. He has stopped driving the family’s truck because the memory of that
frantic ride to the hospital is triggered by the feel of the steering wheel. His wife now handles
all errands that require the road.

He avoids his son’s school functions, terrified that a call will come, that he will have to run to
the hospital again. He avoids the pediatric wing of the hospital entirely, taking a different route
to the market even if it adds twenty minutes. He has stopped visiting his own aging parents, who
live near the hospital, because the proximity is too much.

At night, the dreams come. Not of the illness itself, but of the waiting. He dreams of a white room,
the sound of a single beeping machine, and a voice that says, “We need to act fast.” He wakes in a
cold sweat, his heart pounding, and checks on his son. He does this two, sometimes three times a
night. His wife has started sleeping in the other room.

that he should have driven faster, that his prayers were not enough. He feels he has failed as a

his children. He feels disconnected from his community, unable to share his burden because “they
would think I am weak.”

Mr. T. is a man who survived his son’s illness, but who has not yet survived the memory of it. He
lives in a perpetual state of high alert, a farmer who no longer feels safe on his own land, a
father who cannot trust his own child’s health. He is exhausted, hollow, and haunted by a past that
refuses to stay buried.
