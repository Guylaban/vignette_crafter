**Case Vignette: Mr. L.**

Mr. L. is a 67-year-old married man who has worked as the manager of a mid-sized downtown hotel for
thirty-two years. He presents for his initial sessions with a composed, almost stoic demeanor,
though his hands tremble faintly as he settles into the armchair. He describes the event that
changed everything as “just a Tuesday night.”

It was a cool October evening, eight months ago. Mr. L. had stayed late to reconcile the weekend’s
banquet billing, a task he had performed hundreds of times before. He left through the employee
exit, a side alley he had used for decades. He remembers the crunch of gravel under his shoes, the
smell of damp concrete, and then the sharp pressure of a cold metal object against the back of his
skull. “Don’t move, old man,” a voice said. He froze. The man, young and wiry, took his wallet, his
watch—a retirement gift from his wife—and his grandfather’s silver ring. The robber shoved him hard
against the brick wall. Mr. L.’s glasses snapped, and a shard of lens cut his cheek. He remembers
the sound of footsteps fading, and then the long, hollow silence of the alley.

For three weeks after, Mr. L. did not tell his wife the full story. He said it was a “scare,” a
minor incident. He went back to work the next day, cleaning the blood off his own shirt in the staff
bathroom. He told himself he was fine. But he was not fine.

Now, Mr. L. cannot walk down that alley. He takes a forty-minute detour through the main lobby and
out the front doors, even when it means walking through clouds of cigarette smoke that trigger his
asthma. He has started parking his car three blocks away, in a brightly lit municipal lot, because
the hotel’s garage is “too dark, too tight.” He avoids the employee exit entirely. When a guest
calls his office phone from an unknown extension, his heart slams against his ribs before he
answers. He has begun checking the locks on his office door three times before sitting down, a
ritual he repeats without conscious thought.

At home, his wife has noticed the change. He no longer sits with his back to the living room window.
He insists on keeping the bedroom door open a crack, even in winter, because “I need to hear what’s
coming.” He has stopped wearing his wedding ring because the weight of it against his finger makes
him think of the robber’s hands. He has nightmares, though he calls them “bad dreams.” In them, he
is always in the alley, but the robber’s face is a blur of static, and the gun is always pressing
harder. He wakes in a cold sweat, convinced he heard the gravel crunch again.

His greatest struggle is the silence. Mr. L. is a man who built a career on hospitality, on reading
people, on being in control. Now he feels like a stranger in his own skin. At work, he is sharp with
the front desk staff for minor errors. He cannot tolerate sudden movements—a busboy dropping a tray
sends him into a cold rage. He has begun drinking a single glass of whiskey before bed, something he
never did before, to “take the edge off the jumpiness.” He knows it is not working.

“I used to walk through that hotel like I owned it,” he says, his voice quiet. “Now I feel like I’m
waiting for something bad to happen around every corner. I’m tired, doctor. I’m just so tired.” He
looks down at his bare left hand, where the ring used to be, and says nothing more.
