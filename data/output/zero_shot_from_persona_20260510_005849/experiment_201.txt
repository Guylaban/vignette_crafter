**Patient Vignette: Elena**

Elena is a 70-year-old married woman, a celebrated fashion designer who built her career from a
small sewing machine in her living room. She presents with a composed, elegant demeanor that belies
a profound inner turmoil. She speaks with a slight tremor in her hands, which she often stills by
smoothing the fabric of her own carefully tailored clothing.

The event that shattered her world occurred three years ago, on a warm autumn evening. Elena was
leaving her flagship boutique in the city’s fashion district. As she walked toward her car, she saw
a young woman, a junior designer she had mentored, being violently mugged on the street corner. The
assailant struck the woman, grabbed her bag, and fled. Elena was frozen, just twenty feet away,
unable to move or scream. She watched the entire scene unfold as if in slow motion—the flash of the
metal object, the thud of the impact, the woman’s crumpled form on the pavement. The woman survived,
but Elena did not.

In the months that followed, Elena’s life began to fray at the seams. She stopped walking to her car
alone. She began arriving at work hours early, and leaving only when her husband could meet her at
the door. The vibrant sketches in her studio, once filled with bold colors and flowing lines, became
sparse and monochrome. She found herself avoiding the window of her boutique, preferring the
interior walls. The sounds of the city—a car backfiring, a raised voice, the clatter of a metal
grate—would send a jolt through her body, leaving her breathless and disoriented.

The nightmares started six months after the incident. They are not of the mugging itself, but of a
vague, formless threat. She dreams of being trapped in a dark, endless hallway, hearing footsteps
that grow louder, but she can never turn around to see who is coming. She wakes in a cold sweat, her
heart pounding, convinced that someone is in the room. Her husband, a gentle man, often finds her
sitting upright in bed at 3 a.m., staring at the door.

Elena’s memory of the event is a fragmented, intrusive loop. She cannot recall the assailant’s face,
but she can see the pattern of the young woman’s scarf, the exact angle of the streetlamp’s light,
and the sound of her own shallow, rapid breathing. These images flash into her mind without
warning—while she is pinning a hem, during a fitting, or in the middle of a conversation. She has
learned to pause, to take a sip of water, to excuse herself to the restroom where she can press her
palms against the cool tile until the panic subsides.

Her work, once her sanctuary, has become a source of dread. She cannot bear to be in crowded
showrooms or at industry parties. The close proximity of strangers triggers a visceral response: her
throat tightens, her vision narrows, and she feels a desperate need to escape. She has delegated all
public appearances to her junior partners, a decision that fills her with shame. She tells herself
she is just “getting older,” but she knows the truth: she is afraid of the world she once navigated
with such confidence.

Elena’s relationships have also shifted. She is quick to anger with her husband over small things—a
misplaced set of keys, an unreturned phone call. She feels a deep, persistent irritability that she
cannot explain. She has withdrawn from her grandchildren, making excuses for missed birthdays and
holidays. She feels a heavy guilt, but also a strange relief, in the solitude of her home studio,
where she can control every variable.

her. Her mind is a constant, exhausting calculus of potential danger. She feels hollowed out,
