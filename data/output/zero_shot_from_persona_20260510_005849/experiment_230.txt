**Patient Vignette: Mr. A.**

Mr. A., a 54-year-old software engineer of Middle Eastern descent, presents for therapy at the
urging of his primary care physician. He is a tall, thin man who dresses in neat, understated

fixed on the floor, his voice a low, steady monotone. He is single and has lived alone in a modest
suburban apartment for the past twelve years.

The event that shattered Mr. A.’s world occurred eight years ago, during a business trip to a
regional tech conference. He was 46, a senior architect for a mid-sized firm, respected for his
quiet competence and his ability to solve problems that baffled others. On the last night of the
conference, a colleague—a younger, more charismatic man from a partner company—invited him for a

but he remembers the room spinning, a crushing weight on his chest, and a voice whispering, “Just
relax. You’ll like it.”

deep, physical violation that he could not name for days. The following Monday, back at his desk, he
found himself unable to type. His hands trembled. The smell of his colleague’s cologne—a sharp,
cedar scent—seemed to cling to the air in his cubicle. He began to avoid the break room. He stopped
attending meetings. He told no one.
