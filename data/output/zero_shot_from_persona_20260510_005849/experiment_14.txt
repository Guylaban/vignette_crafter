**Case Vignette: "Maria"**

Maria is a 54-year-old married Latin American woman who presents for therapy at the encouragement of

at her hands as she talks.

The story she tells begins not with a single explosion, but with a morning in July 2012. She was
leading a route clearance patrol outside Kandahar. Her job was to find and neutralize improvised
explosive devices before they could harm her convoy. That morning, she remembers the heat pressing
down like a physical weight, the dust coating her throat. She was scanning the road ahead, her eyes
trained on a patch of disturbed gravel. She signaled the convoy to stop. She dismounted, her heart
thudding against her ribs, her hand steady on her detection equipment. She found nothing. She gave
the all-clear.

forward. It hit a pressure plate IED buried deeper than her equipment could read. The blast was a
wall of sound and heat. She remembers being thrown backward, the taste of copper and grit in her
mouth. She ran to the wreckage. She helped pull one of the soldiers, a young man named Specialist
Reyes, from the burning vehicle. He was alive, but his legs were gone below the knee. She held a
tourniquet on his thigh while she screamed for a medic. She remembers his eyes—wide, unblinking,
fixed on the sky. He didn't scream. He just kept asking her, "Am I going to die, Sergeant?"

He survived. She did not.

For the next decade, Maria continued to deploy, continued to lead patrols, continued to find bombs.
She told herself she was fine. She was a professional. She compartmentalized. She came home to her
husband, a retired electrician, and she did not talk about the work. They had a quiet life in El
