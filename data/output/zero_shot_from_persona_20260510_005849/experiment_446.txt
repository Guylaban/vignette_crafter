**Patient Vignette: Margaret**

Margaret is a 77-year-old widow who presents for therapy at the urging of her daughter. She is a
retired school counselor, a role she held for over thirty years, and she still lives alone in the
same modest house she shared with her late husband, Frank. She arrives for sessions dressed neatly
in cardigans and slacks, her silver hair carefully brushed, yet there is a tremor in her hands that
has nothing to do with age.

The trauma that haunts Margaret occurred on a Tuesday afternoon in late October, 2018. She was at
the high school where she worked, sitting in her small office adjacent to the main hallway. A fire
alarm sounded, and she began her standard protocol: guiding students toward the east exit. But this
was not a drill. A former student, a young man she had counseled three years prior for depression,
had returned with a rifle. Margaret remembers the sound first—a sharp, percussive crack that seemed
to split the air. She remembers the screams, the stampede of feet, the way the fluorescent lights
flickered. She remembers seeing him, his face blank and unrecognizable, as he walked past her door.
She did not run. She pulled two freshmen girls into her office, locked the door, and pressed them
into the corner behind her filing cabinet. She held a finger to her lips, her heart hammering so
violently she was certain it would give her away. For seventeen minutes, they listened to the chaos.
When it was over, six people were dead, including the shooter. Margaret was not physically harmed,
but she has not been the same since.

In the months after, Margaret threw herself back into her work. She told herself that routine was
healing, that her students needed her. But she found herself scanning every hallway, every classroom
door. She began to flinch at the sound of a slamming locker. She started arriving early to check the
exits, and staying late to ensure the building was empty. She developed a hypervigilance that
exhausted her. She stopped eating lunch in the staff lounge because the chatter felt too loud, too
chaotic. She started taking a different route home each day, avoiding the main road where the police
cars had gathered.

Six months ago, at age 77, Margaret retired. She tells me she was "tired of being afraid." But
retirement has not brought peace. In the quiet of her home, the memories are louder. She cannot sit
with her back to a door in a restaurant. She has stopped going to the grocery store because the
crowds feel like a trap. She sleeps in the living room now, on the couch, because she cannot bear
the walk down the dark hallway to her bedroom. She wakes three or four times a night, her body
drenched in sweat, convinced she hears footsteps or the click of a lock. Her daughter found her last
month standing at the kitchen window at 3:00 AM, clutching a fireplace poker.
