**Patient Vignette: Eleanor**

Eleanor is a 61-year-old married woman who works as a policy analyst for a federal environmental
agency. She presents as composed and articulate, her gray hair neatly pinned back, her posture
erect. She sits on the edge of the armchair, hands folded in her lap, and speaks with the measured
precision of someone who has spent decades drafting regulatory language.

The event that changed everything happened fourteen months ago, during a routine site visit to an
industrial facility. Eleanor had conducted hundreds of such visits over her career. This one was to
a chemical processing plant in a rural district, where a subcontractor had reported an "anomalous
release" of a solvent mixture. She was there to observe, to document, to write the report. She
remembers the sharp, sweet smell the moment she stepped out of the car—like nail polish remover and
burnt sugar. The plant manager assured her it was "just a minor venting issue, well within safety
margins." She believed him.

She spent approximately forty-five minutes inside the facility, walking through the processing
floor, taking notes, asking questions. She did not wear a respirator; she was told it was
unnecessary. By the time she returned to her car, her eyes were watering and she had a metallic
taste in her mouth. She drove home feeling slightly nauseated, attributing it to the heat.

That night, she woke at 3:00 AM with a crushing sensation in her chest, her heart hammering, her
skin slick with sweat. She thought she was having a heart attack. Her husband, Richard, drove her to
the emergency room. The doctors ran tests—EKG, blood work, chest X-ray—and found nothing. They
diagnosed anxiety and sent her home.

But the symptoms did not stop. Over the following weeks, Eleanor developed a persistent cough,
intermittent dizziness, and a sensitivity to smells that bordered on unbearable. Perfume, cleaning
products, even the smell of coffee from her husband's mug would trigger a wave of nausea and a
pounding headache. She visited three specialists: a pulmonologist, an allergist, a neurologist. Each
ran tests. Each found nothing. One suggested she was experiencing "post-traumatic stress related to
the exposure." Eleanor dismissed this. She was a policy analyst, not a soldier or a survivor of
violence. She did not have trauma.

She began avoiding certain rooms in her own house. The laundry room, where the bleach was stored.
The garage, where Richard kept paint thinner. She stopped going to the grocery store because the
cleaning aisle made her chest tighten. She started checking the weather forecast obsessively, noting
wind direction, afraid that a chemical plume from the industrial park ten miles away might drift
toward her home.

At work, her performance faltered. She found herself unable to concentrate on documents that once
came easily to her. She would read the same paragraph three times. She began arriving late, then
calling in sick. Her supervisor, a younger woman Eleanor had mentored, expressed concern. Eleanor
felt a deep shame. She had always been reliable, competent, the person others turned to. Now she was
the one who needed accommodation.

The nightmares started about six months ago. They are not about the plant. They are about being
trapped in a room with no windows, the air growing thick and sweet, her lungs filling with something
invisible. She wakes gasping, convinced she is suffocating. Richard has learned not to touch her
when she wakes; she has flailed at him twice.

On the PCL-5, Eleanor scored a 33, endorsing items related to intrusive memories, physiological
reactivity to cues, avoidance of external reminders, and negative beliefs about herself. She does
not see herself as traumatized. She sees herself as someone who made a mistake—who trusted the wrong
person, who failed to advocate for her own safety. She says, "I knew better. I taught the safety
protocols. I just didn't follow them."

She still goes to work, but she has stopped volunteering for site visits. She requests only desk
assignments. She tells herself this is temporary. She tells herself she will get over it. But when
she smells anything chemical—a Sharpie marker, a gas pump, a freshly painted wall—her body reacts
before her mind can intervene. Her throat closes. Her palms sweat. She looks for the exit.
