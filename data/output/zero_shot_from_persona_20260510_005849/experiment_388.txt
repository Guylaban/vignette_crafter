**Case Vignette: "Mara"**

Mara is a 33-year-old network engineer of Pacific Islander descent, recently divorced. She presents
as articulate and composed, though her posture is rigid, and her eyes rarely settle on one point for
long. Over several sessions, she has begun to describe the event that she says "broke something"
inside her.

The story begins two years ago. Mara’s five-year-old daughter, Leilani, began running unexplained
fevers. Mara, a single mother by then, initially dismissed it as a virus. But when Leilani’s joints
swelled and she began to limp, a cascade of medical appointments followed. The diagnosis came on a
Tuesday: juvenile idiopathic arthritis with a rare, systemic onset that could affect her internal
organs. The doctor used the word "life-threatening." Mara remembers the fluorescent hum of the exam
room, the way the doctor’s lips moved but the words seemed to arrive late, like a dubbed film. She
remembers Leilani asking, "Mommy, are you okay?" and realizing she was not breathing.

In the months that followed, Mara’s world narrowed to the hospital. She slept in a vinyl recliner
next to Leilani’s bed, her body tuned to the beep of the IV pump. She learned to read the lab
results like network traffic logs, searching for anomalies. She stopped going to work, stopped
answering calls from her ex-husband, stopped eating meals that weren’t vending machine crackers. She
remembers the day Leilani’s kidneys began to fail. She remembers the exact shade of blue the code
team’s scrubs were. She remembers the social worker handing her a pamphlet on "caregiver burnout"
and feeling a flash of rage so pure it scared her.

Leilani survived. After six months of aggressive immunosuppression, her inflammation markers
normalized. She is now in remission, a rambunctious first-grader who hates vegetables and loves
soccer. Mara tells me this with a flat affect, as if reading a weather report for a distant city.
"She’s fine," she says. "I should be fine."

But she is not fine. At work, where she manages the core network for a regional bank, Mara finds
herself unable to concentrate. She will stare at a terminal emulator screen for forty-five minutes,
the code blurring, because a sudden beep from a colleague’s phone sounds exactly like the cardiac
monitor in the PICU. She has begun avoiding the server room because the hum of the cooling fans
reminds her of the ventilator. She now works exclusively from a cubicle, her back to the wall, a
position she knows is irrational but cannot abandon.

At night, she does not sleep. She lies awake, running "what if" scenarios like fault trees. What if
she had pushed for a second opinion earlier? What if she had missed the first sign of organ failure?
The guilt is a constant, low-voltage current. She has stopped driving past the hospital, taking
three-mile detours to avoid the sight of its glass facade. She cannot watch medical dramas, cannot
hear a child cry in a grocery store without her heart rate spiking.

Her divorce, finalized six months ago, she describes as "collateral damage." Her husband did not
understand why she became "so cold," why she could not just "move on." She did not tell him that
every time he touched her, she felt the phantom weight of a sleeping child in her arms, or that
intimacy felt like a betrayal of the vigilance she still maintains.

Mara’s PCL-5 score is 44, a number that reflects the hypervigilance, the intrusive memories, the
emotional numbing. She knows she is struggling. She came to therapy because her boss gently
suggested it after she snapped at a junior engineer for tapping a pen on a desk. "I don't feel like
myself," she said in our first session. "I feel like a network that's been partitioned. I can't
route anything correctly anymore."

She is still waiting for the alarm to stop sounding in her head.
