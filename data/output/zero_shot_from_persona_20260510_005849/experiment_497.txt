**Patient Vignette: “Malia”**

Malia is a 20-year-old woman of Oceania descent, who presents as quiet and meticulously composed.
She is a single woman working as an auditor for a mid-sized firm. Over our sessions, she has begun
to speak about the year that changed everything.

It started about fourteen months ago, when she was still a temp at the firm. She noticed a man—a
senior data analyst—start to appear in her vicinity with unusual frequency. At first, she thought it
was coincidence. He’d stand in the same line at the coffee cart, take the late elevator just as she
did, and offer small, benign smiles. She dismissed the feeling of being watched as paranoia. She is
an auditor; she is trained to notice patterns, but she told herself she was over-analyzing.

The pattern, however, escalated. He began leaving notes on her desk, folded into origami animals.
The content was always vaguely complimentary—*“You look tired today. I hope you rest.”*—but the fact
that he knew her schedule, her fatigue, made her stomach clench. She remembers the day she found a
photograph on her windshield. It was a picture of her back, walking away from the office, taken from
across the street. No note. Just the photo.

Malia stopped going to the break room. She started taking different routes to her car, checking her
mirrors obsessively. She told her supervisor, who was sympathetic but said the company “couldn’t act
without proof.” She filed a police report, but without a direct threat, they offered little more
than a case number.

The stalker was fired six months later for unrelated misconduct, but Malia’s world had already
shrunk. She describes the feeling now as being wrapped in a cellophane bag—she can see the world
moving outside, but she cannot feel a part of it. She lives in a constant state of *scanning*. In
the grocery store, she counts the exits. In a restaurant, she sits with her back to the wall. She
says she knows logically he is gone, but her body does not believe it.

Her work as an auditor has become her only sanctuary, but even that is fraying. She can sit for
hours, hyper-focused on spreadsheets, because the numbers are predictable. They don’t hide. However,
she has started to flinch at the sound of a printer jamming, and the sight of a folded piece of
paper on her desk—even a sticky note from a colleague—makes her heart pound so hard she sees spots.
She has taken to working from home three days a week, citing “productivity,” but really, she is
hiding.

Her sleep is a battlefield. She sleeps in the living room now, with all the lights on, because the
bedroom has too many blind spots. She has a chair wedged under the doorknob. She describes her
dreams as “loud”—full of running, of a shadow that is always exactly one step behind her. She wakes
up more tired than when she went to bed.

The most painful part, she says, is the isolation. Her friends from university invite her out, but
she cannot tolerate crowds. She declines every invitation, and the texts have stopped coming. She
feels a deep shame, a belief that she is “broken” because she cannot simply move on from a man who
was just “creepy.” She knows intellectually that stalking is a crime of terror, but emotionally, she
feels she is weak for still being afraid.

In session, she sits very still, her hands folded in her lap. She speaks in a low, even tone, as if
reading a financial report about someone else’s life. She is here because her boss told her she
seemed “distant” and suggested she take a leave of absence. The thought of leaving her routine
terrifies her more than staying.

She looks at me with steady, exhausted eyes and says, “I used to be someone who walked fast because
I had places to go. Now I walk fast because I’m afraid of who is behind me.”
