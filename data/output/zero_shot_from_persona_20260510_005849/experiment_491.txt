**Patient Vignette: Margaret**

Margaret is a 76-year-old woman, divorced, and a long-serving city council member. She presents as
impeccably dressed, her silver hair cut in a sharp, no-nonsense bob. She sits forward in her chair,
her posture rigid, her hands clasped tightly in her lap. She describes herself as a "survivor," a
word she uses with a brittle pride that does not reach her eyes.

Her story begins in the kitchen of her childhood home in a quiet, tree-lined suburb. She is seven
years old. Her father, a respected accountant, has come home late again. The air is thick with the
smell of burnt pot roast and her mother’s silent, trembling fear. Margaret is at the table, doing
her homework, when the sound erupts. Not a shout, at first, but a low, guttural accusation that
spirals into a scream. Then the crash of a plate against the wall. She remembers the specific sound
of her mother’s body hitting the linoleum floor, a soft, terrible thud. She remembers the silence
that followed, broken only by her mother’s ragged, wet breathing. Margaret did not scream. She did
not run. She sat perfectly still, her pencil frozen above her arithmetic, learning a lesson that
would define her life: that safety is an illusion, and that to be noticed is to be a target.

For the next nine years, this was the rhythm of her life. The explosions were unpredictable, the
apologies predictable. She learned to read the subtle shifts in her father’s posture, the tightening
of his jaw, the way his hand would curl into a fist before he even spoke. She became hypervigilant,
a state she now recognizes as her normal. She learned to make herself small, to be invisible, to be
the perfect, quiet, high-achieving daughter who never caused a ripple. She escaped into books and
schoolwork, building a fortress of achievement.

She left for college at seventeen and never went back. She married a quiet, gentle man, a stark
contrast to her father. But the safety she craved never materialized. She found herself unable to
trust his quietness, constantly waiting for the other shoe to drop. She would lie awake at night,
listening for the wrong kind of footstep, the wrong tone of voice. Her husband, bewildered by her
constant anxiety and her need for control, eventually withdrew. The marriage ended after twenty
years, not with a bang, but with a long, slow erosion of understanding.

Politics became her new escape. On the city council, her hypervigilance became a superpower. She
could spot a procedural error from across the room, sense a political betrayal before it was
whispered. She built a reputation as a tireless, unyielding advocate, a woman who could not be
intimidated. Her colleagues see her as formidable, even cold. They don’t know that every public
hearing, every tense negotiation, feels like that kitchen floor. The raised voices of public comment
send a jolt of electricity down her spine. A man in a suit pointing a finger in her direction makes
her stomach clench with a terror she has carried for seventy years.

She lives alone now in a meticulously ordered condominium. The locks are checked three times before
bed. The mail is sorted immediately. Any disruption to her schedule, a delayed committee meeting or
an unexpected phone call, can send her into a spiral of irritable anxiety. She has a small circle of
friends, but she keeps them at arm’s length. Intimacy feels like a trap.

She came to therapy not for the past, but for the present. She has started having nightmares—not of
her father, but of a faceless, shouting man in a council chamber. She finds herself snapping at
staff members for minor mistakes, her heart pounding as if she were fighting for her life. The
strategies that have kept her safe for seven decades are starting to fail her. She is exhausted. She
is 76 years old, and she is still that seven-year-old girl, sitting perfectly still, waiting for the
next crash.
