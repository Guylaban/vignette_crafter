**Patient: Fatima**
**Age:** 80
**Occupation:** Retired social worker
**Trauma:** Her son’s life-threatening illness at age 7

---

Fatima is an eighty-year-old widow who lives alone in a small apartment filled with framed
photographs and dried flowers. She speaks with a soft, deliberate voice, often pausing to press her
palm flat against her chest as if steadying something inside. She came to therapy at her daughter’s
urging, after months of refusing to leave her home except for groceries and mosque.

The trauma began fifty-three years ago, when her youngest son, Khalil, was seven. He developed a
high fever that would not break, followed by seizures that left his small body rigid and his eyes
rolled back. Fatima remembers the emergency room as a blur of fluorescent lights and the sound of
her own breath catching. The doctors told her Khalil had bacterial meningitis. They used words like
“sepsis” and “brain swelling.” She remembers a nurse pulling her aside and saying, *“You should call
your husband.”*

Khalil survived. He is now a healthy father of three, living in another state. But Fatima did not
survive the illness the way he did.

For the first few years after Khalil’s recovery, she functioned. She returned to her job as a social
worker at a community mental health center, advocating for families whose children had chronic
illnesses, attending IEP meetings, and coordinating care. She was good at her work—empathic,
precise, relentless. Colleagues described her as “the one who never forgets a detail.” But what they
did not know was that she could not stop remembering. Every fever in a client’s child sent her heart
racing. Every hospital discharge summary she typed made her fingers tremble. She began to avoid
pediatric cases. Eventually, she requested a transfer to adult services, telling her supervisor she
needed “a change of pace.”

Now, in retirement, the avoidance has become total. She does not visit Khalil. She has not seen him
in four years. She tells herself it is the cost of travel, her age, her knees. But the truth is that
every time she considers booking a flight, she feels a tightness in her throat and a heat behind her
eyes. She cannot bear to see him healthy, because seeing him healthy reminds her that he was almost
not.

The nightmares began about ten years ago, after her husband died. In the dreams, Khalil is seven
again, but she cannot find the hospital. She is driving in circles, the backseat empty, the fever
climbing. She wakes up gasping, her nightgown soaked, and spends the rest of the night walking from
room to room, checking the locks, touching the frames of photographs as if counting the living.

Her days are structured now by avoidance and ritual. She does not watch television shows with sick
children. She leaves the room during commercials for children’s hospitals. She has not opened the
box of Khalil’s childhood drawings since her husband’s funeral. She knows where the box is—in the
back of her closet, under a quilt—and she knows she will not open it.

Fatima’s PCL-5 score is 45, indicating severe PTSD symptoms. She reports feeling “numb” during
family gatherings, unable to feel joy when her grandchildren call. She has stopped attending the
mosque’s women’s group because someone brought up a story about a child in the ICU. She says she
feels like a “broken machine” that keeps running the same memory over and over, even though the
emergency is long over.

She does not believe she deserves treatment. “Other mothers lost their children,” she says, her
voice barely above a whisper. “I still have mine. What right do I have to suffer?”

She comes to therapy because her daughter said, *“Mama, you are not here anymore. You are still in
that hospital room.”* Fatima does not know if that is true. But she is tired of the dreams. She is
tired of the box in the closet. She is tired of being afraid of her own son’s face.
