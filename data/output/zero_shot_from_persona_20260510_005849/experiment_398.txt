**Patient Vignette: Mr. K.**

Mr. K. is a 74-year-old, retired welder, a slight man with calloused hands and a stillness that
feels like held breath. He has been married to his wife, Linh, for 49 years. They live in a quiet
suburb, in a house he built with his own hands. He presents for therapy at the urging of his adult
daughter, who is concerned about his "moods."

Mr. K. was born in a small coastal village in Vietnam. In 1968, during the Tet Offensive, his

the guns stopped, broken only by the buzzing of flies.

After the war, he fled by boat. He spent two years in a refugee camp in Thailand, a period he
describes as a "long wait in a cage." He met Linh there. He came to the United States in 1975,
sponsored by a church group. He found work as a welder in a shipyard, a job he held for 40 years. He
was good at it. The work was precise, physical, and loud—a noise he could control with his hood and
his torch.

Now, in retirement, the silence is a problem.

Mr. K. does not sleep. He lies awake next to Linh, his body rigid, listening. He hears the creak of

knows he has moved. He has not told Linh about these moments. He simply goes to the kitchen, drinks
a glass of water, and stares at the dark window until his hands stop shaking. He feels a deep,
