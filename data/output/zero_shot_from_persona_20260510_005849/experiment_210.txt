**Case Vignette: "Mariam"**

Mariam is a 34-year-old Middle Eastern woman who presents with a guarded demeanor, her hands folded
tightly in her lap. She speaks softly, her eyes often fixed on a point just beyond my shoulder, as
if she is watching a scene replay in the air between us. She is a divorced mother of two young
children, and she works as an agricultural laborer in the Central Valley. She came to therapy at the
urging of her primary care doctor, who grew concerned about her escalating panic attacks and
persistent insomnia.

The trauma that shattered her world occurred two years ago, on a hot September afternoon. Mariam was
working in a row of almond trees, the mechanical shaker roaring nearby. She saw a man from her
crew—a quiet father of three named Hassan—stumble and fall into the path of the harvester. She heard
the sound before she understood what it was: a wet, percussive thud followed by a sickening silence
as the machine ground to a halt. She was the first person to reach him. The image is burned into her
memory: the unnatural angle of his arm, the stillness of his chest, the red dust settling on his
face.

In the immediate aftermath, Mariam went numb. She remembers being led away by a supervisor,
answering questions for the police, and driving home in a haze. That night, she could not sleep.
Every time she closed her eyes, she saw Hassan’s face, but in her mind, it was her own face. Or her
son’s face. The image shifts, but the terror remains constant.

Over the following weeks, the nightmares began. They are always the same: she is standing in the
orchard, but the trees are made of flesh, and the harvester is chasing her. She wakes up gasping,
drenched in sweat, her heart pounding so hard she thinks she might die. She has learned to sleep
with the light on, and she keeps her bedroom door open so she can hear her children breathing.

At work, Mariam has become hypervigilant. She cannot stand the sound of the machinery. The low
rumble of a tractor makes her stomach clench and her palms sweat. She has started working at the far
end of the rows, as far from the equipment as possible, even if it means taking longer to finish her
tasks. Her supervisor has noticed and has written her up twice for "slow production." She does not
explain why. She is terrified of being seen as weak, or worse, as someone who cannot do her job.

Her relationships have suffered. She divorced her husband three years before the accident, but they

convinced that if she lets her guard down for even a moment, the memory will ambush her.

Mariam’s emotional world has constricted. She no longer feels joy when her daughter brings home a
good report card. She feels nothing when her son hugs her. She goes through the motions of
motherhood—cooking dinner, helping with homework—but she is a ghost in her own home. She describes

driven past the orchard where it happened. She has not spoken to any of her former coworkers from
