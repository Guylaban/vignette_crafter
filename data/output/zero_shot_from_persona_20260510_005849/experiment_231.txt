**Patient Vignette: Mr. A.**

Mr. A. is a 37-year-old married South Asian man who works as a clinical psychologist in a community
mental health clinic. He presents for therapy at the urging of his wife, who has grown increasingly
concerned about his withdrawal, irritability, and what she describes as “not being present” at home.
He is a quiet, articulate man who speaks with careful precision, often pausing to choose the right
word. He holds his hands folded in his lap, and his posture is rigid.

The trauma occurred eighteen months ago. Mr. A. was walking through a crowded outdoor market in his
city during his lunch break. He had just bought a cup of chai and was checking his phone. He heard a
sound he initially dismissed as a car backfiring. Then came a second, closer sound, followed by
screaming. He remembers the physical sensation of being pushed sideways by the shockwave, the hot
spray of shattered glass against his neck, and the sudden, overwhelming smell of dust and cordite.
He saw a woman in a blue salwar kameez fall to the ground a few feet from him. He did not see the
attacker. He ran, not heroically, but blindly, his mind a blank static of pure animal flight.

In the weeks following the bombing, Mr. A. did what he had always done: he worked. He saw his
clients, listened to their traumas, and felt a strange, hollow competence. He told himself he was
fine. He was a psychologist, after all. He understood the cognitive model of PTSD, the mechanisms of
avoidance and hyperarousal. He believed that this intellectual understanding would inoculate him.

It has not.

In session, Mr. A. describes a world that has become a minefield of triggers. The sound of a
motorcycle backfiring on his street sends him diving for cover in his own driveway. The smell of
diesel fuel at a gas station makes his heart hammer so violently he has to pull over. He has stopped
going to the grocery store because the crowds feel like a trap. He cannot sit with his back to a
door in a restaurant, and he now scans every room he enters for exits and potential cover.

His sleep is a nightmare. He wakes three or four times a night, drenched in sweat, his body rigid,
the sound of the blast echoing in his ears. He has vivid, repetitive dreams where he is frozen in
the marketplace, unable to run, while the woman in the blue salwar kameez turns to look at him with
empty eye sockets. He has learned to fear sleep.

The most painful change, he says, is in his marriage. He and his wife, a teacher, used to be close.
Now, he feels a pane of glass between them. He cannot tolerate her touch at night. Her attempts to
comfort him feel suffocating. He has become short-tempered, snapping at her for small things—a dish
left in the sink, a question about his day. He knows he is hurting her, but the rage feels like a
separate, living thing inside him, a hot, defensive animal that lashes out before he can stop it. He
has started drinking a glass of whiskey every night to take the edge off before bed.

His work, once his identity, has become a source of profound distress. He finds himself dissociating
during sessions with clients who describe their own histories of violence. He will hear his own
voice asking a question, but feel as though he is floating near the ceiling, watching a stranger
perform the role of a therapist. The empathy he once felt so naturally now feels like a liability, a
door that might swing open and let the horror flood in. He has started referring new trauma cases to
colleagues, a decision that fills him with shame.

On his intake, Mr. A. scored a 69 on the PCL-5, a score that reflects the severe, pervasive nature
of his symptoms. He tells me, with a wry, painful smile, that he knows he meets the criteria for
PTSD “by the book.” He knows the avoidance is making it worse. He knows the hypervigilance is
exhausting him. But knowing, he has discovered, is not the same as being able to stop. He feels
trapped, not just by the memory of the bombing, but by his own professional awareness of how broken
he is. He is a man who treats the wounded, and now finds himself unable to stanch his own bleeding.
