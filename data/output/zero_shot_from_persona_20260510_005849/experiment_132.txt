**Patient Vignette: "Kai"**

Kai is an 18-year-old photographer who presents as quiet and watchful. He sits forward in his chair,
hands clasped between his knees, his eyes scanning the room before settling on a fixed point on the
wall. He speaks in short, deliberate sentences, his voice flat, as if he is describing something
that happened to someone else.

Kai was born in a rural village in Southeast Asia. When he was nine, his family fled escalating
conflict in their region, eventually settling in a refugee camp across the border. He remembers the
journey mostly in fragments: the sound of distant artillery, his mother’s hand clamped over his
mouth, the smell of diesel and wet earth. In the camp, he found a discarded film camera. He taught
himself to shoot by photographing the dust storms, the barbed wire, the way light fell on his
younger sister’s face as she slept. The camera became a kind of armor—a way to hold the world at a
distance.

When he was sixteen, his family was resettled in the United States. Two years later, driven by a
sense of duty and a desire to "give back" to a country that had given him refuge, he enlisted in the
Army. He was assigned to a support unit, not a combat role, but his commanding officer quickly
learned of his photography skills. He was tasked with documenting patrols, reconnaissance missions,
and—eventually—the aftermath of engagements.

The event that changed everything happened six months ago. Kai was embedded with a squad during a
night patrol in a dense, unfamiliar region. The mission was routine: clear a village, verify
intelligence. But the intelligence was wrong. The squad walked into an ambush. Kai remembers the
first shot—a sharp, wet crack that seemed to come from inside his own chest. He dropped to the
ground, his camera still around his neck, and began shooting. Not photographs. He wasn't shooting
photographs. He was lying in the dirt, mud in his mouth, watching the man next to him—Private First
Class Miller, who had taught him how to clean his rifle—take a round to the throat. Miller made a
sound Kai had never heard a human make. A gurgling, sucking sound. Kai tried to apply pressure to
the wound, but his hands were shaking too hard. Miller’s blood was warm and thin, pooling under
Kai’s knees. By the time the medevac arrived, Miller was gone.

Kai doesn’t talk about what happened next. He was evacuated with a concussion from a blast wave, but
no visible wounds. He spent two weeks in a medical unit, then was sent home. He was discharged three
weeks ago.

Now, Kai lives alone in a small apartment. He has not touched his camera since that night. His
equipment sits in a duffel bag in the corner of his closet, unopened. He tried to photograph a
sunrise last week, but when he raised the viewfinder to his eye, his hands began to tremble, and he
saw Miller’s face, the way his mouth had opened, the way the light had gone out of his eyes. He put
the camera down and did not pick it up again.

He avoids crowds. He avoids loud noises. He avoids the smell of diesel. He has not been able to
sleep through the night in months; he wakes at 2:47 AM every morning, drenched in sweat, certain he
can hear the crack of gunfire in the parking lot. He has started carrying a pocketknife, though he
does not know what he would do with it.

He tells me he feels "numb," like he is watching his own life from a great distance. He cannot
remember the faces of his parents without effort. He cannot remember what it felt like to be excited

the frames. The dark parts."

feel like I’m here. But I don’t know how to get back."
