**Patient Vignette: "Mr. Chan"**

Mr. Chan is a 46-year-old South Eastern Asian man who presents to therapy at the urging of his fire
department’s peer support team. He has been a firefighter for over twenty years, a career that once
gave him a sense of purpose and brotherhood. He is divorced, living alone in a small apartment, and
has not spoken to his ex-wife or his two adult children in nearly three years.

The trauma that haunts him is not a single fire or rescue. It is the six months he spent as a
volunteer relief worker in a war-torn region, three years ago. He went there believing his skills as
a firefighter could save lives. He describes the first weeks as a blur of dust, noise, and urgency.
He remembers pulling a child from the rubble of a school, the girl’s leg mangled, her eyes wide and
silent. He remembers the smell of wet concrete and copper. He remembers the constant, low hum of
drones overhead.

He tells me, his voice flat, that the worst part was not the violence itself, but the *after*. The
silence. The waiting. The endless paperwork for bodies that would never be identified. He spent two
days digging through the remains of a market, finding only fragments of fabric and bone. He stopped
sleeping. He stopped eating with the other volunteers. He started drinking at night, alone, in his
tent.

When he came home, he expected to feel relief. Instead, he felt a profound, cold emptiness. His
apartment felt foreign. The sounds of his city—traffic, sirens, laughter—seemed muffled, like he was
hearing them from underwater. He returned to work, but the first structure fire he entered sent him
into a panic. The smoke, the heat, the shouting—it was not a fire scene. It was the war zone. He
froze. His captain had to pull him out. Since then, he has been on light duty, answering phones and
doing equipment checks. He avoids the bay where the trucks are parked.

His sleep is a battlefield. He wakes three or four times a night, drenched in sweat, his heart
pounding. The dreams are not literal replays, but fragments: a sound, a color, a smell. The smell of
wet concrete. The sound of a child crying. He will not enter the grocery store because the smell of
the produce section—rotting vegetables and damp cardboard—triggers a full-body flashback. He has

He used to be the one who made the coffee, who told the jokes. Now he sits in the corner of the
firehouse break room, staring at the floor, waiting for his shift to end.

He has not touched alcohol in two months. He says it was making the nightmares worse. But the
sobriety has only revealed the raw, unvarnished truth of his days: he is a man who no longer feels
safe in his own skin. He avoids crowds. He avoids loud noises. He avoids the news. He avoids his
family, because he cannot bear to see the worry in their eyes. He tells me he feels like a ghost,
walking through a world that is still alive, while he is already gone.
