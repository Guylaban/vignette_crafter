**Patient Vignette: “Elena”**

Elena is a 39-year-old married Latin American woman who works as a program coordinator for a local
non-profit that supports immigrant families. She presents as carefully composed, her dark hair
pulled back, her clothes neat but unassuming. She sits forward in her chair, hands clasped tightly
in her lap, as she begins to tell her story.

The assault happened fourteen months ago. Elena had stayed late at the community center, finishing
grant paperwork after a long evening workshop. It was nearly 10 p.m. when she locked up and walked
toward her car in the dimly lit parking lot. She remembers the crunch of gravel under her boots, the
cold air sharp against her face. Then a man’s voice, close behind her. Before she could turn, an arm
locked around her throat, and a hand clamped over her mouth. He dragged her behind the dumpster,
pressed something hard against her ribs—she never saw if it was a knife or a gun—and told her not to
scream. She didn’t. She went still, the way her mother had taught her: *No te muevas, mija. No les
des razón.* Don’t move. Don’t give them a reason.

He took her purse, her phone, her grandmother’s silver cross necklace. He shoved her face into the
gravel and ran. She lay there for what felt like hours, her cheek stinging, her breath coming in
shallow gasps, until a security guard from the building next door found her.

In the months since, Elena’s life has narrowed. She stopped driving to work; her husband, Carlos,
now drops her off and picks her up every day, even though it adds an hour to his commute. She cannot
sit with her back to a door. In meetings at the non-profit, she chooses the chair against the wall,
her eyes scanning the room constantly, cataloging exits. Her colleagues have noticed her flinch when
someone touches her shoulder unexpectedly. They think she’s just stressed.

At night, she does not sleep. Not really. She lies awake, replaying the sound of his voice, the
weight of his arm, the smell of cigarette smoke and sweat. When she finally drifts off, she dreams
of gravel digging into her palms, of running but never moving. She wakes with her heart pounding so
hard she feels it in her teeth. Carlos reaches for her, but she stiffens. She cannot bear to be
held. She cannot bear to be touched without warning.

She has become hypervigilant in ways that exhaust her. She checks the back seat of her car before
getting in, even though she no longer drives. She scans the faces of men in parking lots, on
sidewalks, in grocery store aisles. She avoids the community center entirely now—she works from
home, her laptop propped on the kitchen table, her back to the wall. Her supervisor has been
patient, but Elena knows the grant deadlines are piling up. She feels a constant, low-grade guilt,
as though she is failing everyone.

The worst part, she says, is the silence. She has not told her mother or her sisters what happened.
They would worry, she explains. They would blame her for working late, for being alone, for not
fighting back. She carries the story like a stone in her chest. Sometimes, in the shower, she lets
herself cry, the water drowning out the sound.

When asked what she wants from therapy, Elena looks down at her hands. “I want to stop being
afraid,” she says quietly. “I want to go back to my center. I want to feel like myself again.” But
her voice wavers, as though she does not quite believe it is possible.
