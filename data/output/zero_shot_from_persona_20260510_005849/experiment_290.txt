**Patient Vignette: Forensic Analyst (46, Female, Oceania)**

The patient is a 46-year-old woman of Oceania descent, married, and a forensic analyst for a state
crime laboratory. She presents for therapy at the urging of her husband, who has become increasingly
concerned about her withdrawal, irritability, and “night terrors.” She arrives dressed impeccably in
a dark blazer and pressed slacks, but her hands tremble slightly as she adjusts her wedding ring.
She speaks in a careful, measured tone, as if testifying.

**The Event (Two Years Ago)**

The trauma occurred on a Tuesday afternoon. Her younger brother, a commercial fisherman, was lost at
sea when his vessel capsized during a sudden squall off the coast of the Solomon Islands. The
patient received the news via a frantic phone call from her mother. There was no body. No wreckage
was found for three weeks. Because of her professional role, the patient was asked by the family to
liaise with the local maritime authorities. She spent those three weeks analyzing satellite data,
weather patterns, and communication logs—the very same tools she uses daily to reconstruct crime
scenes. She remembers staring at a sonar image on her work computer, trying to find a shape that
could be a hull, a life raft, a body. “I saw the data before I felt the grief,” she says.

**The Aftermath: The Thinking and Feeling**

In the months that followed, the patient’s analytical mind became a prison. She cannot stop
“reconstructing” the event. She mentally reviews the weather report for that day, the last known
coordinates, the time of the mayday call. She calculates the water temperature, the estimated
survival time, the likelihood of drowning versus hypothermia. This mental review is not a choice; it
is a compulsive loop that plays every night as she tries to fall asleep. She feels a cold, clinical
dread—not the raw ache of loss, but a sterile horror at the randomness and the unrecoverable nature
of the data. “My brother was reduced to a probability,” she says, her voice flat. “I know too much
about how things end. I can’t un-know it.”

**Symptoms in Daily Life: The Doing**

Her work has become a minefield. Every case she analyzes—a homicide, a suspicious death, a vehicle

hypervigilant about his safety—calling him multiple times during his commute, demanding he text her
