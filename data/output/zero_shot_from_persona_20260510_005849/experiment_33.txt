Maria sits at the edge of the chair, back straight, hands resting on her knees as if she’s waiting
for an order. She’s a staff sergeant in the Army, and every part of her posture says *ready*. But
her eyes tell a different story—they flicker to the window, then to the door, then back to me. She
apologizes twice for being late, even though she was early.

She tells me about the earthquake. It was two years ago, during a deployment in a mountainous
region. She was leading a convoy through a narrow valley when the ground began to roll. At first,
she thought it was an IED. That’s what soldiers think. But then the road split open in front of her
vehicle, and the mountain on her left began to fall.

“I remember the sound,” she says. “Not the shaking. The sound. Like the whole world was chewing
itself apart.”

She ordered her team to dismount, to take cover. She pulled a private from the back of a Humvee
seconds before a boulder crushed it. She remembers the private’s name—Martinez. He was nineteen. He
cried. She told him to shut up and move. She didn’t mean to be harsh, she says, but there was no
time.

For six hours, they were trapped. The road was gone. The radio was dead. She had to keep her squad
calm, keep them from panicking, keep them from drinking their water too fast. She checked on each of
them, counted them again and again. She remembers the taste of dust. She remembers the smell of
diesel and blood—though no one in her squad was bleeding. It was an animal, she thinks. A deer,
maybe.

Now, back home, Maria cannot stop counting. She counts the tiles in my office ceiling. She counts
the steps from her car to the front door. She counts her husband’s breaths at night because if she
doesn’t, she’s sure he’ll stop.

She has not slept through the night since the earthquake. She wakes at 3:17 a.m.—the exact time the
shaking stopped—and lies still, listening. She checks the windows. She checks the doors. She runs
her hand along the walls to make sure they’re solid. Her husband, a civilian, has learned not to
touch her when she wakes. “He reached for me once,” she says. “I almost broke his wrist.”

At work, she is still a good soldier. Her evaluations are excellent. But she volunteers for every
night shift, every extra detail. She cannot sit still. When she is idle, she hears the mountain.
When she closes her eyes, she sees Martinez’s face. She has not told anyone that she sometimes
cannot remember the way home from the base, that she has driven past her exit three times in one
week, that she has to write down the simplest tasks because her mind goes blank.

She avoids the news. She avoids rain. She avoids the sound of heavy trucks. Last month, a forklift
dropped a pallet in the motor pool, and she dove behind a crate. Her soldiers laughed. She laughed
too. But her hands were shaking for an hour.

Maria says she came to see me because her husband told her she was “different.” She says it’s
nothing. She says she’s fine. But she has already checked the window three times, and she hasn’t
finished telling me her name.
