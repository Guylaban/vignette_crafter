**Case Vignette: Dr. Anae**

Dr. Anae is a 77-year-old retired surgeon of Oceania descent, divorced, living alone in a seaside
apartment she chose for its view of the horizon. She presents for evaluation at the urging of her
daughter, who has noticed a marked change in her mother over the past two years. Dr. Anae sits
upright in the chair, hands folded in her lap, her posture still carrying the authority of an
operating room. She speaks in precise, measured sentences, but her eyes—dark and watchful—tell a
different story.

The trauma began three years ago, during a routine follow-up for a minor arrhythmia. Dr. Anae, then
still performing surgeries part-time, was told she had a rare, aggressive form of cancer—stage IV,
with a survival rate she knew too well from her own years in oncology. She remembers the exact
moment: the doctor’s voice, the fluorescent hum of the office, the way the clock on the wall seemed
to stop. In that instant, she says, “I became the patient. The one I used to pity.”

What followed was a year of chemotherapy, radiation, and two surgeries. She endured the physical
pain with the stoicism of a surgeon—never complaining, never asking for help. But the terror was
different. At night, alone in her hospital bed, she would replay the moment of diagnosis, over and
over. She began to see her own body as a traitor, a vessel that could turn against her without
warning. She stopped trusting her own heartbeat, her own breath.

Now, two years after being declared cancer-free, Dr. Anae cannot stop scanning. She checks her body
several times a day for lumps, for any asymmetry, for the slightest change in skin texture. She has
memorized the feel of every lymph node in her neck, her armpits, her groin. She keeps a detailed log
of her weight, her blood pressure, her sleep patterns. She has seen three different oncologists for
second, third, and fourth opinions, each time asking the same question: “Are you sure it’s gone?”

Her hypervigilance has bled into every part of her life. She avoids the hospital where she used to
work, taking a longer route to the grocery store to bypass its entrance. She cannot watch medical
dramas or read health articles. The word “cancer” in a conversation sends a jolt through her chest,
and she will excuse herself to the restroom, where she stands over the sink, gripping the porcelain
until her knuckles whiten.

Sleep is a battleground. She falls asleep only to wake at 2:00 a.m., drenched in sweat, certain she
has felt a new pain, a new irregularity. She spends the dark hours pacing her apartment, running her
fingers over her collarbone, her ribs, her abdomen. Her daughter bought her a weighted blanket,
hoping it would soothe her, but Dr. Anae says it feels like the weight of a body on top of her—the
body of her former self, the one that was dying.

She has withdrawn from the surgical community she once led. Colleagues call, but she lets the phone
ring. She cannot bear their questions—“How are you feeling?”—because the answer is always the same:
terrified. She has stopped attending the quarterly grand rounds, stopped mentoring young surgeons.
Her hands, steady for decades, now tremble when she holds a coffee cup.

The most painful part, she says, is the loss of her own certainty. She used to know what to do. She
could cut into a body and find the problem, fix it, close it up. Now she lives inside a body she
cannot trust, and no scalpel, no scan, no surgeon’s hand can cut out the fear. She feels, for the
first time in her life, utterly powerless.
