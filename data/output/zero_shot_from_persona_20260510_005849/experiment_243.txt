**Case Vignette: Amina**

Amina is a 42-year-old married woman of West African descent who works as a train conductor for a
metropolitan transit authority. She presents with a guarded, watchful demeanor, her eyes scanning
the room as she speaks, as if cataloging every exit and potential threat.

She begins her story not with the trauma, but with the life that came before. For fifteen years, she
loved her work. She loved the rhythm of the tracks, the authority of the uniform, the quiet solitude
of the conductor’s cabin at the end of a long line of cars. She was good at her job—calm, precise,
unflappable. She and her husband, a high school teacher, had built a quiet, stable life. They had
two children, now teenagers, and a small house with a garden she tended on weekends.

That life ended three years ago, during a visit to her home country for a family funeral. She was
detained at a military checkpoint outside the capital. The soldiers accused her of being a political
agitator, a charge she denies. They took her to a concrete room with no windows.

Amina describes the next ten days in fragments. She will not describe the specific instruments used,
only the *sound* of them—a metallic click, a low hum, the wet slap of leather against skin. She
remembers the smell of her own sweat and blood, the taste of copper. She remembers the men’s boots,
always polished, and the way they would ask her questions she could not answer, then punish her for
her silence. She learned to dissociate. She would fix her gaze on a crack in the ceiling and imagine
she was on her train, watching the city slide by, counting the stops until she was home.

She was released without explanation, dumped at a bus station with a warning not to speak of what
happened. She flew home the next day.

When Amina returned to work six weeks later, she expected to feel relief. Instead, the first time
she stepped into the cabin, her heart slammed against her ribs. The confined space felt like the
concrete room. The rattle of the train over the tracks was the sound of boots. She began to have
panic attacks in the tunnel—sweating, shaking, a feeling of suffocation. She now takes the long way
to work, walking above ground for an extra mile, just to avoid the dark.

At home, she is a ghost. She cannot tolerate being touched unexpectedly; her husband’s hand on her
shoulder makes her flinch and scream. They sleep in separate rooms. She has not let him see her
undressed in two years. The scars have healed, but she cannot bear the look of pity in his eyes.

Her children have learned to walk quietly. She yells at them for no reason, then feels a crushing
guilt that turns into numbness. She has stopped gardening. The smell of damp soil reminds her of the
floor of the cell.

Amina’s mind is a constant loop of the past. She has nightmares every night—not always of the
torture, but of being trapped. Trapped in an elevator, a car, a closet. She wakes up clawing at the
sheets. During the day, she is hypervigilant. She cannot sit with her back to a door. At the transit
authority, she now refuses to work the late shift. She has been written up twice for insensitivity
to passengers—once for screaming at a man who tapped her shoulder to ask for directions.

She carries a small, smooth stone in her pocket. She rubs it when the memories surge. It is the only
thing that helps, a tiny anchor against the tide.

Amina does not believe she has PTSD. She believes she is broken. She came to therapy because her
husband gave her an ultimatum, but also because she is exhausted. She is tired of the
hypervigilance, the nightmares, the constant, low-grade terror. She wants to stop being afraid of
the dark. She wants to be able to sit with her back to the door and feel safe. She wants to garden
again.
