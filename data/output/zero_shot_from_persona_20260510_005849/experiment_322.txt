**Case Vignette: Forensic Analyst, Age 58**

He still smells the smoke. Not the sharp, chemical smoke of a controlled burn, but the thick, sweet,
oily smoke of a building that was never supposed to burn. That’s the first thing he tells me, three
sessions in, his hands resting flat on his knees, palms down as if pressing something into the
earth. "I can’t get the smoke out of my nose."

The event happened fourteen months ago. He was called to a high-rise office tower downtown—a
suspected arson, a man-made disaster disguised as an electrical fire. He had done this work for over
thirty years. He had walked through the aftermath of bombings, car crashes, and collapsed
structures. He was the one who reconstructed the sequence of destruction, the one who found the
point of origin, the one who told the families what happened. But this one was different.

He arrived on scene at 3:47 AM. The building was still smoldering, the skeleton of it black against
the city lights. He remembers the heat on his face from a hundred feet away. He remembers the sound
of the metal groaning, the glass still falling like rain. He was given a respirator, a hard hat, a
flashlight. He walked in.

Inside, the world was inverted. The floors had pancaked. Desks were upside down. The smell was
overwhelming—melting plastic, burnt wiring, and something else, something he now recognizes as the
smell of human fat and bone. He found the point of origin on the 14th floor, in a storage closet
where a cleaning crew had left a bucket of oily rags. He took his photos, his measurements, his
samples. He did his job.

But on his way out, he found a shoe. A single, black, leather dress shoe, size 10, still laced,
sitting upright in the ash. He picked it up. He turned it over. The sole was partially melted. And
he thought, *Someone was wearing this. Someone walked in this. Someone is missing a shoe.* He put it
back. He walked out.

He hasn't been the same since.

Now, at fifty-eight, he works from home three days a week. His office is in the basement,
windowless, which he prefers. He tells his wife it's for the light—the glare on his monitor—but the
truth is he cannot look at a window without scanning the sky for smoke. He has become obsessed with
fire alarms. He checks the batteries every Sunday. He has three fire extinguishers in the house: one
in the kitchen, one in the garage, one in the hallway. His wife has asked him to stop, but he
cannot.

He avoids the news. He cannot watch coverage of any fire, any explosion, any accident. He changes
the channel or leaves the room. He stopped going to his weekly poker game because one of the men was
a retired firefighter and would talk about the old calls. The sound of a siren on the street makes
his stomach drop. He has started driving the long way to the grocery store to avoid the intersection
where a gas station burned down last year.

Sleep is a problem. He falls asleep fine, but he wakes at 3:47 AM every single night. He does not
know why, but he knows it is the time he arrived on scene. He lies awake, staring at the ceiling,
smelling smoke that isn't there. He has started drinking—two glasses of whiskey before bed,
sometimes three. He tells himself it's to relax. His wife tells him it's too much. He tells her she
doesn't understand.

He has not told anyone about the shoe.

He is irritable. He snaps at his adult son for small things—leaving a light on, not putting a dish
in the sink. He feels a constant, low-level anger, like a pressure behind his eyes. He has stopped
going to family dinners. He says he is tired. He is tired. But it is more than that. He is afraid.
Afraid of the next call, the next fire, the next shoe. Afraid that if he lets himself feel what he
felt in that building, he will never stop feeling it.

He came to therapy because his wife told him she was worried. He sat in the chair across from me,
looked at the floor, and said, "I don't know why I'm here. I'm fine."

He is not fine. He knows it. I know it. And I think, somewhere deep in the ash of his memory, that
shoe knows it too.
