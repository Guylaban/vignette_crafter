**Patient Case Vignette: "T."**

T. is a 24-year-old single male of Oceania descent, working as a pharmacist in a busy community
clinic. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 58, indicating severe PTSD symptoms. He came to therapy
after a colleague noticed he was "not himself" and had begun avoiding certain medication storage
areas.

The trauma occurred six months ago, on a quiet Tuesday evening. T. was closing the pharmacy when he
received a video call from his younger cousin, M., a 19-year-old fisherman. M. was excited, showing
off a new deep-sea rig he had just bought. The call was grainy, the connection poor. Then, in a
single, horrifying second, the screen went sideways. T. heard a sickening crack—like a coconut
splitting—followed by the sound of water. He watched, helpless, as the camera showed the underside
of a boat, then darkness. He screamed M.'s name into the phone for twenty minutes before the coast
guard called his aunt. M. had been knocked overboard by a swinging boom. He did not survive.

In the weeks that followed, T. became a ghost in his own life. He stopped going to the family
gatherings that were the bedrock of his community—the Sunday feasts, the beach volleyball games. He
could not bear the sight of his aunt's face, which seemed to hold the same frozen scream he saw on
the video. His job, once a source of pride, became a minefield.

T. is a meticulous pharmacist. He prides himself on accuracy. But now, every time he reaches for a
bottle of medication, his hands begin to shake. The sound of a glass vial clinking against another
triggers an immediate flashback: the crack of the boom, the splash. He has started to avoid the
"fast mover" section of the pharmacy, where the liquid medications are stored, because the sound of
the bottles shifting in their bins is identical to the sound of water lapping against the boat's
hull. Twice, he has had to excuse himself to the bathroom to dry-heave after a patient asked for a
liquid antibiotic.

His sleep is a disaster. He dreams of the video, but in his dreams, he is the one falling. He wakes
up gasping, convinced he is drowning. He has started sleeping on the floor of his apartment, because
his bed feels too much like a boat. He is constantly on edge, scanning the faces of customers for
danger. He is irritable with his coworkers, snapping at them for minor mistakes. He has stopped
answering his phone, terrified that another video call will bring more bad news.

The most insidious symptom is his guilt. He replays the call endlessly. *If I had just told him to
wait until morning. If I had asked him to call me from the dock. If I had hung up before it
happened.* He feels like he murdered his cousin by being on the other end of that line. He cannot
look at his own reflection without seeing M.'s face in the water.

T. is now a man caught between two worlds. He is a healer, a dispenser of medicine, but he cannot
heal himself. He is a son of Oceania, a people of the sea, but he now fears water so much that he
takes the long way to work to avoid the harbor. He comes to therapy with a quiet, desperate hope,
but also with a deep fear that telling the story again will somehow make it more real, and that he
will drown in it.
