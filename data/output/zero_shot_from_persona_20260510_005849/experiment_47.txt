**Case Vignette: "Kofi"**

Kofi is a 19-year-old African male, a UX designer for a mid-sized tech startup, who presents with a
haunted, watchful quality that belies his youth. He is single and lives alone in a studio apartment,

a friendly, professional rapport with a senior product manager, a woman in her forties. He admired

related praise, then personal compliments, then increasingly specific observations about his daily

a chill, a subtle shift from admiration to unease. He politely asked her to stop. She did not.

The stalking escalated. She began appearing at his usual coffee shop, then at his gym. She left
handwritten notes under his apartment door, detailing the books on his nightstand, the color of his

outside his building, waiting for him. He filed a police report. She was terminated, and a
restraining order was issued. The physical stalking stopped. The psychological stalking did not.

Now, Kofi describes his mind as a "threat-detection engine" that never turns off. In session, he
sits with his back to the wall, his eyes darting to the door whenever a floorboard creaks in the
hallway. At work, he cannot focus on user flows or wireframes. His design process once relied on
deep, uninterrupted concentration. Now, every time a Slack notification pings, his heart slams
against his ribs. He has stopped collaborating in open-plan spaces. He works remotely, from his
locked apartment, but even there, he feels watched. He has installed three locks, a doorstop, and a
camera pointed at the door. He checks the camera feed every few minutes, a compulsive loop he cannot
break.

His sleep is fractured. He wakes at 3:00 AM, convinced he heard the scrape of a key in the lock, or
the soft footfall of someone in the hall. He once loved the quiet of night; now, it feels like a

tracked, desired—fills him with a visceral dread. He has deleted all social media, not for privacy,
but because the thought of someone knowing his location, his habits, his face, feels like handing
them a weapon.

Kofi’s primary feeling is not fear, but a deep, bone-level vigilance. He describes it as “living in
a state of constant, low-grade electrocution.” He feels no anger toward his stalker; instead, he
feels a strange, hollow shame, as if he somehow invited the attention, as if his own openness was a
design flaw. He has stopped listening to music on headphones during his commute. He needs to hear
everything. He needs to know if someone is behind him.

He still designs, but his work has changed. His portfolio once featured bright, welcoming
interfaces. Now, his projects are about security: encrypted messaging, anonymous browsing, panic-
button apps. He cannot look at a user’s journey map without seeing the potential for a predator’s
path. He is a UX designer who now designs for a world he cannot trust.
