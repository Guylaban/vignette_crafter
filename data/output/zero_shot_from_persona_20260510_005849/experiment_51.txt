**Patient Vignette: Aisha**

Aisha is a 24-year-old single woman of Middle Eastern descent who works as a tailor in a small shop.
She presents as meticulous and softly spoken, her hands often folding and refolding the edge of her
sleeve as she speaks. She came to therapy at the urging of her older sister, who has noticed Aisha
has not been herself since the accident four months ago.

It happened on a Tuesday evening. Aisha was driving home from her shop, a short route she had taken
hundreds of times. The rain had just started, a thin sheen on the asphalt. She was stopped at a red
light, her mind half on a wedding dress she was altering, when a pickup truck hit her from behind at
full speed. The impact was so violent that her car was pushed through the intersection and into a
lamppost. She remembers the sound—not a crash, but a deep, metallic groan—and the smell of hot
airbag powder. She remembers the impossible pressure of the seatbelt against her chest, and the
feeling of her head snapping forward, then back. She remembers thinking, with absolute certainty, *I
am going to die here.*

She was trapped for nearly fifteen minutes before the fire department cut her out. She sustained
whiplash, a cracked rib, and deep bruising. The driver of the truck was found to be intoxicated. He
walked away with a scratch.

Physically, Aisha healed. The rib mended. The bruises faded. But something in the architecture of
her mind was irrevocably bent.

Now, she cannot drive. The mere sight of her car keys on the kitchen counter sends a cold spike of
adrenaline through her chest. She has not been behind the wheel since the accident. Her sister
drives her to work, and Aisha sits in the passenger seat with her eyes fixed straight ahead, her
feet pressed hard against the floorboard as if she could brake for them both. If a car approaches
too quickly from behind, she flinches violently, her breath catching. She apologizes to her sister
constantly, a quiet, reflexive *sorry* for a fear she cannot control.

Her work as a tailor, once her sanctuary of precision and calm, has become a prison. The shop is on
a busy street, and the sound of traffic is a constant, low-grade torment. Every screech of tires,
every honk, every engine that revs too loudly makes her scissors freeze mid-cut. She has taken to
wearing noise-canceling headphones, but even then, she finds herself scanning the front window for
headlights, her body tensing for an impact that never comes. Her boss, a kind man, has been patient,
but Aisha knows her output has slowed. She makes tiny, unnecessary mistakes—a seam that is half a
centimeter off, a button sewn in the wrong place. She cannot keep her hands steady.

Sleep is a battlefield. She is terrified of the dark, of the vulnerability of closing her eyes. When
she does sleep, she is back in the car. The impact comes every time. She wakes gasping, her hands
clutching the sheets as if they are the steering wheel, her heart hammering against her cracked rib.
She has started sleeping with the light on, a small lamp on her nightstand that casts long shadows.

She has also stopped going out with friends. The idea of being a passenger in anyone’s car—even a
trusted friend’s—fills her with a dread so total it feels like nausea. She makes excuses. She says
she is tired. She says she has a deadline. The truth is simpler and more terrible: she no longer
trusts the world to be safe. She no longer trusts that a red light means stop, that a Tuesday
evening can be ordinary, that she is not, at any moment, about to be destroyed by forces she cannot
see or control.

When asked how she is feeling, Aisha does not say she is anxious. She says she feels *brittle*, as
if her bones are made of glass and the slightest pressure will shatter her. She is not sad; she is
hollow. She is a tailor who cannot wear her own life anymore—it is a garment that no longer fits,
that pulls at the seams and chokes her at the collar. She is here because she is tired of being
afraid, but she does not know how to stop.
