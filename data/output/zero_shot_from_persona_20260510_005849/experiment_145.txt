**Case Vignette: “Mai”**

Mai is a 49-year-old divorced woman of Southeastern Asian descent who works the night shift as an IT
support specialist for a regional hospital. She presents as composed and precise, her answers
measured, her posture rigid. She rarely makes eye contact, preferring to fix her gaze on a spot on
the wall just above my left shoulder. She has been in the United States for 25 years, but her story
begins long before that, in a small village in Laos.

In 1975, when Mai was 20 years old, she was conscripted into a local militia during the final years
of the Laotian Civil War. She was not a soldier by choice. She was given a rifle and told to guard a
supply depot on the edge of a jungle. She had never fired a weapon before. She remembers the weight
of the metal, the smell of oil and earth, the constant hum of insects. One night, the depot was
overrun. She does not speak of the details of that night, only that she was the only one who walked
out. She walked through the jungle for three days, alone, until she reached a refugee camp in
Thailand. She has not returned to Laos since.

Now, Mai lives alone in a one-bedroom apartment. She keeps the curtains drawn at all times. She
works the overnight shift, from 11 p.m. to 7 a.m., resetting passwords, troubleshooting server
errors, and answering calls from nurses who cannot log into the electronic medical records system.
She chose this shift deliberately. “It’s quiet,” she says. “No one expects me to be awake.” She
sleeps during the day, but her sleep is shallow and fractured. She wakes frequently, sometimes with
her heart pounding, sometimes convinced she hears footsteps in the hallway, even though she lives on
the fourth floor and the building is secure.

Mai divorced her husband, a fellow refugee she met in the camp, twelve years ago. She does not
elaborate on the reasons, only that he “did not understand why I could not let things go.” She has
one adult son who lives in another state. They speak twice a year, on birthdays. She says she
prefers it that way. “I am not good company,” she tells me, flatly.

In session, Mai describes a persistent sense of being on edge. She checks the locks on her door
three times before she can sit down to eat. She avoids crowds, movie theaters, and the produce
section of the grocery store during peak hours. “Too many people behind me,” she explains. “I cannot
see them all.” She does not watch war films or read news about conflict. When a car backfires
outside the clinic, she flinches—a full-body, reflexive jerk—and then stares at the floor, silent,
for nearly a minute.

She reports intrusive memories, but not the kind she can narrate. Instead, she says, she feels a
“pressure” in her chest, a sudden coldness in her hands, and then she is back in the jungle, not
seeing it, but *smelling* it—the wet leaves, the smoke, the metallic tang of blood. These episodes
happen several times a week, often triggered by nothing she can identify. She has learned to ride
them out, to stay still until the world returns. “I don’t fight it anymore,” she says. “Fighting
makes it worse.”

Mai scored a 40 on the PCL-5, indicating clinically significant PTSD symptoms. She came to therapy
not because she wanted to talk about the war, but because her supervisor at the hospital noticed she
