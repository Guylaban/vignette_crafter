**Patient Vignette: Chef, Age 43**

She remembers the exact moment her body became a foreign country. It was a Tuesday, during a
particularly brutal dinner service. She was plating a duck breast, her hands moving with the muscle
memory of twenty years, when a strange, hot tightness spread across her chest. She told herself it
was stress. She told herself it was the heat from the grill. She finished the service, but the
sensation never truly left. It just settled in, a low hum of wrongness beneath her skin.

The diagnosis came three weeks later. A benign, but aggressive, tumor wrapped around her vagus
nerve. The doctors were reassuring, but the word "surgery" landed in her stomach like a stone. It
was the first time she had ever felt truly helpless. In a kitchen, chaos is her domain. She can tame
a flare-up, salvage a broken sauce, command a line of cooks with a single sharp word. But this? She
could not chop it, sear it, or plate it. She could only lie on a gurney and be wheeled away.

The surgery was a success. The tumor was gone. She was told she was lucky. And for a few weeks, she
believed it. She went back to work too soon, desperate to reclaim her territory. But the kitchen had
changed. The sizzle of a pan, once a comforting sound, now felt like a warning. The pressure of the
dinner rush, once a thrilling high, now felt like a vise around her ribs. She began to feel her
heartbeat in her throat, a frantic little drummer she could not silence.

Her partner, a gentle man who runs a bookstore, tries to understand. He holds her when she wakes up
gasping, convinced she is back on that cold operating table. He doesn't complain when she insists on
sleeping with the light on. But he cannot understand why she now hates the smell of antiseptic, or
why she flinches when he touches the scar on her neck. He doesn't know that every time she feels a
twinge in her chest, a full-blown panic sequence starts: the hot tightness, the racing pulse, the
certainty that this time, it is the end.

confident gesture. Now she uses a clean spoon, every time. She cannot stand to have her hands wet

expediting during the rush. She stands at the pass, her hands shaking, watching him do her job. The
PCL-5 score of 80 is just a number on a page. The real score is the way she now measures her life in
the space between her heartbeats, a chef who has lost her taste for anything but fear.
