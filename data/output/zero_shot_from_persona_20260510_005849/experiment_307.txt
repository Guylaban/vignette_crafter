**Patient Vignette: "David"**

David is a 25-year-old man who presents as quiet and watchful, his posture slightly hunched as if
he’s trying to take up less space in the room. He works as a housekeeper at a mid-sized hotel, a job
he has held for three years. He describes it as "good work" because it keeps his hands busy and his
mind elsewhere. He is single and lives alone in a small studio apartment. He came to therapy at the
urging of his supervisor, who noticed he had begun flinching violently when coworkers approached him
from behind.

The story David tells begins in a small, tidy house in a suburb. He was the youngest of three
children. His father was a truck driver, often gone for weeks at a time. His mother, a homemaker,
was the center of his world until he was about six years old. That was when the rules changed. He
recalls that his mother began to use a wooden spoon—not for cooking, but for correction. "I was a
clumsy kid," he says, his voice flat. "I spilled milk. I left my shoes in the hall. I talked too
much." The spoon was for talking too much. Later, it was her hand. Later still, it was whatever was
nearby—a belt, a shoe, a hairbrush.

The logic of the abuse was always the same: she was teaching him discipline. She would tell him,
"This hurts me more than it hurts you," as she struck his thighs and back, places the schoolteachers
wouldn't see. He learned to stand still. He learned not to cry. If he cried, it meant he was being
manipulative, and the punishment would start over. By the time he was ten, he had learned to
dissociate completely. He would stare at a crack in the wall or a pattern in the linoleum and leave
his body behind. He remembers the sensation of floating near the ceiling, looking down at a small
boy being hit, and feeling nothing but a vague curiosity.

His father was rarely home, and when he was, he seemed not to notice. David never told him. He never
told anyone. He believed, deeply and without question, that he deserved it. He was bad. He was
clumsy. He talked too much.

The abuse stopped when he was fifteen. He had grown taller than his mother, and one day, when she
raised her hand, he simply caught her wrist. He held it for a moment, looked at her, and then let
go. He walked out of the house and didn't come back until the next morning. She never hit him again,
but they never spoke of it either. The silence was its own kind of violence.

Now, ten years later, David lives in a world shaped by those years. He describes his daily life as a
series of careful calculations. At the hotel, he cleans rooms on a strict schedule. He prefers the
rooms of guests who have already checked out, so he can work alone. He cannot tolerate being
watched. If a guest stays in the room while he cleans, his hands begin to shake. He apologizes
constantly—for bumping a chair, for the sound of the vacuum, for existing. His coworkers have
learned not to touch him. One well-meaning woman placed a hand on his shoulder to thank him, and he
dropped a stack of clean towels and backed into a corner, his heart pounding so hard he thought he
was having a heart attack. He avoids the break room. He eats lunch in his car.

At home, he keeps his apartment immaculate—a mirror of his work life. He says he cannot relax in a
messy space. He sleeps on the far edge of his bed, closest to the door. He sleeps lightly, waking at
any sound. He has nightmares, though he calls them "bad dreams." He dreams of being small and
trapped, of a door that won't open, of a voice that says, "This is for your own good."

not to take up space, trying not to be a burden. He says, with a hollow laugh, "I guess I learned my
lesson well."
