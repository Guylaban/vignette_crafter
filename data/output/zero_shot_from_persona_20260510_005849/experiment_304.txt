**Case Vignette: Priya**

Priya is a 30-year-old South Asian woman, married for six years, who works as a software engineer
for a mid-sized tech firm. She presents as articulate and composed, though her hands remain clasped
tightly in her lap throughout our sessions, and she often pauses to take a slow, deliberate breath
before answering a question.

She grew up in a suburban home with her parents and an older brother. From the outside, her family
was considered successful and respectable. Inside, Priya describes a household governed by her
father’s unpredictable temper. The physical abuse began when she was around seven years old. She
recalls specific incidents with a clarity that seems to cost her something each time she speaks them
aloud: the sting of an open palm across her face for spilling milk; the sharp pain of a wooden spoon
breaking against her thigh for speaking out of turn at the dinner table; the humiliation of being
locked in a dark closet for hours after receiving a B on a test. Her mother, she says, would often
look away or quietly leave the room. “She wasn’t mean,” Priya tells me, her voice flat. “She just…
didn’t see it.”

Priya learned to survive by becoming invisible. She spent her childhood and adolescence in a state
of hypervigilance, constantly scanning her father’s mood—the set of his jaw, the tone of his
voice—to predict and avoid the next explosion. She excelled in school, not out of passion, but out
of a desperate need for control and a belief that if she were perfect, she would be safe. She left
home for university at eighteen and never moved back. She speaks of that departure with a quiet,
unadorned relief.

Now, at thirty, Priya is a successful software engineer. Her work is a double-edged sword. She is
highly valued for her meticulous attention to detail and her ability to spot errors in complex code
that others miss—a skill honed by a childhood spent watching for danger. But the pressure of
deadlines triggers a familiar, visceral panic. When a project manager sends a critical email, her
heart races, her palms sweat, and she feels a hot wave of shame, as if she is seven years old again
and has done something wrong. She often works late into the night, not because she has to, but
because the quiet of the empty office is the only time she feels her body unclench.

In her marriage, the patterns persist. Her husband, a kind and patient man, often tells her she is
“too jumpy.” He will approach her from behind to give her a hug, and she will flinch, her arm
shooting out defensively. The sound of a door slamming in their apartment building makes her freeze,
her breath catching in her throat. She has difficulty trusting his affection. When he is kind to her
without reason, she becomes suspicious, waiting for the other shoe to drop. Intimacy is fraught; she
sometimes dissociates, feeling as though she is watching her own body from a corner of the ceiling,
a familiar sensation from her childhood closet.

The most distressing symptom for Priya is the intrusive memory. It is not a full narrative, but a
sensory flash: the smell of her father’s cologne mixed with cooking oil, the feeling of a cold wall
against her cheek in the dark, the sound of a belt buckle jingling. These moments hit her without
warning—during a team stand-up meeting, while chopping vegetables for dinner, in the middle of a
conversation with her husband. When this happens, she feels a searing shame, as if she is the one
who did something unforgivable.

She has never told anyone the full story. Not her husband, not her friends at work. She carries it
alone, a dense, silent weight. She came to therapy because she is exhausted. She is tired of the
constant scanning, the flinching, the feeling that the past is not behind her, but is instead a room
she is still trapped inside. She wants to know if it is possible to feel safe in her own skin, even
for a moment.
