**Patient Vignette: Sofia**

Sofia is a 78-year-old woman, a retired librarian, who has been married to her husband, Carlos, for
fifty-two years. She presents with a calm, measured demeanor, her silver hair pulled back neatly,
and her hands resting in her lap. She speaks with a precise, careful cadence, as if choosing each
word from a catalog. She has come to therapy at the persistent urging of her daughter, who has
noticed that Sofia no longer leaves the house except for essential medical appointments.

The story Sofia tells begins not in the present, but in 1979. She was a young mother then, living in
a small city in Central America. Carlos was a university professor. One afternoon, soldiers came to
their home. They did not ask questions. They overturned furniture, tore books from shelves, and
accused Carlos of being a subversive. Sofia remembers the sound of her own breathing, loud and
ragged, as she held their three-year-old daughter against her chest. She remembers the smell of
sweat and gun oil. They were given twenty-four hours to leave the country. They left with one
suitcase and a child’s drawing of a bird.

The journey was a blur of border crossings, bribes, and nights in crowded rooms. Sofia’s mind, she
says, learned a new language: the language of constant scanning. She watched every uniform, every
official, every closed door. She learned to read danger in a man’s posture, in the way a car slowed
down. She kept her daughter’s hand in a grip that left white marks.

They arrived in the United States as refugees. Carlos found work as a janitor. Sofia, with her love
of order and language, eventually landed a job at a public library. For decades, she built a life.
She catalogued books, helped children find stories, and created a quiet, predictable world of Dewey
decimals and due dates. The library was her sanctuary. She could control the silence, the
arrangement of the shelves, the soft rustle of pages. At home, she kept the windows locked, the
blinds drawn, and the radio on low to mask sudden street noises.

Now, at 78, the sanctuary has shrunk. Sofia retired three years ago. She thought she would enjoy the
peace, but instead, the peace feels hollow. She finds herself unable to sit with her back to a door.
In restaurants, she insists on a corner booth, facing the entrance. She has stopped going to
restaurants. She has stopped going to the grocery store. The triggers are not loud explosions or
gunfire. They are smaller. A man in a dark uniform walking past her window. A car backfiring. A
knock on the door from a delivery driver. Each time, her body reacts before her mind can catch up:
her heart slams against her ribs, her palms sweat, and she is suddenly back in that living room in
1979, her daughter in her arms, the soldiers’ boots on the tile floor.

She has nightmares, but not of the soldiers. She dreams of the library. In the dream, the shelves
are all empty. The books are gone. The silence is not peaceful; it is a vacuum, a void. She wakes up
gasping, convinced she has lost everything again.

Sofia is irritable, but only with Carlos. She snaps at him for moving her keys, for leaving the
porch light on. She feels a profound, unshakable guilt. She tells me she should have saved more
people. She should have warned the neighbors. She should have fought back. The guilt is a heavy coat
she cannot take off. She avoids the Latin American section of the library. She cannot look at the
maps. She cannot hear Spanish spoken in a certain accent without feeling a cold dread in her
stomach.

She still keeps her house meticulously organized. The books are alphabetized. The canned goods are
arranged by type. She says it is the only thing that makes her feel safe. But lately, even that is
not enough. She sits in her armchair, facing the front door, and waits. She does not know what she
is waiting for. She only knows that the feeling of safety, the one she fought so hard to build, has
quietly, completely, slipped away.
