**Patient Vignette: "Khalid"**

Khalid is a 41-year-old single man of Middle Eastern descent who has been working in construction
for the past eighteen years. He presents for therapy at the urging of his primary care physician,
following a workplace incident in which he froze on a scaffolding, unable to move or speak for
nearly forty-five minutes after a coworker dropped a steel beam nearby. He has been on medical leave
for three weeks and describes himself as "falling apart."

Khalid was born in a city in the Levant region, the eldest of four children. He recalls his early
childhood as warm and secure, centered around his mother’s kitchen and the sounds of his younger
siblings playing. This changed abruptly when he was about seven years old. His father, a quiet man
who worked long hours as a mechanic, began drinking heavily after losing his job. What followed was
a period of approximately four years during which Khalid witnessed severe domestic violence against
his mother, usually late at night.

He describes the pattern with clinical precision, though he does not recognize it as such. His
father would come home after midnight, the smell of arak filling the apartment. The first sign was
always the sound of his father’s voice rising, accusing his mother of things Khalid could not
understand. Then came the sounds—objects breaking, his mother’s pleas, the thud of fists against
flesh. Khalid would lie rigid in the bed he shared with his two younger brothers, his body locked in
place, his eyes fixed on a crack in the ceiling. He learned to slow his breathing to near silence,
to become invisible. He never cried out. He never ran to help. He tells me this with flat affect,
his hands resting motionless on his knees. "I was a coward," he says. "I just lay there."

The violence ended when Khalid was eleven. His mother finally left, taking the children to a
shelter, then to a small apartment across the city. His father disappeared from their lives
entirely. Khalid never spoke of what he had witnessed, not to his mother, not to anyone. He threw
himself into school, then into work. At sixteen, he began working construction with an uncle, and he
has been on job sites ever since.

In session, Khalid presents as a solidly built man with calloused hands and a careful, deliberate
way of speaking. He maintains a fixed posture, rarely shifting in his chair. His eyes are watchful,
scanning the room periodically, as if assessing exits. When I ask about his current symptoms, he
describes a life that has become increasingly narrow.

For years, he tells me, he managed. He worked hard, stayed single, kept to himself. But about two
years ago, things began to deteriorate. He now experiences nightly nightmares, though he does not
call them that. "I don't sleep," he says. "I just lie there." In the dreams, he is seven years old
again, frozen in his bed, listening. The sounds are the same, but now they blend with the sounds of
the construction site—the clang of metal, the crash of debris. He wakes with his heart pounding,
soaked in sweat, convinced he is still a child.

At work, he has become hypervigilant. He cannot tolerate sudden loud noises. When a coworker calls
his name unexpectedly, he flinches. He has begun to position himself with his back to a wall at all
times, a habit his coworkers have noticed and mocked. He has stopped eating lunch in the break
trailer, preferring to eat alone in his truck. He avoids any conversation about family, about
childhood, about home.

His relationships, such as they are, have withered. He has never been married and has not had a
romantic relationship in over six years. He describes a pervasive sense of emotional numbness, a
feeling of being "behind glass." He cannot cry, cannot feel joy, cannot connect. He spends his
evenings alone in his small apartment, watching television with the sound low, waiting for sleep
that does not come.

The incident on the scaffolding, he says, was the first time he completely lost control. A coworker
dropped a beam twenty feet away. The sound was enormous, a deafening crash that seemed to come from
everywhere at once. Khalid felt his body lock. He could not move his arms or legs. His vision
tunneled. He was not on a scaffolding; he was in his childhood bed. He could hear his mother's
voice, his father's roar. He stood frozen for nearly an hour, unable to speak, until a foreman
climbed up and touched his shoulder, bringing him back.

Khalid looks at me with a kind of exhausted resignation. "I thought I left that house a long time
ago," he says. "But I am still lying in that bed."
