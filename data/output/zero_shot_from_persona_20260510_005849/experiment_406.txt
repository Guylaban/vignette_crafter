**Case Vignette: “Nadia”**

Nadia is a 46-year-old single woman who works as a pharmacist at a busy chain drugstore in a
suburban area. She has been coming to therapy for four weeks, referred by her primary care physician
after she reported persistent insomnia, a racing heart, and a feeling that she is “waiting for the
other shoe to drop.” She is well-groomed, speaks in a measured, precise tone, and often apologizes
for “taking up time.” She has never been in therapy before.

The story she tells begins seven years ago, when her son, Sami, was eight years old. He was a
healthy, active boy who loved soccer and building with Legos. One evening, he complained of a
headache and a stiff neck. Nadia, a pharmacist, initially thought it was a virus. Within twelve
hours, he was febrile, lethargic, and unable to touch his chin to his chest. She rushed him to the
emergency room. The diagnosis came quickly: bacterial meningitis. The doctors told her that if she
had waited another few hours, he might not have survived.

Sami was hospitalized for three weeks. Nadia did not leave his bedside except to shower. She watched
him seize, watched him be intubated, watched the monitors beep and alarm. She negotiated with God,
made bargains, promised to give up everything if he would live. He did live. He recovered fully,
with no lasting neurological damage. Today, Sami is a healthy, athletic fifteen-year-old who barely
remembers the hospital. Nadia remembers every second.

In the present, Nadia’s life is organized around invisible alarms. When she is at work, she cannot
tolerate the sound of a phone ringing. It is a sharp, insistent sound, and it makes her stomach
drop. She has asked her coworkers to text her instead, but the pharmacy’s landline still rings at
the counter. She has started to avoid the counter entirely, hiding in the back to fill
prescriptions. When a customer coughs, she feels a jolt of adrenaline. She catches herself staring
at children who are coughing in the store, her mind instantly calculating their temperature, their
risk. She knows this is irrational. She knows that a cough is just a cough. But her body does not
know.

At home, she checks on Sami three times a night. He is fifteen. He locks his door. She has learned
the sound of his breathing through the door, and if she cannot hear it, she texts him. “You okay?”
He replies, “Mom, stop.” She knows she should stop. She cannot.

The trigger that finally brought her to therapy was a thunderstorm. A loud crack of thunder in the
middle of the night woke her. She sat bolt upright, drenched in sweat, convinced that the sound was
a code blue alarm from the hospital. She ran to Sami’s room. He was asleep. She stood over his bed,
shaking, unable to catch her breath. She did not sleep for the rest of the night. The next morning,
she called her doctor.

Nadia describes her life as a “before and after.” Before Sami got sick, she was confident. She was
the pharmacist who could handle a busy line, a difficult customer, a medication error. Now, she
double-checks every bottle three times. She is afraid of making a mistake that will hurt someone.
She has started to dread going to work. She feels numb, disconnected, as though she is watching
herself from outside her body. She has stopped seeing friends. She has not dated in years. She tells
me that she feels like she is still in that hospital room, sitting in the plastic chair, waiting for
the doctor to come through the door with bad news.

“I know he’s fine,” she says, her voice flat. “I know. But I can’t feel it. I can’t feel safe.”
