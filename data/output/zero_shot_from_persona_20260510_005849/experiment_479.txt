**Case Vignette: Margaret**

Margaret sits upright in the armchair, her hands folded neatly in her lap, the posture of a woman
who has commanded bridges and crews for decades. She is seventy-six, her silver hair cut short and
practical, her eyes a pale, watchful blue. She still wears a sturdy pair of deck shoes, even to a
therapist’s office. She comes because her daughter, a social worker, insisted. “The nightmares are
worse, Mum,” her daughter said. “You’re not sleeping. You’re not eating.” Margaret did not argue.
She knows when a crew member is in distress, even if she struggles to name her own.

The story she tells begins not on the water, but on land. Two years ago, she was in a city she did
not know well, visiting her late husband’s grave in a coastal town. She had taken a wrong turn after
a long day of driving. The neighborhood was unfamiliar, the light was fading, and she pulled over to
consult a paper map—she does not trust GPS. A police car pulled up behind her, lights flashing. She
remembers the confusion first, then the sharp, cold shock. She was ordered out of her car. She did
not move fast enough. She was an old woman, stiff from the drive, and the officer saw hesitation as
defiance.

She remembers the pavement against her cheek. The weight of a knee on her back. The shouting, which
she could not parse. She remembers the sound of her own breath, ragged and thin, and the smell of
hot asphalt and exhaust. She was handcuffed, held for several hours, and released with no charges.
The officers apologized, eventually. Said it was a mistake. A case of mistaken identity.

Margaret does not use the word “trauma.” She says, “It was a bad day. A very bad day.”

But the bad day has not ended. It has followed her onto her ship. For forty-seven years, the bridge
of her vessel was her sanctuary, a place of absolute control and competence. Now, she finds herself
checking the starboard wing three times before she can settle into her chair. She has her first
officer scan the harbor police boat before she will give the order to cast off. She catches herself
scanning the horizon for flashing lights, not for navigational hazards. The other day, a crewman
accidentally dropped a coil of rope near her feet. She flinched so violently she nearly fell.

At night, the dreams come. She is on her bridge, but the wheel is gone. The instruments are dead.
And a voice over the radio, distorted and angry, is telling her to stop the engines. She wakes with
her heart hammering, her sheets soaked in sweat, her hand reaching for a radio that is not there.
She has not slept more than three hours a night in months.

She has stopped going ashore in unfamiliar ports. She makes excuses. She says she is tired, or that
the paperwork is too heavy. The truth is a cold, tight knot in her chest: she is afraid of being
stopped. Afraid of being seen as old, as slow, as a target. She has started carrying her captain’s
license in her breast pocket, a talisman against the feeling of being powerless.

Her crew notices. They whisper. The old captain is jumpy. She snaps at the first mate for no reason.
She has lost weight. Margaret knows they talk. She cannot stop it. She cannot stop the way her body
remembers the weight of the knee, the cold of the metal cuffs, the sheer, stunning violation of a
life lived in quiet, capable dignity.

She looks at me, her hands still, her gaze steady. “I have sailed through typhoons,” she says, her
voice low. “I have brought a ship into port with a dead engine and a broken rudder. I have never
been afraid of the sea. But I am afraid of a man in a uniform. And I do not know what to do with
that.”
