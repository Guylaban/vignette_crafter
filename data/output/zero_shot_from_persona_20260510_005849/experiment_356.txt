**Patient Vignette: Mrs. A.**

Mrs. A. is a 77-year-old married woman, a retired politician of African descent, who presents with a
guarded posture and a voice that is both commanding and brittle. She sits very straight in the
chair, her hands folded in her lap, as if holding herself together by force of will. She begins her
story not with the abuse, but with her former career. "I was a fighter," she says, her chin lifting.
"I fought for my people, for the women. I was on the front lines of the independence movement. I was
not a victim. I was a leader."

The trauma began later, after the fighting was over. She had married a charismatic man, a fellow
activist. In private, he was a different person. The first physical assault came when she was 52,
newly elected to a regional council. He accused her of spending too much time on "men’s work." He
slapped her so hard she fell against the kitchen counter. She remembers the shock more than the
pain. "I thought, 'This is a mistake. This is the stress of the campaign.'" But it was not a
mistake. Over the next decade, the slaps became punches. The punches became a closed fist. He never
hit her face—he was too clever for that. He aimed for her ribs, her back, the soft parts that no one
would see when she stood at a podium.

She coped by compartmentalizing. In the mornings, she would put on her *kente* cloth and her public
face, and she would go to the parliament building. She would argue for bills, shake hands with
diplomats, smile for photographs. Her colleagues called her "Iron Lady." At night, she would return
home and shrink. She learned to read his moods by the way he set down his keys. She learned to walk
silently, to keep her eyes down, to apologize for things she had not done. The beatings were not
daily, but the vigilance was. It was a full-time job.

The physical abuse stopped when he died, seven years ago. She did not mourn him. She felt a vast,
hollow relief, followed by a strange, creeping terror. The house grew quiet. She expected the
silence to heal her. It did not.

Now, at 77, Mrs. A. lives with her adult daughter, who is a nurse. She has not worked in five years.
She says she "retired," but the truth is she could no longer trust her own mind. The world feels
unsafe in a way she cannot articulate. She avoids the kitchen, especially the counter where she fell
