**Patient Vignette: "Martha"**

Martha is a 47-year-old married woman who works as a plumber. She arrives to sessions precisely on
time, often still wearing her work boots with traces of dried mud and pipe sealant on the laces. She
sits forward in her chair, her hands folded tightly in her lap, and speaks in a low, steady voice
that belies the tension in her shoulders.

She describes her childhood in a small village as one of constant vigilance. Her earliest clear
memory is of her mother’s hand striking the side of her head when she was four years old. She had
spilled a cup of milk. The punishment was swift, silent, and absolute. From that point on, she
learned to read the air in the room—the angle of her mother’s jaw, the speed of her breath, the way
her fingers would tap on the table before an outburst. Martha says she became "invisible." She
stopped asking for things. She stopped crying when she was hurt. She learned to move through the
house like a shadow, never making noise, never leaving a trace.

The physical abuse continued throughout her childhood. Her mother used whatever was at hand: a
wooden spoon, a belt, a shoe. Once, a hot iron. Martha recalls being sent to school with welts on
her back and being told by the teacher that she must have been "naughty." She internalized that
message completely. She believed, for decades, that she was a bad child who deserved what happened
to her.

She left home at seventeen and came to this country, working first as a cleaner, then as an
apprentice to a master plumber. She found the work grounding. It required focus, strength, and a
kind of physical certainty she had never felt in her body. She could fix a leak, unclog a drain,
rebuild a pipe system from scratch. It was the first time she felt competent. She met her husband, a
quiet electrician, on a job site twelve years ago. He is gentle with her, she says. He never raises
his voice. But she admits she still flinches when he reaches for her in the dark.

In the present, Martha’s life is organized around avoidance. She does not visit her family. She has
not spoken to her mother in over twenty years. She avoids crowded spaces, loud noises, and any
situation where she might feel trapped. She drives twenty minutes out of her way to avoid a certain
street where she once saw a woman hit a child in a parking lot. She cannot watch movies with any
form of violence, even fictional. Her husband handles all phone calls to her family, all holiday
planning, all unexpected visitors.

At work, she is known as reliable but quiet. She prefers jobs where she works alone, in crawlspaces
or basements, where no one can see her. She says she feels safest when she is underneath a house,
surrounded by pipes and darkness, with only her tools and the sound of running water. But even
there, the memories intrude. She will be tightening a valve when suddenly she is four years old
again, her mother’s hand coming toward her face. Her heart pounds. Her hands shake. She has to stop,
breathe, wait for the world to return.

She has nightmares most nights. In the dreams, she is always small. She is always running. She never
reaches the door. She wakes up gasping, her husband’s hand on her shoulder, and she lies still for a
long time, not speaking, trying to convince herself she is safe.

When asked what she wants from therapy, Martha looks down at her hands. She says she is tired of
being afraid. She says she wants to be able to walk into a room without scanning for exits. She
wants to stop apologizing for existing. She wants to feel, just once, that she is allowed to take up
space.

symptoms. She reports feeling numb most days, disconnected from her own life. She describes herself
