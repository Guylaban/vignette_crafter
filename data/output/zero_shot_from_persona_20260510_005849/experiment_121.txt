**Patient Case Vignette: “Aisha”**

Aisha is a 21-year-old female, a surgical intern at a major urban hospital. She presents as
composed, articulate, and clinically detached during our sessions, though her hands often tremble
when she describes the events of the past year. She is single, lives alone in a small apartment near
the hospital, and has no close family in the country. She emigrated from a coastal West African
nation three years ago on a merit scholarship for medical school.

The trauma occurred fourteen months ago, during her final year of medical school. Aisha was home for
a brief holiday when a catastrophic cyclone struck her coastal city. She describes the night as a
“wall of water and sound.” She and her family—her mother, father, and younger brother—fled to the
second floor of their home. The water rose to their chests within minutes. Her father, a fisherman,
tied them all together with a rope. Aisha recalls the precise, clinical details: the smell of diesel
and sewage, the sound of corrugated roofs tearing, the sensation of her brother’s small body pressed
against her back as they clung to a collapsed beam for eight hours.

The family survived, but their home was destroyed. Her father lost his fishing boat, the family’s
sole income. Aisha returned to her medical program two weeks later. She did not speak of the event.
She threw herself into her surgical rotations, working sixteen-hour shifts, sleeping in the on-call
room. She told herself that staying busy was a form of strength. She graduated with honors and began
her surgical internship three months ago.

In session, Aisha describes a profound shift in her inner life. She feels “hollow,” she says, as if
the cyclone washed away something essential. She is unable to feel joy or connection. She has
stopped calling her family; their voices trigger a visceral tightening in her chest that she cannot
control. She has not told them about her nightmares, which occur almost nightly. In the dreams, she
is back in the water, but now she is operating on her brother, trying to suture his wounds while the
current pulls them both under. She wakes gasping, her sheets soaked with sweat.

Her work, once a source of purpose, has become a minefield. Last week, during a routine emergency
appendectomy, the patient’s blood pressure dropped suddenly. The beeping of the monitors became, in
Aisha’s words, “the sound of the cyclone.” She froze. Her hands would not move. Her attending
surgeon had to physically guide her hands away from the field and take over. She excused herself to
the restroom and vomited. She has since been avoiding the operating room, volunteering for
administrative tasks, which her colleagues have noted with confusion. She is terrified they will
discover she is “broken.”

She has become hypervigilant. She checks the weather forecast obsessively, sometimes thirty times a
day. She cannot tolerate the sound of heavy rain. When a thunderstorm passed through the city last
week, she spent the night in her bathroom, curled in the bathtub, with a trauma kit she assembled
from the hospital supply closet—bandages, a tourniquet, a flashlight. She knows it is irrational,
but the fear is total. She has begun drinking two glasses of wine each night to fall asleep, a habit
she is ashamed of but cannot stop.

Aisha describes herself as “a machine that used to work.” She feels she is failing her family, her
patients, and herself. She is attending therapy at the urging of her residency program director, who
noticed her withdrawal. She sits very still in the chair, her hands folded in her lap, and speaks of
her mother’s face in the rising water as if it were a photograph she cannot put down. She does not
know how to live with the memory. She only knows she cannot outrun it anymore.
