**Patient Case Vignette: "Ravi"**

Ravi is a 26-year-old South Asian man, married for two years, who works as a text editor for a mid-
sized publishing house. He presents for therapy at the urging of his wife, who has grown concerned
by his increasing withdrawal and irritability over the past six months.

The traumatic event occurred eleven months ago, on a late winter evening. Ravi was driving home from
a late shift at the office, taking a familiar shortcut through a neighborhood adjacent to his own.
He recalls the sky was clear, the streets wet from earlier rain. He was stopped at a red light,
listening to a podcast, when he saw a police cruiser’s lights flashing in his rearview mirror. He
had not been speeding or driving erratically. He pulled over immediately, hands at ten and two,
heart already beating faster. He says he has always been an anxious driver, a feeling he attributes
to growing up in a household where his father was a strict, unpredictable disciplinarian.

What happened next, Ravi describes in fragments, often with his eyes fixed on a point just above my
shoulder. Two officers approached. One, a younger man with a shaved head, immediately demanded he
step out of the vehicle. Ravi asked, “What’s wrong, officer?” He remembers the officer’s response:
“Don’t talk back. Out. Now.” He was pulled from the car, his arms twisted behind his back, and
slammed face-first onto the wet hood. He felt the cold metal against his cheek, the weight of a knee
pressed into his lower back. He was handcuffed, searched, and held there for what felt like an
eternity—perhaps ten minutes. He was not told why. He heard the other officer searching his vehicle,
the sound of his glove compartment opening, papers rustling. He thought of his wife, of his mother,
of the shame of being seen like this by a neighbor. He did not resist. He tried to explain he was an
editor, a resident, that he had done nothing. His voice, he says, sounded thin and far away to his
own ears. Eventually, the younger officer returned, said there had been a “mistake,” that a car
matching his description had been reported stolen. They uncuffed him, handed him his license, and
walked away without an apology. Ravi sat in his car for twenty minutes, his hands shaking so badly
he could not grip the steering wheel. He drove home in silence, and did not tell his wife what
happened for three days.

In the months since, Ravi’s life has contracted. He no longer drives at night. He takes public
transport, even if it adds an hour to his commute. He avoids that specific street entirely. He has
developed a hypervigilance to the sight of police cruisers. If he sees one while walking, he will
cross the street, duck into a store, or turn around. At work, his concentration has suffered. He is
an editor, a job that demands quiet, sustained attention to text. He finds himself reading the same
paragraph over and over, the words blurring as his mind replays the sensation of the hood against
his cheek. His colleagues have noticed he is jumpy. He has snapped at a junior editor for tapping a
pen on a desk, the sound triggering a startle response that leaves him flushed and embarrassed.

At home, he is irritable and withdrawn. He argues with his wife over small things—the volume of the
television, the way she loads the dishwasher. He has stopped wanting sex. He feels emotionally numb,
describing a “glass wall” between himself and the world. He cannot explain why he feels so angry, or
why he feels a deep, unshakeable shame, as if he had done something wrong. He has nightmares, not of
the arrest itself, but of being trapped—in a car, in a room, in his own skin—with no way out. He
wakes in a cold sweat, heart pounding, and lies still, listening to the silence of the apartment,
waiting for a knock on the door that never comes. He has stopped going to social events. He feels
safest in the small, windowless office at work, where he can close the door and control the
lighting.

Ravi is a thoughtful, articulate man who struggles to reconcile his professional identity as a
careful, rational editor with the chaotic, frightened person he has become. He says he just wants to
“feel normal again,” but he cannot pinpoint where that normalcy went, or how to begin looking for
it.
