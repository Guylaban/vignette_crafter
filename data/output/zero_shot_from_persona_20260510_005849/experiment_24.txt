**Patient Vignette: Mrs. A.**

Mrs. A. is an 80-year-old African woman, a retired midwife, who has been married to her husband, a
retired schoolteacher, for fifty-three years. She presents as a dignified, soft-spoken woman, though
her hands, which once delivered hundreds of babies, now tremble slightly as she adjusts her
headwrap. She describes her life before the accident as one of "purpose and rhythm"—a life lived in
service to others, in her village and in her home.

The traumatic event occurred eighteen months ago. During the heavy rainy season, Mrs. A. was
returning from a neighboring village where she had assisted with a difficult birth. The road, a
narrow dirt track, had become a slick, muddy river. As her driver navigated a sharp bend, the car
hydroplaned, spun, and plunged down a steep embankment, rolling twice before coming to rest against
a tree. Mrs. A. was thrown from the back seat. She remembers a sound of tearing metal, a crushing
pressure on her chest, and then the smell of wet earth and gasoline. She lay pinned for nearly two
hours before rescuers arrived. She sustained three broken ribs and a fractured pelvis, but the
physical wounds have long since healed.

What has not healed is the terror that now lives inside her. She cannot ride in a car. The mere
sight of her husband pulling their old sedan out of the garage sends a jolt of electricity through
her body. Her heart pounds, her breath becomes shallow, and she is instantly back in that moment—the
sound of the metal, the smell of the earth. She now walks everywhere, even to the market three miles
away, carrying her groceries in a worn cloth bag. Her husband, understanding but heartbroken, has
learned to park the car two blocks away so she does not have to see it.

The memories intrude without warning. A sudden rain shower against the window can trigger a full
flashback, where she feels the mud sucking at her clothes and hears the frantic voices of the
rescuers. She has stopped attending her church's weekly women's group because the drive there, even
if she were to go, feels insurmountable. She has also stopped taking calls from young mothers in the
village who once sought her advice. "My hands are not steady enough to hold a baby anymore," she
says, though her hands are steady when she is at home, folding laundry.

dream where she is in the overturned car, but instead of the road, it is floating down a river, and
she cannot find the surface.

to bring life into the world, to be a calm presence in chaos. Now, she feels "broken," "useless."
