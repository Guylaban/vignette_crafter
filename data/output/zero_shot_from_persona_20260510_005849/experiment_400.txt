**Patient Vignette: “Marcus”**

Marcus is a 29-year-old jeweler who presents as lean, with steady hands that occasionally freeze
mid-gesture when he speaks. He arrives to sessions wearing dark, practical clothing, often with
traces of metal dust under his fingernails. He is single, lives alone in a small apartment above his
workshop, and describes his life as “quiet by design.”

He begins by telling me that he has not slept through the night in eighteen months.

The story he tells begins three years ago. Marcus had been working as a jeweler in a mid-sized city,
but felt a growing restlessness—a need to do something “real” with his hands. Through a humanitarian
organization, he volunteered for a six-month deployment as a logistics coordinator in a conflict
zone in the Middle East. He was responsible for tracking supply shipments—food, water, medical
kits—for displaced families. He was not a soldier, he emphasizes. He was just a man with a clipboard
and a satellite phone.

The first three months were difficult but manageable. He describes the dust, the heat, the constant
sound of generators. He remembers the children who would wave at his convoy. He remembers the taste
of sweet tea shared with local staff. He felt useful.

Then came the market bombing.

It was a Tuesday afternoon. Marcus had been delivering supplies to a distribution point near a
crowded market. He was unloading crates when the first explosion hit—a mortar, he later learned. He
was thrown against a wall. His ears rang. The world went gray. When his vision cleared, he saw
people running, falling, screaming. A young girl, maybe eight years old, lay motionless a few feet
away, her dress torn and bloodied. He remembers trying to reach her, but his legs would not move.
Someone pulled him into a truck. He does not remember the rest of that day.

What he does remember is what happened next. For the remaining three months, he stayed in the zone.
He continued his work, but something inside him had shifted. He began checking every corner, every
doorway, every parked vehicle. He stopped sleeping. He stopped eating with others. He started
carrying a small metal tool—a jeweler’s file—in his pocket, running his thumb over its ridges to
stay grounded. He never told anyone.

When he returned home, he expected relief. Instead, he found that the war had followed him.

Now, eighteen months later, Marcus works alone in his workshop, repairing watches and setting
stones. He chose this profession, he says, because it demands precision and silence. But the silence
has become heavy. He cannot tolerate loud noises—a car backfiring on the street makes him drop his
tools. He avoids crowded places, including the grocery store. He has not spoken to his mother in
four months because her voice on the phone triggers a memory of the girl’s mother screaming. He has
nightmares three to four times a week: the market, the dust, the stillness of that small body. He
wakes with his hands clenched, the jeweler’s file pressed into his palm.

He drinks two glasses of whiskey every night to “take the edge off.” He has stopped answering calls
from the humanitarian organization. He tells me he feels like a broken watch—still ticking, but not
keeping time.

Marcus looks at his hands as he speaks. They are steady now, but he says they tremble when he is
alone. He wants to feel useful again. He just does not know how.
