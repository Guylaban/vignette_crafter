**Patient Vignette: "Captain L."**

Captain L. is a 29-year-old South Eastern Asian woman, single, and a fighter pilot for her country’s
air force. She presents for therapy at the urging of her commanding officer, who noticed a sharp
decline in her performance and morale over the past six months. She is lean, with a precise manner
of speaking, and sits with her back straight, hands clasped in her lap, as if still in uniform.

The traumatic event occurred eighteen months ago, during a multinational training exercise over open
ocean. Captain L. was flying lead on a two-ship formation. Her wingman, a close friend and fellow
pilot named "Riko," was flying her wing. The mission was routine: a simulated intercept. As they
banked hard to rejoin after a split, Captain L. saw it happen. A sudden, catastrophic mechanical
failure in Riko’s aircraft—a hydraulic lock, later determined—sent the jet into an unrecoverable
flat spin. Over the radio, she heard Riko’s voice, calm at first, then sharp with panic as she tried
to eject. The ejection system fired, but the seat trajectory was wrong. It struck the vertical
stabilizer of her own aircraft. Captain L. watched her friend’s parachute deploy, but it was limp, a
tangled shroud. The ocean swallowed Riko whole.

The search and rescue effort lasted 72 hours. Captain L. was grounded, debriefed, and sent home. She
was told it was not her fault. She knows this intellectually. She was the flight lead, but the
mechanical failure was a "no-fault" event. But in her mind, the sequence plays on a loop, a high-
definition horror film she cannot turn off.

In the six months after the crash, she threw herself into flying. She volunteered for every sortie,
every night mission. She told herself she was honoring Riko’s memory. She felt a grim, hollow pride
in her precision. Then, the cracks appeared. During a routine low-level navigation, she saw a flock
of birds lift from a field. For a split second, her brain interpreted the motion as a parachute. Her
hands went cold. She pulled the stick back too hard, sending the jet into a sharp climb, nearly
causing a stall. The flight surgeon grounded her for a week after that.

Now, she cannot fly. She is on permanent desk duty pending a psychological evaluation. She describes
her days as a "gray fog." She wakes at 3:47 AM every night—the exact time of the crash on the
mission clock—drenched in sweat, heart pounding. She avoids the officer’s mess because the sound of
laughter, or the clatter of a tray, can trigger a flashback. She has stopped answering calls from
her squadron mates. She deleted Riko’s number from her phone, but she still sees it in her mind.

Her concentration is shattered. She was once able to memorize complex tactical data sheets in
minutes. Now, she reads the same paragraph of a maintenance report three times and retains nothing.
She is irritable, snapping at her ground crew for minor errors. She feels a constant, low-grade
guilt that she describes as a "radio static in my chest." She believes, irrationally, that if she
had just turned a half-second sooner, or called a different command, Riko would be alive. She has
started drinking alone at night, two or three glasses of wine, to quiet the static and delay the
nightmare.

She tells me, "I used to be a pilot. Now I’m just a person who sits in a chair and remembers." She
doesn’t cry when she says this. She stares at a point on the wall, her jaw tight. She is not
suicidal, but she has stopped caring about the future. The sky, which was once her home, now feels
like a lid on a coffin.
