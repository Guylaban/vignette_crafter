**Patient Vignette: Aisha, Age 23**

Aisha is a tall, composed young woman who speaks with a deliberate calm that feels more like a held
breath than genuine peace. She works as an environmental scientist for a state agency, spending her
days analyzing soil samples and water quality data in a quiet lab. She came to therapy at the urging
of her supervisor, who noticed she had stopped eating lunch with colleagues and would sometimes
freeze mid-pipette, staring at nothing.

The trauma happened fourteen months ago, on a Tuesday. Aisha’s younger brother, Kofi, was nineteen.
He was a university student, studying mechanical engineering, and he called her that morning to ask
if she wanted him to pick up her favorite *jollof* rice from the place near his campus. She said
yes, distracted, already running late for a field site visit. He laughed and said, "Don’t work too
hard, big sis."

Two hours later, Aisha’s mother called. Aisha was standing in a marsh, knee-deep in muddy water,
collecting samples. She remembers the phone felt cold against her ear. Her mother’s voice was not a
voice but a sound—a raw, tearing noise. Kofi had been hit by a bus while crossing the street. He
died instantly. The *jollof* rice was still in a plastic bag beside him when the paramedics arrived.
Aisha did not scream. She simply let the phone fall into the water.

In the months that followed, Aisha did not cry. She organized the funeral. She wrote the eulogy. She
held her mother’s hand. She returned to work after three weeks because she felt her grief was too
heavy to carry in a small apartment alone. She told herself that being busy was the same as being
okay.

But the world has changed for Aisha. She no longer trusts silence. At the lab, when the centrifuge
hums and the fluorescent lights buzz, her heart begins to pound. She hears the phone ringing in her
mind—that specific ringtone she set for her mother. Sometimes, when a colleague’s phone vibrates on
the counter, she flinches so hard she nearly knocks over her samples.

She avoids the lunchroom because someone left a newspaper open to a page about a traffic accident.
She has stopped driving. She takes the bus, but even that is becoming impossible. Last week, the bus
stopped short, and a man’s elbow brushed her shoulder. She gasped, shoved past two passengers, and
got off three stops early, shaking. She walked the remaining two miles to the lab in the rain, her
boots filling with water.

At night, Aisha lies awake. Not because she is thinking—but because she is *waiting*. She waits for
the phone to ring again. She waits for bad news. She has started checking the news websites every
hour, scanning for bus accidents, for names of the dead. She is convinced that if she stops
checking, someone else she loves will die.

She has not told her mother this. She has not told anyone that she sometimes sees Kofi’s face in the
reflection of her computer screen, or that she flinches when a door slams, or that she has started
counting the seconds between her heartbeats to make sure she is still alive.

During her first session, Aisha sat very still, her hands folded in her lap. She described her work
in precise, technical terms—pH levels, turbidity, contamination thresholds. When asked about Kofi,
she paused for a long time. Then she said, very quietly, "I still have his last text message. I
can’t delete it. But I also can’t open it. It’s just... sitting there."

She looked at the floor. Her voice did not waver. But her hands trembled, just slightly, as if she
were holding something too cold to touch.
