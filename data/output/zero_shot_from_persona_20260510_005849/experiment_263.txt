**Patient Vignette: “Elena”**

Elena is a 30-year-old married woman who works as a bank teller. She presents for therapy at the
encouragement of her husband, who has grown increasingly worried about her over the past eighteen
months.

She describes the trauma as beginning on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon. A man she had seen in the
bank a few times before—always polite, always waiting in her line—approached her window just before
closing. He slid a note across the counter. The note said he had a gun, that he would not hesitate
to use it, and that she was to do exactly as he said. He did not want money. He wanted her.

For the next six hours, Elena was held in the bank’s back office, a small windowless room used for
storage. The man bound her hands with zip ties and forced her to kneel on the concrete floor. He
questioned her about bank procedures, about alarm codes, about security schedules. When she could
not answer, or when he did not believe her, he hurt her. He burned her forearms with a cigarette. He
struck her face with the butt of his gun. He pulled her hair so hard that clumps came out. He told
her that if she screamed, he would kill the cleaning staff he had locked in the break room. She
believed him.

She remembers the sound of her own teeth breaking against the metal of the gun. She remembers the
smell of her own burning skin. She remembers the cold floor against her cheek, the weight of his
boot on her back, the way she stopped fighting because fighting made it worse.

The police arrived after a silent alarm was triggered by a manager who had hidden in the vault. The
man was arrested. Elena was taken to a hospital. She was treated for burns, contusions, a fractured
jaw, and two broken fingers. Physically, she healed. But she has not felt safe since.

Now, eighteen months later, Elena cannot stand in a line. She cannot wait at a counter. She has not
returned to work. Every morning, her husband leaves for his job, and she stays home, circling the
house, checking locks. She checks them three times, sometimes four. She cannot sit with her back to
a door. She cannot be in a room without windows. She has stopped going to the grocery store, stopped
meeting friends for coffee, stopped driving past the bank. The sight of a man in a suit—any
suit—makes her heart race so violently that she has to sit down.

She sleeps in the living room now, on the couch, with the lights on. Her husband sleeps alone in
their bed. She has not let him touch her since the attack. She flinches when he reaches for her,
even to hand her a glass of water. She knows this hurts him. She cannot stop it.

She has nightmares most nights. In them, she is still on that concrete floor. She is still kneeling.
The man is still talking, but his words are nonsense now, a low hum that fills her ears. She wakes
up gasping, her hands clenched into fists, her jaw aching where the bones were broken.

She is irritable. She snaps at her husband for small things—leaving a cabinet open, breathing too
loudly. She feels guilty afterward, but the guilt only makes her angrier. She is hypervigilant. She
cannot relax. She cannot let her guard down. She feels like she is still waiting for something
terrible to happen, even though the man is in prison. Even though she knows he is locked away.

She tells me, in a quiet voice, that she feels like she is still in that room. That she never really
left. That the zip ties are still around her wrists, even though she can see her hands are free.

She does not know how to get out.
