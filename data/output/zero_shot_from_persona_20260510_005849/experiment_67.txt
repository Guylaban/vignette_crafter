**Patient Case Vignette: "Dr. Anya V."**

Dr. Anya V. is a 46-year-old married veterinarian from a small island nation in Oceania. She
presents as a composed, articulate woman, her hands folded neatly in her lap, though her eyes hold a
distant, watchful quality. She speaks with a deliberate calm, as if measuring every word before it
crosses her lips.

The trauma occurred three years ago, during a period of political unrest that escalated into armed
conflict on her home island. Anya was not a soldier. She was the director of a small animal clinic

shell hit the building across the street. The sound was not a bang, she says, but a *tearing*—like

concrete and something else, something sweet and metallic.

She does not tell me about the worst part at first. It comes out in fragments over several sessions.
A young mother brought in a dog, a small terrier mix, that had been hit by shrapnel. The dog was

bullet. The boy died in the clinic, on the same table where the dog lay. Anya had to choose—to try
to save the dog or the child. She chose the child. She failed. The dog also died. She tells me this
story with a flat, hollow voice, her gaze fixed on a point on the wall behind me. She does not cry.

Now, Anya lives in a quiet suburban town in a different country, a place of safety and green lawns.
She runs a successful small animal practice. But the war lives inside her, a constant, uninvited
guest. Her days are governed by a set of invisible rules. She cannot tolerate the sound of a

to excuse herself to the bathroom to breathe into a paper bag. She has stopped watching the news
