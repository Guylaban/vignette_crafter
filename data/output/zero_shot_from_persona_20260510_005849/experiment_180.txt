**Patient Vignette: Mark, Age 33**

Mark has been coming to sessions for three weeks now. He is a lean, soft-spoken man who tends to
arrive early, often sitting in the waiting room with his hands folded, his eyes fixed on a spot on
the wall. He dresses neatly but without flair—dark jeans, a plain t-shirt, a worn leather jacket. He
is a graphic designer, a job he once loved for its precision and control, but now the very act of
sitting at his computer feels like an exercise in survival.

The story he tells begins about fourteen months ago. He was driving home from a late client meeting,
a light rain falling on the windshield. It was just past midnight. He remembers the streetlights
casting long, wet shadows. He was tired, but not impaired. A patrol car pulled him over for a broken
taillight. He was calm, compliant. He handed over his license and registration without a word.

Then, the officer’s flashlight beam swept the back seat of Mark’s car. There was a half-empty water
bottle, a jacket, a gym bag. The officer asked him to step out. Mark complied. He remembers the cold
air on his skin, the feeling of his heart beginning to pound. He was told to put his hands on the
hood of his car. He did. Then, without warning, the officer’s knee drove into the small of his back,
forcing his chest against the metal. He heard the officer shouting, but the words were a blur. He
felt his arms wrenched behind him, the cold bite of handcuffs. He was on the ground, face pressed
against the wet asphalt, the taste of oil and rain in his mouth. He was told he “matched the
description” of a suspect from a nearby robbery. He was held for three hours in a holding cell
before being released. No charges were ever filed. The officer who arrested him never apologized.

Mark tells me this story in a flat, detached voice, as if reading a police report for someone else.
But his hands betray him. They grip the arms of the chair, knuckles white, then release, then grip
again. He cannot sit still.

Since that night, his world has shrunk. He no longer drives after dark. He takes back roads to avoid
police cruisers. He checks his rearview mirror obsessively, sometimes pulling over to let a car pass
just to feel a moment of relief. At work, he cannot focus. The hum of the office printer triggers a
startle response that makes his heart race. He has begun working from home exclusively, but even
there, the sound of a siren on the street sends him under his desk, breath shallow, waiting for it
to pass.

He has stopped seeing his friends. He divorced his wife six months ago, not because of a single
fight, but because he could not bear to be touched, to be questioned, to feel vulnerable in his own
home. He sleeps in a separate room now, alone. His sleep is broken by nightmares—not of the arrest
itself, but of the weight of the officer’s knee, the smell of the asphalt, the sound of his own
voice begging, though he cannot remember what he said.

He tells me he feels “hollow.” He cannot remember what it felt like to be safe, to be normal. He
looks at old photographs of himself and feels nothing—no recognition, only a deep, aching distance.
He says he is tired. Not sleepy, but tired in his bones. He cannot imagine a future. He cannot
imagine feeling better.

He is here because his ex-wife called him one night after he sent her a text that read, “I don’t
know why I’m still here.” He doesn’t elaborate on that text. He just looks at the floor and says, “I
don’t know what else to do.”
