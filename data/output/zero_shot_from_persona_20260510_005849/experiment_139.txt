Maria is a quiet, meticulous young woman who presents as polished and composed, but there is a
brittleness beneath her calm. She is an accountant at a mid-sized firm, where she is known for her
precision and her reluctance to take lunch breaks. She describes herself as “always watching the
clock,” but not in the way her coworkers do. For Maria, time is something that can betray you.

The trauma began when she was sixteen, in the spring of her sophomore year. Her younger brother,
Mateo, then nine, was diagnosed with acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Maria’s parents were immigrants
from Guatemala, working long hours in a restaurant and a cleaning service. When Mateo got sick, the
family’s fragile stability collapsed. Her mother quit her job to be at the hospital. Her father
worked double shifts to pay for treatment, often coming home after midnight, exhausted and silent.
Maria became the de facto parent for her brother during the day, and the household manager at night.

She remembers the hospital room in excruciating detail: the beeping of the IV pump, the smell of
antiseptic, the way Mateo’s veins would collapse during chemo, the nurses’ forced cheerfulness. She
remembers sitting in a plastic chair for eight hours, doing her homework by the dim light of the
bathroom, never leaving because she was terrified that if she did, the machines would stop. She
remembers the night he spiked a fever of 104, and the code blue call. She was alone with him when it
happened. For ten minutes, she held his hand and recited multiplication tables in her head, because
counting was the only thing that kept her from screaming.

Mateo survived. He is now fifteen, healthy, and plays soccer. But Maria never returned to being a
teenager. She graduated high school early, finished her accounting degree in three years, and took
the first job that offered stability. She lives alone in a small apartment with a white noise
machine that runs all night.

In sessions, Maria speaks in a flat, measured tone. She describes her current life as “fine.” She
does not date. She has two friends from college, but she sees them once a month. She avoids
hospitals, doctor’s offices, and any television show with a medical scene. She does not visit her
family on holidays because she cannot bear to see Mateo healthy—not because she is angry, but
because her body still expects him to be sick. When she hears a child cough in a grocery store, her
heart rate spikes and she has to leave her cart and walk out.

At work, she is hypervigilant. She triple-checks every spreadsheet, not because she doubts her math,
but because she cannot tolerate the thought of a mistake that might cost someone something. She has
not taken a sick day in two years. She believes that if she stops moving, if she stops controlling
every variable, something terrible will happen. She sleeps four hours a night. She dreams of
beeping, and of a room with no windows.
