**Patient Vignette: Aisha**

Aisha is a 36-year-old married woman of Middle Eastern descent who works as a construction worker.
She presents as alert but guarded, her body held in a tight, coiled posture as if bracing for
impact. She speaks in a low, measured tone, often pausing to look over her shoulder at the closed
door of the office.

The story begins eighteen months ago, on a humid Tuesday afternoon. Aisha was on a high-rise
scaffold, securing rebar, when a searing pain erupted in her chest. She thought it was a pulled
muscle—she’d had them before. But the pain radiated up her jaw, and her left arm went numb. She
remembers the foreman screaming for someone to call 911, the sound of her own breath whistling
through clenched teeth, and the sensation of the sky tilting as she was lowered to the concrete. In
the ambulance, a paramedic said the word “heart attack,” and Aisha, who had never been sick a day in
her life, felt the world end.

She survived. A stent was placed, and the cardiologist called it a “minor event” with a good
prognosis. But Aisha does not feel like a survivor. She feels like a ghost haunting her own life.

The first weeks at home were a fog of terror. She refused to sleep, convinced her heart would stop
if she closed her eyes. She began checking her pulse obsessively, pressing two fingers to her neck
until the skin was raw. Every twinge in her chest—a muscle spasm, gas, a tight bra strap—sent her
into a spiral. She would stand in the bathroom, gripping the sink, waiting for the crushing pain to
return. Her husband, Hassan, found her there one night, sobbing, whispering, “I’m not ready to die.”

Returning to work was supposed to help. Instead, it became a daily ordeal. The construction site,
once a place of competence and camaraderie, is now a minefield of triggers. The beep of a reversing
truck sounds like a heart monitor alarm. The smell of diesel fuel reminds her of the ambulance. The
sensation of her hard hat pressing on her temples recreates the pressure of an oxygen mask. She
cannot go above the third floor without her vision tunneling. Her crew mates have learned not to
touch her from behind; she has nearly swung a hammer at a man who tapped her shoulder to get her
attention.

Her mind is a battlefield. She has flashbacks not of the event itself, but of the *feeling*—the
absolute certainty that she was dying. These come unbidden, triggered by the most mundane things:
the weight of her own heartbeat after climbing stairs, the sight of her own reflection in a dark
window. She avoids everything that raises her heart rate: sex with Hassan, walking too fast, even
laughing at a joke. She has stopped driving, terrified that a panic attack behind the wheel will
cause an accident. She cannot watch medical dramas, cannot read news stories about people
collapsing, cannot even look at the bottle of aspirin in the medicine cabinet.

She is hypervigilant in a way that exhausts her. She scans her own body for signs of betrayal. She
monitors her breathing, her pulse, her energy level, constantly calculating the risk of every

her own name. She has trouble concentrating on the blueprints; her mind drifts back to the scaffold,
to the whistle of the paramedic’s breath.

chewing his food, for leaving the TV on. She feels a constant, low-grade rage at her own body—for

about her health. She has not told her mother about the nightmares, the ones where she is buried
alive under a pile of concrete, her heart a frantic bird in a cage.
