**Case Vignette: "Ms. A."**

Ms. A. is a 28-year-old elementary school teacher who has been in a relationship with her partner,
Mark, for three years. She presents to therapy after a referral from her primary care physician, who
noted she has been missing work and appears "on edge."

Ms. A. describes the traumatic events as beginning approximately ten months ago, during the previous
school year. She first noticed a man, later identified as a former parent of a student, waiting in
his car near the school parking lot after dismissal. She thought nothing of it at first. But over
the following weeks, he began appearing more frequently—outside the school, at the grocery store
near her apartment, and eventually, on her street. He never spoke to her, but he would stare, and
sometimes he would smile. She remembers the feeling of her stomach dropping the first time she saw
him outside her home. "I thought I was being paranoid," she says, her voice quiet. "I told myself I
was just stressed from work."

The stalking escalated. He began leaving notes on her car windshield—not threatening, but
unsettling. One read, "You have a nice smile." Another said, "I like the way you teach." She changed
her route to work. She stopped going to the gym after dark. She asked the school security guard to
walk her to her car. She filed a police report, but without a direct threat, they offered little
more than a "no-contact order" and advice to document everything. The man was eventually arrested
for violating the order, but he was released on bail pending a court date. He has not been seen in
three months, but Ms. A. does not feel safe.

Now, in the present, Ms. A. is barely able to function as she once did. She has taken a leave of
absence from teaching, unable to face the classroom. The sight of a parent waiting in the car line
triggers a cascade of physical symptoms: her heart pounds, her palms sweat, and she feels a sudden,
overwhelming urge to flee. She has stopped going to the grocery store alone. She installed a home
security system and checks the locks three times before bed. She cannot sit with her back to a door
in a restaurant. Mark tries to be supportive, but she feels a growing distance between them. "He
doesn't get it," she says. "He says, 'He's gone, you're safe.' But I don't feel safe. I feel like
I'm waiting for the other shoe to drop."

Her sleep is fragmented. She has recurrent nightmares—not of the man's face, but of the sensation of
being watched. She will wake up gasping, convinced she heard a footstep on the porch. During the
day, she finds herself scanning crowds, her eyes darting from face to face, looking for his. She has
become irritable with Mark, snapping at him for small things, then feeling guilty. She has withdrawn
from her friends, canceling plans last minute because the thought of leaving the house feels
impossible. She describes a constant, low-grade dread that sits in her chest, like a stone.

The most distressing symptom, she says, is the way her mind has turned on her. She cannot walk past
a parked car without her brain conjuring an image of him sitting inside. She avoids the school
entirely, even though she loved her job. She feels a profound sense of shame. "I'm a teacher," she
says, her eyes welling with tears. "I'm supposed to be the one who helps kids feel safe. And now I
can't even help myself feel safe." She feels broken, not by a single violent act, but by the slow,
insidious erosion of her sense of security in the world. She is no longer the person she was before
she knew his face.
