him. That was the pattern for the next 35 years.

The abuse was cyclical. Periods of calm, almost suffocating tenderness, would be broken by a sudden

shifts in his posture, the tightening of his jaw, the way his voice dropped an octave. She became an

She stopped wearing earrings he hadn't bought her. She told herself she was keeping the peace.

Her work as a database administrator became her sanctuary. In the clean, logical world of SQL
queries and data schemas, there was no unpredictability. She could build structures that functioned
perfectly, that made sense. She would stay late at the office, running reports that no one asked
for, just to delay going home. Her colleagues saw her as quiet, efficient, and reliable—a "rock."
They didn't know she was hiding bruises under long sleeves.

He died five years ago, of a sudden heart attack. Margaret felt a wave of relief so profound it
terrified her. She told no one. The first year of widowhood was a strange, hollow peace. She cleaned
out his closet, donated his suits, repainted the house. She thought she was healing.

But she wasn't. Now, at 67, Margaret cannot sleep without the bedroom door locked and a chair wedged

lot, where she can see all the approaches.
