**Case Vignette: “Mara”**

Mara is a 30-year-old woman of Oceania descent, who presents as composed and direct, though a
tightness settles in her jaw when she speaks of the past. She is a parole officer, a role she chose
after returning home, believing that structure and accountability might help her make sense of the
chaos she witnessed. She is divorced, living alone in a small apartment that she keeps meticulously
organized.

The story begins three years ago, when Mara was a relief worker for an international NGO in a
conflict zone in the Middle East. She was not a soldier, but a logistics coordinator tasked with
ensuring food and medical supplies reached a besieged city. She had been there for six months, and
the work felt meaningful, even amid the constant drone of artillery. The trauma did not occur in a
single explosive moment. It accumulated like dust.

One afternoon, her convoy was ambushed. The gunfire was sudden and deafening. She remembers the
smell of diesel and hot metal, the sound of a colleague—a local translator named Tariq—calling her
name before a bullet struck him. She held pressure on his wound, her hands slick with blood, until
he stopped breathing. She was pinned in the wreckage for four hours before a rescue team arrived.

In the months that followed, Mara completed her rotation and returned home. She did not speak of
Tariq. She did not speak of the dust or the sound of the call over the radio. She threw herself into
her work as a parole officer, believing that if she could just keep moving, the memories would stay
behind her. They did not.

Now, Mara experiences the world as a place where danger is always waiting. In her office, when a
parolee raises his voice, her vision narrows. She feels her heart accelerate, and she has to grip
the edge of her desk to remind herself she is in a fluorescent-lit room, not a burning vehicle. She
has stopped driving through underpasses because the echo of tires sounds like gunfire. At night, she
wakes with a start, reaching for a weapon that is not there, her sheets soaked with sweat.

Her relationships have withered. Her husband, a kind and patient man, could not understand why she
would flinch when he touched her shoulder from behind. He could not understand why she needed the
bedroom door open at all times, or why she would sit in the corner of the living room, facing the
entrance, even during dinner. The divorce was quiet, and she felt relief more than grief.

At work, Mara is effective but brittle. She maintains a professional distance, but she finds herself
hypervigilant during home visits. She scans every room for exits, notes the placement of heavy
objects, and sits with her back to the wall. Her colleagues see her as competent, even cold. They do
not know that she has stopped attending the annual memorial for fallen aid workers because the sight
of photographs makes her feel like she is drowning.

She drinks coffee in the morning to stay alert, and wine in the evening to quiet her mind. She has
not told anyone about the nightmares, the way she sometimes sees Tariq’s face in the reflection of a
window, or the way her hands still feel slick with blood even when they are clean. She came to
therapy because her supervisor noticed she had started to avoid field visits, and she was afraid of
losing her job. She does not yet know how to say that she is not afraid of the parolees—she is
afraid of the moment when the world goes quiet and she is back in that ditch, holding a man who is
already gone.
