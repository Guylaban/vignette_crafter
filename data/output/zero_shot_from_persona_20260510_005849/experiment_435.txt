**Patient Vignette: Mr. A.**

Mr. A. is a 78-year-old, married man of Middle Eastern descent, who presents for an initial
consultation at the urging of his adult daughter. He is a retired roofer, a trade he learned from
his father and practiced for over fifty years. He sits forward in his chair, hands clasped, his
posture stiff. He speaks in a low, deliberate voice, often pausing to look at the floor.

The event that changed everything happened forty-two years ago. Mr. A. was working on a commercial
building in the industrial district. He was applying a new, fast-drying waterproofing compound to a
flat roof. The day was still, windless. He remembers the smell—a sweet, chemical odor that clung to
his throat and nostrils. He worked for hours, the fumes pooling around him in the heat. By late
afternoon, his head pounded, his vision swam, and his chest felt like it was being squeezed in a
vise. He collapsed on the roof. A coworker found him, barely conscious, and called an ambulance.

He was in the hospital for three weeks. The doctors told him he had suffered acute chemical
pneumonitis and solvent poisoning. He was lucky to be alive. For months afterward, he coughed up
phlegm streaked with black, and his lungs ached with every breath. He returned to work, but he never
used that compound again. He never spoke of it. He told himself it was an accident, a fluke, a bad
day.

Now, at 78, the past is no longer past. It began about two years ago. He was in his garden, and a
neighbor started a gas-powered leaf blower. The sharp, acrid smell of exhaust hit him, and suddenly
he was back on that roof. His heart slammed against his ribs. He couldn’t catch his breath. He
dropped his trowel and stumbled inside, his hands trembling.

Since then, the triggers have multiplied. A whiff of paint thinner. A passing truck’s diesel fumes.
The smell of bleach in a hospital hallway. Any strong chemical odor can send him into a state of
high alert. He describes it as a “switch” flipping in his head. He feels a wave of dizziness, his
throat tightens, and he is overcome by a crushing sense of doom. He is convinced, for a terrifying
minute, that he is about to suffocate.

He has changed his life to avoid these triggers. He no longer drives, because the smell of gasoline
at the pump is too much. He has stopped visiting his son, who lives near a refinery. He avoids
hardware stores, gas stations, and the cleaning aisle at the grocery store. His wife does all the
shopping. He spends most of his time indoors, in a house he has made as odorless as possible. He has
stopped using any cleaning products, even soap.

His sleep is broken. He wakes several times a night from dreams about the roof, the heat, the
suffocating pressure. He often feels a phantom tightness in his chest, even when the air is clean.
He is irritable with his wife, snapping at her for small things, then feeling a wave of shame. He
has withdrawn from his grandchildren, afraid that a sudden smell from their home or their clothes
will trigger an episode.

Mr. A. describes himself as a man who “fixed things.” He fixed roofs. He fixed his own truck. He
fixed things around the house. Now, he says, he feels broken. He cannot fix this. He cannot outrun a
smell. The world, once a familiar place of work and family, has become a minefield of invisible
threats. He spends his days in a state of quiet, constant vigilance, waiting for the next sweet,
chemical odor to pull him back to that afternoon on the roof.
