**Case Vignette: Mr. A.**

Mr. A. is a 74-year-old European man who has been attending sessions for the past four weeks,
referred by his primary care physician following a recent decline in his physical health and a
marked increase in anxiety. He is a retired university professor who now works two days a week as a
private tutor for high school students in history and literature. He lives with his partner of
twelve years, a retired librarian, in a small, ground-floor apartment.

The event that has reshaped Mr. A.’s life occurred eighteen months ago. He was visiting a coastal
town he had known since childhood to attend a friend’s memorial service. A sudden, severe storm,
which the local news later described as a “once-in-a-century” flood event, trapped him in his rental
cottage. He describes the water rising not as a wave, but as a silent, relentless presence that
filled the ground floor within minutes. He spent the night on the roof, in the dark, listening to
the water and the wind, and to the sounds of objects—cars, furniture, parts of buildings—colliding
in the current below. He was rescued by a neighbor in a small boat just before dawn. He has not
returned to that town.

In session, Mr. A. is a composed, articulate man who often begins by apologizing for his “rambling.”
He describes his mind as “foggy” and “unreliable,” a stark contrast to the sharp, structured
thinking he relied on for decades as a scholar. He reports that his sleep has been “shattered” since
the flood. He wakes, drenched in sweat, three or four times a night, convinced he hears the sound of
water lapping against the walls of his bedroom. He now sleeps in a recliner in the living room, as
lying flat in a bed makes him feel as though he is “drowning.” His partner has tried to reassure
him, but he finds himself snapping at her, then retreating into a guilty silence.

The most debilitating symptom is his hypervigilance. Mr. A. now has a compulsion to check the
weather forecast. He does this dozens of times a day, on his phone, on the radio, and on the
television. He has installed a weather alert app that makes a specific chime; the sound of any
notification on his phone now causes a sharp spike in his heart rate. He has begun to map the
elevation of every building he enters, and he carries a small, waterproof bag containing a change of
clothes, a flashlight, and a portable charger. He calls this his “kit.” He knows this behavior is
irrational—he lives in a landlocked city, on the second floor—but the feeling of dread is
overwhelming.

His work as a tutor, once a source of purpose and intellectual joy, has become a minefield. He finds
he cannot concentrate on a student’s essay for more than ten minutes before his mind skitters away
to a memory of the black, oily water. He has started to cancel sessions on days when the sky is
overcast and heavy, telling his students he feels unwell. He feels a profound shame about this, as
he believes he is failing the young people who depend on him. He recently told me, “I used to teach
them how to build an argument. Now I can’t even build a sentence in my own head.”

His relationship with his partner has grown strained. He feels emotionally distant, unable to share
the vivid, recurring images that intrude into his mind during the day: the sight of a child’s doll
floating past him, the sound of his own breathing in the dark. He has withdrawn from their shared
activities, such as evening walks and cooking together. He spends most of his free time sitting in
his recliner, watching the Weather Channel, or staring at the ceiling. He feels a pervasive sense of
a foreshortened future, telling me that he no longer makes plans for holidays or visits to his
grandchildren, as he is convinced that “something is going to happen” to end it all. He is not
suicidal, but he lives in a state of constant, low-grade anticipation of the next disaster.
