**Patient Vignette: Miguel R.**

Miguel is a 62-year-old married man, a former professional dancer and current choreographer, who
presents with a guarded but weary demeanor. He sits forward in his chair, his hands resting on his
knees as if poised to rise, a posture he describes as "ready for anything."

The trauma occurred fourteen months ago. Miguel was driving home from a rehearsal late at night. A
drunk driver ran a red light and T-boned his sedan at an intersection. Miguel remembers the sound—a
deafening, metallic crunch that seemed to happen inside his own skull—and the sensation of the car
spinning, then the world tilting sideways. He remembers the smell of gasoline and the cold of the
shattered window against his cheek. He was trapped for twenty minutes before emergency services
arrived. He sustained a fractured pelvis, three broken ribs, and a concussion. He was in the
hospital for ten days and underwent two surgeries.

Now, fourteen months later, Miguel is physically healed. His pelvis has mended, and he has regained
nearly full range of motion. But he has not returned to the stage. He cannot.

The first thing he noticed, in the weeks after the accident, was the hypervigilance. He describes it
as a "new kind of hearing." At home, the creak of a floorboard makes him freeze. A car horn outside
sends a jolt through his chest. He stopped driving entirely; his wife, Elena, drives him everywhere.
When they are in the car, he finds himself bracing his foot against the floorboard, as if pressing a
phantom brake. He watches the intersections with a fixed, unblinking stare, cataloguing every
vehicle's speed and trajectory. "I'm not in the car," he says. "I'm in the collision, over and
over."

This hypervigilance has bled into his work. As a choreographer, he used to move freely across the
studio floor, demonstrating steps, correcting posture, his body a living instrument of instruction.
Now he stands at the back of the room, near the exit. He cannot bear to have dancers approach him
from behind. A sudden touch on his shoulder—even from a well-meaning dancer—makes him flinch and,
once, caused him to lash out verbally. He has started arriving late to rehearsals, deliberately
missing the first fifteen minutes, because the silence before the music begins feels too much like
the silence after the crash.

He has nightmares. They are not of the crash itself, but of its aftermath: the sound of his own
breathing, the smell of the airbag dust, the feeling of his legs being pinned, unable to move. He
wakes up sweating, his heart hammering, convinced he is still trapped. He has started sleeping in
the guest room because his thrashing wakes Elena.

The most painful change, he says, is the loss of spontaneity. Miguel was known for his joy in
movement—a quick spin, a playful leap. Now he feels "stiff." Not in his muscles, but in his spirit.
He avoids crowded places. He no longer takes the subway. He has stopped going to the theater to
watch other performances. "I don't trust the world anymore," he says quietly. "I used to feel like I
was part of the dance. Now I feel like I'm waiting for the music to stop."
