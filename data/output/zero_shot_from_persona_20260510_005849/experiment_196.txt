**Case Vignette: “Leo”**

Leo is a 24-year-old painter who lives with his girlfriend, Maya, in a small apartment in the city.
He came to therapy at Maya’s urging, after she noticed he had stopped sleeping through the night and
had become “jumpy” around her. Leo initially said he was fine, but after a few sessions, he began to
tell a story that started about eight months ago.

The relationship with his ex-partner, a man named David, had been intense from the start. Leo was
23, new to the city, and working freelance on residential interiors. David was charismatic, older,
and seemed to understand Leo’s quiet, careful nature. For the first few months, Leo felt seen. Then
the criticism started—small at first. David would say Leo’s paint fumes were giving him headaches,
that Leo was “too sensitive,” that he needed to “toughen up.” The first time David shoved him, Leo
didn’t call it abuse. He told himself David was just stressed about work.

The violence escalated over six months. Leo remembers the worst night: a Thursday in late autumn. He
had just finished a mural commission—a large, abstract piece for a coffee shop—and came home with
paint still under his fingernails. David was drunk. He accused Leo of flirting with a client. Leo
tried to explain, but David grabbed him by the collar, slammed his head against the kitchen wall,
and then punched him twice in the ribs. Leo fell to the floor. David stood over him, breathing hard,
then walked away. Leo lay there for a long time, staring at the linoleum, feeling the cold seep
through his shirt. He did not call the police. He did not tell his friends. He cleaned the blood off
his face, and the next morning, he finished the mural.

He left David two weeks later, after a second, similar incident. He moved into a shared studio
space, then into the apartment with Maya. He thought that would be the end of it.

But the event has not ended. In therapy, Leo describes how his body still remembers. He has trouble
sleeping—not because of nightmares, but because he wakes at 3:00 a.m. with his heart pounding,
feeling a weight on his chest like someone is pressing him into the bed. He avoids loud noises. When
Maya raises her voice to call him from another room, he flinches. He has stopped painting at home.
He works only on commissioned pieces in a rented studio, and even there, he cannot paint anything
figurative—only abstract shapes, layered and blurred, because “faces feel too close.”

He avoids intimacy with Maya. He says he feels “numb” during sex, and sometimes he sees David’s
face. He does not tell Maya this. He does not tell anyone. He has started drinking three or four
beers most nights to help him sleep, and he has stopped returning calls from his family. He feels a
constant, low-level irritability—snapping at Maya for small things, then apologizing, then hating
himself for the apology.

Leo’s PCL-5 score is 39, indicating a high level of posttraumatic symptoms. He endorses intrusive
memories, efforts to avoid thoughts and feelings related to the trauma, negative beliefs about
himself (“I should have left sooner,” “I am weak”), and hypervigilance. He cannot walk past a bar
where he used to meet David without feeling his throat tighten.

In session, Leo sits very still. He speaks in a low, measured voice. He often looks at the floor.

I want to paint again. I want to be normal.” He does not mention David by name. He refers to him as
“that person.”

He is still with Maya. He loves her. But he is terrified that if she sees the fear in him, she will
leave.
