**Case Vignette: “Sophia” (Age 28, Tax Consultant)**

Sophia is a 28-year-old single woman of South Eastern Asian descent, who works as a tax consultant
for a regional accounting firm. She presents as articulate and composed, dressed in a tailored
blazer and neat blouse, but her hands are restless—folding and unfolding a tissue in her lap. She
speaks in a measured, almost clinical tone, as if she is recounting a case file rather than her own
life.

The trauma began two years ago, during a business trip to a neighboring country. Sophia, then a
senior associate, was tasked with auditing a large manufacturing client. On her third night, after a
late dinner with colleagues, she was abducted from the parking lot of her hotel. Two men forced her
into a van, blindfolded her, and drove her to an unknown location. For the next 72 hours, she was
held in a small, windowless room—a concrete box with a single fluorescent light that never turned
off. She was bound to a chair for most of that time, allowed only brief, supervised trips to a
filthy bathroom. Her captors demanded that she sign false documents implicating her firm in tax
evasion, threatening to harm her family if she refused. She remembers the smell of stale cigarette
smoke, the sound of a distant generator, and the constant, low hum of the fluorescent light. She
signed the papers on the second day.

After a ransom was paid, she was released on a roadside at dawn. She returned to her home city, but
she did not return to her life.

In the months that followed, Sophia could not be alone in a room without a window. She moved her
desk at work to face the door, and she now keeps her office blinds open at all times. She has not
taken a business trip since. The sound of a humming fluorescent light—any light—triggers a cold
sweat and a racing heart. She cannot sit in the back seat of a car without feeling her throat close.
At home, she sleeps with all the lights on, and she has installed multiple locks on her bedroom
door, which she checks three times before bed.

Her work has become a refuge and a prison. As a tax consultant, she is meticulous, detail-oriented,
and thrives on order. But now, her need for control has become consuming. She spends hours reviewing
her own work, convinced she has missed a hidden error that will expose her to retaliation. She has
begun to see her clients as potential threats—people who might be hiding something, who might trap
her. She avoids meetings with new clients, delegating them to junior staff. Her performance reviews
have slipped from “excellent” to “average,” though no one has confronted her yet.

Socially, she has withdrawn. She stopped seeing friends because she cannot tolerate being in crowded
restaurants or bars—too many exits, too many strangers. She has not dated since the abduction. She
feels numb, as if a glass wall separates her from other people. She says she feels “like a ghost in
my own life.”

The worst is at night. She wakes three or four times, convinced she hears footsteps or the hum of a
generator. She has nightmares where she is back in the concrete room, but this time, no one comes to
release her. She has started drinking—a glass of wine to fall asleep, then another when she wakes.
She has not told her family about the drinking, or about the flashbacks that strike when she sees a
van on the highway.

Sophia recently began seeing a therapist after her primary care doctor suggested it. She is
hesitant, skeptical that talking will help. She describes herself as “fine,” then pauses, and her
eyes fill with tears that she quickly wipes away. “I just want to feel safe again,” she says, her
voice barely above a whisper. “I don’t remember what that feels like.”
