**Patient Vignette: A. (Age 30, Female, School Counselor)**

A. presents as a composed, articulate woman who sits very still in the armchair, her hands folded in
her lap. She is a school counselor at a large public high school, a role she has held for five
years. She is married to a teacher at the same school. She came to therapy at the urging of her
husband, who has grown concerned about her "disappearing acts."

The event happened eleven months ago, on a Tuesday afternoon in late spring. A. was driving home
from a district meeting. A car ahead of her ran a red light at a major intersection. A. slammed on
her brakes, avoiding a collision by inches. Before she could process the near-miss, a police
cruiser, which had been stopped in the oncoming turn lane, activated its lights and siren and pulled
in behind her. The officer, a large man with a shaved head, approached her window. He did not ask if
she was alright. He demanded her license and registration, his voice flat and loud. She fumbled for
her purse. He told her to step out of the vehicle. She complied, her legs shaking. He made her stand
on the curb, hands on the hood of her car, while he ran her information. He told her she had "failed
to yield to an emergency vehicle." She tried to explain she had been avoiding an accident. He told
her to be quiet. He wrote her a citation. He did not ask if she was hurt. He did not offer any
explanation. He simply left, leaving her standing on the curb, the afternoon sun hot on her back,
the citation a thin, cold piece of paper in her hand.

She drove home in a state of numbness. She did not tell her husband. She thought she was fine. She
was a counselor. She helped students process their own traumas. She knew the language of coping. She
told herself it was a bad minute in an otherwise good day.

The first sign something was wrong came a week later. She was in the grocery store. A man in a dark
blue jacket walked past her. She froze. Her heart hammered against her ribs. She could not breathe.
She left her cart in the aisle and walked out of the store. She drove home and sat in the driveway
for forty minutes, unable to get out of the car. She told herself she was being dramatic.

At work, she has become vigilant. She scans the parking lot before she gets out of her car. She
chooses her seat in the faculty lounge so she has a clear view of the door. She flinches when she
hears a siren, even on the school’s PA system. She has started taking a different, longer route to
work to avoid the intersection. She cannot listen to the police scanner in the security office
anymore. She used to find it reassuring; now it sounds like a threat.

The nightmares started three months ago. She dreams of the blue lights, the sound of the siren, the
officer’s voice telling her to be quiet. She wakes up drenched in sweat, her heart pounding,
convinced she is back on that curb. She cannot sleep in the same bed as her husband anymore. She
sleeps on the couch, a blanket pulled over her head, a small nightlight on in the kitchen. She tells
him she has a stomach ache.

Her husband, James, has noticed. He says she is "not herself." She snaps at him when he asks simple
questions. She has stopped cooking, stopped going to their weekly board game night with friends. She
has become irritable. Last week, a student knocked on her office door unexpectedly and she screamed
at him to go away. She apologized later, but the student looked scared. She felt a wave of shame so
intense she wanted to disappear.

The most troubling symptom, the one that finally brought her here, is the avoidance. She is avoiding
her own life. She has stopped driving. She takes the bus. She has stopped going to the gym. She has
stopped answering her phone. She feels like she is living in a glass box, watching the world move
around her, but she cannot touch it. She feels detached from her own body, as if she is watching a
movie of herself. She knows, intellectually, that she is safe. She knows the officer was just doing
his job. But her body does not know. Her body remembers the sound of his voice, the feel of the hot
metal of the car hood under her hands, the helplessness of being told to be quiet. She is a
counselor who helps others find their voice, and she cannot find her own. She is here because she is
exhausted. She is tired of being afraid.
