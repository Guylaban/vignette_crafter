**Case Vignette: Mr. L.**

Mr. L. is a 49-year-old married European male who serves as a city council member. He presents for
therapy at the urging of his wife, who has grown increasingly concerned about his irritability and
social withdrawal over the past six months. He is well-groomed and articulate, but his posture is
rigid, and he avoids direct eye contact when speaking about his past.

The story begins not in a council chamber, but in a dusty, sun-baked village in a country he can no
longer bear to name aloud. Twenty-three years ago, Mr. L. was a platoon leader. He remembers the
heat, the smell of diesel and cumin, and the constant, low-grade hum of fear. The defining moment
came during a routine patrol. An IED detonated under the lead vehicle. Mr. L. was thrown clear, but
he was the first to reach the wreckage. He pulled his driver, a young man named Private First Class
Miller, from the burning hull. Miller’s legs were gone. Mr. L. held a tourniquet on what remained,
trying to keep a conversation going, telling Miller about the fishing cabin he planned to buy.
Miller died in his arms, looking at him with an expression Mr. L. calls "the question." *Why didn’t
you save me?* Mr. L. still hears that question, not as a memory, but as a whisper that can cut
through any conversation.

In the months following his return, he functioned. He married his college sweetheart, earned a law
degree, and entered politics. He built a reputation as a pragmatic, steady hand. He learned to
compartmentalize. But the compartments have begun to leak.

Mr. L. now lives with a constant, low-voltage current of hypervigilance. During city council
meetings, he cannot sit with his back to the door. He scans the public gallery for threats, not for
constituents. When a veteran’s advocate spoke passionately about funding for homeless shelters, Mr.
L.’s heart rate spiked, his palms grew slick, and he disassociated for nearly thirty seconds, seeing
the advocate’s face morph into Miller’s. He has started delegating public hearings, claiming a "full
schedule," when in reality the crowds feel like a kill zone.

His sleep is a battleground. He wakes three or four times a night, drenched in sweat, having just
slammed his fist into the mattress, trying to extinguish a phantom fire. He avoids the news,
particularly any coverage of military action, because the sound of a helicopter on television can
trigger a full-blown panic attack. He has stopped attending the annual Fourth of July barbecue his
wife hosts, unable to tolerate the sudden, sharp crack of fireworks.

His wife reports he has become "hollow." He no longer laughs at her jokes. He has stopped initiating
intimacy. He finds himself staring at the wall for long stretches, mentally replaying the drive back
to base, the smell of the burning Humvee clinging to his uniform. He feels a profound, corrosive
guilt. He believes he is a fraud, a man who built a life on a lie of competence, while the real man
died in that desert with Miller. His PCL-5 score of 42 is consistent with a severe level of PTSD
symptomatology, but he does not know that number. He only knows he feels like a ghost haunting his
own life, a councilman who cannot govern his own mind.
