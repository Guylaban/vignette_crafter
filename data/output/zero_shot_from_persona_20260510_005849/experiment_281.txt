**Case Vignette: Elena**

Elena comes to session with a composed, almost professional demeanor, her posture straight and her
hands folded in her lap. A retired physical therapist, she still carries herself with the quiet
authority of someone who spent decades guiding others through recovery. She is 74, married for 48
years to a retired engineer, and lives in a tidy suburban home with a garden she tends meticulously.
She does not look like someone who fled a war, and she says this often.

The trauma began in 1993, during the Bosnian War. Elena was 43, a physical therapist in a Sarajevo
hospital. She describes the siege not as a single event but as a slow, grinding collapse of the
world she knew. She remembers the sound of shelling as a constant rhythm—a dull, percussive
heartbeat that never stopped. She remembers the smell of damp concrete and cordite. She remembers
the weight of the body she had to carry from the ward to the morgue one afternoon, a young man whose
leg she had been treating for shrapnel wounds. He had been killed by a sniper while trying to fetch
water.

She and her husband, Amir, escaped in a UN convoy after three months. They crossed the border into
Croatia, then to Germany, and finally to the United States as refugees. She remembers the moment of
leaving most vividly: standing at the back of a truck, clutching a single suitcase, watching the
city she had known her entire life disappear into smoke. She did not cry. She told herself she was a
professional, trained to stay calm under pressure.

Now, three decades later, Elena has not cried about it once. She says this with a faint, almost
apologetic smile. She is here because her husband insisted. He told her, gently, that she has not
been sleeping. That she startles when the mail truck rumbles down the street. That she has stopped
visiting their daughter in Chicago because the drive feels "too long."

Her PCL-5 score is 35, indicating clinically significant PTSD symptoms. But Elena does not see
herself as traumatized. She sees herself as someone who "handled it." She has spent her life helping
others rebuild their bodies after injury; now, she cannot name the injury in her own mind.

The symptoms are subtle but pervasive. She avoids news reports about war, even documentaries about
nature. She once loved gardening, but now the sound of a lawnmower makes her chest tighten. She
cannot sit with her back to a door in a restaurant. She has not been to a movie theater in ten years
because the darkness and the sudden loud noises feel like an ambush.

Her sleep is fragmented. She wakes at 3:47 a.m. every night, her heart pounding, her hands cold. She
does not remember a nightmare, only a feeling of falling. She lies still, listening to the house
settle, and tells herself it is nothing. In the morning, she makes coffee, folds laundry, and
answers the phone in a steady voice. No one would know.

The most troubling symptom, for her, is the hypervigilance. She has always prided herself on her
calm, clinical presence. Now, she finds herself scanning parking lots for exits. She checks the
locks on the doors three times before bed. When her husband sneezes loudly, she flinches so hard she
drops her teacup. She feels ashamed, as if her body is betraying the discipline she built over a
lifetime.

She has not told anyone this. Not her daughter, not her friends at the senior center, not even Amir.
She is here, she says, to "get back to normal." But when asked what normal would look like, she
pauses for a long time. She cannot remember.
