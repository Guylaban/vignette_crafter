**Case Vignette: "Leilani"**

Leilani is a 35-year-old married woman of Oceania heritage who presents for therapy at the urging of
her husband, after several months of what she calls "just being on edge." She is an accountant at a
mid-sized firm, a job she once found grounding and predictable. Now, she describes the office as a
place where she feels "trapped."

The trauma occurred eight months ago, on a Thursday evening in late autumn. Leilani had stayed late
at work to close out a quarterly report. She remembers the exact time—7:47 PM—because she had texted
her husband, "Leaving now. See you in 20." The parking garage was dim, the fluorescent lights
humming. As she reached her car, a man appeared from between two vans. He was young, she recalls,
with a hood pulled tight around his face. He demanded her purse. She froze. He shoved her against
the driver’s side door, and she felt the cold metal of a blade press against her ribs. "Give me
everything," he hissed. She handed him her bag, her phone, her work laptop. He ran. She stood there,
shaking, for what felt like an eternity before a security guard found her.

In the weeks that followed, Leilani tried to "get back to normal." She reported the mugging to the
police, bought a new phone, and replaced her work files from a backup. But the normal she sought has
remained elusive. She now arrives at the office before sunrise, refusing to walk through the parking
garage after dark. If a colleague asks her to stay late, she makes an excuse. Her supervisor has
noticed, and she fears her performance reviews will suffer. "I used to love the quiet of the office
after everyone left," she says. "Now, the silence feels dangerous."

Her husband, a gentle man named Tane, has become her anchor. But even he cannot fully reach her. At
night, she startles awake at the slightest sound—the creak of the house settling, the distant bark
of a dog. She often sees the man’s face in her mind, though she never saw it clearly. She has
stopped wearing her wedding ring because she feels it makes her a target. She avoids the cash
register at the grocery store, preferring self-checkout, because standing in line makes her feel
"trapped" and vulnerable to being watched.

Leilani describes a constant state of vigilance. She scans every room she enters for exits. She has
memorized the security camera locations at her office. She no longer listens to music on her

find me there too."

Her relationship with Tane has become strained. She has withdrawn from their usual Friday night
dinners out, preferring to stay home with the blinds drawn. She flinches when he touches her

visceral terror that grips her. "I don't recognize myself," she tells me, her eyes welling. "I used

The memory of the mugging is not a story she can tell; it is a presence that lives inside her. It
intrudes without warning: the metallic taste of fear, the pressure of the blade against her ribs,

racing. She has begun to wonder if she is "going crazy." She is not. She is trapped in a moment that
ended eight months ago, but which has never truly passed.
