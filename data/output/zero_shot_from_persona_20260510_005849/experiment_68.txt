**Patient Vignette: Samira**

Samira is a 51-year-old married woman of Middle Eastern descent, who presents as composed and
articulate, though her hands occasionally tremble as she smooths the fabric of her skirt. She has
managed a large downtown hotel for the past twelve years, a role she once loved for its fast pace
and precision. Now, she says, the job feels like a trap.

The trauma occurred fourteen months ago, during what Samira describes as a "routine maintenance
oversight." A cleaning crew, unfamiliar with the hotel’s new industrial-grade disinfectant, mixed
two incompatible chemicals in a sealed storage closet on the third floor. Samira was walking past
the door when she heard a muffled pop and a hiss. Before she could step away, a cloud of pale, acrid
gas billowed into the hallway. She inhaled deeply before she could cover her mouth.

For the first few seconds, she felt only a burning in her throat and a sharp, metallic taste. She
yelled for everyone to evacuate, her managerial instincts overriding her own panic. By the time the
fire department arrived, her eyes were streaming, and she was coughing so violently she could not
stand. She spent four hours in the emergency room, receiving oxygen and bronchodilators. The doctors
told her the exposure was acute but not life-threatening. Her lungs cleared within two days.

But her mind did not clear.

Now, Samira cannot walk past the storage closet on the third floor without her heart slamming
against her ribs. She takes the stairs instead of the elevator to avoid that hallway, even though it
adds ten minutes to her rounds. She has instructed housekeeping to leave all closet doors propped
open, a policy that has confused her staff. When a guest complained about a faint smell of bleach in
their room last week, Samira excused herself, went into her office, and hyperventilated into a paper
bag for fifteen minutes.

At home, she has become hypervigilant about cleaning products. Her husband, a kind and patient man,
now buys only unscented soap and vinegar-based cleaners. But last month, when he used a spray
bathroom cleaner without checking the label, Samira flew into a rage she could not explain. She
threw the bottle in the trash and did not speak to him for two hours. She later apologized, but the
shame lingers.

She avoids public spaces where strong smells might surprise her—gas stations, nail salons, the
laundry aisle at the grocery store. She has stopped cooking with garlic or onions because the sizzle
of oil reminds her of the chemical hiss. She eats plain rice and boiled vegetables, a diet that has
left her tired and thin.

Sleep is a battlefield. She wakes at 3:00 AM most nights, her throat tight, convinced she smells
that pale gas again. She gets up, walks through the house, opens windows, and checks the stove. Her
husband has learned not to ask where she is going.

At work, she is still competent, but brittle. She delegates tasks she once handled
herself—inspecting storage rooms, approving cleaning supplies, meeting with maintenance crews. Her
assistant now handles those duties, and Samira feels a quiet shame watching her do them. She has not
told anyone at the hotel about the incident, not even her regional manager. She fears being seen as
weak or, worse, a liability.

Samira knows something is wrong. She feels it in her chest when she walks through the lobby and sees
a mop bucket. She feels it in her stomach when a guest sneezes and she flinches. She feels it in her
bones when she thinks about the future and sees only a narrowing hallway, lined with sealed doors.
