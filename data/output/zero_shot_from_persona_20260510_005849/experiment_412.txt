**Patient Vignette: Mr. A.**

Mr. A. is a 72-year-old man who lives alone in a one-bedroom apartment in a quiet suburban complex.
He is a retired software engineer, having spent the bulk of his career working for a defense
contractor. He is thin, with a precise manner of speaking, and his posture is rigid, as if he is
bracing himself for a sudden impact. He presents for an initial consultation at the urging of his
primary care physician, who noted a significant spike in his blood pressure and chronic insomnia.

The story he tells begins in 1971. He was 24 years old, a junior engineer on a classified project.
During a business trip to a politically unstable country in Southeast Asia, he was detained by local
security forces. He was accused of espionage—a charge he insists was a case of mistaken identity.
For three weeks, he was held in a concrete room. He describes the interrogation sessions with a
chilling, technical detachment, as if he is recounting the debugging of a faulty code. The methods,
he says, were “systematic.” He was kept in stress positions for hours. He was deprived of sleep. He
was hooded. He was subjected to what he calls “the water treatment.”

For decades, Mr. A. did not speak of this. He returned to the United States, completed his project,
and had a successful career. He never married. He worked long hours, finding solace in the clean,
logical structure of programming languages. “The code never lies,” he tells me. “It either compiles
or it doesn’t.” He retired at 65, and for a time, the structure of his days held.

Now, at 72, the structure is crumbling. He wakes at 3:00 AM every morning, drenched in sweat, his
heart pounding. He describes the feeling as “a system overload.” There is no specific dream, he
says, just a sensation of pressure on his chest and the sound of a metal door slamming shut. He has
become hypervigilant about his apartment. He has installed three deadbolts on his front door, and he
checks them twice before bed. He cannot tolerate the sound of a helicopter overhead, a noise that is
common in his neighborhood due to a nearby hospital. When he hears it, he freezes, his hands
clenching into fists, and he is pulled back to the concrete room.

His social world has contracted to near zero. He has a single friend, a former colleague, but he has
stopped answering his calls. “I don’t have the energy for the translation,” he says. He explains
that he feels a constant need to translate his internal state into acceptable social language, and
the effort has become exhausting. He spends most of his time in his home office, tinkering with old
code on a vintage computer. He finds the silence of the machine comforting.

The most troubling symptom, to him, is a profound emotional numbness. He describes it as a
“flatlined EKG.” He cannot feel joy, but more disturbingly, he cannot feel anger or sadness. He
recently attended a funeral for his brother. He stood at the grave, felt nothing, and was terrified
by his own emptiness. “I am a simulation of a person,” he says. “I perform the correct algorithms
for grief, but there is no data behind the output.”

He does not see himself as a victim. He sees himself as a machine that was damaged by a foreign
system. His PCL-5 score of 34 indicates a high probability of PTSD, but he resists the label. He is
here, he says, not for healing, but for a “diagnostic.” He wants to know why his operating system is
crashing. He is wary of me, of the therapeutic process, viewing it as another interrogation. He sits
on the edge of the chair, ready to leave, but he does not leave. He stays, and he speaks, because
the code has finally, for the first time in fifty years, produced an error he cannot solve alone.
