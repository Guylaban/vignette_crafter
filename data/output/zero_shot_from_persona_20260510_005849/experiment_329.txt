**Patient Vignette: Mrs. L.**
**Age 74, Female, South Eastern Asian, Married, Software Engineer**

Mrs. L. is a 74-year-old woman who presents for an initial evaluation at the urging of her husband,
who says she has “not been herself” for the past two years. She is a retired software engineer who
still consults part-time from home, writing code for a small tech firm. She describes herself as
“always the one who fixed things,” a problem-solver who could debug any system. But now, she says,
“the system is broken, and I can’t find the error.”

The trauma began three years ago, when her 42-year-old daughter, Anh, was diagnosed with a rare,
aggressive form of leukemia. Mrs. L. remembers the exact moment: the phone call in her home office,
the sterile voice of the oncologist, the way the numbers on her monitor blurred into meaningless
symbols. She had always believed that if she worked hard enough, planned carefully enough, she could
protect her family. But this was a problem she could not solve with code. For eighteen months, Anh
underwent chemotherapy, stem cell transplants, and multiple hospitalizations. Mrs. L. spent every
night by her daughter’s bedside, watching the monitors, learning the medical jargon, trying to
anticipate every crisis. She slept in a chair, ate vending machine crackers, and stopped answering
calls from her other children. She told herself she was being strong, but inside, she was holding
her breath.

Anh is now in remission. She is healthy, back at work, raising her own children. But Mrs. L. cannot
feel relief. Instead, she feels as though she is still in the hospital room. She describes a
constant, low-level dread, like a hum in her ears. She avoids the neighborhood where the hospital is
located. She cannot watch television shows that mention cancer, even in passing. When her husband
suggests visiting Anh’s family, Mrs. L. finds excuses: a headache, a deadline, fatigue. She knows
this makes no sense—her daughter is fine—but the thought of walking into that house, seeing Anh’s
face, triggers a physical response. Her heart races, her palms sweat, and she feels an urge to run,
though there is nowhere to go.

At night, she dreams of beeping machines and the smell of antiseptic. She wakes up at 3:00 AM,
convinced she has missed something, that the cancer has returned and she has failed to notice. She
checks her phone obsessively, scrolling through old text messages from the hospital, rereading the
lab results she saved in a folder on her desktop. She has stopped going to her weekly book club. She
canceled her gym membership. Her work, once a source of pride, now feels hollow. She stares at lines
of code for hours, unable to concentrate, her mind drifting back to the hospital room.

Her husband says she is “stuck.” She agrees. She says she feels like a computer that has frozen on a
single error message, looping endlessly. She is ashamed of her fear. She tells herself that other
mothers have lost children, that she should be grateful. But gratitude does not stop the nightmares.
It does not stop the way she flinches when her phone rings. It does not stop the hollow feeling in
her chest, as if something vital has been deleted and cannot be recovered. She is here because she
is tired of living in a past she cannot debug.
