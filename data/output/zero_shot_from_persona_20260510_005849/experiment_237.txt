**Patient Case Vignette: "Mei-Lin"**

Mei-Lin is a 61-year-old married woman of South Eastern Asian descent who presents to the clinic at
the urging of her husband, a retired engineer. She is a sculptor of considerable renown in her
community, known for her large-scale, abstract bronze works that evoke themes of resilience and
growth. She has been married for thirty-eight years and has two adult children who live in a
neighboring state.

Mei-Lin’s trauma occurred two years ago, in the late afternoon. She was driving home from her
studio, a converted warehouse she shares with two other artists, when she witnessed a multi-car
pileup on the highway. She was not physically injured, but her car was stopped about thirty yards
from the initial collision. She saw a sedan flip several times and then erupt into flames. She saw
the driver, a young man, trapped inside, his arm reaching out through the shattered window before
the fire consumed the vehicle. She remembers the sound of the metal twisting, the horn that wouldn’t
stop blaring, and the smell of burning rubber and gasoline that seemed to fill her car even with the
windows rolled up.

In the immediate aftermath, Mei-Lin functioned on autopilot. She gave a statement to the police, her
voice steady, her hands only slightly trembling. She called her husband and told him she would be
late for dinner. She did not cry. For the next three weeks, she worked obsessively in her studio, a
frantic energy driving her. She began a new piece, a towering figure she called “The Reaching Arm,”
but she couldn’t finish it. She kept stopping, staring at the bronze, and then walking away.

The nightmares began about a month after the accident. They are not replays of the crash, but
abstract, terrifying dreams. She dreams of being trapped in her own studio, the walls closing in,
the air thick with the smell of scorched metal. She wakes gasping, her heart pounding, and cannot go
back to sleep. Her husband tells her she sometimes cries out in her sleep, a single, sharp syllable.

The hypervigilance is the most debilitating symptom in her daily life. She can no longer drive on
the highway. She takes back roads to her studio, a route that adds forty minutes to her commute. She
cannot sit with her back to the door in a restaurant. She has stopped going to gallery openings,
which were once a vital part of her professional life, because the crowds feel suffocating and she
needs to be able to see every exit. The smell of a barbecue or a gas stove can send her into a
panic, causing her to feel dizzy and nauseous.

Her work has changed. She used to work with a confident, almost aggressive physicality, hammering
and welding with focused passion. Now, she finds herself avoiding the forge and the welding torch.
The heat and the sound of the metal are too triggering. She has shifted to working only with cold
clay and stone, a medium she finds quieter but less fulfilling. She describes her current pieces as
“smaller, more contained.” She is afraid of the scale of her old work. She has not been able to
complete a single large piece since the accident.

Mei-Lin is irritable with her husband, snapping at him for small things, like the way he chews his
toast or the volume of the television. She feels a deep, pervasive guilt. She obsesses over the
young man in the car, wondering if she could have done something, if she should have gotten out of
her own car. She knows this is irrational, but the thought loops through her mind dozens of times a
day. She avoids the news and any mention of traffic accidents.

She has not told her children about the depth of her struggles. She tells them she is “just tired”
or “working through a difficult piece.” She feels a profound sense of shame that this event, which
she only witnessed, has so profoundly derailed her. She prides herself on being a strong, creative
person, and she cannot reconcile that identity with the woman who now jumps at the sound of a car
backfiring and cannot bring herself to finish a sculpture about a reaching arm. She feels hollowed
out, as if the fire that day consumed not just a life, but a vital part of her own.
