**Patient: Kofi, age 22**

Kofi sits very still in the chair, his posture rigid, his hands resting flat on his thighs as if
he’s bracing himself for an impact. He is a school principal, a position of immense responsibility
for a man so young, but he carries himself with a gravity that makes him seem older. He tells me he
was born in a small village in West Africa, the eldest of four children. He describes his father, a
farmer, as a man of few words and a heavy hand.

The story begins when Kofi was seven. He remembers the exact sound: the sharp *thwack* of a leather
belt against his own skin, followed by the sting that felt like fire. The reason was a minor one—he
had lost a single coin his father had given him to buy salt. The beatings were not a single event
but a climate. They were a weekly, sometimes daily, occurrence. His father’s anger was a predictable
storm, triggered by a dropped cup, a low test score, a perceived look of defiance. Kofi learned to
read the tension in his father’s shoulders, the slight tightening of his jaw. He learned to make
himself small, to speak in a whisper, to anticipate the blow before it landed. His mother would look
away. The other children would hide.

He escaped through education. He was brilliant, a fact that seemed to both anger and, later, impress
his father. He earned a scholarship to a secondary school in the capital, then a university degree.
He never went back. Now, he is the principal of a small, struggling primary school in a different
city. He is in a relationship with a kind woman named Amara, a nurse who works long shifts.

In session, Kofi describes his life as a constant state of high alert. He does not use the word
“hypervigilance.” He says, “I am always watching.” He watches the faces of his teachers for a
flicker of anger. He watches the children in the yard for a sudden, aggressive movement. When a
child falls and scrapes a knee, his own heart races before he can even process the sound. He does
not yell. He never yells. Instead, he speaks in a low, measured tone that his staff finds “calm” and
“authoritative.” But inside, he tells me, his mind is a screaming noise. He feels his father’s
presence in the room, a phantom limb of dread.

The most vivid intrusion comes not in nightmares, but in the daily grind. When he has to discipline
a student, even for a small infraction like talking in class, his hands begin to tremble. He sees
not the child’s face, but his own seven-year-old face, looking up in terror. He feels a nauseating
wave of guilt, as if *he* is the one about to be punished. He cannot bring himself to use any form
of consequence that feels punitive. He avoids conflict so completely that his school is beginning to
suffer from a lack of structure. Teachers complain he is too lenient.

He sleeps in a separate room from Amara. He tells her it’s because he kicks in his sleep, which is
true. He doesn’t tell her that he wakes up three or four times a night, drenched in sweat, having
just heard the sound of that belt buckle jingling. He doesn’t tell her that he checks the locks on
the doors and windows three times before he can even try to lie down. He is terrified of being a
father. He and Amara have discussed marriage, children. The thought sends a cold spike of fear
through his chest. He is convinced that if he has a son, he will become his father. Or worse, that
his own son will somehow trigger the rage he keeps so tightly bottled, and he will destroy
everything he has built.

He has a PCL-5 score of 71. He does not know what that number means. All he knows is that he is
tired. He is bone-tired from the constant watching, the constant bracing, the constant effort of
being a calm man in a world that feels, to him, like it is always one wrong move away from violence.
