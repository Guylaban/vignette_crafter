**Patient Vignette: Priya (Age 45, South Asian, Single, Plumber)**

Priya arrives to session still in her work boots, a fine layer of copper dust settled into the
creases of her jeans. She apologizes for the smell of solder, then laughs—a short, hollow
sound—saying it’s better than the smell of the hospital. She has been a plumber for twenty-two
years, one of the few women in her union, and she took pride in the physical certainty of her work:
the torque of a wrench, the predictable logic of a pipe system. That certainty is gone now.

The trauma began eighteen months ago, though she didn’t recognize it as such at the time. She woke
one morning with a strange, deep ache in her ribs, like a dull pressure behind her lungs. She
ignored it for three weeks. When she finally went to the emergency room, a CT scan revealed a
massive pulmonary embolism—a blood clot the size of her thumb lodged in her right pulmonary artery.
The doctor used words like “critical” and “immediately,” and Priya remembers the fluorescent lights
of the ICU flickering above her as a nurse shaved her chest for a central line. She remembers
thinking, *I’m not done. I have a job site to finish on Thursday.*

She survived. The clot was dissolved with thrombolytics. But something else lodged itself in her
chest that day—a permanent, visceral memory of her own body betraying her. In the months that
followed, she began to feel like a stranger in her own skin.

At work, she finds herself unable to crawl into tight spaces under sinks. The feeling of being
enclosed, of the walls pressing in, triggers a cascading panic. She will stand in a client’s
bathroom, staring at the access panel, her heart hammering against her sternum until she has to walk
outside. She tells her boss she has a “bad back.” She has lost three regular contracts this quarter
because she cannot finish the jobs. The financial strain is real, but she cannot explain the real
reason—that her body feels like a ticking bomb, that any sudden twinge in her chest sends her into a
spiral of certainty that she is dying again.

At night, she sleeps sitting up in a recliner. Lying flat makes her feel the phantom pressure in her
ribs, the sensation of the clot. She has not slept in a bed in over a year. Her dreams are not
replays of the hospital, but of water—flooded basements, pipes bursting in walls, a slow, rising
tide she cannot stop. She wakes gasping, her hands numb.

Her social world has shrunk to the size of her living room. She is single, and her family—her mother
and two sisters—live three hours away. They call, but she lets it go to voicemail. She cannot bear
to hear her mother’s voice, because her mother’s voice sounds like the phone call she almost had to
make. Priya avoids all reminders of illness: she cannot watch medical dramas, she crosses the street
to avoid walking past a pharmacy, and she has not been to a grocery store in six months because the
sight of the blood pressure machine by the pharmacy counter makes her dizzy.

She is hypervigilant in a way that exhausts her. Every heartbeat is monitored. Every ache is
catalogued. She checks her pulse obsessively—ten, twenty times a day—pressing two fingers to her
neck until she finds the rhythm, then waiting for it to skip. She carries a small bottle of baby
aspirin in her pocket, even though her doctor told her she no longer needs it. She cannot throw it
away.

The worst part, she says, is the isolation of it. She cannot tell her coworkers why she is failing.
She cannot tell her mother that she is afraid to fall asleep. She is a plumber—she fixes things. But
she cannot fix this. She sits in the chair across from me, her hands folded in her lap, and she
says, quietly, “I used to know what my body could do. Now I just wait for it to break again.”
