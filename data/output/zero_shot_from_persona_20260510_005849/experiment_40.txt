**Patient Vignette: “Amina”**

Amina is a 66-year-old South Asian woman, married for forty-two years, who has worked as a midwife
for over three decades. She presents as composed and articulate, though her hands tremble slightly
when she removes her cardigan in the office. She sits very still, as if holding herself together by
an act of will.

She begins her story in 1971, during the Bangladesh Liberation War. She was twenty-two, a newly
trained nurse working in a field hospital near the border. She describes the smell of rain-soaked
earth and the constant distant thud of artillery. One evening, the hospital was overrun by Pakistani
soldiers. She was separated from her colleagues and forced to treat wounded soldiers for three days.
She was not physically assaulted, she says carefully, but she watched as other women were taken
away. She heard screams she still hears at night. On the third day, she escaped by hiding in a
supply truck that carried bodies to a mass grave. She lay among the dead for hours, holding her
breath, feeling the cold skin of strangers pressed against her own.

After the war, she moved to the United Kingdom as a refugee. She married a man from her village, had
two children, and built a quiet life. For years, she told no one about that week. She believed she
had left it behind. She became a midwife, drawn to the intimacy of birth, the way new life could
feel like an antidote to death. She delivered hundreds of babies. She was good at her job—calm,
steady, a hand to hold.

But in the last five years, something has shifted. She notices it most during night shifts. The
dimmed lights of the delivery room remind her of the field hospital. When a woman screams during
labor, Amina feels her chest tighten, her vision narrow. She has to excuse herself. Twice, she has
frozen mid-step in the corridor, unable to move, seeing not the hospital corridor but the mud and
blood of 1971. Her colleagues think she is having panic attacks. She lets them think that.

At home, she cannot sleep. She lies awake beside her husband, listening for sounds she does not want
to name. She avoids documentaries about war. She cannot watch news footage of conflict zones. Her
husband has noticed she flinches when he touches her unexpectedly. She has stopped cooking meals
that require her to handle raw meat—the texture reminds her of the bodies in the truck.

She scores 74 on the PCL-5, endorsing severe re-experiencing symptoms, avoidance of trauma-related
stimuli, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and marked hyperarousal. She reports nightmares
three to four times per week, often waking in a cold sweat, disoriented. She avoids discussing her
past with her children, who know only that she “had a difficult time during the war.” She feels
guilty that she survived when others did not. She wonders if she deserves to feel safe.

She came to therapy because her daughter, a psychologist, noticed she was withdrawing from family
gatherings. Amina says she does not want to be a burden. She says she has managed for fifty years.
But her voice cracks when she says the word “managed,” and she looks down at her hands.

She does not ask for a diagnosis. She asks if it is possible to stop feeling like she is still in
that truck.
