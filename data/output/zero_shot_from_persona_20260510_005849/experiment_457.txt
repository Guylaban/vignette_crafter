**Case Vignette: Firefighter, Age 41, Life-Threatening Illness**

The patient is a 41-year-old married female firefighter who has been on medical leave for the past
four months. She presents with a guarded posture, sitting at the edge of the chair, her hands
clasped tightly in her lap. She speaks in short, clipped sentences, her gaze often fixed on a point
just beyond the therapist’s shoulder.

She begins by describing the onset of her symptoms, which she traces back to a morning eighteen
months ago. She had just finished a 24-hour shift, her body humming with the usual exhaustion, when
she noticed a persistent, dull ache in her lower back and a strange, metallic taste in her mouth.
She dismissed it as dehydration and muscle strain, a common occupational hazard. Over the next two
weeks, the fatigue became a leaden weight in her bones. She started having night sweats so severe
she would wake up shivering, the sheets soaked through. She attributes her decision to see a doctor
not to her own concern, but to a comment from her captain, who told her she looked “gray.”

The diagnosis of acute myeloid leukemia was delivered in a small, windowless room at the hospital.
She remembers the hematologist’s mouth moving, but the words felt like they were being spoken
through water. She remembers the exact pattern of the ceiling tiles—a grid of off-white squares with
tiny pinprick holes. She remembers thinking, *I have to call the station. I have a shift tomorrow.*
She did not think about her own mortality in that moment. She thought about the truck, about her
crew, about the checklist she had left half-finished in the bay.

The treatment was brutal. She describes the chemotherapy as a “controlled burn,” a phrase she uses
without irony. She spent six weeks in isolation, her immune system a ghost. She lost her hair, her
muscle mass, her sense of taste. She lost the identity that had taken her twenty years to build. In
the firehouse, she was the one who could carry a 180-pound man down three flights of stairs. In the
hospital, she could not lift her own head off the pillow to drink water. She describes a specific
moment during her third round of chemo: a fever spiked, her blood pressure crashed, and a code blue
was called on the floor. She heard the commotion from her room, the rapid footsteps, the beeping of
machines. She knew, with a terrifying certainty, that the code was for her. She was not afraid of
dying. She was afraid of being *helpless* while it happened. She remembers the cold sensation of a
central line being inserted into her neck, and the feeling that her body was no longer her own—it
was a piece of equipment that had failed.

She has been in remission for six months now, and she has returned to light duty. But she is not the
same. She describes a visceral, gut-level dread that has no specific trigger. She cannot walk into
the fire station without her heart hammering. The smell of the diesel fumes in the bay, once a
comforting scent of readiness, now makes her nauseous. The sound of the station alarm—a sound she
has heard thousands of times—causes her to freeze, her mind blank, her hands shaking. She has
started checking her pulse constantly, a compulsive ritual she cannot stop. She will be in the
middle of a conversation, and she will lose the thread of it, her attention consumed by the
sensation of her own heartbeat.

Her husband, a paramedic, has been supportive, but she feels a gulf between them. He wants to talk
about the future; she can only think about the past. She has nightmares, but they are not about the
disease itself. They are about the firehouse. She dreams of the truck rolling out without her, of
the crew calling her name on the radio, of a fire she cannot reach. She wakes up in a cold sweat,
her body rigid, her mind replaying the sound of the hospital code blue over and over, but in the
dream, the beeping is the station alarm.

She does not use the word “PTSD.” She says, “I think I’m broken. I think the job broke me. Or maybe
the sickness did. I don’t know which one I am anymore.” She looks down at her hands, which are no
longer calloused from the hose, but are soft, pale, and trembling. She says she is not afraid of
another fire. She is afraid of the moment before the fire, when the alarm goes off, and she has to
decide if she can trust her own body to carry her into the burning building.
