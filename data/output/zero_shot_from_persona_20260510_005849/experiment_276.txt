the compressor. Two men came in through the back. They were not robbers. They were men who knew she
would be there, knew she worked alone, knew she was undocumented and afraid of the police.

They took her to a basement. She does not describe the basement in detail, but she tells me about

behind her back until her shoulders ached for months afterward. They left her in a corner, in the
dark, for hours at a time. She stopped screaming after the first day. She learned to count the
seconds between the sounds of their footsteps.

She escaped when the door lock jammed and one of the men had to kick it open. She ran, barefoot,
through an alley, and flagged down a bus. She never filed a police report. She moved north, changed
her name, and got her HVAC certification through a trade program. She tells me she is safe now. She
says this flatly, like a fact she has checked off a list.

But safety does not live in her body. She cannot sleep with her back to a door. She sleeps on the
floor of her apartment, curled against the wall, because beds feel too exposed. When a customer
calls her to say they will be home for a service visit, she finds herself arriving early and sitting
