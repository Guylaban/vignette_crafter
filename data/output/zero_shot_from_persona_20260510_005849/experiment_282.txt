**Patient: Robert, Age 70, Married, Salesperson (Retired)**

Robert presents as a composed, neatly dressed man who sits upright in the chair, his hands resting
flat on his thighs. He begins our sessions by apologizing for his "poor memory" and for taking up my
time, a habit he says he learned from forty years in sales—"always make the other person feel
comfortable." He has been retired for five years, but his voice still carries the practiced, even
cadence of someone who spent decades reading a room.

The event that brought him here happened eleven months ago, in the parking lot of a grocery store.
It was a Tuesday afternoon. He had just bought a rotisserie chicken and a bag of oranges for his
wife, Helen. As he loaded the bags into the back of his sedan, a younger man approached him from
behind, grabbed him by the collar of his jacket, and slammed his head against the car door frame.
Robert remembers the sound of his glasses skittering across the asphalt more clearly than the pain.
The man demanded his wallet. Robert, instinctively, said "Take it, it's fine, it's fine." The man
took the wallet, shoved Robert to the ground, and ran.

Robert got up, drove home, and told Helen he had tripped on a curb. He did not want her to worry. He
did not file a police report. "It was just a wallet," he says. "I had forty dollars in it."

For the first few months, Robert noticed a tightness in his chest whenever he walked into a parking
lot. He began parking as close to the store entrance as possible, even if it meant circling for ten
minutes. Soon, he stopped going to the grocery store altogether. Helen does the shopping now. He
tells her he is "just tired" or that his knees are acting up.

His sleep is the worst. He wakes at 3:17 AM every morning—the exact time he estimates the assault
occurred—with his heart pounding and his hands numb. He lies still in the dark, listening to the
house settle, convinced he hears footsteps in the gravel driveway. He does not wake Helen. He gets
up, makes a cup of decaf, and sits in the living room until dawn, running his thumb over the edge of
his old sales badge from the dealership. He tells himself it was nothing. He tells himself he is
being dramatic.

At family dinners, his daughter notices he has stopped sitting with his back to the door. He now
chooses the seat against the wall, the one with a clear sightline to the entrance. When his grandson
runs up behind him and taps his shoulder, Robert flinches so hard he knocks over his water glass. He
apologizes quickly, says he is "just jumpy these days."

The hardest part for Robert is the shame. He was a salesman. A good one. He read people for a
living—their posture, their eyes, the micro-expressions that told him whether they were ready to buy
or just browsing. He prided himself on being aware. And yet, he did not see the man coming. He did
not defend himself. He said "Take it, it's fine." He replays that moment constantly, trying to
rewrite it into a version where he stands his ground, where he is not a seventy-year-old man
crumpled on the asphalt.

He told me, during our third session, that he feels like a ghost in his own life. He is present, but
not really there. He watches Helen move through the kitchen, he hears his daughter laugh on the
phone, but he is always listening for something else—a footstep, a scrape, a voice that is too
close. He is waiting for the next shove. And he is exhausted.

He came to therapy because Helen found him sitting in the dark at 4 AM, crying silently. He could
not explain why. He just said, "I can't stop thinking about the chicken." When she looked confused,
he clarified: "The rotisserie chicken. I left it on the ground. I never even picked it up."
