**Case Vignette: Ms. L.**

Ms. L. is a 36-year-old woman of South Eastern Asian descent, living with her partner in a large
metropolitan city. She presents as articulate and composed, though her hands remain clasped tightly
in her lap throughout the session. She works as an urban planner, a career she chose to create order
and functionality out of chaos. She has been in a committed relationship for four years, and while
she describes her partner as "patient and kind," she admits she has felt increasingly distant from
him over the past year.

The traumatic events began three years ago, when Ms. L. volunteered for a six-month deployment with
a humanitarian organization in a conflict zone. She had always believed in the power of
infrastructure to rebuild lives, and she was assigned to assess and design temporary housing for
displaced families. For the first four months, the work was grueling but meaningful. She lived in a
shared container unit near the front lines, and the constant hum of drone activity became a white
noise she learned to ignore.

The turning point came on a Tuesday afternoon. Ms. L. was in a schoolyard that had been converted
into a distribution center, helping to organize a food drop for several hundred families. She
remembers the heat, the dust, and the sound of children laughing nearby. Then, without warning, a
mortar shell struck the far corner of the compound. The blast wave threw her against a concrete
wall. She remembers the ringing in her ears, the smell of burning metal and dust, and the sight of a
young boy—no older than seven—lying motionless a few feet from her, his hand still clutching a
plastic water bottle.

Ms. L. was not physically injured, but she was unable to move for several minutes. She was evacuated
by her team and spent the next week in a state of mechanical functioning, completing her tasks
without any memory of doing them. She returned home two weeks later, her deployment cut short.

In the months following her return, Ms. L. threw herself back into her work as an urban planner. She
took on the most complex projects, working late into the night, believing that if she could just
design better cities, she could prevent the kind of chaos she had witnessed. But her mind began to
betray her. She cannot walk through a crowded public square without scanning rooftops for snipers.
She avoids outdoor markets, parks, and schoolyards. The sound of a car backfiring on her commute
sends her heart racing, and she finds herself crouching behind her desk in her office, unable to
explain her behavior to her colleagues.

At home, she has become hypervigilant. She checks the locks on her apartment door three times before
bed. She sleeps on the couch, because the bedroom window faces the street, and she cannot bear the
vulnerability. Her partner has tried to comfort her, but she has pushed him away, unable to
articulate why she flinches when he touches her from behind. She has nightmares nearly every
night—not of the blast itself, but of the boy’s hand, reaching for her from a pile of rubble. She
wakes up gasping, drenched in sweat, and cannot fall back asleep.

Ms. L. has stopped seeing her friends. She finds small talk unbearable. She has not told her family
about what happened, because she fears they will see her as weak or broken. She drinks two glasses
of wine each evening to quiet her mind enough to sleep, though she knows it only makes the
nightmares worse.

Her PCL-5 score is 56, reflecting severe PTSD symptoms. She describes feeling as though she is
"living behind glass," watching the world move around her but unable to participate. She came to
therapy because her partner told her he feels like he is "living with a ghost." She does not want to
be a ghost. She wants to feel like herself again, though she is not sure who that person is anymore.
