**Patient Vignette: Aisha, Age 18**

Aisha is an eighteen-year-old Middle Eastern woman, a first-year architecture student who presents
as quiet and watchful, her dark eyes often fixed on a point just beyond the person speaking to her.
She came to therapy at the urging of her academic advisor, who noticed her grades slipping and her
increasing withdrawal from studio projects.

She tells her story in fragments, often stopping to check the therapist’s face for signs of
judgment.

It happened eleven months ago, during her final year of high school. Aisha was walking home from a
late study session at the library, a shortcut through a narrow alley she’d taken a hundred times.
She heard shouting, then a sound she can only describe as “wet and wrong.” She turned the corner and
saw two men attacking a third. She didn’t see the knife until it was already in the victim’s chest,
the blade disappearing and reappearing in a rhythm that felt, she says, like a terrible dance.

She froze. She couldn’t scream. She couldn’t run. She watched until the attackers fled, leaving the
man slumped against a dumpster. Aisha was the one who called 911, her hands shaking so badly she
dropped her phone twice. She remembers the operator’s calm voice, the blood spreading on the
concrete, the man’s eyes open but not seeing.

The man survived, but Aisha did not.

She returned to school a week later, determined to be normal. She sat in class, took notes, nodded
along. But her mind was not in the room. She says she was “still in the alley,” replaying the scene
on a loop. She started avoiding the route home, then any route that felt narrow or dark. Soon she
was avoiding leaving her house at all.

Now, as an architecture student, Aisha spends her days in a bright, open studio. But she cannot
focus. She finds herself designing buildings with no windows, no corners, no shadows. Her professors
praise her “bold, minimalist vision,” but Aisha knows the truth: she is building cages. She draws
walls that are too thick, corridors that are too narrow. She sketches emergency exits in every room,
even bathrooms. She cannot stop planning for escape.

At night, she dreams of the alley. She wakes up with her heart pounding, convinced she can hear the
wet sound of the knife. She checks her phone for news about the victim, though she knows he is
alive. She checks it obsessively, sometimes thirty times an hour, as if the story might change.

She has stopped eating lunch in the campus cafeteria. Too many people, too many exits, too many ways
to be trapped. She eats alone in the studio, with the door locked. She has stopped responding to
texts from friends. They think she is busy with her portfolio. She is busy trying to breathe.

Aisha’s hands, which once drew delicate curves and soaring arches, now tremble when she holds a
pencil. She has not told her parents about the attack. They would worry, she says. They would call
her dramatic. She says this without anger, as if stating a fact.

She is here because she is tired. Tired of the loop, tired of the walls she keeps building, tired of
being eighteen and already afraid of the dark. She wants to design buildings that feel safe, but she
no longer knows what safe feels like. She wants to stop watching the alley in her head. She wants to
be able to take a shortcut again, even if she never takes it.

She sits in the chair, her hands folded in her lap, and waits. She is waiting for someone to tell
her what to do. She is waiting for the knife to fall again. She is waiting to see if the world will
ever feel solid beneath her feet.
