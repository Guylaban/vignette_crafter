**Patient Vignette: The Unfinished Sentence**

The patient is a 31-year-old, divorced South Eastern Asian woman who works as a freelance
journalist. She presents for an initial evaluation following a referral from her primary care
physician, who noted significant weight loss and persistent insomnia over the past four months. She
is articulate and direct, but her hands remain clasped tightly in her lap throughout the session,
and her gaze frequently drifts to the corner of the room as though she is listening for something.

She begins her story with a date: March 17th. “That’s when the story ended,” she says, though she
immediately corrects herself. “No. That’s when the story *broke*.” She had been on assignment in a
neighboring province, covering a story on migrant fishing communities. Her younger brother, Sam—a
marine biologist, two years her junior—had been her unofficial research partner on the piece. He was
the one who knew the fishermen’s names, who could translate the local dialect, who made people trust
them. That morning, she had texted him a photograph of a broken fishing net, captioned with a joke
about the state of their investigation. He had replied with a voice note, his voice bright and
hurried. “I’ll call you tonight. I found something. It’s big.”

He never called. At 6:47 PM, she received a call from her mother. Sam had been caught in a riptide
while diving off the coast. By the time the search team found him, it was too late.

In the weeks that followed, the patient did not cry. She wrote. She wrote the story they had been
working on, filing it under a joint byline, even though the editor asked if she wanted to drop his
name. She refused. “He was there,” she said flatly. “He found the data.” She attended the funeral in
a state she describes as “professional,” shaking hands and accepting condolences with a practiced
composure that alarmed her family. Her mother later told her, “You looked like you were reporting on
your own brother’s death.”

Now, eight months later, she cannot write. She sits at her desk for hours, staring at a blinking
cursor, the same unfinished sentence at the top of the page: *“The fishermen believe the sea is a
ledger…”* She cannot finish it because she cannot remember the rest of what Sam said about that
metaphor. She replays his voice note obsessively, but the file is corrupted—she dropped her phone in
saltwater the day he died. She has tried to find the original recording, but it is gone. The silence
where his voice used to be is a physical weight in her chest.

The patient’s daily life has become a series of rituals to avoid the memory of the phone call. She
no longer answers calls after 6:00 PM, letting them go to voicemail. She has not listened to a voice
note from anyone in four months. She changed her ringtone to a generic chime, but she still hears
the old one—Sam’s favorite song—playing in her head at random moments, often when she is trying to
fall asleep. She has stopped using her phone for anything other than essential texts. “It’s a dead
thing now,” she says. “Like him.”

Her work has suffered. She has missed three deadlines, and her editor has placed her on a reduced
assignment schedule. She recently declined a prestigious fellowship to cover climate change in the
Pacific. “I can’t get on a plane,” she admits. “The last time I flew was to go to his funeral. Every
time I see an airplane contrail in the sky, I think about that flight. The way the seatbelt felt.
The way the flight attendant smiled at me and I wanted to scream.”

She is divorced. The marriage ended two years before Sam’s death, but she notes that her ex-husband
was the one who found her on the bathroom floor three months ago, unable to breathe, convinced she
was drowning. “I was having a panic attack,” she says clinically. “I thought I was dying. I felt
water in my lungs.” She has not told her mother about the panic attacks. She has not told anyone
about the nightmares—recurring, vivid dreams in which she is underwater, searching for Sam’s body,
and every time she reaches for him, he turns into a telephone that is ringing, ringing, ringing.

She sleeps three to four hours a night, usually after 3:00 AM, when she is too exhausted to stay
awake. She has lost fifteen pounds because she cannot taste food. “Everything tastes like salt,” she
says. She has stopped going to the coffee shop where she used to work, because the barista plays a
playlist that includes Sam’s favorite song. She has stopped visiting her parents because their house
still smells like the incense from his funeral. She has stopped talking to her friends because they
ask too many questions.

“I am a journalist,” she says, finally looking directly at the clinician. “I’m supposed to find the
story. But I can’t find the ending. And I can’t stop looking.”
