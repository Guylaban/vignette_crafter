**Patient Vignette: Elena**

Elena is a 57-year-old Latin American woman, a divorced physical therapist who has been seeing me
for several weeks. She presents as meticulously composed, her posture erect, her silver-streaked
hair pulled back in a tight, efficient bun. Her hands, strong and capable from decades of work,
often rest in her lap, but I notice them clench into fists when she speaks of the rain.

The story begins in the early hours of a Tuesday morning, two years ago. Elena’s home in the coastal
town of San Miguel was nestled on a low hill. She had lived there for fifteen years, a sanctuary
she’d bought after her divorce. The storm had been forecast, but no one predicted the dam’s failure.
She remembers the sound before the water: a low, groaning roar that vibrated through the
floorboards. Then the wall of mud and debris hit. She was thrown against her kitchen cabinets, the
air forced from her lungs. For what felt like hours, she clung to a floating countertop, the water
black and cold, carrying the screams of her neighbors. She saved herself by kicking a hole in her
own roof and scrambling onto the tiles, where she watched her life—her photographs, her furniture,
her garden—dissolve into a churning, brown sea.

In the present, Elena’s life is a study in careful management. She returned to work at the
outpatient clinic three months after the flood, and on the surface, she is a model professional. Her
patients, many of them elderly, find her patient and kind. But she tells me she now dreads the rain.
The moment the first drop hits her windshield, her heart begins a frantic, arrhythmic pounding. She
will cancel appointments, inventing headaches or car trouble, and drive home in a cold sweat, her
knuckles white on the steering wheel. She has installed a weather app on her phone that gives her
hourly warnings, and she checks it obsessively, sometimes fifty times a day.

Her sleep is a battlefield. She has nightmares, but they are not the dramatic flashbacks she
expected. They are more insidious: dreams of being unable to find her keys, of her car’s brakes
failing, of a slow, rising dampness in her bedroom walls. She wakes at 3:00 AM, her body rigid, her
mind racing through escape routes. She has a "go-bag" packed in her closet—a change of clothes, a
flashlight, a bottle of water, a copy of her divorce papers. She doesn’t know why the papers are
there, only that it feels wrong to leave them behind.

The most difficult part, she says, is the feeling of being untethered. As a physical therapist,
Elena’s entire career has been built on the logic of the body—the predictable mechanics of muscle
and bone. She used to find peace in the symmetry of a rehabilitation plan. Now, she feels a
constant, low-level dread that the ground beneath her feet could vanish at any moment. She avoids
the coast. She cannot watch the news. When a patient mentions a vacation to a beach town, she feels
a surge of irrational anger.

She has not dated since the divorce, and she tells me she prefers it that way. Her only son lives in
another state, and their phone calls are brief, filled with her careful reassurances. She does not
want him to worry. She does not want anyone to see the woman who still, sometimes, feels the cold
water at her throat. She has not told her colleagues. She is afraid they will see her as fragile, as
damaged. She is a healer, she reminds me. She is not supposed to be broken.

Today, she sits in my office, her hands still. She describes a recent evening when a sudden downpour
caught her walking from her car to her front door. She froze, unable to move, the rain plastering
her hair to her scalp. She stood there for ten minutes, trembling, until a neighbor’s porch light
clicked on. Only then did she run inside, her breath ragged, her body remembering what her mind
tries so hard to forget.
