**Vignette: The Unfinished Wireframe**

He describes the beginning not as a moment of collapse, but as a slow, creeping error in the system.
For three weeks in the spring of last year, Sam felt a persistent ache in his right side, a dull
throb he initially dismissed as a muscle strain from his standing desk or poor posture during late-
night coding sessions. He was a UX designer for a large health-tech firm, and his life was a series
of user flows, journey maps, and iterative fixes. He figured he could debug his own body with enough
sleep and ibuprofen.

The diagnosis arrived on a Tuesday. Stage II colorectal cancer. The doctor’s words felt like a
corrupted file—fragmented, unreadable. Sam remembers the fluorescent light in the exam room buzzing
at a frequency that seemed to vibrate in his teeth. He remembers his wife, Lin, squeezing his hand
so hard her knuckles went white. He remembers thinking, *This is a terrible interface for bad news.*

The treatment was brutal. Surgery to remove the tumor, followed by six months of chemotherapy. He
lost forty pounds. He lost his hair. He lost the ability to work, which for Sam was not just a job
but a framework for his identity. He spent his days on the couch, watching the shadows of leaves
move across the ceiling, feeling his body betray him like a faulty prototype. The chemo fog was
thick; he could not focus on a single line of code. He felt like a ghost haunting his own home.

That was fourteen months ago. The scans are clean now. The doctors use words like "remission" and
"excellent prognosis." Sam is back at work, but something is fundamentally broken in his operating
system.

He cannot stop checking for errors. Every morning, he wakes in a cold sweat at 4:17 AM—the exact
time his first chemo infusion began. His heart races as he runs a full system diagnostic on his
body: he presses on his abdomen, feels for lumps, analyzes the color of his urine, the texture of
his skin. If his stomach gurgles, he freezes, certain the cancer has returned and metastasized. This
is not a rational thought; it is a reflex. He spends forty-five minutes a day in the bathroom,
conducting these silent, terrified audits.

At the office, his performance has cratered. He used to be known for his intuitive, fluid designs.
Now, he cannot make a decision. He stares at a blank Figma canvas for hours, paralyzed by the fear
of a single wrong click. A minor bug in a prototype sends him into a spiral of catastrophic
thinking: *This is the end. This error will ruin the launch. I will be fired. We will lose our
health insurance. I will get sick again and die.* He has started leaving meetings abruptly, claiming
he has a headache, just to escape the pressure of a normal conversation. The sound of a colleague
coughing near his desk makes him flinch; he immediately reaches for the hand sanitizer in his
pocket.

He has not touched his wife in months. Not because he does not love her, but because intimacy feels
like a vulnerability he cannot afford. Sex reminds him that he has a body, and his body is a source
of terror. He sleeps in the guest room now, curled on his side, the door slightly ajar so he can see
the hallway light. He needs to know he can get to the bathroom quickly if the nausea comes back—even
though the nausea has been gone for nearly a year.

Last week, his boss gently suggested he take a leave of absence. Sam nodded, but inside he felt a
familiar, crushing weight. He is thirty-five years old. He survived. But survival, he is learning,
is not the same as being alive. He feels like a wireframe with no content—a structure that is
perfectly clean, but utterly empty.
