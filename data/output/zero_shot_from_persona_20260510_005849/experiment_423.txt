**Patient Vignette: Graphic Designer, Age 33**

It started with a single comment on a design forum. She had posted a logo she’d created for a local
coffee shop—clean lines, warm colors, a small steam curl rising from the cup. The comment was from a
user she didn’t recognize: *“Nice work. You have a good eye for detail. I like how you see the
world.”* She remembers feeling a small, pleasant surprise. Feedback was always welcome.

Then came the direct messages. At first, they were professional, almost flattering. He asked about
her software, her process. She answered politely, as she did with most inquiries. But soon, the
messages shifted. They became personal. He mentioned the street where she lived, the café she
visited every Tuesday. *“I saw you today. You were wearing a blue scarf. It looked nice.”* Her
stomach dropped. She didn’t remember seeing anyone watching her.

She stopped posting. She made her social media private. She changed her username. But he always
found a way. An email from a new account. A comment on a shared project file. Once, a small envelope
taped to her car windshield—inside, a sketch he had drawn of her face, from a photo she’d never
posted online. Her husband wanted to call the police. She agreed, but she felt hollow. The police
took a report, but without a direct threat, there was little they could do.

That was eight months ago. Now, she works from home exclusively. Her desk is positioned so she can
see the front door and the window. She has a small spray bottle of pepper spray clipped to her
chair, hidden under a throw blanket. She tells herself it’s just for peace of mind. She no longer
answers emails after 6:00 PM. She changed her phone number twice. She deleted all her design
portfolios from the internet. She tells her clients she is “focusing on private commissions.”

She used to love walking to the corner store for a coffee. Now, she plans her outings like military
operations. She checks the street before stepping outside. She takes different routes home each
time. She once drove twenty minutes out of her way because a silver sedan—the same model as one she
saw parked outside her house a month ago—was three cars behind her. She never saw the driver’s face.

Her husband tries to be patient. He holds her when she wakes up gasping at 3:00 AM, convinced she
heard footsteps on the porch. But he doesn’t understand the hypervigilance that lives under her
skin. He doesn’t see how she scans every face in every grocery store aisle, how her shoulders stay
tight even when she’s just sitting on the couch. He says, “He’s probably moved on by now.” She nods.
But she doesn’t believe it.

At night, she lies awake, replaying every message, every comment, every detail she might have
missed. She feels watched even when she’s alone. The ceiling fan sounds like a camera shutter. The
neighbor’s dog barking sounds like a warning. She has stopped answering the door altogether. When
she does leave the house, she wears a baseball cap and sunglasses, even in winter. She keeps her
keys between her knuckles when she walks to her car.

The worst part isn’t the fear itself. It’s the shame. She feels foolish for being scared of a
stranger who never actually touched her. She feels angry at herself for letting someone she never
met take away her sense of safety, her career, her ability to walk down the street without feeling
like prey. She knows the police report is closed. But her mind remains open, waiting, listening for
a sound that doesn’t belong.
