James is twenty-one years old, a chef at a busy farm-to-table restaurant in the city, and he has not
been able to sleep through the night in eleven months. He shows up for his afternoon shift at four,
but lately he has been coming in at two, just to sit in the walk-in cooler with the door cracked,
letting the cold air wash over his arms. He tells me the cold helps him think. He tells me he cannot
think at all.

The story he tells begins a year ago, when he was still a line cook at a different place, a fast-
casual spot that did not care about its staff. He was twenty, fresh from culinary school, working
doubles to prove himself. There was a sous chef there, a man ten years older, who took James under
his wing. James admired him. The man taught him how to break down a chicken, how to season by feel,
how to move through a rush without panic. James trusted him.

One night, after close, the sous chef asked James to stay and help with inventory. The rest of the
crew had gone. The lights in the dining room were off. James remembers the hum of the walk-in
freezer, the smell of bleach and old grease. He remembers the sous chef’s hand on his shoulder, then
his neck, then the small of his back. He remembers freezing. He remembers thinking, *This is not
what I think it is.* He remembers not saying no. He remembers not saying anything.

What happened next, James describes in fragments. The concrete floor against his cheek. The sous
chef’s weight. The sound of his own breathing, too fast, like he was running. Afterward, the sous
chef helped him stand up, handed him a towel, and said, “You’re okay. You’re fine. Let’s not make
this weird.” James drove home in silence. He showered for forty minutes. He did not tell anyone.

The next day, he went back to work. He stayed for three more months. He says he does not know why.
He says he kept thinking if he just acted normal, it would stop feeling real. But it did not stop.
Every time the sous chef walked past him, James’s stomach dropped. Every time a hand touched his
shoulder—a coworker reaching for a plate, a customer tapping him for ketchup—he flinched. He started
dropping things. His knife work got sloppy. He cut himself twice in one week, on purpose, he thinks,
though he is not sure.

He left that job without giving notice. He found the new place, the farm-to-table restaurant, and
told himself it was a fresh start. But the symptoms followed him. He cannot stand being in the walk-
in freezer for more than a few seconds—the hum, the cold, the door clicking shut—so he uses the
cooler instead, even though it is less efficient. He avoids the back hallway where the dry goods are
stored because it reminds him of the inventory room. He cannot have anyone stand behind him while he
works the grill. He has snapped at two dishwashers for approaching him from behind. He knows it is
irrational. He feels ashamed.

At night, he does not sleep. He lies awake replaying the details, or he dreams about them and wakes
up with his heart pounding, convinced the sous chef is in his apartment. He has started drinking
after his shift, just enough to dull the edge, but it does not help. He has stopped seeing friends.
He has not been on a date since it happened. He tells me he feels like he is living behind glass,
watching himself go through the motions of being a chef, being a son, being a person, but none of it
feels real.

He is here because his mother called him last week and said he sounded hollow. He did not know what
else to do.
