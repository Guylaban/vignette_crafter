**Case Vignette: "The Honorable Mrs. A."**

Mrs. A., a 75-year-old retired judge of Middle Eastern descent, presents as a woman of formidable
composure. She is impeccably dressed, her silver hair swept back, her posture erect. She speaks with
the measured, deliberate cadence of someone who has spent a lifetime weighing evidence. Yet, in the
first session, her hands, resting on her lap, tremble faintly. She apologizes for this, calling it
"a nervous habit."

The story she tells begins not in a courtroom, but in a kitchen. Three years ago, her husband,
Karim, a retired university professor, was making his morning tea. She was in the garden, pruning
roses. She heard a soft thud. She found him on the tile floor, the mug shattered beside him, a look
of serene surprise on his face. A massive coronary. Instant. She knew, as a former judge, that the
scene was final. There was no ambiguity. No time for goodbyes.

In the weeks that followed, Mrs. A. did what she had always done: she organized. She handled the
funeral, the legal matters, the calls from family in Beirut and London. She did not cry. She told
herself she was being strong. She returned to her volunteer work at the legal aid clinic. She told
friends she was "managing."

The managing, however, is a fragile construct. Now, nearly every morning, she finds herself standing
in her kitchen, unable to move. The sound of the kettle whistling sends a jolt through her chest,
sharp and electric. She has stopped using the ceramic mug Karim drank from; it sits in a cabinet, a
silent accusation. She avoids that corner of the kitchen entirely, her body veering left as she
walks to the sink, as if navigating an invisible barrier.

Her sleep is a battleground. She goes to bed early, exhausted, but her mind refuses to surrender.
She sees the scene in high-definition: the steam from the tea, the angle of his arm, the way the
light fell across his face. It is not a memory; it is a visitation. She will sometimes gasp, her own
heart pounding so violently she fears it will stop. She has taken to sleeping in the guest room,
claiming her own bed feels "too big."

The most perplexing change, to her, is her inability to decide. She, who once parsed complex legal
arguments and delivered binding judgments, now finds herself paralyzed by a menu. She will stare at
a list of entrees for ten minutes, her mind a blank, a low-grade panic rising. She has left grocery
carts half-full in the aisle, overwhelmed by the sheer number of choices. A colleague from the bench
recently called to ask her opinion on a pro bono case. Mrs. A. listened, felt a familiar clarity
forming, and then, abruptly, her mind went white. She could not speak. She told the colleague she
had a headache and hung up. She has not returned the call.

She is irritable with her children, who check on her too often. She snapped at her daughter last
week for rearranging the spices. "I know where things are," she said, her voice sharp. What she
meant was: *I need to control this one small space, because everything else is chaos.*
