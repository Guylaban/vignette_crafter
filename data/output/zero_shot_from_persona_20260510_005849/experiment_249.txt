**Case Vignette: “Mai”**

Mai is a 52-year-old married woman who works the morning shift at a fast-food restaurant in a busy
urban center. She came to therapy at the urging of her husband, who has grown increasingly worried
about her since the flood that devastated their coastal hometown three years ago.

Mai’s story begins on the morning of the storm. She remembers the sky turning a strange, bruised
yellow, and the radio crackling with warnings in a language she still struggles to read. Her husband
was at work in the city, and she was home alone with their youngest grandson, then age four. The
water came not as a wave, but as a slow, relentless rise. By the time she realized the levees had
failed, the water was already lapping at the front steps. She grabbed the boy, climbed onto the
roof, and held onto a chimney for six hours. She watched her neighbor’s house collapse, heard
screams that were swallowed by the wind, and felt the boy’s small, trembling body pressed against
her chest. She prayed to ancestors she had not spoken to in decades.

They were rescued by a boat, but Mai did not sleep for three days afterward. In the shelter, she
could not stop scanning the horizon for water. She kept her grandson within arm’s reach at all
times, refusing to let him out of her sight even to use the bathroom. When she finally returned to
her home—a shell of sodden drywall and mud—she found the family altar overturned, the photographs of
her parents ruined. She gathered the wet paper scraps and placed them in a plastic bag, but she
could not bring herself to throw them away. The bag still sits in the back of her closet.

Now, three years later, Mai works the fry station at a chain restaurant. She is fast and efficient,
but her manager has noticed she flinches every time the pressure fryer releases steam. She cannot
stand the sound of rushing water—a sink, a toilet flush, even a kettle boiling. At home, she has
stopped taking showers. She takes sponge baths instead, and only when her husband is in the house.
If she hears rain on the roof, her chest tightens, and she finds herself walking to the highest
point of the apartment—the second-floor bedroom—without realizing she has done it.

She avoids the news. She cannot watch weather reports. When her husband suggests they visit the
coast for a weekend, she becomes silent and rigid, and he knows not to push. She has not returned to
her hometown in three years. She tells herself it is because of work, but the truth is she cannot
bear to see the empty lots where houses used to be, or the new levees that look too thin.

Her sleep is fragmented. She wakes at 3:00 AM most nights, drenched in sweat, her heart pounding. In
the dream, she is always on the roof again, but this time the water keeps rising, and her grandson
is no longer in her arms. She has learned to stay awake after these dreams, sitting in the dark,
counting the minutes until dawn. She drinks coffee at work to stay alert, but the caffeine makes her
jittery, and she has been written up twice for snapping at customers.

Her husband has tried to help. He holds her when she shakes, but she often pulls away, unable to
explain that his touch feels like drowning. She feels guilty—he is a good man, and she knows she is
difficult to live with. She has stopped going to temple, stopped visiting her sisters, stopped
answering the phone. She tells herself she is fine, that the flood was a long time ago, that she
should be grateful they survived. But gratitude feels like a lie she cannot make herself believe.

In session, Mai sits on the edge of the chair, her hands clasped tightly in her lap. She speaks in a
low, even voice, as if she is describing someone else’s life. She does not cry. She says she came
because her husband is worried, not because she needs help. But when asked what she remembers most
about that day, she closes her eyes for a long moment and whispers, “The water was so cold. I didn’t
know water could be that cold.”
