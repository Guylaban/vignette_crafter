**Patient Vignette: "Marcus"**

Marcus is a 34-year-old married firefighter who presents for therapy at the urging of his wife,
Lisa. He has been a firefighter for twelve years and is known within his station as steady, capable,
and unflappable. He is a man who has always taken pride in his ability to remain calm when others
panic. He speaks in a measured, deliberate way, often pausing to choose his words with care. He is
tall and broad-shouldered, but he carries himself now with a subtle slump, as though a weight is
pressing down on him from above.

The trauma that changed him happened two years ago, when he volunteered for a three-week deployment
as part of an international relief effort in a war-torn region. He had done disaster response
before—hurricanes, floods, a building collapse—but nothing like this. He tells me that the first
thing he noticed was the silence. Not the silence of peace, but the silence that follows an
explosion, when the air itself seems to be holding its breath.

He was assigned to a makeshift medical triage unit in a bombed-out school. His job was to help
extricate survivors from rubble and transport the wounded. On the third day, a young boy was brought
in, maybe eight years old, with a leg wound that was already turning black. Marcus helped carry him
to the operating table. The boy was lucid, looking up at him with wide, terrified eyes. He said
something in a language Marcus did not understand, but the meaning was clear: *Help me.* Marcus held
his hand while the doctors worked. The boy died thirty minutes later.

That night, back in the bunk room, Marcus could not sleep. He lay in the dark, staring at the
ceiling, replaying the boy's face. He felt a cold, hollow sensation in his chest, as though
something essential had been scooped out of him.

The rest of the deployment was a blur of similar moments. A woman clutching a dead infant. A man
screaming for his wife, who was buried under concrete. Marcus did his job. He was efficient. He did
not cry. He did not break down. He told himself he was fine.

He returned home and tried to resume his life. Lisa was relieved to have him back. They went out to
dinner. He sat across from her, and she talked about her work, her mother, the garden. He nodded,
but he was not there. He was back in that school, smelling smoke and blood and dust. He began to
feel a strange, persistent irritability. Small things—a dropped fork, a slow driver—would trigger a
surge of anger that felt almost volcanic. He would bite his tongue, clench his fists, and walk away.

At work, his crewmates noticed he was quieter. He stopped telling jokes. He stopped joining them for
beers after a shift. He told them he was just tired. But the truth was that he felt disconnected
from them, from everyone. They talked about house fires and car accidents as though those were the
worst things in the world. Marcus knew better. He would look at them and think, *You have no idea.*

The nightmares started about a month after he returned. They are always the same: he is back in the
triage unit, but the boy is alive. The boy is looking at him, and Marcus cannot move. He tries to
reach out, but his arms are lead. The boy’s eyes fill with accusation. Marcus wakes up gasping,
drenched in sweat, his heart pounding so hard he thinks he might be having a heart attack. He does
not tell Lisa. He lies still, breathing slowly, waiting for the feeling to pass.

He has become hypervigilant. He scans crowds for threats. He avoids news coverage of war or
disaster. He cannot watch movies with explosions. He feels a constant, low-level tension in his
shoulders, as though he is bracing for impact. He drinks two or three beers most nights to take the
edge off. He knows it is not a solution, but it helps him get to sleep.

The thing that disturbs him most is the numbness. He used to feel a sense of purpose, a pride in his
work. Now he goes through the motions. He fights fires. He saves people. He feels nothing. He looks
at his wife, who loves him, and he knows he should feel something, but the feeling does not come. He
tells me this with a flat, tired voice. Then he looks at the floor and says, “I don’t know who I am
anymore.”
