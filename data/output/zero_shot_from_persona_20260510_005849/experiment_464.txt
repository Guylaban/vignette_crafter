**Case Vignette: "Ravi"**

Ravi is a 24-year-old South Asian man who works as a junior diplomat. He has been in a committed
relationship with his partner, Priya, for three years. He presents with a guarded, polite demeanor,
often pausing before answering questions, as if carefully choosing which pieces of himself to
reveal.

Eighteen months ago, Ravi was posted to a consulate in a city that was, at the time, on the brink of
escalating civil conflict. He was not a soldier; he was a desk officer, processing visas and
managing communications. The war came to him. He describes the first three months as a slow,
creeping dread—the hum of drones overhead, the sudden absence of birds, the way the air smelled of
smoke and metal. He stopped sleeping well then, but told himself it was just the heat and the jet
lag.

The event that broke him happened on a Tuesday. He was in a convoy returning from a field office.
The road was blocked by a burning vehicle. Gunfire erupted from a side street. Ravi remembers the
sound more than the sight: the percussive *crack-crack-crack* that seemed to come from inside his
own skull, the screaming of his driver, the wet, ragged sound of the driver’s breath stopping. Ravi
was not hit. He was pulled from the wreck by local security forces and spent four hours in a bunker,
covered in someone else’s blood, listening to the shelling.

He was evacuated to a third country within 48 hours. He did not cry. He did not tell his
supervisors. He filed a report in sterile, bureaucratic language. He called his parents and told
them he was "fine, just tired."

Now, Ravi works at a headquarters building in a safe, quiet capital city. But he does not feel safe.
He cannot sit with his back to a door. He scans rooftops when he walks to his car. The sound of a
motorcycle backfiring on the street sends him into a cold, dissociated state where he feels as
though he is watching himself from a great distance. He has stopped attending the weekly diplomatic
receptions, citing "workload." In truth, the crowded rooms feel like traps; the clinking of glasses
sounds like shrapnel.

His relationship with Priya is strained. She tells him he is "not here." He knows she is right. He
has become emotionally unavailable, irritable, and hypervigilant. He snaps at her when she surprises
him from behind. He avoids intimacy because it requires him to be present, and presence is painful.
He has nightmares nearly every night—not always of the gunfire, but often of the silence that
followed it. He wakes up drenched in sweat, convinced he can still smell the diesel and blood.

Ravi experiences intense guilt. He replays the day of the attack, asking himself why he did not pull
the driver harder, why he did not stay, why he is alive. He has intrusive images of the driver’s
face, which he sees superimposed on strangers in the grocery store. He has begun drinking alone in
the evenings, not enough to be drunk, but enough to blur the edges of his thoughts.

He came to therapy because Priya threatened to leave. He said, "I don't think I have PTSD. I think
I'm just weak. Other people survived worse. I just sat in a car." He does not see that sitting in
that car, and living with the aftermath, is its own kind of battle. He is a diplomat who cannot
negotiate peace within himself.
