**Case Vignette: Mr. A.**

Mr. A. is a 41-year-old South Asian man who presents for therapy at the urging of his partner, Maya.
He is a park ranger for a large national forest, a job he has held for the past twelve years. He
describes himself as having always been a quiet, observant person who found peace in the solitude of
the backcountry. He speaks slowly, choosing his words with care, and often looks down at his hands
as he talks.

The trauma occurred three years ago, during a routine solo patrol in a remote section of the park.
He was checking a trail for storm damage when he came upon a group of men illegally harvesting
timber. They were armed. Before he could radio for backup, he was surrounded, disarmed, and forced
into the back of their truck. For the next 72 hours, he was held in a small, abandoned hunting
cabin. He was bound, blindfolded, and kept in a state of near-total sensory deprivation. He had no
idea where he was or if anyone was looking for him. He was given water but very little food. The men
spoke to him only to threaten him, telling him that if he made a sound, they would leave him in the
woods for the animals. He describes the experience not as a series of violent acts, but as a
profound, crushing silence punctuated by the sound of his own heartbeat and the rough feel of the
rope on his wrists.

He was eventually rescued when a search party, using a helicopter, spotted the truck. The men were
arrested. Mr. A. was physically unharmed, but he says he “hasn’t felt right” since that day.

In the months following the rescue, he returned to work. He felt he had to. The park was his life.
But he found he could no longer tolerate the quiet. The silence of the forest, once a source of
comfort, now feels like a threat. He finds himself constantly scanning the tree line, listening for
footsteps that aren’t there. His heart races when a branch snaps. He has started taking a partner on
every patrol, a practice he once resisted as an intrusion on his solitude. He now carries a
satellite phone that he checks obsessively, and he has memorized the coordinates of every ranger
station. He feels a deep, unshakable shame about this, believing he has become weak.

His sleep is fragmented. He has recurrent nightmares, not of the men or the threats, but of the
sensory details: the feeling of the blindfold pressing against his eyes, the smell of damp earth and

body rigid. He avoids the section of the park where it happened, claiming it is “just easier” to let
other rangers handle that area. When Maya asks him about his day, he gives one-word answers. He has
withdrawn from their shared activities, like hiking and cooking, preferring to sit alone on the back
porch, staring at the yard.

panic attack. He tells me he feels like he is “living inside a glass box,” watching the world from a

shame, and tired of the constant, low-grade dread that has replaced the stillness he once loved.
