**Vignette: “Kai”**

Kai is a 22-year-old, single South East Asian man who works as a UX designer for a small tech
startup. He presents as thoughtful and articulate, though his speech is often punctuated by long
pauses, as if he is searching for a word that feels safe enough to say. He describes himself as
“always having been a planner,” someone who found comfort in structure, wireframes, and user flows.
That sense of control began to dissolve on the afternoon of the monsoon flood.

It happened two years ago, during the rainy season in his hometown. Kai was visiting his family for
the holiday. The storm came quickly, with little warning. Within hours, the river that ran through
the valley had swollen beyond its banks. He remembers the sound first: a low, grinding rumble that
grew into a roar, like a freight train passing through the house. Then the water came—brown, fast,
and cold. He and his younger sister climbed onto the roof. They held onto each other for twelve
hours as the water rose, carrying cars, trees, and the remains of houses past them. He remembers her
grip tightening every time she heard a cry from the darkness. He remembers telling her, “It’s okay,
we’re okay,” even though he was certain they would die.

They were rescued by a neighbor in a small boat just before dawn. His family survived, but the house
was destroyed. The neighborhood was unrecognizable.

Now, Kai lives alone in a city apartment on the third floor. He chose the unit specifically because
it is above ground level. He cannot sleep if he hears rain. The first time it stormed after the
flood, he found himself standing in the hallway, fully dressed, with his keys in his hand, unable to
remember how he got there. He has since installed weather alerts on his phone, but he checks them
compulsively—sometimes twenty times a day, even when the sky is clear. He keeps a “go bag” by the
door with a change of clothes, a flashlight, and a copy of his lease. He knows this is irrational.
He does it anyway.

At work, Kai is known for being meticulous but quiet. He struggles in open-plan meetings where the
hum of conversation and the clatter of keyboards feels like a rising tide. He has started wearing
noise-canceling headphones even when no music is playing. His manager has commented that he seems
“distracted” and “less collaborative.” Kai has not told anyone about the flood. He worries that if
people know, they will see him as fragile, or worse, that they will ask questions he cannot answer
without shaking.

He avoids the news. He cannot watch footage of any natural disaster—not even a documentary about
volcanoes. His body reacts before his mind does: his heart races, his palms sweat, and he feels a
pressure in his chest as though the air is being squeezed out of him. He has stopped going to the
gym because the sound of the sprinkler system being tested triggered a full panic attack. He left in
the middle of his workout and sat in his car for forty minutes, gripping the steering wheel, waiting
for his hands to stop trembling.

Socially, he has withdrawn. He used to enjoy hiking and camping. Now the thought of being outdoors,
especially near a river, makes him feel nauseous. He has not visited his family in over a year. He
tells himself it is because of work, but the truth is he cannot bear to see the rebuilt house. It
looks the same, but it feels different. He dreams about the water almost every night. In the dreams,
he is always holding his sister’s hand, and she is always slipping away.

Kai describes himself as “fine,” and then he pauses. “I’m not fine,” he says quietly. “I just don’t
know how to stop being what the flood made me.”
