**Patient Vignette**

Mai is a 54-year-old electrician, divorced, living alone in a small house she bought twenty years
ago. She came to therapy at the urging of her union representative, who noticed she had been calling
in sick more often and had nearly electrocuted herself twice on the job. Mai does not think she has
PTSD. She thinks she is just tired and maybe getting old.

The trouble began three years ago, when she was diagnosed with a rare, aggressive form of bacterial
endocarditis. The infection attacked her heart valves. She remembers the morning she woke up with
chills, a fever, and a strange hollow feeling in her chest. She drove herself to the emergency room
because she had no one else to call. Within hours, she was in the ICU, septic, with doctors telling
her she might not survive the night. She remembers the cold pressure of the oxygen mask, the beeping
of the monitors, and the way the fluorescent lights hummed overhead. She remembers thinking, *I am
going to die in this room, and no one will know until they find my body.*

She did survive, after two surgeries and a month in the hospital. But the person who came home was
not the same person who went in.

At first, Mai thought the panic attacks were just her body adjusting. But the feeling never went
away. She cannot be in a room that is too quiet, because the silence reminds her of the ICU at
night. She cannot lie flat on her back without feeling the phantom weight of the oxygen mask. She
checks her pulse constantly—sometimes twenty times an hour—pressing two fingers to her wrist until
she is sure her heart is still beating. She has stopped going to the doctor even for routine
checkups, because the smell of antiseptic makes her nauseous. She once fainted in a CVS parking lot
after walking past a display of stethoscopes.

Her work as an electrician has become a minefield. She has been in the trade for thirty years, and
she used to pride herself on her steady hands. Now, when she climbs a ladder, her heart pounds so
hard she can feel it in her throat. She has started avoiding jobs that require her to work in
confined spaces—attics, crawlspaces, basements—because the feeling of being trapped triggers a wave
of dizziness and a sensation that she cannot breathe. Twice, she has cut a live wire by mistake, a
mistake she never made before. Her foreman has started pairing her with a younger apprentice,
ostensibly for safety, but Mai knows it is because they do not trust her anymore.

She has also stopped sleeping. Every night, she lies awake, waiting for the pain to come back, for
the fever to return. When she does sleep, she dreams of the hospital room: the beeping, the needles,
the moment the surgeon told her she might not make it. She wakes up gasping, her sheets soaked with
sweat. She has started drinking a glass of wine before bed, then two, then three. It helps her fall
asleep, but the dreams still come.

Socially, Mai has withdrawn. She used to have a small circle of friends from the union hall, but she
has stopped answering their calls. She cannot stand the sympathetic looks, the questions about how
she is feeling. She has not gone on a date in two years. She tells herself she is too old for that
anyway. The truth is, she is afraid to let anyone see her like this—afraid they will see the woman
who nearly died, not the woman who used to fix transformers and laugh at dirty jokes.

She sits in my office with her arms crossed, her eyes fixed on the floor. She tells me she does not
need therapy. She tells me she just needs to get back to work. But her hands are trembling, and she
is checking her pulse again.
