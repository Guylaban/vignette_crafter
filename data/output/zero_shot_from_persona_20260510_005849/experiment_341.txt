**Patient Vignette: Mr. A.**

Mr. A. is a 51-year-old North American man, a widower, who has been the principal of a mid-sized
public elementary school for the past twelve years. He presents as a composed, articulate man,
dressed in a pressed button-down and slacks, though there is a noticeable tremor in his hands when
he reaches for the water glass on the table. He begins by apologizing for his "short fuse" at work
lately, a trait he says is "completely unlike" the man he used to be.

The story begins eighteen months ago. Mr. A. had always been the picture of health—a former
collegiate athlete who still ran five miles every morning before the school day began. During a
routine physical, his doctor noted an irregularity. What followed was a blur of appointments, scans,
and a word that now feels like a physical blow: **cancer**. Stage III colorectal cancer. He
remembers the precise moment the oncologist said the word, the way the overhead fluorescent light
flickered, and the smell of antiseptic hand sanitizer in the room. He felt, he says, as though he
had been "unplugged from the world." The floor felt like it was tilting.

The treatment was brutal. Surgery, followed by six months of aggressive chemotherapy. He tried to
maintain his role as principal, working from a hospital bed in his office during the worst of the
infusions, the chemo pump a constant, humming reminder in his pocket. He felt a desperate need to
project normalcy, to be the steady anchor for his staff and 800 students. But inside, he was
drowning. He remembers the night he vomited so violently he thought his stitches would burst, and
the terror that he would die alone in his bathroom, his wife having passed away from a heart attack
five years prior.

Now, the cancer is in remission. The scans are clean. His doctors have given him a clean bill of
health. But Mr. A. does not feel healed. He feels hollowed out.

His days are now governed by a relentless, exhausting vigilance. He cannot sit through a faculty
meeting without his mind drifting to the sensation of a new lump, a phantom ache, a sudden fatigue.
Every minor physical sensation—a headache, a muscle twinge, a stomach gurgle—is a potential herald
of the disease's return. He has become a hyper-vigilant scanner of his own body, a job he performs
dozens of times a day. He has visited his primary care physician eight times in the last three
months for non-specific complaints, each time leaving with a "you're fine" that offers no comfort,
only a temporary reprieve before the next worry cycle begins.

The most profound change is his relationship with time. He used to be a planner, a man who looked
forward to retirement and grandkids. Now, he cannot look ahead. He cancelled his subscription to a
travel magazine because the images of future vacations felt like a cruel joke. He finds it
impossible to imagine himself alive in a year. When a teacher asks about a curriculum plan for the
fall semester, a cold dread settles in his stomach; he feels a profound, irrational certainty that
he will not be there to see it through.

He has also become irritable, a trait that terrifies him. Last week, a first-grader accidentally
knocked over a stack of papers on his desk. Mr. A. felt a surge of white-hot rage that was entirely
disproportionate. He didn't yell, but he saw the child's face crumple in fear. He apologized
profusely, but the image of the child's frightened face haunts him. He sees in that child's eyes a
reflection of his own vulnerability, a fragility he cannot bear.

Sleep is a battleground. He avoids it, because sleep brings the dreams. Not nightmares of monsters,
but vivid, clinical replays: the cold gel of the ultrasound, the claustrophobic hum of the MRI
machine, the metallic taste of the chemo. He wakes in a cold sweat, his heart hammering,
disoriented, reaching for a wife who is no longer there. He spends the small hours of the night
walking the darkened halls of his own home, checking his pulse, checking the locks, checking for a
threat he cannot name but feels with absolute certainty is coming for him. He is a man who survived,
but who has not yet returned to the land of the living.
