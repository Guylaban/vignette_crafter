**Case Vignette: "Captain M."**

Captain M. is a 42-year-old married man who has worked as a ship captain for over twenty years. He
presents as a sturdy, weathered man whose hands remain steady, but whose eyes carry a distant,
watchful quality. He has been referred by his primary care physician after reporting persistent
insomnia, irritability, and a growing inability to manage his crew.

The traumatic event occurred fourteen months ago, during a routine cargo run along a well-known
coastal route. Captain M. was navigating his vessel through a busy shipping lane at night when a
smaller, unlit fishing boat crossed directly into his path. Despite his immediate evasive maneuvers
and emergency horn blasts, the collision was unavoidable. He felt the deep, shuddering impact
through the deck plates, heard the terrible grinding of metal against fiberglass, and then the
sudden, sickening silence. He was the first to the rail, peering into the dark water. He saw debris,
a single floating life ring, and then nothing. The other vessel sank within minutes. Two fishermen
were lost. The official investigation later cleared Captain M. of any fault, citing the fishing
boat’s lack of navigation lights and the intoxicated state of its operator.

In the weeks immediately following the collision, Captain M. functioned as expected. He completed
his voyage, filed his report, and attended the required safety meetings. He told himself he had done
everything right, that it was a tragic but unavoidable accident. He did not cry. He did not seek
help. He returned to sea after the mandatory leave.

The change was gradual at first. He began to find himself standing at the helm for hours, staring at
the radar screen, double- and triple-checking every blip. He started running collision drills with
his crew more frequently, his voice sharp and impatient. He stopped sleeping in his cabin,
preferring the captain’s chair on the bridge. When he did sleep, he dreamed of the sound—the low,
resonant groan of metal giving way. He would wake with his heart pounding, convinced he had felt the
ship strike something.

He no longer enjoys the quiet of the night watch. The darkness on the water now feels hostile, full
of hidden threats. He has become hypervigilant, scanning the horizon constantly, unable to trust his
own instruments. His crew has noticed. They whisper that the captain has lost his nerve. He
overheard a deckhand say, "He’s afraid of the dark now." The comment stung because it was true.

At home, his wife describes him as "gone." He sits at the dinner table but does not eat. He stares
out the window at the street, as if waiting for something to happen. He has stopped talking about
his work. When she asks, he becomes irritable and snaps at her, then retreats to the garage. He
cannot tolerate loud noises. A car backfiring on the street sends him into a cold sweat. He avoids
driving at night. He has not touched alcohol, fearing he would not stop.

The most distressing symptom for him is the intrusive memory. It is not the collision itself that
returns, but the moment after—the silence, the empty water, the life ring bobbing alone under the
searchlight. He sees it when he closes his eyes, when he blinks, when the dishwasher hums in a
particular key. He knows, intellectually, that the accident was not his fault. But he cannot escape
the feeling that he should have seen the boat sooner, that he should have done *something* more. He
carries a heavy, wordless guilt.

Captain M. has not returned to active command. He is currently on medical leave, pending a fitness-
for-duty evaluation. He came to therapy because his wife told him he was "scaring the kids" with his
distance. He does not believe therapy will help. He sits on the edge of the chair, arms crossed,
ready to leave. But he keeps coming back. He says he just wants to be able to sleep through the
night. He says he wants to stop seeing the life ring. He does not know how to move forward from a
moment that is already over.
