Isabel Rivera had spent nearly four decades trying to outrun a night she could never name. She was
seventy-eight now, married, and a librarian who had traded the narrow, humid streets of her
childhood in San Salvador for the quiet, climate-controlled stacks of a municipal library in Texas.
The refugee trauma that had uprooted her family decades ago still lived in her body as a silent,
unshaped weight. She could not put the frightening event into words because her mind had refused to
package the chaos of the forced march, the dark basement, and the sudden violence into coherent
narrative. Instead, the experience remained trapped as raw sensory imprints. When she walked past
the library’s newly installed citrus-based cleaners or caught the faint, chemical scent of her
husband’s old sedan, she would sometimes smell things that she smelt when the frightening event
happened. That olfactory recall would bypass her reasoning and drag the memory forward, and when she
remembered the event she felt intense guilt, a heavy, wordless conviction that she should have been
able to stop what happened. As the olfactory recall struck, her body instantly shifted into a state
of hypervigilance. Years of surviving a landscape where danger could arrive without warning had
wired her to scan for threat. In the library, this meant she could no longer sit quietly at her
desk. She paced the stacks, being superalert or watchful or on guard, her eyes darting to the
emergency exit signs and the faces of patrons, trying to map every possible danger. Her
hypervigilance forced her to hyperfocus on sensory details, and that relentless watchfulness made
her acutely sensitive to passing smells, which in turn pulled the nonverbal memory forward again,
heavy and unspoken. Because the constant scanning frayed her cognitive resources, she found herself
having difficulty concentrating on cataloging new arrivals or balancing inventory sheets. The
exhaustion from staying on guard made her retreat to the only spaces that felt manageable. She only
felt safe in a very limited number of places, shrinking her world to her bedroom and the driver’s
seat of her car, abandoning garden clubs and evening errands to stay within those narrow perimeters.
To manage the exhausting weight of being constantly on guard, Isabel threw herself into keeping busy
with other things and went over and over why the event happened to her. Over months, this busyness
became a habit; she reorganized donation boxes until her knees ached and ran endless errands,
telling herself that occupation would keep the panic from catching up. Yet the mental energy she was
avoiding consistently surfaced as rumination. She repeatedly blamed herself for what happened,
convinced that a different choice or a different route would have kept her parents safe. This
relentless self-questioning and self-directed guilt prevented the traumatic memory from settling,
acting as a lever that constantly pried open the nonverbal memory and flooded her with intense guilt
whenever she tried to look away. The very strategies she used to survive the day only kept her
physiological alarm bells ringing. Her busyness and meaning-making ruminations refused to let her
nervous system rest, and by refusing to deactivate her internal radar, these habits fed directly
back into her hypervigilance, widening the gap between her and the world. The library had become a
minefield of sensory triggers she could no longer navigate, and her husband noticed how she flinched
at the hum of the HVAC system or how she stopped checking her mail, terrified of phone calls that
might bring news from the old country. She had come to believe that if she ever let her guard down,
the past would consume her all over again. What finally brought Isabel to the clinic was a morning
when she stood in the library’s aisle for an hour, unable to process the titles on her desk while
her heart raced as though the old men were still in the basement. Her daughter had gently placed a
referral on her bedside table, and Isabel, exhausted by forty years of running from an unspoken
past, was terrified that she was losing her mind. She hoped for nothing more than a quiet afternoon,
a mind that would stop circling its own guilt, and the chance to simply be without scanning the room
for a danger that had already passed.
