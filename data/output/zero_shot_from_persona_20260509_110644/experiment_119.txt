he reassured her that he would be in a secure compound and that his role was mainly behind a screen.

That belief does not hold for long. Within days, he is hearing gunfire at night and seeing injured
families arrive at the field site covered in dust and blood. The first memory he returns to again
and again is of a child brought in after an attack on a village. He remembers the child’s small
shoes, one still tied, and the silence in the room when the team realizes there is nothing more they
can do. He is not the one providing medical care, but he feels as if he is witnessing everything. He
says that in the beginning he keeps telling himself to focus on the work: post the updates,
translate the messages, reassure donors, keep the phones charged, keep the story moving. But the
images he is trying to organize for others begin to arrange themselves inside him.

As the weeks pass, the danger becomes ordinary in the way that terrifies him most. He learns the
sound of incoming shelling, the difference between nearby and far away, the exact moment the camp
goes quiet before panic starts. He watches colleagues flinch at every loud noise. He starts sleeping
in short stretches, shoes on, phone in hand. He avoids looking directly at the bodies of those who
die before help arrives, but he cannot avoid the smell of the clinic tent or the sound of grieving
families outside. He finds himself feeling numb during the day and flooded at night. When he calls
his wife, he keeps his voice light and tells her he is managing well. He does not tell her that
sometimes he sits on the edge of his cot unable to remember what year it is, or that he has started
pressing his palms into his thighs to prove to himself that he is still present.

he is back in the camp, waiting for the next impact.
