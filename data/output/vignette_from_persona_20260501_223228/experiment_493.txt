circling without wanting to dwell on had happened at work six months earlier, during what had
started as an ordinary Friday afternoon. A man came into the branch wearing a heavy jacket despite
the heat, shouted something political that Narin only remembers in fragments, and reached into a bag
while customers dropped to the floor. In those seconds Narin froze. He remembered seeing an older
customer stumble near the counter and hearing himself do nothing except crouch and wait, eyes fixed
on the carpet, certain that any movement would get someone killed. The police later called it a
terrorism-related incident; the device the man carried did not fully detonate, but the threat had
been real. What stayed with Narin was not only the fear that he and others might die, but the
conviction that when everything depended on him, he had discovered something shameful about himself.

In the weeks afterward, he had come to believe that he was no longer capable of the steadiness and
competence that had once defined him. Before the attack he had taken pride in being calm with
customers, good under pressure, the dependable one in his family after the divorce. Now he was
certain he could not rely on himself. What tormented him most was the memory of staying down behind
the counter while the older customer struggled in front of him. He knew other people had also
frozen, and he could repeat that to himself in a detached way, but it did not touch the deeper
conclusion he had drawn: that he had acted against his values, that when tested he was cowardly,
ineffective, and not the man he thought he was. From that point, the world felt less safe because he
no longer experienced himself as someone who could manage danger.

That loss of faith in himself sharpened into constant watchfulness. Walking from the bus stop to his
apartment, Narin repeatedly glanced over his shoulder, checking whether someone was coming up behind
him. At night he woke abruptly and scanned the room, sometimes getting up to make sure the door was
locked and the windows were secure before he could try to sleep again. At the bank, the front
entrance had become the place he monitored most. If a customer lingered outside or came in wearing a
backpack or bulky coat, he felt his body tense before he had fully registered why. In his own mind
this made sense: if he had already failed once when it mattered, he could not afford to miss any
sign of danger now. His vigilance was driven as much by distrust of himself as by distrust of other
people.

hated how sharply he snapped. If a supervisor commented on his distractedness, he took it as
evidence that he really was damaged and unreliable. Waking in the night to check the apartment left
him exhausted at work, and the fact that he could not simply sleep through until morning seemed to
prove that he was no longer capable in the basic ways other people were. Each backward glance on the
street, each surge of anger, each night spent on alert fed the same conclusion: he could not trust
his own reactions, and the attack had exposed a weakness he would not be able to undo.

closing to reconcile drawers, and took on tedious tasks others avoided because being busy at the
branch was preferable to going home alone with his thoughts. He was aware that he was driving
himself, but the alternative felt worse. He also worked hard not to think about the attack at all;
if an image of the older customer near the counter came to mind, he pushed it away immediately and
focused on balancing receipts or arranging deposit slips. Since the divorce he had become even more
emotionally shut down, avoiding closeness with anyone who might ask what was wrong or want more from
him than he felt able to give. Yet the more he overworked, suppressed, and kept people at a

needed constant distraction, who could not bear his own thoughts, did not seem competent or solid to
him; it seemed like further proof that he could not rely on himself.

himself and the place where the danger still felt closest. He came after an outburst at a customer
who had reached suddenly into his coat for a phone, followed by a night in which he woke three times
to check the apartment and did not get back to sleep before dawn. What frightened Narin most was not
only that another attack could happen, but that in the next moment of crisis he would again become
the version of himself he despised. What he hoped, cautiously, was that treatment might help him
understand whether that judgment was really true, and whether he could stop living as if safety
depended on never letting his guard down.
