Ethan is a 19-year-old fast food worker who came to treatment months after a hurricane tore through
his town. During the storm he had been in his family’s small house with his mother and younger
sister, listening to the wind rise from something strange and distant into a force that seemed to
press on the walls from every side. He remembers the lights going out, the rooms turning flat and
dark except for brief blue flashes through the windows, and the smell that filled the house as rain
forced its way inside—wet wood, mud, damp insulation, and something sour from the flooded kitchen.
At the height of it, part of the roof gave way and water came pouring in. He can still picture his
mother yelling for them to get low and move to the hallway while debris struck the outside of the
house. What stayed with him was not just that he thought they might die, but the feeling that in
those minutes the world had become uncontrollable and unsafe.

In the weeks afterward, Ethan found that remembering the storm did not feel like recalling something
finished. When it came back, it came back with the same terror, as if his body had not understood
that it was over. His memories were mostly images: the sagging ceiling, the dark hallway, water
spreading across the floor, his sister crying with her hands over her ears. Sometimes, when his mind
touched the event, he could smell the same mix of wet wood and floodwater he had smelled that night,
and that sensory jolt made the memory feel present rather than past. Those moments frightened him
partly because they seemed to confirm what he had already started to believe about himself: that the
storm had changed him in some permanent way, that he was no longer the easygoing, happy person he
used to be.

Over time Ethan came to describe himself in harsh, global terms. He had been the one friends joked
with, the one who could take things in stride, and now he felt miserable most of the time and alien
to himself. He told himself that the person he had become was almost unrecognizable, and he was
certain the change was for the worse rather than a phase he might grow out of. Those beliefs shaped
how he moved through each day. Because he assumed he could not handle strain the way he once did, he
began avoiding anything he thought might be stressful. Before even ordinary interactions, he would
rehearse what to say in his head, trying to prevent himself from freezing, sounding strange, or
letting other people see how different he felt. He also worked hard to push away thoughts of the
hurricane, as if allowing them in would prove that he was damaged beyond repair.

That effort to manage himself narrowed his life. At the restaurant where he worked, the lunch rush
became something he dreaded not only because of the pace, but because stress itself had started to
feel dangerous. If a customer seemed impatient or a manager called his name sharply from across the
kitchen, Ethan would already be running through lines in his head before he answered. He volunteered
less for the front counter and tried to keep to repetitive tasks when he could. He avoided
conversations in the break room unless he had prepared for them first. The more he organized his day
around sidestepping pressure and suppressing reminders of the storm, the more convinced he became
that he really had changed for the worse; in his mind, needing all this effort just to get through a
shift was evidence that the old Ethan was gone.

His attempts not to think about the hurricane did not loosen its hold. If anything, the memories
seemed to hit harder for being fought off. A whiff of bleach mixed with standing water near the mop
sink at work, or a flash of rainwater running past the drive-through window during a storm, could
pull up vivid pictures and that same wave of terror before he had words for what was happening. Each
return of the memory fed his belief that he was permanently damaged and would never be happy in the
way he had been before. And because he saw those reactions as proof of what he had become, he
doubled down on avoiding stress, rehearsing conversations, and forcing the thoughts away, which only
made his world feel smaller and his sense of himself more bleak.

By the time he sought help, Ethan was still showing up to work, but with increasing effort and
dread. He had started to worry that if he kept living this way he would lose his job or stop
recognizing himself altogether. What finally brought him in was the realization, after a recent
rainstorm left him shaken in the stock room by the smell of wet cardboard and cleaning chemicals,
that he could not keep outrunning this. He says he wants “to be normal again,” but what frightens
him most is that normal may be gone for good.
