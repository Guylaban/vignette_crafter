Mele had worked as a veterinarian for years before she was caught in a war zone while visiting
extended family in the Pacific. What she describes most clearly is not a single coherent story but
shards: the concussion of loud blasts, a crush of bodies moving for cover, someone grabbing her arm
hard from behind and pulling her toward a vehicle, the sensation of being trapped in the back seat
as other people shouted over one another while the car sped through streets she could barely see.
Even now the memory comes back “like a film” that starts playing without her consent, but whole
sections are missing, replaced by disconnected flashes and bodily sensations. When she does
remember, shame rises quickly alongside fear. She is ashamed that she froze, ashamed that she had to
be dragged, ashamed that she cannot assemble the event into a clean narrative or explain why she
survived when others were still in danger. Over and over she returns to the thought that what
happened was profoundly unfair and that nothing meaningful was ever done about it.

In the months after she came home to the United States, those fragments began fastening themselves
to ordinary sensations. Loud noises were the most obvious; a dropped metal tray at the veterinary
clinic or a barking dog slamming into a kennel door could throw her back into the soundscape of
blasts and shouting, and within seconds the film of the event was running again in her mind.
Unexpected touch was nearly as potent. If a technician brushed past her without warning in a crowded
treatment room, she would jerk, with the remembered grip on her arm returning first as a bodily
certainty before the images caught up. Riding in a car was manageable if she drove alone, but being
driven by someone else, or even sitting in the passenger seat with her husband, brought back the
trapped, helpless feeling of being carried through danger by other people. Each trigger seemed to
pull memory to the surface, and each episode left her feeling humiliated by how little control she
had over her own mind.

Those same moments also hardened the way she made sense of herself. When she startled violently at a
colleague’s touch or tensed in the passenger seat while her husband drove, Mele did not see these as
symptoms; she took them as proof that she could not rely on herself. In her mind, if she were
stronger or more competent, she would be able to stay composed. The fact that ordinary sounds or
someone else’s hand on her shoulder could undo her felt like evidence that she was still helpless,
still vulnerable to bad things happening with no way to stop them. The unfairness of what had
happened remained central for her, but it was braided with a harsher private conclusion: that when
danger came, she had failed herself.

Those beliefs fed directly back into the way the trauma lived in her mind. Because she had come to
believe she was fundamentally unreliable and unable to prevent harm, the memories replayed with an
accusatory quality. The same fragments returned again and again, not just as fear but as a mental
review of what she had not done, what she should have anticipated, when she should have moved. The
more the scenes replayed, the more ashamed she felt, and the more convinced she became that the
event revealed something deficient in her. Even gaps in the memory did not soften this; if anything,
the disconnected pieces seemed to make her doubt herself further, as though not remembering clearly
were one more sign that she could not be trusted, even by herself.

Over time she became exquisitely sensitive to anything that might set the whole process off. At the
clinic she preferred to position herself where she could see the door and the movement of staff
around her. She stayed alert to clattering instruments, raised voices, and the sudden press of
bodies in treatment spaces. What frightened her most was the sense that a trigger could come from
anywhere and that she would be caught unprepared again. Because the trauma memory could erupt so
quickly from a loud sound or unanticipated touch, she was constantly scanning for danger, bracing
before anyone came near, unable to relax even in exam rooms she had worked in for years. At night
she had difficulty falling asleep because staying alert felt safer than letting down her guard; if
she relaxed, she feared she would miss something and be defenseless again.

This relentless vigilance deepened her view of the world and of herself. Lying awake, listening for
noises in the house, or watching every movement around her at work, she felt she was living as if
catastrophe were always one second away. To Mele, that seemed to confirm the lesson she had drawn
from the war zone: bad things can happen without warning, nothing will make them right, and she
cannot count on herself to stop them. Her husband had begun to do most of the driving because she
became so tense with him at the wheel, and at work she kept more distance from staff and flinched
when they approached from behind, which had started to affect how easily she could move through a
busy clinic day.

What finally brought her to treatment was not a new trauma but the recognition that her life had
narrowed around these reactions. After recoiling when a technician touched her unexpectedly during a
procedure and then spending the night awake, replaying the scene from the war zone in broken loops,
she felt both ashamed and frightened by how little had changed. She says she wants help because she
is tired of living “on watch” and tired of feeling hijacked by sounds, touch, and car rides, but
what she is most afraid of is that this means the war never really ended for her and that she may
never again be able to trust herself enough to feel safe.
