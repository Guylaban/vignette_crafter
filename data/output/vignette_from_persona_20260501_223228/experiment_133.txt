beginning of his difficulties to the day his younger sister drowned during a flash flood in their
home region while he was visiting family. The rains had come suddenly and violently, turning
familiar roads into moving brown water. He still spoke about the sight of it with a kind of stunned
precision: water rushing where there should have been pavement, people shouting over one another,
the smell of mud, gasoline, and wet debris thick in the air. He had been close enough to see her
struggling and close enough to hear her voice, but not close enough to reach her. What remained with
him was not only the loss itself but the helplessness of watching someone he loved disappear into
water that seemed to swallow everything around it.

In the weeks after he returned to campus, Kwame found that ordinary parts of daily life could
abruptly pull him back into that moment. Walking across the university after heavy rain, passing a
campus fountain, or seeing video of flooding on a student's laptop could set something off in him
before he had fully registered what he was looking at. The same happened with certain damp, earthy
smells after a storm, especially when standing near basement entrances or wet stairwells in the
older academic buildings where he taught. During stressful stretches of the semester, this seemed to
happen even more easily. In those moments his body reacted before his mind caught up: his chest
tightened, his muscles braced, and a wave of fear and dread rose so quickly that he felt ambushed by
himself. Even when he could tell, intellectually, that he was standing in a safe hallway or at the
front of his lecture room, he could not relax. He would remain keyed up long afterward, unsettled by
how immediate the danger felt.

Over time, that constant state of readiness changed the way he moved through his days. Kwame had
come to believe that because his body could erupt into alarm without warning, he had to stay alert
to anything that might set it off. He noticed water everywhere. He checked weather apps repeatedly
before leaving home, watched the sky from his office window, and altered his route across campus to
avoid the reflecting pool and the low walkway that collected rainwater. If a colleague suggested
meeting at the café overlooking the river, he would suggest somewhere else without explanation.
During particularly demanding parts of the term, when he was juggling lectures, office hours, and
faculty obligations, he became even more watchful for the smell of wet earth or the sight of pooled
water, certain that stress itself made him vulnerable to being overwhelmed. What frightened him most
was the speed of it: the sense that his body knew catastrophe was coming and that his mind would
always be too late.

That watchfulness left him exhausted and irritable. He described carrying strong feelings he could
not easily name in the moment—fear most clearly, but also anger and guilt that seemed to flare up
whenever he thought about his sister's final minutes. Students had begun to notice that after storms
he was more tense in class, more likely to grip the podium and hurry through discussion. On one
occasion, when rain lashed against the lecture hall windows and the room picked up the damp smell of
wet coats, he lost his train of thought entirely. His heart pounded, his shoulders locked, and he
had to dismiss the class early. The incident embarrassed him deeply, but it also reinforced his
sense that these reactions could strike anywhere. Since then, he had scanned his environment even
more carefully for signs of water, flooding, or the smells that reminded him of that day, as if
anticipating them might spare him from being overtaken again.

Although he lived alone and kept much of this private, the constriction in his life had become
harder to ignore. He was choosing offices and classrooms based on proximity to windows and exits,
leaving campus quickly during storms, and dreading weather forecasts in a way that felt
disproportionate even to him. He finally sought help after realizing he was planning his teaching
around avoiding flooded walkways and rainy-day conditions rather than around his students' needs.
Sitting in the clinic, he said he was tired of living as though disaster were always just ahead of
him. At the same time, he was afraid that if he let his guard down even slightly, the force of what
happened to his sister would hit him all at once. What he hoped for, carefully stated, was not to
forget her, but to be near rain, water, or even stress itself without his body instantly telling him
he was back there again.
