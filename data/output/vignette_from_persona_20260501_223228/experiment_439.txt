and able to sit with complex threats until they made sense. At 65, widowed and living alone, she had
been driving home late one evening after an on-site incident response when a truck ran a red light
and hit the driver’s side of her car. She remembered the impact as fragments of image more than a
story: the windshield crazed with cracks, dashboard lights glaring, glass suspended in the air. What
stayed with her most sharply was the smell—burned rubber, gasoline, and the hot metallic odor inside
the crushed car—and the crushing pain through her ribs and left hip as she fought to breathe and
waited for help. In the ambulance and later in the hospital, she understood with terrible clarity
that she might die.

In the months afterward, the accident did not settle into the past. When it came back, it came as
pictures: the angled hood, the spidered glass, the red wash of brake lights. At times, thinking of
the crash brought back the smell so vividly that she would stop and look around her apartment as if
something were burning. With the memories also came pain in the same parts of her body that had been
injured, enough that she would brace herself and think, not for the first time, that her body had
betrayed her. The return of those sensory fragments made the accident feel unfinished and immediate,
and she had come to believe not only that her own body was unreliable now, but that what happened to
her had been profoundly unfair, an arbitrary violation for which nothing meaningful had been put
right.

That sense of unfairness and bodily betrayal began to organize how she lived. She went over and over
why the event had happened to her, replaying details late into the night as if relentless analysis
might produce an answer commensurate with the damage. At the same time, she avoided anything that
might stir feeling, whether painful or pleasurable; music she once liked stayed off, she stopped
answering invitations from coworkers, and she kept conversations clipped and factual. She also tried
to force the accident out of mind altogether, telling herself to erase it whenever an image
surfaced. Beneath this was another humiliating conviction: that other people could see the accident
on her, as if her stiffness, her hesitations, or simply her face announced what had happened.

certain that if she let herself relax into sleep, she would be caught off guard again. She lay in
bed listening for sounds in the building, delaying sleep because she felt she needed to stay alert,
and she woke repeatedly to check locks, windows, and the hallway outside her apartment. This
constant sense of danger fed back into what she believed about herself: if safety could vanish in a
second, then her body truly was not dependable, and the world’s indifference to what had happened
felt even more stark. The more exposed she felt, the more she withdrew from anything emotionally
stirring and returned to brooding over the accident, as though vigilance in the daytime and mental
reviewing at night were the only ways to prevent another catastrophe.

alerts and after-hours escalations were routine, but now a late-night call, the smell of overheated
electronics in the server room, or the glare of monitors in a dim office could pull up vivid crash
images with shocking speed. When those images hit, she felt the old pain in her side and hip and
became instantly watchful, scanning doors, windows, and the parking lot when she left the building.
On weeks when she tried hardest to push the accident out of mind and flatten all feeling, the
memories seemed to return more abruptly and sensorially, and the nights after an on-site shift were
often the worst. Because she was already sleeping lightly and waking to check whether she was safe,
the images arrived into a mind that was half-braced for danger, making them feel less like memories
than warnings.

Over time, she stopped staying for team dinners after difficult cases and started requesting work
she could complete from home, not wanting coworkers to notice how tense she became by evening. She
told herself this was practical, but privately she feared they could see the crash on her just by
looking. She was exhausted by the combination of rumination and broken sleep, and still she could
not stop either one; being on guard seemed necessary, yet it kept the accident close, and trying to
seal the memory away only made it return in smell, image, and pain.

What finally brought Mele to treatment was a recent night after a routine security incident. Back in
her apartment, she caught the faint odor of hot wiring from her laptop dock and was suddenly back in
the car, fully awake, gripping her ribs and checking every room before dawn. The next morning she
realized she could no longer tell herself this would pass on its own. What frightened her most was
that she might spend the rest of her life trapped between feeling too unsafe to sleep and too
ashamed to be seen, but she also allowed herself a small hope that someone might help her stop
living as if the collision were still happening.
