Amina is a 42-year-old married woman who has worked for years as a train conductor, a job she once
valued for its routine, vigilance, and steady contact with passengers. During a period of political
violence in her country of origin before she immigrated to the United States, she was detained and
tortured. She cannot give a clean account of the detention from beginning to end. What comes instead
are hard visual fragments: a dim room, a guard’s hand moving suddenly toward her, the sight of blood
on someone else’s face, smoke in the air, and stretches of terrible silence that felt more dangerous
than shouting because they seemed to mean something was about to happen. When these memories come,
she does not feel she is remembering from a distance; she feels pulled back into the room as if it
is happening again in the present.

In the months and years afterward, certain moments began to carry the force of the detention. A
sudden drop into complete silence can make her body brace before she has even had a thought. On her
train route, if a platform briefly goes still after the doors close, she feels a rush of dread. The
smell of smoke from a passenger slipping in from a station platform or from a maintenance area can
bring back the image of that airless room at once. Seeing visible injuries on others, even something
ordinary like a commuter with a split lip or a teenager with fresh stitches, catches her in the same
way. These cues do not simply remind her of what happened; they bring back the pictures so vividly
that she is once again inside them, and because the memories arrive in disconnected images rather
than a story she can place in the past, the triggers remain potent and unpredictable.

Over time, Amina began trying to manage these moments in ways that made sense to her. When silence
falls around her, when she notices smoke, or when she catches sight of a wound, she starts scanning
her own body for danger or injury, touching her ribs, neck, and arms as if she might find fresh
evidence that something has already happened. She steps outside to smoke whenever she can,
especially after one of these jolts at work, telling herself it steadies her. She also runs through
worst case scenarios in her mind—if someone entered the cab, if someone lunged on the platform, if
she were trapped and unable to call for help—because being mentally ahead of disaster feels safer
than being surprised. The same thing happens after the memories intrude on their own. Once the
images of the detention have taken hold, she checks her body again, lights another cigarette, and
rehearses what she would do next time, trying to regain control over an experience that still feels
unfinished.

Her days are now organized around a sense that threat is close. At home she listens for sounds that
might mean someone is entering the house, especially at night, and any sudden movement from her
husband in the dark can put her immediately on guard before she recognizes him. Even when she knows
rationally that she is in her own bedroom or seated in the conductor’s compartment on a familiar
route, she cannot relax. Because she lives in this keyed-up state, she leans more heavily on the
habits she believes keep her prepared: checking her body, smoking, and mentally walking herself
through catastrophe after catastrophe. These strategies bring only brief relief. In practice they
keep her attention fixed on danger, and she notices every shift in noise, every movement around her,
every physical sensation that might signal harm.

That constant readiness seems to feed the memories as well. When she is listening for intruders or
flinching at abrupt gestures from passengers, the old images are never far away; the posture of
waiting for danger makes the detention feel close to the surface. A station can go quiet for a
second and she is back in the visual fragments of the cell. A passenger’s quick turn toward her can
merge with the remembered image of a captor’s arm. In turn, the return of these fragments leaves her
more certain that she must stay alert, because to her the vividness of the memory feels like proof
that the danger has not truly ended.

Her efforts to stay prepared have also changed the texture of the memories themselves. The more she
rehearses worst case scenarios, the more the images of what happened seem to splice themselves into
imagined future attacks, so that past and anticipated disaster blur together. Repeatedly checking
her body keeps her attention on physical vulnerability, which makes the remembered scenes feel
immediate and bodily rather than finished. Even smoking, which she reaches for as a way to settle
herself, can trap her in the same network of cues when the smell hangs on her clothes and brings
back the smoky air she remembers from detention.

By the time she sought help, Amina was exhausted and ashamed of how small her world had become
despite still forcing herself to work. She had started timing cigarette breaks around difficult
stretches of her route and dreading the quiet moments between station announcements. At home, her
husband had begun to notice that she startled at his movements and remained tense long after they
had locked the doors for the night. What frightened her most was the feeling that her mind and body
no longer knew the difference between then and now. What she wants, cautiously, is to be able to
tell what happened without being dragged back into it, and to sit in a quiet room without waiting
for harm.
