drifted from a nurse’s station radio and later from his own phone when relatives called to check in.
Darkness had become fused with helpless waiting. Waking in the night now carried him straight back
to those hours when he believed his son might die before morning.

returning abruptly. If he caught sight of a child’s ribs while helping his family in the fields, or
noticed a hand, a mouth, a chest moving under a shirt, he could feel his body jolt as though he were
back beside the hospital bed. At night, if he woke in the dark before dawn, the memory did not feel
like remembering. It felt present, immediate, as if he were once again leaning over his son and
checking whether he was still breathing. Certain songs were especially potent. A melody playing from
a truck radio at the farm stand or from a neighbor’s phone could bring a sudden fragment of the
hospital into his mind and then leave him shaken, trying to orient himself to where he actually was.

Because these moments came on so forcefully, Sokha began organizing his life around not being caught
by them. He stayed up very late most nights, often sitting with the television on or scrolling on
his phone until exhaustion forced him to sleep, because lying down in the dark felt too close to
those nights of waiting. He also found reasons to keep away from people. At the farm, he took
solitary tasks when he could and avoided lingering conversations, especially with other families,
because he feared being trapped while one of those flashes hit him. The songs and the dark had
taught him that ordinary moments could turn suddenly unbearable, and he came to believe that
distance and wakefulness were the only ways to stay ahead of it.

Yet the less he slept and the more he narrowed his days, the more brittle everything became. When he
was overtired from delaying sleep, dawn chores in the fields felt unreal and exposed. In that fog,

long before sunset. Avoiding people also made the triggers feel larger. Without conversation or

swallowing, a hand resting limp in someone’s lap. His efforts to prevent the fear had the effect of
making the world feel more crowded with reminders.

did not arrive as a story with a beginning and end. He would get only his son’s lips, or the sight

the hospital to return. He knew this was straining the household; his wife had begun asking why he
was never really present with her, and at work he was less available during shared tasks, choosing
isolation over the risk of being seen shaken.

Over time, the memories also seemed to teach him what to fear. A flash centered on his son’s mouth

his life bracing against reminders he could not reliably avoid. What finally brought him in was the

longer be fully with his family after sunset, and that if he kept living this way, the nights would
always belong to the hospital.
