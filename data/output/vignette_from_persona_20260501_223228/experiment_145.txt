Linh Tran is a 49-year-old divorced Vietnamese American woman who works in IT support for a large
office complex, moving between cubicles, server rooms, and conference spaces with a badge clipped
neatly to her blouse. Before seeking treatment, she had spent years trying to keep one part of her
life sealed off from the rest. During her military deployment, she was caught in an attack in which
aircraft circled overhead while smoke and the acrid smell of burning materials spread through the
area. What stayed with her was not a full story she could tell straight through, but the sensory
certainty of it: the pounding approach of helicopters, the air thick with burnt odor, and the bodily
conviction that danger could descend before she had time to think. In session, it is clear that
these are not distant memories to Linh. They are conditions she feels she can suddenly be dropped
back into.

That collapse happens most often at work. If a burnt smell comes from overheated wiring, scorched
coffee, or a malfunctioning printer, her body reacts before she can orient herself; her chest
tightens, her heart races, and she feels a jolt through her arms as if she has already been found.
The same thing happens when aircraft pass low over the building. She will stop in the middle of
troubleshooting a computer and listen hard, already scanning the room. In those moments strangers
feel especially dangerous to her. A delivery person stepping off the elevator or an unfamiliar
contractor entering the IT area can make her instantly wary, and she finds herself looking behind
her repeatedly in hallways and parking lots, checking whether someone is following. The reminders do
not just upset her; they sharpen her sense that threat is present now.

Over time that constant readiness has made the ordinary environment feel full of possible signals.
Linh enters any unfamiliar part of the office already alert to sounds overhead, to odd smells, to
who is walking near her. She notices burnt odors quickly, sometimes before anyone else comments on
them, and she tracks aircraft noise with a reflexive intensity. Because she is so often checking
behind her and monitoring strangers’ movements, the workplace has become a place where reminders
seem to leap out at her. She has come to experience the building not as predictable but as a setting
where danger cues are always on the verge of appearing.

She manages this in ways that make sense to her in the moment, though they have narrowed her life.
When the threat feeling surges, she avoids conversations about her deployment or anything
emotionally loaded, convinced that if she starts talking she will lose control of what comes up. She
also steers away from situations likely to stir strong feelings at all, declining social gatherings
at work and finding reasons to leave when office chatter turns personal or intense. On some of her
worst days, after a trigger has left her agitated and unable to settle, she has hurt herself
physically to interrupt the images and bodily alarm, describing it less as wanting injury than as
needing something sharp and immediate to stop the spiral.

These habits have not brought relief for long. Because Linh cuts off conversations and stays away
from emotionally charged situations, the smells and sounds that set her off remain raw and
unprocessed, still carrying the force of the original danger whenever they arise. Even at work,
where she tries to keep interactions strictly technical, avoiding discussion means she is alone with
the meanings the triggers carry for her. And when she uses pain to yank her attention away, the
aftermath leaves her more on edge, ashamed, and watchful, scanning the office and parking garage
with renewed suspicion. The more she lives this way, the quicker her body seems to react to the next
burnt smell or passing aircraft, and the stronger her sense becomes that she has to stay on guard
around other people.

The cost has become visible. Coworkers know her as competent but distant, and she has started
arranging her tasks to minimize contact with unfamiliar staff, volunteering for back-room equipment
fixes rather than front-desk requests. She takes longer routes through the building if a corridor
feels too exposed. What finally brought her to treatment was a recent incident when the smell of
something burning from a break-room toaster coincided with a helicopter overhead; she froze in the
middle of assisting an employee, then later found herself checking behind her repeatedly all the way
to her car, certain someone might be there. She came in frightened by how small her world has become
and by how quickly an ordinary workday can still turn into a scene of immediate danger. More than
anything, Linh hopes she can get through a day without her body acting as if the attack is happening
again.
