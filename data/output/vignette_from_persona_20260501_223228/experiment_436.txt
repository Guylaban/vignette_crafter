Ethan Mercer was 33 when he came for treatment, a married federal prosecutor who had built his
career on being composed, precise, and hard to shake. The attack happened nine months earlier, on a
weekday afternoon at the downtown courthouse complex where he worked. He had gone downstairs between
hearings to meet a witness liaison near the public entrance. He remembered the smell first, though
even now he could not place it neatly in sequence: a sharp alcoholic odor from a man weaving near
the security line, something sour on his breath as he argued with a guard. After that, the story
broke apart. There was a bang that did not immediately register as an explosion, then another sound
and a pressure wave that seemed to hit his chest and ears at once. People dropped, glass burst
inward, and the lobby became a blur of dust, alarms, and shouting. What stayed with him most was the
conviction, formed in an instant, that he should have moved faster, shouted sooner, done something
to get people back. He had helped after the blast, but in his mind that fact never carried the same
weight as the seconds before it.

In the months after, Ethan found that he could not tell a clear story of what had happened, even to
himself. When he tried, he got stuck on fragments: the alcohol smell, a woman’s shoe on the floor,
the concussive bang, the sense that time had narrowed. The memories did not feel like ordinary
recollections that could be reviewed and set down. They came with a force that made them feel more
real than the rest of his day, as if the courthouse lobby were suddenly overlaying the room he was
actually in. At those moments it did not seem past at all; it felt current, immediate, as though his
body had not understood that the blast was over.

What frightened Ethan almost as much as the attack was what he had come to believe it said about
him. He was certain that he had failed in a basic duty to protect other people, and he repeated to
himself that he would never forgive his own inaction. Working as a prosecutor had once rested on a
broad confidence in institutions and in other people’s stated intentions. Since the bombing, that
had narrowed into suspicion. He watched faces in courthouse elevators, studied hands and coats in
hallways, and felt convinced that people were not what they seemed. Those beliefs sharpened whenever
he was jolted by reminders of the attack. On mornings after late-night trial preparation, when he
came to work exhausted and mentally thin, he was harsher with himself and more certain that his
fatigue proved he would miss warning signs again. If someone passed him in the courthouse corridor
smelling of liquor or mouthwash, his stomach dropped and his mind went straight back to the man near
security; in the same instant he was flooded with the thought that he should have recognized danger
and failed. A slammed file drawer or a construction bang outside chambers could make him scan the
floor plan for exits while privately condemning himself for trusting anyone around him.

Those appraisals seemed to pull the memories closer rather than settle them. The more Ethan
interrogated himself about the seconds before the blast—what he should have noticed, whether a
better prosecutor, a better man, would have prevented it—the less coherent the event became. He
would sit at home trying to explain it to his wife and instead get seized by vivid shards that felt
as if they were happening in his living room. He was not reviewing the attack so much as being
dropped back inside it, with the same sensory force and the same absence of sequence. And once the
memory arrived in that immediate, overreal way, it deepened the verdict he had already reached: if
the scene still felt this alive, then to him it meant the failure was not finished, not absolved,
and that he remained the man who had not protected people when it counted.

That loop had begun to shape his work and marriage. At the courthouse he timed his movements to
avoid the public entrance where the bombing occurred, taking a longer route through secured staff
corridors whenever he could. He hated how deliberate this had become, because it confirmed his sense
that the world was full of concealed threat and that he could not trust himself to manage it. When
he was tired, the memories came more easily and the day felt dangerous from the outset; once a
memory had been stirred up, even routine noises or a trace of alcohol on someone’s breath seemed
primed to set him off again. His wife told him he looked perpetually braced, and he admitted that he
no longer believed he could read people accurately. He sought help after freezing in court when a
loud bang from nearby construction made the courtroom disappear and the lobby come back with full
force. What brought him in was not only the fear of another episode in public, but the deeper
conviction underneath it: that if he could not make sense of what happened, he would remain the man
who failed others, and that any lapse—especially when tired—could mean he would fail them again.
