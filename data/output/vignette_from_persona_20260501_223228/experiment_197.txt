Carlos Mendoza is a 65-year-old special education teacher who came to treatment decades after his
military trauma, describing a life that had narrowed around watchfulness and exit plans. He served
in the Army in his early twenties and was deployed in a combat zone where, during one attack, he was
trapped with other soldiers in a damaged structure after a blast. He remembers the sudden concussive
force, the ringing and dizziness that made the room tilt, and the metallic, chemical taste that
filled his mouth as dust and blood mixed in the air. Men were shouting over each other, and he was
trying to orient himself fast enough to find a way out before another hit came. The moments that
stayed with him were bodily and immediate: the sick lightheadedness, the taste, the absolute need to
know where the exit was.

In the years after he returned home, those same sensations remained strangely potent. If Carlos
feels dizzy or lightheaded now, even briefly, his body reacts as if it has been dropped back into
that damaged room. A certain sharp, metallic taste can do the same. He also notices that talking
directly about what happened can bring on the same shift in him. During intake, when he tried to
describe the attack, he became visibly more tense and began glancing toward the office door. He said
that once those sensations or memories start, he does not trust himself to stay settled, and he
immediately wants to position himself where he can leave. In restaurants, school meetings, and even
in therapy, he chooses a seat near the exit, keeps an eye on the door, and avoids conversations that
might stir strong emotion because he expects that if he lets himself feel too much, he will lose
control of his bearings.

Those triggers do not just make him want to escape; they convince him that danger may already be
near. Unexpected or loud sounds are especially difficult. A locker slamming in the hallway at
school, a student knocking over a chair, or a fire drill can send a jolt through him, and in that
instant he becomes superalert, scanning for where the sound came from and how quickly he could move
out. The dizziness and taste are frightening in part because they seem to announce threat before he
has time to think. He described it as his body “catching something” before his mind does. Once that
alarm is activated, he starts automatically identifying escape routes before he can feel comfortable
anywhere.

Over time, this sense of threat has shaped how he moves through his workday. As a special education
teacher, he often has to attend IEP meetings with parents, administrators, and support staff, but he
enters each room by first locating the doors and deciding where he will sit. He scans the
environment repeatedly, noting who is behind him, what noises are building in the hallway, and
whether anything might block his path out. Because he is so focused on staying prepared, he avoids
emotionally charged conversations when he can, particularly if a student’s crisis or a colleague’s
question might pull him into talking about military service or other distressing topics. He tells
himself this is just being practical. Still, the more intensely he experiences the world as
unpredictable and potentially dangerous, the more tightly he relies on these habits of staying near
the exit, monitoring the room, and steering away from strong feeling.

That pattern has also made the triggers more powerful. Carlos notices sounds other people dismiss,
and because he is already keyed up and tracking every change in the room, small shifts in his body
stand out immediately. A moment of lightheadedness while standing in a crowded classroom can feel
ominous rather than ordinary. In the faculty lounge, an aftertaste from coffee or cafeteria food can
stop him cold because he is already on guard enough to register it as significant. Even talking
about the trauma becomes harder once he has spent the whole day braced for threat; the subject feels
less like a memory and more like something that could overwhelm him in the present. His efforts to
stay ready bring temporary relief, but they also keep him focused on cues that confirm he is not
safe.

The strain has become more visible in recent years. Colleagues know him as dependable but tense, and
he has started declining school events that feel crowded or emotionally intense. He can still teach,
but he does so from a position in the classroom that lets him watch the door, and he feels ashamed
that students’ sudden noises can affect him so strongly. He sought help after becoming dizzy during
a parent meeting and immediately losing track of the conversation while planning how to get out of
the room. What frightened him most was how quickly the old urgency took over. He says he wants to
keep working and does not want his life organized around doors and exits anymore, but he is afraid
that if he stops staying on guard, he will be caught off guard in exactly the way he once was.
