Mai Tran is a 29-year-old combat pilot who came to treatment months after her younger brother was
killed overseas while she was stateside on a training rotation. The call reached her in a ready room
between sorties. What has stayed with her is not a complete narrative so much as a set of frozen
images: the chaplain’s face on a screen, the flat official language, her brother’s last photograph
in uniform on her phone, and later the funeral with relatives speaking in fragments about what a
family loses when one person is gone. Since then, the event has never felt fully in the past to her.
The memory returns like a film already cued up, running over and over without her consent, and at
other times as stark visual stills, as if she is looking at photographs in her mind rather than
remembering something finished.

In the first weeks after his death, Mai tried to keep functioning by narrowing her life to work. On
base she could get through checklists and flight preparation, but ordinary moments became charged in
ways she did not expect. Casual conversation in the squadron lounge about someone losing a parent, a
safety debrief that drifted toward family notification, or simply being asked what happened to her
brother could make her physically uncomfortable before she even understood why. In those moments,
her surroundings seemed to pull the whole memory back without warning, vivid and complete. Just
hearing the event spoken about, or hearing loss spoken about more generally, could bring back the
same visual sequence with such force that she felt briefly pinned inside it.

Those triggers also struck at what she had come to believe about herself. Whenever the subject of
the death came up, Mai’s mind did not stop at grief. She was immediately back inside the accusation
that she should have protected him and failed. Being a pilot had once been central to how she
understood herself; now it sharpened the sense that there was something wrong with her as a person
if she could train to protect strangers and still not save her own brother. At the same time, the
formal notifications, the distance, and the way others offered sympathy she found unbearable
hardened another conclusion: other people could not really be relied on. The conversations she most
dreaded were not only painful; they seemed to confirm these judgments every time they were stirred.

Once those beliefs had taken hold, they fed the memories further. The replay rarely arrived as a
neutral recollection. It was pulled tight around the same meanings—her failure, her defectiveness,
the certainty that no one would truly hold her up if she let herself lean on them. When the images
came, she searched them automatically for what she had missed, as if staring hard enough at those
mental photographs might reveal the moment she should have acted differently. Instead, the memory
grew more vivid and more punishing.

Mai responded by trying forcefully not to think about any of it. When the film of the event started
in her mind, she would push it away, turn to mission planning, clean her apartment late into the
night, or leave a room if other pilots began talking about deaths in their families. She stopped
answering messages from acquaintances who asked how she was doing because she could not bear being
reminded by other people. She also found herself pulling back from the beginnings of friendship or
romance. It made sense to her that closeness was dangerous: people could be lost, and people could
fail you, and needing them only exposed something weak and damaged in herself.

Over time, these habits tightened her world rather than quieting it. The harder she tried not to
think about her brother, the more abruptly the images broke through, triggered by some small detail
in her environment before she could prepare. Avoiding conversations about loss meant she never
stayed long enough anywhere for the discomfort to settle; instead, the topic itself became more
charged, and even mild references could set off the same full replay. Keeping new people at a
distance left her increasingly alone after flights and on weekends, and in that isolation her
conclusions about herself deepened. If she could not do what came naturally to other people—trust,
grieve openly, let herself depend on someone—then that seemed like more proof that something was
wrong with her.

By the time she sought help, the problem had begun to threaten the life she had built. She was still
flying, but she was expending enormous effort to stay composed around others, and the squadron had
noticed how quickly she shut down if conversation moved anywhere near death or family. She came in
after leaving a gathering abruptly when another pilot mentioned losing a cousin; by the time she
reached the parking lot, the whole memory was back in full, and with it the familiar certainty that
she had failed the person she loved most. What frightened Mai most was not only the persistence of
the images but the fear that this was revealing her true character—that she was fundamentally
defective and permanently unable to rely on anyone. What she hoped, cautiously, was that she might
find some way to remember her brother without being seized by the same punishing film each time his
loss enters the room.
