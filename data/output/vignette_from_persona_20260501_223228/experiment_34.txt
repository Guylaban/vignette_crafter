David Mercer, a 68-year-old school counselor, came to treatment after more than a year of trying to
manage on his own following an assault that took place on his walk back to the parking garage after
an evening district meeting. A man who smelled strongly of alcohol stepped out from behind a
concrete pillar, grabbed him, and shoved him hard against the wall. What stayed with David was not a
smooth narrative of the attack but a set of sharp, frozen images: the glint of the man’s belt buckle
under the garage light, the blur of a hand at his collar, the concrete close to his face, the sour
alcohol on the man’s breath. He was taken to the hospital afterward for evaluation, and the sting of
antiseptic on his skin and in the room became bound up with the same fear. When he speaks about it
now, he does not tell the story straight through. He describes “pictures,” a few moments that stand
alone, and says that remembering them brings back the same terror he felt at the time, as if no time
has passed at all.

In the months after the assault, these fragments began to break through without warning. At school,
walking past the nurse’s office after surfaces had been cleaned, he would catch that hospital-
antiseptic smell and suddenly be back in one of those snapshots, his body seized by the original
terror before he had time to think. If a parent arrived for a conference carrying the faint smell of
alcohol from a lunch drink or the night before, a piece of the memory would flash up and vanish just
as quickly, leaving him shaken and embarrassed. What surprised him most was that emotional closeness
could work the same way. When his wife sat beside him and tried to ask what he had been feeling
since the attack, or when a distressed student leaned in with trust and vulnerability, something
about the intimacy itself seemed to open the door to those same abrupt fragments. The memory did not
come as a full account he could sort through. It came as isolated pictures and the immediate
conviction that he was in danger again.

David responded by trying not to be fully present when the memories broke through. He learned to
blank out deliberately, to go distant in his own words, as if leaving his body could get him through
the wave of terror that arrived with the images. He also began avoiding people more generally,
keeping his office door partly closed when he could, shortening conversations in the hallway, and
limiting casual contact with colleagues. At home he became especially guarded with his wife. Because
moments of emotional closeness could stir up those vivid fragments, he began to avoid intimacy too,
steering conversations toward practical matters, going to bed later than she did, and pulling back
whenever she tried to reach him emotionally. From his point of view this made sense: if remembering
meant reliving, then distance and numbing were the safest options available.

Over time, though, these habits seemed to make the world feel narrower and more loaded. The more
David avoided people and retreated from closeness, the more charged ordinary encounters became when
he could not avoid them. In his work as a counselor, the job itself put him directly in situations
he had started to fear: sitting in close emotional contact with students, listening to family
crises, being present with tears and trust. He found himself bracing before appointments, and the
very fact of someone turning toward him with feeling could act as a trigger. He would catch a whiff
of disinfectant in the counseling office after the custodian had cleaned, or alcohol on a parent at
dismissal, and those isolated, terrifying images would hit even harder against the blankness he had
been trying to maintain. His efforts to leave his body and keep others at a distance seemed to leave
him less able to absorb what was happening around him, so the smells and moments of closeness stood
out with more force, immediately tied to the assault.

His marriage has been strained by this withdrawal. His wife has told him she feels she is living
beside him rather than with him, and David does not disagree, though he feels ashamed rather than
angry. He is frightened by how quickly a smell or a moment of connection can make the memory appear
in pieces and disappear again, leaving him disoriented. What finally brought him in was an incident
at school when a student, crying over a family emergency, reached for a box of tissues just after
the office had been cleaned. The antiseptic smell rose, David felt the familiar snapshots slam into
him, and he went mentally blank in the middle of the session. He is seeking help now because he is
afraid that if he cannot stop retreating from people, he will lose both the work that has defined
him and the closeness of his marriage; at the same time, he still hopes there might be a way to
remember what happened without being overtaken by the same terror every time.
