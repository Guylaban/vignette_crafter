Ravi Patel, a 42-year-old tax inspector, came to treatment nearly a year after a car accident in
which he had seriously injured another driver. He had been driving home after a long day, distracted
for a moment at an intersection, when his car struck the side of a smaller vehicle. What remained
with him most sharply was not a complete sequence but fragments: the metallic impact, the sudden
blare of a horn, and, above all, the sound that followed from the other car—shouting and crying that
he still heard in his head at unexpected moments. Some parts of the scene were unnaturally vivid,
like the crushed driver’s door and the other man’s face as people gathered; other stretches were
simply absent. Ravi could recount a few seconds with painful clarity and then hit a blank space that
left him feeling as if the worst part might be missing.

In the months afterward, he became preoccupied with what the accident meant about him. He had come
to believe that he had failed in a basic duty to protect other people, and this belief carried a
deep sense of humiliation. He was certain he had exposed something unforgivable in himself. Just as
disturbing was the feeling that the world no longer followed the ordinary rules he had trusted—that
a routine drive home, a mundane evening, could turn in an instant into irreversible harm. Those
conclusions seemed to shape the way he remembered the crash. He returned again and again to the most
condemning fragments, while the gaps in memory grew more menacing to him; the missing parts felt
less like ordinary incomplete recall and more like proof that something even worse had happened,
something that would confirm how badly he had failed.

That broken quality of memory, in turn, strengthened the way he judged himself. Because he could
hear the cries and the horn as if they were happening again, while whole sections stayed blank, Ravi
experienced the accident as unfinished in his mind. He would think, if he could not even hold the
event together properly, then he did not deserve to move past it. The sharply preserved moments
seemed to him like evidence for the prosecution; the missing moments left room for his imagination
to fill in further blame. He did not describe confusion so much as moral certainty: whatever he
could not remember, he assumed it reflected his failure.

His daily life as a tax inspector gave him little room to escape reminders. Driving to field visits,
he found himself slowing abruptly whenever he saw a car with side-panel damage or fresh dents, and
the sight could pull up the same split-second image of impact along with the remembered sounds. If
colleagues casually asked about the accident or if conversation touched on traffic injuries, he
would feel his body tense before he said very little. Funerals became especially difficult. When an
older relative died, Ravi stood at the cemetery hearing prayers while his attention fixed on the
nearness of graves and grieving families; the atmosphere fused instantly with his sense that someone
else had nearly died because of him. These reminders set off a familiar effort to clamp down on his
mind. He tried hard not to think about the crash at all, and when the images broke through he told
himself he deserved the distress. At other moments he would mutter that it could have been worse,
that at least the man had survived, but the comparison brought only brief relief and never settled
anything.

Over time those habits tightened around him. The more he pushed the memories away, the more jaggedly
they seemed to return, attached to damaged cars, grief rituals, or any direct mention of the event.
He began taking longer routes to avoid the intersection where the collision had happened and
dreading workdays that required driving across town. At home, his wife noticed that he went silent
whenever family members discussed illness, funerals, or accidents. Yet his private way of handling
it—insisting he should suffer, trying to shut the memories down, reminding himself others had it
worse—left him feeling even more disgraced. If he still needed to punish himself, he reasoned, that
only proved the depth of his failure. And each new surge of shame made the memory fragments feel
harsher and more accusatory the next time they came.

By the time Ravi sought help, he was exhausted by how little space remained in his life. A recent
cemetery service for a family friend had left him hearing the crash sounds for the rest of the day,
and the following week he had to pull his car over on the way to an inspection after seeing a
vehicle that had been hit at the side. What frightened him most was the thought that this was now
permanent—that he was the kind of man who had failed to protect another person and should not be
allowed peace. What he hoped for, though he said it cautiously, was to find some way to remember
what happened without feeling condemned by it every time.
