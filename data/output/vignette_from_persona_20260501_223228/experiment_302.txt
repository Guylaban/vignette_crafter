Anna Keller is a 51-year-old civil servant who came to treatment after more than a year of living
inside what she describes as an ever-shrinking map of safety. The trauma occurred at work, in a
government office building where she had spent years feeling competent and predictable. One
afternoon there was a toxic chemical exposure in the building. What Anna remembers most vividly is
not a coherent sequence but a scene of sudden confusion: alarms, people speaking over one another,
fragments of instructions she could not make sense of, and the sickening realization that something
dangerous was in the air while no one seemed able to explain clearly what was happening. In those
first minutes she was trying to understand shouted directives and panicked office chatter, but the
language around her blurred into incomprehensible noise. Since then, that same kind of confused
speech has carried the force of the event for her.

In the weeks after, Anna found that ordinary unpredictability began to feel intolerable. If a
meeting changed without warning, if several people in the hallway spoke at once, if someone at home
or at work wanted to discuss the exposure, her body reacted before she could think. She would grow
tense and guarded, become sharply alert to unexpected sounds, and feel that only a few places—her
home office, her bedroom, one particular corner of her workplace with the door shut—were truly safe.
Strangers who approached her in routine settings, even in the lobby of her building or at the
grocery store, felt potentially dangerous simply because she could not anticipate them. The triggers
did not remain outside her; they pulled her into a state of threat in which the world again felt
confusing and unsafe.

Those same moments also exposed how fragmented the memory remained. When coworkers asked what had
happened that day, or when a family member gently invited her to talk about it, Anna often started
and then lost the thread. She would stop mid-sentence, unable to explain clearly, with the memory
slipping away just when she tried to grasp it on purpose. It was not only that she did not want to
talk; she could not seem to put the worst of it into words. Hearing others discuss the event, or
being surrounded by overlapping speech she could not decode, brought back the felt quality of the
exposure more readily than any deliberate remembering did. The trigger seemed to reach the memory
more easily than her mind could.

Because she could not trust herself to speak coherently when caught off guard, Anna began rehearsing
conversations before nearly any interaction. Before entering a supervisor’s office, answering a
phone call, or even greeting a neighbor, she would silently script possible exchanges, trying to
protect herself from confusion. She also became rigidly avoidant of being touched, flinching from
casual contact in hallways and pulling away from even benign gestures. Medical settings became
especially difficult: hospitals, ambulances, physical examinations. She would postpone appointments
and invent reasons not to go. Part of this was fear of being forced back into the atmosphere of the
exposure, but part of it came from the humiliating sense that if anyone asked her to describe what
had happened, she would once again lose access to the story and be left defenseless. Her difficulty
retrieving and verbalizing the memory fed the very strategies she relied on to manage daily life.

Over time those strategies narrowed her world further. Rehearsing every interaction made unfamiliar
encounters feel even more loaded, so that spontaneous conversations or sudden questions became
triggers in their own right. Avoiding medical cues meant that ambulances on the street or mention of
a checkup could jolt her into alarm. Keeping distance from touch and limiting herself to “safe”
places left strangers feeling more foreign and intrusive, and her vigilance to sound intensified. At
work, where civil service had once meant steady routine, she now scans for abrupt changes in tone,
overlapping voices outside her office, or unscheduled conversations. If she hears confused chatter
in a corridor, she becomes certain something is wrong before she knows what is being said. In that
state, trying to recall the original event becomes even harder; her mind goes blank or fragments,
which only confirms to her that she is still not safe because she cannot fully understand what
happened.

The more threatened Anna feels, the more potent the triggers become. An unexpected loud sound primes
her to hear ordinary conversation as chaotic. Feeling unsafe around strangers makes unpredictable
situations seem charged from the outset. Once activated, she becomes acutely aware again of not
being able to retrieve a clear, verbal account of the exposure, and that gap frightens her almost as
much as the memory itself. She has come to believe that if she cannot put the event into words, then
she cannot protect herself from anything like it happening again.

What finally brought Anna to seek help was the realization that this pattern was beginning to
endanger both her work and her marriage. She has started avoiding in-person meetings whenever
possible, and her husband has become hurt and confused by how often she pulls away from touch. She
came in after panicking when coworkers began discussing the exposure during an office conversation
and she found herself unable to speak, unable to think, and desperate to get back to one of the few
places she still considered safe. What frightens her most is that her life will keep contracting
around these pockets of safety; what she hopes, cautiously, is that she might someday be able to
hear confusion, speak about what happened, and remain in the world without feeling under threat.
