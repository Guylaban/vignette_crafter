and trying to establish himself in a field he had once found exciting. He came to treatment after
months of feeling as if his life had narrowed into vigilance. When he describes what happened, he

routines became contaminated by fear. A person he had briefly interacted with online began sending
him messages that quickly turned intrusive and possessive. At first the contact seemed merely
uncomfortable, but it escalated into repeated texts from new numbers, comments that referred to
where he had been that day, and appearances near places he regularly used. What unsettled him most
was the way the stalker seemed to know his patterns before he had told anyone them directly. Walking
out of his office one evening and seeing the same figure across the street, half-lit by a bus
shelter ad, gave him the feeling that nowhere in his day was truly private anymore.

In the weeks that followed, Kwame began to make sense of the experience in ways that have remained
fixed. He had come to believe that the world is fundamentally dangerous, not in an abstract way but
in the specific sense that an ordinary day can turn threatening without warning. He speaks as though
safety is a temporary illusion other people are naive enough to trust. From his perspective, danger

and for him this is not a figure of speech but a rule he now lives by. He scans people’s faces on
the train, notices who is standing too long outside the building, checks whether a car has passed
twice. At 19, he sounds older when he talks this way, as if the stalking taught him a lesson he did
not want but now feels compelled to obey.

person who pauses near reception. If he has to stay late to finish a prototype, he becomes acutely

after dark. He chooses his seat in meeting rooms based on sightlines to the door. Even when there is

without treating every ordinary moment as the start of another threat.
