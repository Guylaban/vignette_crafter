Kwame is a 34-year-old parole officer who came to treatment after months of feeling as if his life
had narrowed to vigilance, dread, and the effort of getting through each day without being
overwhelmed. He had been in a relationship with a woman who became physically abusive behind closed
doors. What he returns to most often is one night after an argument in their apartment, when she
cornered him in the kitchen, struck him repeatedly, and shoved him hard enough that he hit the
counter and bit the inside of his mouth. He can still summon the metallic, bloody taste from that
moment. Later, when he was treated for his injuries, the sharp antiseptic smell of the hospital
fixed itself in his mind alongside the sound of her voice during the assault, so that now those
sensory details seem fused to the event rather than separate from it.

In the weeks afterward, situations that resembled pieces of the assault began to feel charged with
danger. At work, if he passed the medical unit in the courthouse annex and caught the smell of
disinfectant, his body would go rigid before he had time to think. If a client’s relative looked at
him in a way he experienced as sexualized, or if he found himself alone in a room with another man
during a home visit, he became instantly watchful, shoulders tight, eyes moving over exits, hands,
posture. He described constantly studying other people for signs they might turn on him. Those same
moments also pulled the assault into the present: with the hospital smell in particular, he heard
her words in his head as if spoken again, tasted blood in his mouth, and for several seconds lost
the sense that it was a memory rather than something happening now. Each trigger also carried a
bitter conclusion. Being set off so easily seemed to him proof that what happened had ruined his
life, and he would think with anger that it was deeply unfair that she had done this and faced no
real consequence.

As these episodes accumulated, Kwame came to believe that he was no longer the man he had been. He
said more than once that his life felt destroyed, and on his worst nights he was not sure it was
worth living like this. The force of the memories drove those judgments deeper. When the scene came
back with her voice in his head and the taste in his mouth, it did not feel like recollection but
re-entry, and afterward he took that loss of footing as evidence that the abuse still owned him. The
more persistently his body stayed on alert, the more he read that tension as confirmation that the
danger had not ended inside him. Even before anything happened in a room, he was already scanning,
already braced, and he understood that constant readiness as a sign that the trauma had permanently
damaged him.

From there, his efforts to cope began to organize his life. Because he had come to see vulnerability
as unsafe and any relief as precarious, he pushed away positive feelings when they appeared, cutting
off moments of warmth with his current partner because letting himself feel good seemed like letting
his guard down. Because he expected danger in other people, he avoided eye contact, especially in
the office waiting area and during field visits, telling himself it was better not to invite
attention. Because disagreement had preceded the worst assault, he started avoiding conflict
altogether, backing down quickly with coworkers and softening decisions with parolees to prevent any
confrontation. These habits made immediate sense to him, but they also kept his body tense and
searching. By not looking directly at people, he felt less able to read them and watched them even
more covertly. By steering around disagreements, he became more sensitive to any hint that one was
emerging. By flattening positive emotion, he lived in a state with little relief from alarm.

Those strategies also seemed to make the memories easier to trigger. In sessions he noticed that
when he spent a day avoiding eye contact and swallowing irritation, he was more likely to be thrown
by a glance he read as sexual or by being left alone with a man in a conference room. On those days
the antiseptic smell from a clinic hallway could bring everything back at full intensity. He had
begun to associate whole stretches of his work environment with the possibility of being set off,
and that expectation itself made him enter them already braced. The loop had tightened enough that
he was pulling back not only from clients and colleagues but from his partner, who told him he was
present in body and nowhere else.

What finally brought him in was a recent incident at work when the smell of disinfectant outside an
interview room made him hear his ex-partner’s voice so vividly that he had to leave the floor. He
sat in his car afterward convinced that nothing would ever change, furious at the unfairness of what
had been done to him, and frightened by how often the thought came that maybe life was not worth
living if this was all it would be. He is seeking help because he wants the memories to feel past,
not present, and because he is afraid that if he keeps living this guarded and hunted, he will lose
both his work and the relationship that has so far survived with him.
