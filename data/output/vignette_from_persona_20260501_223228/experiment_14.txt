still talked about deployment in fragments rather than as a story with a beginning and end. The
event she circled around happened during a route-clearance mission overseas, when an explosion
ignited a vehicle ahead of hers. She remembered the sudden bloom of flames, the searing heat

confusion. Those moments were preserved in sharp pieces rather than a narrative. She could not put
them into a clear sequence even in the privacy of her own mind; what returned instead were flashes
and sounds, especially the shouted commands and panic she heard that day.

Back home, those same sensory details became potent in ordinary life. At work, if she passed a
training burn pit or caught sight of fire during demolition exercises, her body reacted before she
could think. A burst of heat from industrial equipment against her skin or a soldier yelling across
the motor pool could make her go rigid and instantly ready for danger. What unnerved her just as
much was the silence that sometimes followed routine activity; an unusual quiet in a work area made
her feel that something terrible was about to break loose. In those moments she was not only tense
but unable to focus on what was in front of her. The trigger could also bring back the event whole
and without warning, so that a smell of heat or the sight of flames was enough for her to hear the
shouting in her head again as if she were back there.

Those sudden returns of memory shaped what she believed about herself and the world. Each time the
scene intruded so forcefully, Marisol took it as proof that something was wrong with her, that other
people could move on from war and she could not. She had come to believe the world was fundamentally
dangerous, not just in combat but everywhere, and the randomness of what happened felt intolerably
unfair to her. Seeing fire or hearing shouting did not simply startle her; it seemed to confirm that
danger was always near and that she had been left to carry an injustice no one had put right. Over
time she became sensitized to those cues themselves, scanning for heat, noise, and changes in the
atmosphere as if her mind were teaching itself where the next threat would come from.

Her beliefs then began to govern how she lived. Because she was certain the world was dangerous and
because she felt flawed for reacting so strongly, she tried to avoid anything that might be
stressful. She turned down optional field exercises, lingered in administrative tasks when she
could, and at home steered away from family cookouts where open flames and loud voices were likely.

trying to shut down whatever rose up inside her. She also checked her surroundings constantly, in
the office, on base, in parking lots, noticing exits, listening for changes in sound, looking for
anything that might signal threat. That constant management of herself made her feel even more
unlike the person she used to be, reinforcing her conviction that there was something defective in
her.

The memories themselves fed this pattern. Because they arrived without warning and brought the
shouting back so vividly, Marisol began organizing her days around not being caught off guard. She
avoided stressors and kept herself emotionally blunted because she feared that any strong feeling
would open the door to the full memory. Yet the more she lived in this narrowed, watchful way, the
more tense her body remained. She was on alert so much of the time that concentration became
difficult even during routine engineering planning, and once she was already keyed up, the next
silence, shout, or wave of heat struck with even more force. Hypervigilance made the memories easier
to set off; the memories intensified her sense of present threat.

Over months this tightened into the life she now found herself living. Her constant scanning meant
she noticed every flare, every raised voice, every change in temperature, which made those triggers
feel omnipresent. Avoiding stress and numbing herself had shrunk her world but had not made it feel
safer. Instead, the restrictions seemed to prove that the world really was dangerous and that she
really was damaged by what had happened. Her marriage had grown strained by her distance, and at
work she was ashamed that she could no longer concentrate as she once had in the very environment
where she was expected to lead calmly.

Marisol finally came for help after freezing during a stateside training exercise when flames rose
unexpectedly and someone shouted for equipment. She recovered quickly enough that others may not
have understood what happened, but she spent the rest of the day convinced she could hear the old

life had become organized around waiting for it. She said she wanted, more than anything, to be able
to stand in her own work environment without feeling that danger was already there, and to
