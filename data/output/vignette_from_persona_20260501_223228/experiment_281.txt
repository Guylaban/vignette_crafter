Elisabeth Fischer is a 74-year-old physical therapist who came to treatment after months of sleeping
lightly and startling at ordinary sounds in her own house. She grew up in Europe and, as a child,
fled with her family during wartime. What she described most vividly was one night in a crowded
temporary shelter after a hurried escape: the sharp burn of something singed nearby, adults speaking
in strained voices about whether it was safe, and the sour, medicinal smell of alcohol from spilled
spirits and disinfectant mingling in the air. She remembers strangers pressing close in the dimness
and the sense that danger might enter at any moment. Even now, when she tries to talk about that
period in any detail, she finds herself no longer feeling like an older woman in a therapist’s
office but like the frightened girl listening and watching for what would happen next.

In the years afterward Elisabeth built a life in the United States, married, and worked for decades
as a physical therapist. She is proud of having been steady and useful to other people. Still,
certain moments have remained oddly potent. If a patient or family member mentions wartime
displacement, or if conversation turns to her own past, she can suddenly see the shelter in flashes
rather than as a story she is choosing to remember. The memories come mostly as pictures—the close
walls, the faces, the doorway—and with them she hears the sounds that were there at the time, voices
and movement in the dark. The smell of alcohol on a patient’s breath or the scent of rubbing alcohol
in the clinic can do the same, as can the odor of something burnt in a staff break room or at home
if toast catches. Those cues do not simply remind her; they make her feel that the event is
happening now.

Because the memories arrive with that immediate, present-tense quality, Elisabeth has come to live
as though danger is liable to reappear. At home, especially in the evening, she listens for sounds
that might mean someone is entering the house. If footsteps pass outside or a door settles, her body
reacts before she has time to think, and she often feels briefly certain something is wrong even
when nothing is. Strangers approaching her in ordinary settings—a man coming too directly toward her
in a parking lot, an unfamiliar delivery person at the door—feel threatening in a way she knows is
excessive but cannot shake. The more she notices herself scanning and reacting, the more keyed up
she becomes to smells, snippets of conversation, and other cues that might set her off again.

Over the past year these reactions have shaped her routines. At work she is constantly checking her
surroundings, keeping track of entrances and who is coming down the hallway, especially if there is
the smell of disinfectant or alcohol in a treatment room. That vigilance gives her a momentary sense
of readiness, but it also keeps the old material close to the surface; once she is scanning, images
and sounds from the shelter are more likely to break in. The same is true at home. She does a quiet
circuit of locks and windows and remains alert for sounds of entry, and in that state the night of
flight returns more easily, complete with the burnt smell and the sense of crowded danger. When the
memories flare, her conviction that strangers may pose a threat intensifies again.

Elisabeth has also begun drinking more in the evenings than she once did, usually telling herself
she needs something to settle her nerves after a day of being on guard. Sometimes it blunts the
tension briefly, but it does not loosen the grip of the past; if anything, sitting with a drink
often turns into dwelling on who she used to be before all of this was stirred up, and from there
she slips back into vivid images and sounds she cannot stop. On difficult days, she avoids
conversations that might touch her history because she knows that talking about it can trigger the
whole sequence, yet the effort of keeping it all contained leaves her listening even more sharply
for signs of danger.

Her husband has noticed that she no longer seems able to relax in the evenings and that she watches
visitors with a wary eye. Elisabeth finally sought help after realizing that the clinic, once a
place where she felt competent and grounded, had become another setting where ordinary smells and
approaches from unfamiliar people could make her feel as if she were back in that shelter. What
frightens her most is not only the memories themselves but the fear that she is losing the boundary
between then and now. What she hopes for is simple and urgent: to be able to smell alcohol or
something burnt, to hear a noise at night, or to speak about her past without being taken back into
it.
