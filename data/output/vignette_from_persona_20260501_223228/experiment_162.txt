Evelyn is a 76-year-old radiologist who spent part of her late career volunteering with a civilian
medical team in a war zone. She still speaks about that period in fragments rather than a story.
What comes back most strongly is not a clear sequence but sensory pieces: the sharp metallic smell
inside the imaging unit, the concussion of shelling that made the walls tremble, the sight of
injured civilians being rushed in, and a moment when strangers pushed through a corridor in panic
while she froze, unable to tell who needed help and who might be dangerous. When she tries to
describe what happened, she becomes visibly stuck. She says she “knows something terrible happened”
and can feel it in her body, but cannot put the worst of it into words. Efforts to recall it
deliberately leave her with blanks and then, just as suddenly, a wave of shame so intense that she
looks away and goes silent, as if there is something about her own response in that moment she
cannot bear to face.

In the months after she returned home, those broken, shame-laden recollections began shaping how she
moved through the world. Because the memory would not come as a coherent narrative, ordinary
situations acquired the same uncertain quality as that hospital corridor. A stranger stepping too
close in a grocery store, an unfamiliar parking garage, a person approaching her car window at a
stoplight could all produce the same immediate sense that danger might be hidden just out of view.
She became constantly watchful, scanning faces, exits, body movements, and tone of voice. She had
come to believe that if she could not clearly know what had happened then, she could not trust
herself to judge what was safe now. Unfamiliar places felt especially intolerable because she could
not quickly assess them, and strangers in ordinary settings no longer registered as neutral; they
felt potentially threatening before they felt human.

That vigilance, in turn, seems to make the memory even harder for her to approach. By the time she
sits down and tries to think directly about the war, she is already tense, alert to sounds in the
house, glancing toward the window, noticing who is outside. Under that pressure, the memory narrows
further into bodily unease and scattered images rather than words. What frightens her most is the
helplessness of that experience: she braces for danger in the present and then finds that the past
remains inaccessible except for shame and flashes she cannot organize.

Over time Evelyn developed a life built around not feeling too much. She avoids anything that might
stir her emotionally, not only obviously upsetting material but also occasions that might bring
excitement or tenderness. She has largely stopped making future plans with her husband because
looking ahead feels exposing, almost reckless, as though stability could vanish without warning. At
home she spends long hours on her phone or online, moving from news to games to trivial searches in
a way she recognizes as numbing. These habits make sense to her because whenever the memory edges
closer—whenever she tries to tell herself what happened or even notices the shame gathering in her
chest—she reaches quickly for distraction or emotional flatness. Not letting herself feel becomes
her way of keeping the unspeakable memory from taking shape.

Those same strategies have gradually tightened her world and sharpened her sense of threat. In the
radiology department where she still works part-time, she keeps to familiar routes, dislikes being
assigned to an unfamiliar clinic area, and is especially unsettled when patients’ family members or
delivery staff she does not know approach her unexpectedly in hallways. She finds herself studying
them before she can hear what they are saying. When she has spent a weekend buried in her phone,
avoiding conversation and putting off even small plans, she returns to work feeling less grounded
and more on edge, as though she has done nothing to test whether the world is actually safe. The
more she lives by withdrawal and distraction, the less confidence she has in unfamiliar
environments, and the more necessary her scanning seems.

Yet those same habits also leave the trauma unchanged and unreachable. Because she cuts off feeling
so quickly and turns away from anything that might lead toward the past, the memory remains exactly
as she fears it: disjointed, wordless, and saturated with shame. She cannot examine it long enough
to form a fuller account, and that failure confirms for her that something about it is unbearable.
By now her husband has noticed that she no longer agrees to trips, avoids new restaurants, and goes
quiet when he asks about the future. She finally sought help after becoming panicked when an
unfamiliar man approached her in the hospital parking lot to ask for directions and she reacted as
if she were back in danger. What she wants most is to stop living in a state of constant
watchfulness, but what she fears is that if she lets herself remember, what comes back will prove
something shameful about who she was in that moment.
