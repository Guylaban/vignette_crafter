At 68, Ama had been married for many years, but when she spoke about the abuse by her husband, what
came through first was not a single incident so much as the atmosphere that had formed around it:
evenings when his mood shifted without warning, the way she learned to read the set of his jaw, the
smell of damp earth coming in from outside after he had been in the yard, and the sickening
knowledge that she was most at risk when she was cornered and emotionally exposed. Some of the worst
assaults had followed moments when she was alone with him at night, already undressed for bed, with
the house quiet and no one else to hear. The bedroom itself became tied to those moments of
helplessness, and even now sleeping alone or anywhere unfamiliar could make her body react as if she
were back there, bracing for him to come through the door.

In the years since, Ama had not found that these memories stayed in the past. A faint outdoor smell
after rain, the scent of soil from a potted plant, or simply lying down by herself at night could
bring the whole scene back without warning. She described the memory as forcing its way in, complete
and immediate, before she had time to prepare. Yet once it began, she would also cut it off almost
at once, pushing it away before she could fully take in what she had remembered. That left her
caught in a painful pattern: she never felt she had really put it to rest, and because the memory
could erupt from ordinary things around her, the world felt crowded with reminders she could not
fully predict.

Ama had come to organize her days around not letting her mind settle. Since becoming unemployed, she
filled the hours with constant tasks—cleaning one room and then another, reorganizing drawers,
finding errands to run, keeping the television on in the background—not because these activities
gave her pleasure, but because stillness gave the memory room to intrude. When something in her
surroundings pulled the abuse into mind, she became even more determined to stay busy and mentally
occupied, shifting immediately to a chore, a phone game, or some small practical task. She also
noticed herself going along with what other people wanted, even in minor situations, because feeling
emotionally vulnerable stirred too much of the same danger; compliance still felt like the quickest
way to reduce risk.

What frightened Ama most was how thoroughly the memory had altered her sense of safety. Because it
could arrive so suddenly and vividly, she no longer trusted that danger was truly over, even inside
her own home. She said there were only a very limited number of places where she could relax at all,
and even there the feeling was fragile. At night she struggled to fall asleep because some part of
her felt she needed to remain alert, listening to the house and noticing every sound. That constant
watchfulness made sleeping alone especially difficult; bedtime was not simply lonely for her, it
felt exposing.

Living in that guarded state shaped the way she tried to manage herself. Because she felt on edge
even at home, Ama kept herself occupied from morning until late evening, as if movement could hold
the fear at bay. She deferred quickly to others, especially when she sensed tension, believing that
disagreeing or asserting herself might leave her too exposed. Over time her world had narrowed
around a few “safe” places and routines. Anything outside those familiar zones—an overnight stay, an
unfamiliar room, even stepping into a garden and catching the smell of fresh soil—was more likely to
set her off, and knowing that made her cling harder to the habits she thought protected her.

Those habits, however, had their own cost. The more Ama used busyness and mental distraction to get
through the day, the less tolerance she had for quiet moments, and the more easily ordinary
sensations stood out as threatening. Because she rushed to cover over any stirrings of memory, a
smell from outdoors or the prospect of being alone in bed seemed to hit her with full force each
time, as if nothing had ever been processed or finished. Her efforts to stay safe had gradually made
her life smaller and more constricted, even within her marriage and home.

By the time she sought help, Ama was exhausted. A recent night spent sleeping by herself had left
her pacing the house until dawn, alert to every creak, while the smell of wet dirt from outside had
pulled the abuse back so sharply that she could hardly breathe before forcing the image away again.
She came to treatment because she could see how trapped she had become by the need to stay busy,
agreeable, and watchful. What she feared most was that she would never again feel safe enough to
rest. What she hoped, quietly, was to be able to sit still in her own home and know that the past
was not about to step into the room.
