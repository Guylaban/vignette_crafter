David is a 63-year-old rabbi who came to treatment after months of telling himself he should be able

forced their way into his family’s neighborhood, shouting orders in the street and striking people
with whatever they carried. What remains most vivid to him is not a complete story but sharply
preserved moments: the sight of weapons in men’s hands, the sound of voices suddenly raised, and the
shock of being grabbed and pulled before he understood what was happening. Even now, he says his
body reacts before his mind does, as if it still lives in that earlier world. In session he speaks
quietly, but when he describes those moments he becomes visibly tense, as though the old danger is
present again.

For years David built a life that looked steady from the outside. He became a rabbi, known for
warmth and composure, and he now lives with a long-term partner who has grown increasingly aware of
how narrow his sense of safety has become. The difficulties intensified over the last few years as
the congregation became larger and more physically informal. During greetings after services, an
unexpected hand on his shoulder can make him flinch hard enough to lose his train of thought. If a
visitor enters wearing something at the belt that resembles a weapon, or if a disagreement in the
social hall turns into shouting, he feels himself pulled out of the present. In those moments he
immediately tries to push his attention somewhere else—onto the order of the prayer books, the
agenda for a committee meeting, the coffee urn—anything to distract himself from the rush in his
body. He also grows more distant afterward, withholding affection and emotional openness from his
partner because closeness leaves him feeling too exposed.

These episodes do not feel random to David. Each time he startles, he takes it as evidence that
something is wrong with him: his body, in his words, has become an enemy that refuses to understand
when he is safe. He has come to believe he cannot rely on himself if danger appears, and he compares
himself harshly to an imagined stronger person who would have stopped what happened long ago. The
trigger and the meaning arrive almost together; the raised voice or sudden touch does not just
frighten him, it confirms his conviction that he is fundamentally unsteady. Once he is thinking this
way, the world seems easier to ignite. A hand reaching for a friendly embrace, a ceremonial object
held too abruptly, even loud enthusiasm from congregants can register first as threat, because he is
already braced for proof that he is not in control.

every setting—sanctuary, restaurant, family gathering—according to where he feels most protected,
usually near an exit or against a wall where no one can come up behind him. He notices where voices
are rising before he can follow what is being said, and his concentration fractures whenever he is
trying to monitor the room. At work this has become painful: during pastoral meetings he sometimes
realizes he has missed the thread of what someone is telling him because he was tracking movement in
the doorway or the possibility that someone might touch him unexpectedly. The more he watches for
danger, the more quickly he spots reminders of it, and ordinary noise or motion can take on the
force of the past.

His efforts to manage this have, in his own mind, become further proof of personal failure. Because
he keeps distracting himself and restricting how emotionally close he allows himself to be, he tells
himself he deserves to suffer for what happened and for the ways he has disappointed others since.

unreliable; when he spends a whole meeting focused on where the exits are, he concludes that someone
else would have coped better. Those judgments feed back into his vigilance. Feeling that he cannot
depend on himself, he becomes even more careful about where he sits and how much of the world he
lets in, and in that tightened state he is even more sensitive to shouting, to anything weapon-like,
to the shock of unprepared touch.

reactions had made him unsafe to others as well as to himself. At home that night, when his partner
tried to comfort him, he pulled back again. He says he is afraid that his world will continue
shrinking until only a few places feel tolerable, and that he will become a man who can preach

body can stop living in the old emergency, and whether closeness can feel possible without danger.
