assaulted by a man he had picked up late at the end of a shift. He described the attack in a flat,
strained voice, but the details were sharply preserved. He had been exhausted after a long day on
his route and had agreed, against his better judgment, to let the passenger remain on board while he
finished paperwork in a quiet terminal area. The man moved behind him in the narrow space near the
driver’s seat, and what David remembers most is the sound before he fully understood what was
happening: the hiss of the bus doors, the rattle of the loose partition, the man’s breathing close
to his ear. He felt trapped by the confined driver’s area and overwhelmed by terror and humiliation
as the assault unfolded in the seat where he had always felt in control.

In the weeks afterward, those same kinds of sounds began to pull the memory back with punishing
force. The hydraulic sigh of the doors, a sudden mechanical clatter, even passengers shifting and
talking behind him while he drove could make the event return as if it were happening again. He said
the memory did not come back as something distant or narrative; it played “like a film,” complete
and immediate, and with it came the same terror he had felt during the assault. On days when he was
very tired, especially toward the end of a route, he was even more vulnerable to being thrown back
into it. Fatigue seemed to weaken whatever distance he could usually keep, and then the replay would
come with full intensity.

As those episodes accumulated, David had come to believe that what happened had revealed something
final and unforgivable about people. He was certain that people could not be trusted, and the work
he had once taken pride in now felt like daily exposure to that truth. Having strangers board his
bus no longer felt routine; each rider behind him was a reminder that anyone might cross a line
without warning. He spoke too of having lost faith more broadly—not only in other people, but in the
basic idea that life was ordered or decent. When the sounds on the bus or the press of other people
in the vehicle set him on edge, what frightened him was not just the possibility of remembering, but
the conviction that the world had already shown him what it really was.

The memories themselves deepened those beliefs. Each time the assault replayed in his mind, David
was flooded not only with terror but with intense shame about his body. He could not remember the
event without also feeling disgraced by what had been done to him physically, and that shame
hardened into the sense that something about him had been spoiled. He found himself returning again
and again to the thought that he should have seen it coming, should have protected himself, should
never have been vulnerable in that seat. Remembering the attack did not soften it through
repetition; it seemed to confirm his mistrust and his loss of faith, as though every replay was
proof that he had learned the right lesson from it.

Those beliefs, in turn, made the world feel crowded with triggers. Because he no longer trusted
people, driving with passengers became charged from the moment someone stepped on board and moved

him in a different way too: exhaustion meant feeling less defended, less solid, closer to the
helplessness he despised in himself. Even ordinary bus noises took on a special menace because he
was already braced for danger and violation. By the time he heard a sharp metallic sound or sensed
movement behind him, his mind was primed to connect it with the assault.

That anticipation fed back into the way the memories returned. On routes with heavier passenger
loads, David would monitor every sound behind him, and the vigilance itself seemed to make the film
begin. Once a fragment appeared—the hiss of the door, the feeling of someone standing too close—it
became hard to stop the rest from unspooling. At home he was more withdrawn with his wife,
embarrassed by how often he checked the locks and how little comfort he could take from her
reassurance. He kept driving because the family needed the income, but he had started dreading late
shifts and feeling trapped in his own driver’s seat.

What finally brought him to seek help was a recent shift when, after several nights of poor sleep, a
passenger’s movement behind him and the sound of the door closing sent him into such a vivid replay
that he had to pull over until he could steady himself. He was frightened by how quickly terror and
shame could still take over, and by how certain he had become that no place with other people in it
was safe. More than anything, David wanted to know whether he could drive, remember, and live in his
own body without feeling ambushed by the same scene over and over again.
