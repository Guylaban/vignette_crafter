Linh Tran is a 28-year-old tax consultant who came to treatment after months of telling herself she
should be able to “just move on” from being held captive by a man she had briefly dated. Last year,
after an argument in his apartment, he took her phone, locked the door, and kept her there overnight
and into the next day, blocking the exits each time she tried to leave. What remained most vivid for
Linh was not only his control of the room but the way her body ceased to feel like her own. She
remembered being acutely aware of where he was standing, how quickly he could cross the space, and
the sound of him shifting outside the bathroom door when she tried to put distance between them.
Even now, when she speaks about that period, she does so with a flat, embarrassed tone, as though
the most intolerable part is that it happened inside her own body and under circumstances she
believes she should have prevented.

In the weeks after she got out, Linh did not describe herself as frightened so much as on guard. In
her apartment she began listening constantly for signs that someone was entering, pausing when the
building hallway creaked and holding her breath at the sound of footsteps near her door. At
restaurants and conference rooms at work, she angled for seats with her back against the wall so she
could scan the room and keep the entrances in view. If a coworker moved suddenly beside her desk,
she jerked hard enough to spill coffee or lose her train of thought, her body reacting before she
could remind herself that she was in an office, not trapped again. She had come to experience safety
as something fragile and positional, dependent on seeing everything around her before it could reach
her.

Those reactions did not stay merely physical in her mind. Each time she startled violently when
someone passed too close, or found herself straining to hear whether anyone was outside her
apartment, Linh took it as evidence that something in her had changed for the worse. She was ashamed
of how alert she remained and read that vigilance as proof that she had failed to protect herself in
the first place. Sitting rigidly in client meetings with her chair pressed to the wall, she would
think that if she had acted differently that night—if she had not gone over, if she had not argued,
if she had noticed the danger sooner—she would not now be someone who scanned every doorway. The
fact that the man who held her had faced no consequences deepened this. To Linh, the world seemed to
be confirming that what happened could be done to her without anyone being held accountable, and her
continued watchfulness made that conclusion feel even more true.

Over time this belief settled into a punishing private logic. When her whole body flinched at
unexpected movement, she did not interpret it as the residue of captivity; she experienced it as
humiliation. What frightened her most was not only the possibility of danger but the exposure of how
affected she still was. She felt ashamed of what had happened to her body during the assault and
captivity, and that shame returned whenever her body reacted in public as if the past were present.
On busy days during tax season, when coworkers leaned over cubicle partitions or appeared suddenly
at her shoulder to ask about a return, she felt a flash of alarm followed immediately by self-
contempt. In her mind, the startle and the scanning meant she was weak, damaged, and somehow
complicit in the fact that this had happened at all.

By the time she sought help, these patterns had narrowed her life. She was still doing her job well
enough on paper, but only by exhausting herself with constant vigilance in the office and at home.
She chose the same seat in every meeting room, arrived early to claim a chair that let her watch the
door, and felt her concentration fracture whenever someone moved abruptly nearby. At night, instead
of unwinding after work, she found herself listening for sounds at her apartment door and mentally
rehearsing what she would do if someone came in. The more she lived this way, the more certain she
became that the captivity had exposed some defect in her judgment and left a permanent stain on her
body. She finally came to treatment after startling so dramatically when a colleague stepped beside
her in the office that the whole team turned to look. She said she could tolerate being scared in
private, but not the growing conviction that this was now who she was: someone unprotected,
unvindicated, and marked by what had been done to her. What she hopes for is modest but urgent—to
feel safe in a room without calculating every exit, and to stop blaming herself every time her body
remembers before she can think.
