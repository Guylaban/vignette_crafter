afternoons a week at a neighborhood branch library, shelving returns and helping children find
books. He had been at a downtown civic festival two years ago when a bomb detonated near the food

precision: the crack of the blast, the metal barricade twisting, a child’s shoe in the street,
people shoving past an older woman who had fallen. What grips him most, even now, is the sense that
in those seconds ordinary rules vanished. He cannot think about the event without returning to the
sight of people trampling one another to get away, and that moral rupture has become part of what
makes certain moments in the present feel intolerable.

Since then, his memory of the bombing has stayed vivid and cinematic, as if a strip of film starts
running in his mind with no warning. He describes it as the whole scene coming back at once, not in
fragments but in full detail. At the library, if patrons begin arguing loudly at the circulation
desk, or if he overhears conversations about terrorism, public violence, or even “how people only
care about themselves,” the memory arrives immediately and completely. Around the anniversary each
year, he begins bracing days in advance, because he knows the date itself can pull the scene closer.
In this way, the memory has made particular triggers sharply potent: talk about human cruelty,
reminders of the date, and examples of people behaving badly all feel as if they open a trapdoor
beneath him.

He has organized his life around staying away from those moments. He no longer goes with his wife to
church anniversary banquets, street fairs, or crowded holiday events. At work he asks coworkers to
cover children’s programming days when the library is busiest, and he times his shelving for the
quietest hours so he can avoid clusters of people gathering near the front desk. If a patron starts
discussing an attack they saw on the news, or complains bitterly about how selfish people have
become, Kwame finds a reason to retreat to the stacks. He has also begun turning down things that
might lift his mood—family cookouts, his granddaughter’s music recital, even small outings his wife
plans—because feeling hopeful or light seems dangerous to him, too easily broken. The same vivid
memory that ambushes him has taught him to pull back from crowds, difficult conversations, and even
pleasure, in the belief that distance is the only way to stay steady.

withdraws from family gatherings or sits alone in the staff room instead of joining colleagues, he

scene begins replaying again. Emptying his days of bustle and of joy has left long stretches in
which the film can run uninterrupted. His wife has noticed that after he refuses an outing and
remains home in his chair, he is often less present by evening, as though he has traveled somewhere
she cannot reach. The more he escapes into his own mind and narrows his world, the more available
the memory becomes.

Those same habits have also made his triggers harder to avoid, not easier. Because he no longer
tolerates busy public places, even ordinary disorder now stands out sharply: a rude exchange in the
library, teenagers pushing past one another at the computer station, a heated discussion in the
break room. His avoidance has thinned his life down to a few settings, and within them he scans
quickly for signs of people behaving badly or for talk that might turn toward violence. The
anniversary has become especially charged because his family now tiptoes around it, which only
leaves the date looming larger in the house. What he tries not to encounter has become easier to
notice.

By the time he came for help, the pattern had tightened around both work and home. His wife told him
she felt she was losing him to silence and distance, and the library director gently raised concerns
after he twice left the front desk abruptly when patrons began discussing an overseas bombing. Kwame
himself was shaken by how quickly the memory still takes him over, and by how small he has let his
life become. He says he wants, at his age, to sit at his granddaughter’s recital and walk through a
crowded room without feeling the film start again. What frightens him most is the possibility that
if he lets himself rejoin the world—even for something good—one careless conversation, one act of
selfishness, or one date on the calendar will pull him straight back to that day.
