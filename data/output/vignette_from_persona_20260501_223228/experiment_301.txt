Omar Haddad was 34, married, and working as a publisher for a small independent press in Chicago
when his symptoms began after an assault on his younger brother. He had been the one to answer the
phone that night and then to ride the bus to the emergency department, arriving in fragments of
information rather than a coherent story: his brother had been attacked outside a bar, there had
been shouting, a weapon flashed, people standing around doing nothing. What stayed with Omar was not
a single orderly memory but broken pieces that refused to join up—his brother’s torn shirt, the
fluorescent glare in the trauma bay, a stranger’s face turned toward him, and, threaded through it,
the sharp sounds he still heard in his head afterward: yelling, the scrape of shoes, the clipped
urgency of voices. In the weeks that followed, the scene would return like a film he had not chosen
to watch, replaying over and over, except that even in replay it felt incomplete, as though the
worst part existed just off frame.

Very quickly, ordinary moments began to feel dangerous. Riding the bus to work became especially
potent because it echoed the trip he had taken that night, and once he felt trapped in a crowded
aisle, he found himself studying every passenger’s face and posture to work out who might turn
violent. If someone’s expression hardened or a shoulder bag shifted suddenly, his attention snapped
to umbrella tips, metal thermoses, heavy locks—anything that could be used as a weapon. He had the
same reaction when he was very tired, especially late in the day at the office after long editorial
meetings; exhaustion made him feel less able to judge what was around him, and he became even more
vigilant. Even being naked in the shower could set it off: the exposure of having no protection, no
way to defend himself or anyone else, brought on a quick scan of sounds outside the bathroom door
and an urgent sense that he was vulnerable.

Those triggers also opened directly into memory. On the bus he would suddenly hear the event again
inside his head, not as a thought but as if the sounds were happening in the present. Fatigue made
the film start rolling more easily, and when he was undressed he was struck by disconnected flashes
of his brother bloodied and unprotected, images that came without a clear beginning or end. Because
the memories arrived in pieces, Omar kept trying to mentally reconstruct what had happened, but the
effort only seemed to sharpen certain sensory fragments and leave the larger story missing.

Over time, the memories themselves made the world feel more threatening. A replay of the emergency
room or the imagined moment of the assault could leave him spending the rest of the day reading
danger into strangers’ faces, certain that harm could erupt with almost no warning. The sounds in
his head primed him to look for the next sign of violence, and he began avoiding unfamiliar parts of
the city because he believed that if he could not quickly assess exits, objects, and people, he
would miss the warning signs again. At work this had become concrete: as a publisher he used to meet
authors in new cafés, attend launches across town, and take public transit between events. Now he
pushed for meetings in his own office, asked colleagues to represent the press at unfamiliar venues,
and rerouted himself away from buses whenever he could.

The pattern also ran in the other direction. Because he was constantly scanning faces, doorways, and
objects that could become weapons, he kept encountering details that matched the fragments already
lodged in his mind. A tense jaw, a raised voice on the street, the metallic gleam of a bike lock on
a table could make the film start again. His watchfulness for threat made bus rides, tired evenings,
and the exposed few minutes after stepping out of the shower feel even more charged, as though these
situations had proved themselves dangerous. In that way, the places and states he feared became
easier to trigger, and each trigger came preloaded with expectation.

By the time he sought help, Omar was sleeping little and arriving home wired and distant, his wife
noticing that even during routine errands he seemed to be tracking everyone around them. He felt
ashamed of how narrow his life had become, especially at work, where avoidance was beginning to
affect his reliability. What frightened him most was not only the persistence of the memories but
the sense that he was being trained by them to expect violence everywhere: on the bus, in a
stranger’s face, in any unfamiliar room, even in moments of private physical vulnerability. He came
to treatment wanting, above all, to stop living as if the attack on his brother were still about to
happen, and afraid that if he did not get help soon, the world would continue shrinking around him.
