Mina is a 35-year-old married physical therapist who came to treatment months after leaving a
violent boyfriend she had dated before her marriage, an experience she still speaks about in
fragments. In the relationship, the abuse often came in ordinary domestic moments that turned
suddenly coercive. He would grab her wrist or upper arm hard enough to stop her from walking away,
pin her in place when she tried to end an argument, and force her to stay in the room while he
berated her. What stayed with her most vividly was the feeling of heat on her skin during those
episodes—the warmth of his hand clamped around her, the trapped, flushed sensation in her body when
she could not move, and the unbearable certainty that her own wishes no longer mattered. Even now,
when she tries to tell the story straight through, she loses the thread, skips over crucial parts,
and then goes quiet because the emotion rises so fast she feels she cannot keep going.

In the years since, those memories have not settled into the past. A warm hand placed on her forearm
during a routine interaction, a patient gripping her unexpectedly during therapy, even the specific
sensation of heat against her skin can make the scene come back as if it is happening again. At work
this is particularly difficult, because physical contact is built into the day. When a patient
resists an exercise or clutches at her as she guides a movement, the bodily sense of being overruled
returns immediately. In those moments she is not simply remembering; she feels seized by the old
panic and humiliation, and afterward she often becomes preoccupied with preventing anything similar
from happening again. She scans new patients and family members in the clinic for signs of
volatility, watches closely for people who seem controlling, and mentally rehearses how she could
avoid being cornered. She also finds herself dwelling on the person she was before the abuse,
comparing that version of herself to who she believes she is now.

What frightens Mina almost as much as the memories is what they seem to say about her. When the
episodes come back with such force and she cannot narrate them clearly, she takes that as evidence
that something is wrong with her at a deeper level. She has come to believe the abuse happened
because of the way she acted—that she should have spoken differently, left sooner, resisted better.
The emotional flooding of recall leaves her feeling exposed and ashamed, as though the event
revealed a defective part of her that other people might somehow see. She does not describe the
abuse only as something terrible that happened to her; she describes it as something humiliating
that marked her permanently.

That belief has shaped the way she moves through the world. Because she feels there is something
flawed in her judgment and character, she spends long stretches mentally revisiting the
relationship, trying to identify the exact moment she could have prevented it. She goes over
arguments, gestures, and decisions, convinced that if she could just locate the mistake she made,
she might protect herself from ever being trapped again. She also watches people intensely. In the
clinic, if a patient’s spouse answers for them too quickly or a man stands too close in the
treatment room, Mina’s attention narrows at once. Her vigilance is not abstract; it is driven by the
conviction that she missed warning signs before and must not miss them again.

At the same time, those beliefs make the world feel easier to trigger. Because being physically
overruled has become tied in her mind to personal weakness and humiliation, even small experiences
of lost autonomy can sting with disproportionate force. A supervisor changing her schedule without
much discussion, a patient refusing to follow instructions and grabbing her arm to redirect her, or
even affectionate touch from her husband when she is not expecting it can ignite the old sense that
her boundaries do not count. Warmth on her skin or an uninvited hand is no longer just sensation; it
lands on a meaning she already carries about herself. In turn, the more she scans for dangerous or
controlling people and the more she rehearses how things should have been prevented, the more
quickly she notices touch, heat, and moments of being directed by others. Her world has become
organized around anticipating those sensations before they arrive.

At home, she has become careful in ways her husband experiences as distance. She pulls away from
casual touch unless she has initiated it, becomes tense if he urges her to do something when she is
undecided, and later retreats into long, self-critical brooding about how different she used to be.
What finally brought her to seek help was a recent incident at work when a patient, frustrated and
off balance, grabbed both of her wrists during a transfer. Mina froze, felt the memory surge into
the present, and had to leave the room in tears, unable afterward to explain coherently to her
supervisor what had happened. She comes to treatment afraid that she has been fundamentally damaged
and will always live at the mercy of touch, heat, and coercion, but also hoping that the past might
someday feel like something over rather than something waiting to happen again.
