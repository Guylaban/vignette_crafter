Elaine Morgan, a 60-year-old American politician, traced the start of her problems to a campaign-
season evening two years earlier when she left a tense town hall after a shouting match with
protesters outside the venue. She was driving herself home, her neck already tight from hours of
turning between aides and reporters, when she entered an intersection and struck another car. The
other driver survived but was seriously injured. Elaine remembers fragments rather than a continuous
story: the violent jolt through her neck, the sound of yelling, the sickening sight of the other car
crumpled at an angle. What has stayed with her is not a coherent narrative she can summon at will,
but isolated sensory pieces that still feel immediate.

In the months after the crash, the things that set her off were painfully specific. Raised voices in
legislative meetings, demonstrators shouting outside her office, a stab of neck pain after a long
day at the capitol, even the sight of cars that had been hit and left on the roadside could make her
body react at once. Her chest tightened, her stomach dropped, and she would feel flooded with fear,
shame, and dread before she had even fully registered what had caught her attention. At those
moments she would also find herself pulled into the crash again: she could hear yelling in her head
as if it were happening now, and when the memory broke through she felt pain in her neck in the same
place she had felt it that night.

Those reminders did more than frighten her. Each time they appeared, Elaine was thrown back into the
meanings she had attached to the accident. Seeing damaged cars or hearing shouting did not just
remind her of danger; it revived her certainty that the event happened because of the way she acted,
that she had failed to do more to stop it, and that she had been publicly exposed in a way she could
never live down. She had come to believe that she was permanently humiliated by the accident, not
only as a driver but as a public figure whose mistakes carried consequences for others. The more
intensely she reacted, the more convincing these conclusions felt. A surge of panic or guilt in a
committee hearing could leave her thinking, with renewed force, that her body was proving the truth
of her disgrace.

Her memory of the accident became organized around this same sense of threat and blame. When she
tried deliberately to remember what happened in sequence, she often found she could not reach it.
Yet when she was upset, the memory returned in involuntary shards—sound first, then impact, then
pain. Those fragments seemed to arrive with their own emotional charge, and the physical reactivity
that came with them made the event feel unresolved and present. Because the memories came back this
way, she was all the more certain she had never done enough in the moment and never would forgive
herself. In turn, that self-condemnation seemed to draw the memory closer, as if her mind kept
circling the scene in search of some missed chance to have prevented it.

Over time Elaine began structuring her life around not setting any of this off. She avoided driving
altogether and refused to ride with staff unless absolutely necessary, often rearranging public
appearances or joining by video when others expected her in person. At work she became vigilant
about avoiding anything that might be stressful: contentious meetings, loud rallies, difficult press
scrums. She also narrowed her life more broadly, keeping away from anything likely to stir strong
feeling, pleasant or unpleasant, because even excitement felt risky if it made her body rev up. That
emotional constriction made her days flatter and more rigid, but to Elaine it seemed necessary. She
believed that if she let herself feel too much, or entered another high-pressure situation, she
would be overwhelmed, exposed, and forced back into what she had done.

These strategies brought short-lived relief, but they tightened the problem. The less she drove or
rode in cars, the more alien and charged vehicles became; passing a collision scene from the window
of a staff SUV could be enough to send her into a spiral of neck pain, auditory recall, and guilt.
Avoiding arguments and public events made shouting seem even more intolerable when it did occur, so
a raised voice in a caucus room now hit her with disproportionate force. Because she kept turning
away from difficult situations and from her own feelings, she never developed a fuller, steadier
account of the accident; instead the memory remained patchy, intrusive, and bodily. That patchiness
fed her self-blame—if she could not fully remember, she assumed she must have failed in some
unforgivable way—and the shame attached to that belief made her retreat further.

By the time she sought treatment, the consequences were visible in both her work and private life.
Staff had noticed that she lost focus in briefings whenever tempers rose, and she had begun
declining in-person appearances that once defined her career. Divorced and living alone, she spent
more evenings at home, carefully managing her environment to avoid distress. What finally brought
her in was a recent incident in which protesters shouted outside a municipal event; Elaine froze,
felt searing pain in her neck, heard the crash-era yelling in her head, and could not concentrate
enough to deliver her remarks. She came to treatment frightened that her world was shrinking around
the accident and equally frightened that if she stopped avoiding it, she would be swallowed by guilt
and humiliation she already felt she deserved.
