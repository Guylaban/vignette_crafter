Miguel Alvarez is a 55-year-old Navy officer who sought help only after months of finding that the
same few moments from a deployment kept breaking into the present with a force he could no longer
contain. Years earlier, while moving through a civilian area in a war zone, he had been part of a
convoy that came under sudden attack near a crowded street. What has remained with him is not the
broader operation but a sharply bounded scene: civilians running, the sound of screaming mixing with
gunfire, dust and smoke thick enough to sting his eyes and coat his mouth, and a family caught in
the open while he was pinned down and unable to reach them. In his account, the worst part is not
uncertainty about what happened but his conviction that he saw people who needed protection and did
not get to them in time. He returns again and again to the same conclusion: he should have protected
them, and he failed.

In the first years after the event, Miguel told himself to stay disciplined and carry on. Outwardly
he did. He remained in service, kept his routines, and said little at home to his partner about that
day. But privately he had come to believe that his reaction to the event revealed something damning
about him. He was certain that a stronger man would have acted differently, moved faster, done more,
saved someone. The harshness of that judgment shaped the way the memory came back to him. When he
remembered the attack, what surged up first was often not only the scene itself but an immediate
wave of guilt so strong it felt like accusation. Even in quieter moments, when he tried to think
through what had happened, his mind circled back to his own supposed weakness and to the thought
that he would never forgive himself for not doing more.

At other times the memory did not arrive as reflection at all. It took him over. A backfiring engine
on base, the metallic rattle of equipment, or the sight of civilians clustered near a checkpoint
during port security duties could pull him abruptly into the past. In those moments the event felt
completely real across all his senses at once: the crack of shots, the grit in the air, the pressure
in his chest, the visual flash of people falling and scrambling. He did not experience these as
ordinary recollections. For a few seconds, he was there again. And because the memory returned with
that level of force and immediacy, it seemed to him like proof rather than symptom. Each reliving
ended in the same moral reckoning: if this was the scene his mind kept bringing back, then it must
be because he had truly failed in the most important duty he had, to protect others when it
mattered.

There were also moments that disturbed him for a different reason. Sometimes he could recount the
sequence of events in a flat, detached way, as if he were describing something that had happened to
another officer. He would notice that he could say the facts while feeling almost nothing. Rather
than relieving him, that numbness fed the same brutal interpretation of himself. He took the
emotional deadness as another sign of defectiveness, telling himself that either he was too weak in
the moment of danger or too hollow now to deserve forgiveness. The memory, whether vividly alive or
strangely empty, kept delivering him back to the same fixed beliefs.

That loop has increasingly shaped his daily life in the Navy. During routine duties he finds himself
scanning ordinary sounds and scenes for anything that resembles that street, and when something
does, he braces for the memory he believes he deserves to endure. On days when he is pulled back
into the attack, he becomes quieter and more remote with his partner that evening, unable to explain
why a noise at work has left him feeling like a coward all over again. He has not expanded the story
over time; if anything, it has narrowed. The event is now organized in his mind around the instant
he did not reach those civilians, and every return to that instant deepens his certainty that his
inaction defines him.

By the time he came to treatment, Miguel was exhausted by the sense that the past was not staying
past. He said what frightened him most was not only that the memory still arrived as if it were
happening now, but that each time it did, it confirmed the kind of man he believed himself to be. He
wants help because he can no longer keep these moments contained inside a career built on composure,
and because some part of him still hopes there may be a way to remember what happened without being
sentenced by it every time.
