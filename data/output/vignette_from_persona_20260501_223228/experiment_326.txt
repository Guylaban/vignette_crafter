identity had once given him a clear place in the world. Before that, he had worked as a relief
worker in a war zone, attached to teams pulling civilians from damaged buildings after shelling. The
event he returns to most often is not one clean scene but a shattered sequence around an explosion
at a crowded shelter. He remembers a sudden bang, the pressure in his body, pain striking through
his side and legs, dust, shouting, and then fragments he cannot reliably place in order: a child
crying, a doorway blocked, a woman begging, men pushing past others to get out first, someone making
a crude remark about what had happened to women in another district. When he tries to tell it, the
words fail him at the worst points. He says he knows what happened, but “it won’t line up,” and
often stops speaking altogether, rubbing at the places in his body that hurt again when the memory
comes.

force. At the station, if equipment drops with a sharp bang or a call comes in over radio chatter
followed by a sudden blast from a training video, he is no longer just in the apparatus bay; the
scene returns in broken pieces, and with it the same bodily pain. Comments about sexual violence,
even overheard casually in the locker room or in news coverage playing in the kitchen, bring back
the shelter and what he believes he failed to stop. People misbehaving in small selfish ways—drivers
ignoring emergency lanes, bystanders filming instead of helping, younger firefighters laughing off
suffering—hit him harder than he can explain. In those moments he is flooded not only with the
memory but with the conviction that he crossed lines he can never uncross, that under pressure he
acted against the man he believed himself to be.

What has taken hold most deeply is the meaning Minh gives those memories. He has come to believe his
life was destroyed there, not in the dramatic sense of physical ruin but in the quieter sense that
nothing since has fit. Divorced and increasingly detached at the station, he feels he belongs
nowhere: not with civilians who have not seen what he saw, not with the men he served beside
overseas, and not fully with his firefighting crew, who know him as competent but distant. The more
the memory breaks apart when he tries to recount it, the more certain he becomes that this confusion
reflects something rotten in him—that he did something unforgivable and cannot even speak it
plainly. Those conclusions make his triggers feel even more charged. A bang is not only a sound; it
is proof that the past is waiting. Silence is not rest; in a room gone suddenly quiet, he becomes
certain danger is about to break through.

think about the war zone at all, cutting off conversations, changing channels, forcing his mind
blank during the commute. He performs private mental rituals—repeating phrases, counting in set
patterns, silently reviewing who is on shift—in the belief that if he does them correctly, something
terrible may be prevented. When the pressure rises too high, especially after a vivid return of the
shelter or an evening spent stewing over what kind of man he was there, he has hurt himself
physically to release the surge inside. He describes this without drama, as if it were grim
maintenance.

Yet these efforts have tightened the problem around him. The harder Minh suppresses the memories,
the more they return jagged and unsorted, with the same bodily pain and speechless panic. The

that unusual silence on a call, an offhand cruel joke, or a distant bang can ignite fear, anger,
guilt, and shame all at once. His irritability has become harder to contain. At work he snaps at
probies for minor mistakes and has shoved a locker door so hard a captain pulled him aside.
Afterward he sees this anger as further evidence that he is no longer who he was, which only deepens
his sense that he has been ruined.

pain; the memory came back in disordered flashes; then he erupted at a coworker with a ferocity that
frightened everyone, including himself. Later, alone, he was overwhelmed by the thought that he
