Nina is a 19-year-old Southeast Asian American woman who has been working as a hairdresser since
shortly after finishing school. She came to treatment months after a car accident in which she was
driving a friend home at night and misjudged a turn at an intersection. Another car struck the
passenger side. Her friend survived but was seriously injured. Nina remembers the sound first: the
violent crack of impact and the burst of horns and shouting afterward. Even now, what comes back to
her is less a story than a set of images—the headlights cutting across her windshield, twisted
metal, her friend slumped beside her. At times she can describe the scene in a flat, oddly detached
way, as if she is talking about something that happened to someone else; at other times the same
images bring an immediate wave of grief so strong that she stops speaking and cries for the friend
she believes she should have protected.

Since the accident, ordinary moments have taken on the force of reminders. At the salon, when a
metal cart slams shut or a hair dryer drops with a bang, Nina feels her whole body jolt before she
can think. In those moments she is flooded by the picture of cars colliding, and her chest tightens,
her hands shake, and she has to steady herself against the counter. She has stopped driving
altogether and will not ride with friends unless there is no way out of it. She also keeps track of
the date of the crash and avoids anything that might mark it; as that day approaches she becomes
more withdrawn and insists on working extra shifts rather than being with people who might mention
it.

These reminders do not stay confined to the accident itself. A loud noise or the sight of damaged
cars on the road quickly becomes, in her mind, evidence that she is still not safe and cannot let
herself relax, even in places she knows are secure. She scans for exits as soon as she enters the
salon, restaurants, or any building, locating the fastest way out before she can settle. What
frightens her most is how quickly her body reacts: she has come to believe she can no longer trust
it, because it surges into panic and trembling without warning. The same reactions leave her
convinced that something in her has changed for the worse, that if danger came again she would
freeze or fail someone again.

Nina’s view of herself hardened around the idea that she failed in the one thing that mattered. She
was the driver; her friend was hurt; to Nina that means she should have prevented it. When customers
raise their voices or seem impatient, she becomes overly agreeable at once, apologizing too much,
changing her tone, and trying to please them before there is any conflict. She describes this not as
kindness but as a way of staying ahead of danger. Because she feels she cannot rely on other people
to keep her safe or understand what she carries, she does not ask for rides unless absolutely
necessary and avoids conversations that might bring up the accident. Her beliefs about having failed
and about her own body being unreliable also make the crash feel close at hand: she notices every
screech of brakes outside the salon and every sudden bang in a way she never did before.

Over time, the things Nina does to get through the day have narrowed her life and tightened the
problem. Avoiding driving means she spends much of her week arranging rides around her boyfriend’s
schedule, yet even as a passenger she watches the road tensely, bracing at intersections and staring
at nearby cars as if impact is about to happen again. At work, her habit of scanning doors and
listening for abrupt sounds leaves her keyed up through entire shifts, and by the end of the day the
accident returns in sharp visual fragments. Because she tries not to think about the anniversary and
gives people as little room as possible to upset her, the memories seem to arrive more abruptly when
they break through—brief, vivid pictures followed by either numb retelling or crushing grief. Each
time that happens, she feels freshly certain that she failed her friend and cannot count on herself,
which makes her more watchful and more avoidant the next day.

What finally brought Nina to seek help was a recent incident in the salon when a tray crashed to the
floor behind her. She spun, shaking and tearful, then had to hide in the break room because all she
could see was the intersection and the passenger seat. She was humiliated that coworkers saw it, but
more than that, she was terrified by how trapped she feels inside this pattern. She says she wants
to be able to ride in a car without bracing for impact and to stop feeling that one moment has
defined her as someone who failed, though part of her is afraid that if she loosens her guard,
another person will get hurt.
