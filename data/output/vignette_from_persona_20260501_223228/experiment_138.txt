visiting friends when another car crossed the median and hit him head-on. Linh did not witness the
crash itself, but the way she learned about it remained seared into her: the phone ringing before
dawn, the flat, official voice asking if she was his next of kin because their parents were
overseas, the long fluorescent hallway at the hospital where no one would meet her eyes for more
than a second. By the time she arrived, he was already gone. What she remembered most clearly was
the abruptness of it, the way an ordinary night had split into a before and after in a matter of
minutes. Since then, safety had stopped feeling believable to her.

In the first weeks after the funeral, Linh told herself she needed to keep moving. She returned to
her job as a journalist sooner than her editor expected, partly because deadlines gave shape to the
day and partly because being alone in her apartment felt intolerable. Yet even in familiar settings
she could not settle. In the newsroom, when the police scanner crackled from a colleague’s desk or a
phone rang sharply behind her, her shoulders would tense and stay that way for hours. She found
herself scanning faces, listening for changes in tone, noticing every siren outside the building and
every vibration from her own phone on the table, bracing before she even knew what she was bracing
for. She would look around and know, intellectually, that she was in a secure office in the middle
of the afternoon, surrounded by coworkers discussing copy edits and city council meetings, but her
body would not accept that she was safe enough to relax.

Over the following months, this constant alertness spread into the rest of her life. At home she
kept the television on low volume at night, not to watch it but to break the silence, because
silence made every creak in the building stand out. If a family member texted later than usual, she
would check her phone repeatedly until she heard back. She began sleeping lightly, waking to sounds
she once would not have registered at all: tires on wet pavement outside, a neighbor’s footsteps in
the hallway, the click of the refrigerator turning on. She described feeling as if some part of her
was always standing guard, certain that if she let it down for even a moment, another call would
come and everything would be shattered again.

The emotional tone of her days changed just as dramatically. Fear was the easiest feeling to name,
but it was threaded with anger and guilt in ways she found harder to explain. She was angry at the
driver, angry at the randomness of it, angry at herself for not having called her brother that
evening, as if one small change might have interrupted the chain of events. She also felt ashamed of
how little patience she now had with ordinary complaints from other people. When friends talked
about trivial work frustrations, she would feel a flash of bitterness and then shame for having it.
Some nights she sat on the floor of her apartment and cried with a kind of exhausted horror at how
quickly a life could vanish, how no amount of intelligence or planning could protect the people she
loved.

Her work environment kept confronting her with the same sense of danger. As a journalist, she was
expected to monitor breaking news, and every alert about a highway collision or sudden death hit
with unusual force. She did not need to be assigned those stories for them to affect her; even
seeing a headline open on a colleague’s screen could leave her heart racing. She started positioning
herself at the edge of editorial meetings so she could step out if her chest tightened. Coworkers
noticed that she startled easily and seemed perpetually keyed up. One editor, initially impressed by
her diligence, later commented that Linh looked as if she had not exhaled in months.

By the time she sought help, nearly a year had passed. Her marriage had already ended before her
brother’s death, and living alone now seemed to magnify everything. She came to treatment because
she was tired of feeling hunted by ordinary life, tired of never being off duty inside her own mind.
What frightened her most was not only the intensity of her fear but the possibility that this was
the only sensible way to live now—that vigilance was the price of loving anyone, and that if she
ever truly relaxed, she would be caught unprepared for the next devastating loss. At the same time,
she was beginning to hope, cautiously, that she might not always have to feel this watchful and raw,
that remembering her brother might someday come without her whole body acting as if disaster were
already at the door.
