Anjali Patel is a 42-year-old married woman who came to treatment months after a period of stalking
that began on her route between housekeeping jobs and spread into the spaces where she should have
felt safest. She works as a maid in private homes, moving quietly through other people’s kitchens
and bedrooms, and it was after one of those jobs that she first became aware of the same man
lingering near the apartment complex entrance. At first he stared too long, in a way that made her
feel stripped down and assessed, and later she began noticing the smell of alcohol on him when he
stepped too close to her on the sidewalk and once outside her building. During one encounter he
grabbed her hard enough to leave pain that lingered in her arm and shoulder. What stays with her
most is not a clean sequence but fragments: the sour taste that rose in her mouth, the alcohol on
his breath, the humiliating certainty that he was looking at her as if she were there for his use.

In the weeks that followed, Anjali did not develop a settled account of what had happened. When she
tried to tell her husband, she would get partway into it and then push the memory away before it
fully formed, as though letting it continue would make the whole scene happen again. She says she
cannot tell a clear story even to herself. Instead, the memory returns in pieces, sometimes with
startling sensory force, especially the taste in her mouth from that moment, so immediate that she
stops what she is doing and presses her tongue to her teeth as if she could get rid of it.

Those fragments are easily set off in her daily work. If a male employer or a man in a hallway lets
his gaze linger in a sexual way, her body reacts before she can think; if she smells alcohol on a
resident’s guest or passes a trash area with beer bottles, the scene surges back in flashes. Even
physical pain has become loaded. An ache in the same arm after carrying laundry or scrubbing a
bathtub can pull her straight back into the encounter. In those moments she becomes certain danger
is close. At home she checks the door and windows repeatedly, sometimes returning to the lock
several times after her husband has already gone to bed, and she remains on guard even in the
apartment. Silence frightens her now; if the hallway outside goes suddenly quiet, she feels
something bad is about to happen.

That constant vigilance has, in turn, made the memory harder to leave in the past. Because she is
listening for footsteps and scanning the peephole, ordinary pauses in sound or the creak of the
building sharpen her sense that he could reappear, and the more on edge she feels, the more the
unfinished fragments of the stalking press forward. A quiet room can fill with the remembered taste
and the sense of his closeness. She has come to dread the very conditions that remind her of how
alert she must stay, so that being watchful does not calm her; it keeps the experience active and
near.

Anjali has tried to manage this in ways that make immediate sense to her. She avoids friends, partly
because casual questions about work or the neighborhood might bring the memory up before she can
stop it. Before almost any interaction, even speaking to a client at the door, she rehearses what
she will say, trying to prevent surprises and keep herself from sounding frightened. She worries
obsessively about bad things happening to people she cares about, especially if her husband is late
coming home, because in her mind danger appears suddenly and without warning. Yet these habits have
narrowed her life. The rehearsing makes every glance and smell more noticeable, and staying away
from friends leaves her alone with the fragments she is trying not to feel. The harder she tries to
push the memory away or organize herself around not being caught off guard, the more quickly a
sexualized look, the smell of alcohol, or pain in her arm seems to strike with full force, and the
more she circles back to the locks and to listening into the silence.

By the time she sought help, her world had become small and exhausting. She was taking longer in
clients’ homes because she kept stopping at windows and entryways, and she had begun turning down
some jobs if she thought she might have to pass men gathered outside buildings. She came in after a
night when an unusual hush in the hallway sent her checking the front door over and over, then left
her shaking with the taste of the event in her mouth. What frightens her most is that she is no
longer sure whether she is remembering the past or sensing that it is about to happen again. What
she hopes, quietly, is to be able to come home, lock the door once, and believe for even a few
minutes that she and her family are safe.
