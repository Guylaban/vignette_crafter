“fine” and then finding himself increasingly unable to move through ordinary parts of the day
without a jolt of dread and shame. Before coming to the United States, he had lived as a civilian in

but a cluster of moments: the crack and boom of incoming fire, the shouted names of neighbors as
people searched through dust and confusion, and the heavy smell of churned soil and concrete after
blasts tore open streets and courtyards. He remembers standing outside after one attack, dirt thrown
up into the air, hearing someone call a familiar name again and again with no answer. Even now, when
he talks about that period, he does so flatly, as if the feeling has drained out of the memory while
the details remain painfully sharp.

In the years since, Omar has built a life around dance, teaching and rehearsing in a studio where
music, bodies, and repetition usually give him structure. Yet the very texture of daily life keeps
catching on what happened. A sudden percussive sound in rehearsal, a bass note that lands like an
explosion, or construction noise from the street can make his body go rigid. If he passes a park
after rain or steps near a planter outside the studio and catches the smell of wet earth, he is
abruptly back in the aftermath of bombardment, with the ground itself feeling dangerous. Hearing
certain Arabic names—especially when called loudly across a room—hits him in a similarly private,
destabilizing way, as though the present moment has been pierced by voices from those chaotic
searches after an attack.

These moments do not end with surprise. They quickly harden into the beliefs that now organize much
of his inner life. When a sound or smell catches him off guard, Omar becomes certain that whatever
happened to him is still visible on him, that other people in the studio can see it in his face and
posture before he has said a word. He will look at his students and fellow dancers and think they
must notice something broken, some mark that gives him away. In those same moments he feels abruptly
hollowed out, “dead inside,” as he has put it, disturbed by how little warmth or comfort he can find
even when the danger has passed. The triggers also pull him toward a bleak conviction that the world
is fundamentally stripped of goodness—that people did not protect one another, that life can be
shattered at any second, and that whatever faith he once had in people or in a higher order has been
worn away beyond repair.

the studio. During rehearsal, if a loud sound from the street breaks through the music, he may lose
the thread of the choreography and retreat into mechanical correction, speaking to dancers in
clipped, overcontrolled ways. If someone calls out a name that reminds him of the war, he can no
longer stay inside the creative moment; instead he becomes preoccupied with how exposed he seems,
convinced the room can read his history on him. The smell of dirt from potted plants in the lobby or
from the sidewalk after rain can leave him emotionally blunted for the rest of the session, going
through the motions while feeling absent from himself. Each episode seems to confirm what he has
come to believe: that something essential in him has gone numb and that trust—in people, in life, in
anything beyond bare survival—was lost there and never came back.

much as emptied out. He sits with her in silence, aware that she wants closeness, but privately
convinced there is nothing alive in him to offer. When a name on television or in conversation
echoes one from that time, he withdraws further, watching her face for signs that she can see where
he has gone. He knows this fear is difficult to explain without sounding irrational, yet to him it
feels obvious: trauma is not hidden, it radiates outward, and others must be able to detect it.

What finally brought Omar to seek help was not a single crisis but the accumulating humiliation of
feeling split off from his work and marriage. After freezing during a rehearsal when construction
noise erupted outside and then spending the evening convinced his dancers had seen him unravel, he
admitted to his wife that he was frightened by how dead he felt inside and by how completely he had
lost faith that people, life, or God could hold anything safely. He comes to treatment wanting
relief from the constant sense of exposure, but what he is most afraid of is that the emptiness is
permanent and that the war has already told the deepest truth about the world.
