Nadia is a 55-year-old HVAC technician who came to treatment after months of telling herself she
could manage if she stayed alert enough. She was raised in the Middle East and fled during a period
of war and displacement after a chaotic crossing in which she was moved with others through
floodwater at night, pressed into a cramped transport and then hidden for hours in a wet, silent
building while they waited to be moved again. What stays with her are not broad summaries but sharp
fragments: cold water rising around her legs, the slick roughness of a soaked concrete wall under
her palm, the unbearable quiet when everyone understood they had to make no sound. Even now, when
she tries to tell the story straight through, it feels unreal to her, “like it happened to somebody
else,” and yet the moment she gets close to the worst parts she is hit with shame and so much
feeling that she has to stop speaking.

In the years since resettling in the United States, those fragments have remained strangely alive.
Water is especially potent. A flooded curb after a storm, a retention pond beside a commercial
building, even standing water in a mechanical room can pull the memory up with a force that
surprises her. The same happens with certain textures—wet concrete, slimy painted block, damp grit
under her hand. Uncertainty is just as dangerous in a different way: if she does not know exactly
what is behind a door, who else is in a building, or how long she will be somewhere, the old memory
starts pressing forward. Those moments do not feel like ordinary remembering. The story becomes both
vivid and impossible, and she can suddenly feel overwhelmed and ashamed, as though she is back
inside something she still cannot fully believe she survived.

Nadia works alone much of the day, driving between apartment complexes and commercial sites,
climbing into basements, utility corridors, and rooftop access rooms. Before entering anywhere
unfamiliar, she studies where the exits are and keeps a path in mind in case she needs to leave
quickly. Once inside, she checks corners, doorways, windows, and the movements of other people
almost automatically. She tells herself this is just common sense, but it is also her way of keeping
the memory from taking over. If she knows the escape route and has already scanned the surroundings,
she feels she might be able to control what comes up inside her. At the same time, the habit keeps
her attention fixed on danger; silence in an empty hallway or mechanical room becomes charged, and
the more she watches for threat, the easier it is for a glance at pooled water or the feel of a damp
wall to hit her like a trigger.

Her mind returns often to what others did to her during that period—those who forced movement at the
wrong time, those who abandoned people, those who made decisions while others paid the price. She
dwells on this for hours, especially at night, circling the same grievances and betrayals. The
rumination seems to start as an effort to make sense of the past, but it leaves the memory raw and
close to the surface. Afterward she is more likely to startle at silence, more certain that danger
is near, and more vulnerable to slipping into flashback states in which she feels for a few seconds
as if she is back in that wet, airless waiting place. Those episodes frighten her most. She can be
at work holding a flashlight in a utility room and suddenly feel the past drop over the present;
afterward she doubles down on planning her exits and scanning every soundless space she enters.

Over time, this has narrowed her life. She avoids certain service calls when she can, especially
sites with basement flooding or large water tanks, and she lingers near doors rather than focusing
fully on repairs. Coworkers have noticed that she prefers not to be assigned to enclosed lower-level
jobs. She is divorced and lives alone, and at home the apartment can become difficult when it is too
quiet; unusual silence makes her feel that something bad is about to happen, and that sense of
danger brings the memory surging back again. She has come to believe that if she stops monitoring,
she will be caught off guard and overwhelmed, yet the constant vigilance keeps her keyed up and
leaves the past close enough to break through.

What finally brought Nadia in was a recent call to a partially flooded building after heavy rain.
She touched a wet wall while looking for the unit, froze, and for a moment acted as if she were back
in the crossing, unable to think beyond getting out. She was shaken by how quickly it happened and
ashamed that she had to leave the job for another technician to finish. She is seeking help now
because she is afraid that the past is becoming stronger than the life she has built, and because a
part of her still hopes she might be able to walk into a quiet room, do her work, and feel that she
is in the present rather than waiting for the worst to happen again.
