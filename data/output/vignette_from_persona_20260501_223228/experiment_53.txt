certainty that no one was coming. She had appealed for help and protection and had come away
convinced that the people and systems that should have intervened had failed her completely. Even
now, when she spoke about it, her face tightened with anger and shame together, as if she were still
trying to understand how she could have been left there.

brought back the same bodily alarm she had felt in captivity, and she said she could not make
herself believe that nighttime was harmless just because she was home now. Underneath that fear was
a painful set of conclusions she had drawn about herself and the world: if institutions had failed
her once, they could fail her again; if the experience had left her feeling permanently dirty, then
nowhere outside a very restricted zone could feel fully safe; and if she was already so shaken by
small upsets, then she was not someone who could reliably manage danger. Those beliefs gave the fear
a constant, convincing logic.

That sense of ongoing danger began shaping her days. At work, if colleagues suggested an evening

a routine, though privately she was intent on being home before dark. She often stayed late in the
office not because she felt comfortable there after hours, but because transitioning through the
parking garage and arriving home at night felt unbearable; yet once home she checked the locks
repeatedly and settled only into a single chair in the living room that she thought of as her “safe
spot.” Her emotional life also constricted. Fear was the most obvious feeling, but there was also
anger at having been abandoned, shame when she remembered how exposed she had felt, and guilt
whenever she imagined the different life she might have had if the captivity had never happened.

household tasks until she felt mentally dulled. She also stayed up very late, sometimes until two or
three in the morning, because going to bed meant lying still in the dark with too much room for
memory and fear. And when sleep would not come, she drifted into relentless “if only” thoughts—if
only she had not been there, if only someone had stepped in, if only her life had taken a different
path. These strategies offered brief relief from the nightly sense of threat, but they left her more
exhausted, brittle, and emotionally flooded the next day.

apart. She took this as proof that she could not cope with even the slightest upset. The harder she
pushed the trauma out of mind, the more alien and defiling it seemed when it broke through; she
would feel suddenly contaminated by what had happened, as though the event had marked her in a way
she could never fully wash off. Those appraisals made the world feel more dangerous again,
especially at night, and reinforced her conviction that safety existed only in narrow, carefully
managed spaces.

as though she was living inside a bunker. What finally brought her to seek help was not one dramatic
crisis but the recognition that her life had become organized around avoiding fear and still never
feeling safe. She said she was afraid that if she kept living this way, her world would shrink to a
chair, a locked door, and sleepless nights. At the same time, she allowed herself a small hope: that
she might someday be able to go to bed without dread and stop feeling that what was done to her was
still happening inside her.
