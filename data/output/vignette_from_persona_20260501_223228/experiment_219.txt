At 78, Nabil Haddad had spent most of his life as a careful man, the kind of environmental scientist
who checked field protocols twice and prided himself on staying calm under pressure. He still worked
part-time, consulting on site safety and water testing, and until the accident he had taken quiet
satisfaction in being useful. The event he returns to happened during a routine field visit with a
younger technician. They were moving equipment near a steep, uneven embankment by a monitoring site,
talking over the noise of wind and machinery. Nabil has struggled to put the moment into words in
session. He says he knows exactly what happened, yet when he tries to tell it, his mouth seems to
stop around the worst part. What can be gathered is that he gave a direction he believed was safe,
the other man shifted position with the gear, lost footing, and fell badly. Nabil remembers the
awful immediacy of the aftermath more vividly than any coherent sequence: the man on the ground, the
rasping, heavy breathing, his own body turning cold as he realized that his instruction had played a
part.

In the weeks that followed, those sensory fragments stayed sharper than any narrative. When he did
remember, it was not like recalling something completed and in the past. He would suddenly feel as
though he were back at the site with the injured man in front of him, the scene pressing in on him
with a force that made ordinary surroundings fall away. At other times he would say the whole thing
felt unreal, too extreme to have happened in his own life, as if he were speaking about another
person’s disaster. His wife would ask him to tell her what was coming back to him, and he would sit
with his hands clenched, embarrassed and frustrated, saying he knew but could not find the words.

What has become most striking is how easily specific moments in daily life pull him into that state.
Heavy breathing is the strongest. If he hears someone short of breath in the hallway at work, or a
colleague comes in from climbing the stairs breathing hard outside his office, Nabil feels himself
jolt internally before he has time to think. In the same instant he is no longer simply an older man
at his desk reviewing environmental reports; he is back beside the embankment, with the injured
technician’s breathing in his ears, and the memory arrives with the force of something happening
now. He has had similar episodes in less obvious situations. When his grandchildren are laughing and
he notices himself beginning to feel relaxed, or when something in the house feels especially
peaceful, that very sense of happiness can frighten him. He has come to associate letting his guard
down with the ordinary, harmless mood that existed just before the accident. A good moment can
abruptly tip into reliving, as though feeling calm were what allows the scene to break through.

Physical affection has become difficult in much the same way. His wife, Samira, describes him
stiffening when she hugs him, and Nabil says the closeness can instantly transport him to the bodily
urgency of that day—the pressure, the helplessness, the awareness of another person’s vulnerable
body in his hands. Within seconds, the memory becomes present-tense and fragmented again, more
sensation than story, and he pulls away not because he does not love her but because he is
overtaken. He feels ashamed afterward, especially given how long they have been married, but shame
has not made the reaction any less immediate.

These episodes have narrowed his life. At work he keeps his office door partly closed and avoids
accompanying younger staff into the field unless absolutely necessary. He prefers email to
conversation and is uneasy when anyone enters his space flushed or winded from outside. At home he
has become watchful during family gatherings, wary of warmth, laughter, and embraces because he no
longer trusts pleasant moments to stay pleasant. The more often he is caught off guard by a sound or
by his own brief happiness and thrust back into the accident, the less confidence he has that the
memory will ever settle into something he can simply remember and describe.

He sought help only after Samira told him that she felt she was losing him in small, repeated ways.
Nabil agreed, though even in the intake he faltered trying to explain why he had come. What
frightened him most was not only that the accident still felt as if it were happening, but that at
his age he could no longer rely on his own mind to hold it in words instead of in these overwhelming
returns. What he hopes for, cautiously, is to be able to sit with his wife, hear ordinary sounds,
and feel a moment of peace without being taken back to the day he believes he changed another man’s
life.
