Harjit Singh is a 64-year-old warehouse worker who came to treatment after months of sleeping only
in fragments and feeling, as he put it, “finished.” He is a married South Asian American man who had
managed a steady, physically demanding routine for years, but since being held captive during a
robbery at his warehouse, his days have become organized around getting through the evening and his
nights around trying not to be overtaken by fear. The assault happened near the end of a late shift,
when most of the loading bays were quiet and the light outside had already gone. Two men forced him
into a back storage area, threatened him, and kept him shut in for hours. What stayed with him most
vividly was the darkness, the sense of being trapped and unable to predict what would happen next,
and the moment he noticed movement near some pallets and became convinced there were snakes in the
room, whether real or imagined. He remembers staring into corners and at the floor, straining to see
in the dim light, waiting for something else to strike.

In the weeks afterward, certain moments began to grip him with a force he did not expect. As the
same time of day approached when the captivity had begun, he would become tense and restless at
work, glancing at the clock and feeling his body brace. In the warehouse, where late afternoon
shadows stretch between shelving and loading docks, talk from coworkers about pests or a photo of a
snake on someone’s phone could jolt him immediately back into that storage room. Night was worst.
Waking in the dark or even anticipating bedtime made his chest tighten because darkness no longer
felt neutral; it felt like the place where he had once been trapped and undefended. In those moments
he responded by trying not to sleep at all, staying up in the living room with lights on until
exhaustion forced him to lie down, asking his wife again and again to tell him he was safe and that
nothing was wrong, and repeatedly checking his body for signs that something had happened to him
without his noticing.

Those same triggers also keep him in a state of constant danger readiness. Near dusk he becomes
intensely watchful, and once the sun goes down he says he cannot feel safe in the same way other
people seem to. If he wakes in darkness, he sits upright listening, scanning the room, and when he
walks through the parking lot after shift he repeatedly looks behind him to make sure no one is
following. A passing mention of snakes or a picture of one is enough to make him sweep his eyes
across the ground and corners automatically. His wife has noticed that even at home he checks locks,
peers down the hallway, and startles if she enters a room unexpectedly, as though some part of him
is still in that enclosed space waiting for the next threat.

Harjit has come to believe that what happened proved something terrible about him and the world. He
is certain that he cannot stop bad things from happening to him; the captivity is no longer just a
memory of being overpowered once, but evidence, to him, that safety can disappear without warning
and that he will be helpless when it does. That belief sharpens his vigilance, especially at night.
Because he sees danger as both unpredictable and unstoppable, he feels he has to stay superalert, to
watch the dark, to check behind him, to remain on guard in ways that leave his body continually
braced. He also avoids letting himself think directly about the event because he fears that if he
really lets it into his mind, he will not be able to handle it.

Over time, the ways he has tried to manage have deepened these beliefs rather than easing them. The
more he avoids sleep and lies awake with the lights on, the more the night itself comes to feel
unlivable and overpowering, as if even bed is a place where he has no control. His repeated requests
for reassurance bring only a few minutes of relief before the doubt returns, and each time it does
he feels more defeated by how little comfort lasts. Checking his body again and again leaves him
focused on vulnerability, searching for proof of harm and never feeling certain he is intact. He has
begun to say, quietly, that he is not sure life is worth living anymore if this is what every
evening will become.

Those habits have also made the triggers spread and harden. Because he delays sleep and remains
awake into the darkest hours, he has more time to notice every creak, every shadow, every moment of
waking in the night, so darkness itself has become increasingly charged. Because he keeps scanning
his body and surroundings, he is quicker to notice sensations, sounds, and images that remind him of
captivity. At work this has started to cost him. He lingers to avoid crossing the dim lot alone,
loses focus near the end of shift when the light changes, and has become preoccupied whenever
coworkers joke or talk about snakes. His wife, meanwhile, is exhausted by his nightly need for
reassurance and frightened by how hopeless he sounds.

He finally sought help after a recent night when he woke in the dark, searched his body for
injuries, checked the house repeatedly, and then sat awake until dawn convinced he would never feel
safe again. What frightened him most was not just the fear itself, but the sense that his world had
narrowed to avoiding night and enduring it. What he wants, though he says it cautiously, is to be
able to come home from work, go to bed beside his wife, and believe that darkness is only darkness.
