Maliha is a 52-year-old Southeast Asian American woman who has worked the evening shift at a fast
food restaurant for years, steady and reliable in a routine she once found comforting. She is
married, and before the disaster she had taken pride in being the calm one in her family, the person
who kept going when others were upset. That changed after the hurricane that tore through her
neighborhood the previous year. She remembers the night in pieces rather than as a whole: the
pressure in the air before landfall, the sudden cracking sound as part of the roof gave way, water
pushing under the door, her husband shouting from another room, and the darkness after the power
failed. What remains most vivid is not a full story but jagged fragments that intrude without
warning—the sound of pounding wind, the slam of debris against the house, the sensation that there
was nowhere safe to stand. When those fragments come back, she feels flooded so quickly that she has
to stop what she is doing and brace herself until the feeling passes.

In the weeks after the storm, Maliha had come to believe that what happened said something permanent
about her life rather than about one terrible night. She was certain that bad things could come at
any time and that she had no real way to stop them. She also felt estranged from the world around
her, as if other people had resumed ordinary life while she had been left outside it. Even at home
with her husband, she often felt detached, telling herself that she would never again feel normal
warmth or ease. Those beliefs seemed to open the door to the memory rather than settle it. Whenever
she caught herself thinking that nowhere was truly safe and that she could not protect herself, the
storm would force its way back in disconnected flashes, as if her mind were proving the point to
her.

Her nights became the hardest part of the day. Darkness now feels dangerous to her in a way daylight
does not. She lies down tired but cannot settle, listening for changes in the house and tensing at
every unexpected sound from outside. A slammed car door, a neighbor’s trash bin tipping, a sudden
gust against the siding can jolt her upright before she has time to think. Underneath that vigilance
is a conviction that she is still vulnerable, that if something starts again she will be helpless to
stop it. Because she has come to see herself as someone to whom catastrophe can simply happen, she
scans constantly for signs of danger, especially after sunset, and sleep has become broken and thin.

The memories and the threat feeling now feed each other so tightly that she rarely experiences one
without the other close behind. At work, when the ice machine drops a load loudly or someone bangs a
metal tray in the kitchen, her body reacts before she can remind herself where she is. She goes
rigid, heart racing, and in that instant the storm comes back in shards—the roof cracking, objects
hitting the house, her husband yelling in the dark. The more the memory bursts in this way, the more
certain she becomes that she has to stay on guard for the next sudden sound. By the end of her shift
she is exhausted from listening and watching, yet when she gets home she still cannot let go of the
sense that nighttime is when danger finds people.

That same vigilance seems to keep the memories close to the surface. She listens so intently for
unexpected noise that every sound becomes a possible beginning, and once her body is keyed up the
images break through more easily. Sometimes while closing the restaurant she hears the back door
rattle in the wind and instantly loses her place, overcome by emotion so sharply that she has to
step into the storage area and wait for herself to come back. She cannot tell the story of the
hurricane smoothly even in therapy; it arrives as broken scenes that are too intense to hold onto
for long. This, too, has hardened her view that something in her has been permanently altered. She
thinks other people can move on because they still belong in ordinary life, while she remains stuck
watching for the next blow.

By the time she sought help, the strain was showing both at home and at work. Her husband had begun
sleeping through her repeated checking of windows and doors, but he worried about how little she
rested and how quickly loud sounds could undo her. At work she has started dreading the evening
shift, not because of the tasks themselves but because the restaurant’s clatter and the dark parking
lot outside keep her feeling poised for disaster. What finally brought her in was the fear that this
was now her permanent future: sleepless, easily shattered by noise, cut off from ordinary feeling,
and unable to stop the storm from returning to her mind. What she hopes for, though she says it
cautiously, is not to erase what happened but to feel that night become past tense instead of
something that is still waiting for her after dark.
