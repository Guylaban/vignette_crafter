Nadia Rahman was 32 when she came for treatment, a divorced South Asian American woman who worked as
a hospital chaplain and had built much of her identity around being able to sit with other people’s
pain and help them name it. What happened to her was torture during a period of detention overseas
while she was visiting extended family. In the first session she tried several times to describe it
and each time seemed to arrive at the edge of the memory and stall. She would say she knew exactly
what had happened, that it was all there, but when she tried to tell it in order, the words seemed
to fall away. She lost the thread mid-sentence, pressed her fingers hard into the arms of the chair,
and shook her head as if language itself had become unreliable. What remained most vividly were
sensory fragments: men in uniform standing over her, the scrape of boots, the metallic clatter of
doors, hands pinning her wrists and shoulders when she struggled, the unbearable certainty of being
physically overpowered. The memory lived in her as something she could feel and hear more easily
than speak.

In the months after she returned home, Nadia found that the parts she could not put into words were
often the parts that surged up most forcefully in daily life. A clipped command in a security
officer’s voice at the hospital entrance, the sight of police uniforms in the emergency department,
or the sound of keys and heavy shoes in a corridor could bring her to a sudden stop. If someone
brushed past her in a crowd or she felt hemmed in between people in an elevator, her body reacted
before she could explain to herself why; she would go rigid, breathless, with the old sense of being
held down returning in full force. What frightened her was that these reactions felt tied to
something she knew had happened yet could not narrate clearly. Because the memory came back as
sound, pressure, and image rather than a story she could tell herself, those same sounds,
sensations, and uniforms in the present hit her with a terrifying immediacy.

Her work as a chaplain made these encounters hard to avoid. She moved through hospital corridors
where security staff were posted, crossed open atriums and exposed parking areas between buildings,
and was expected to approach families and staff she had once known as part of her ordinary pastoral
rounds. Over time she began altering her routes through the hospital to avoid large open lobbies
where she felt visible and undefended. She lingered in side hallways, took service elevators, and
timed her movements to reduce the chance of passing uniformed personnel. Outside work she stopped
meeting people she had socialized with before the detention, partly because their questions invited
explanation and partly because she could not bear the shame of freezing when asked what had
happened. On the worst evenings, when the unformed memory pressed in and she could not stop hearing
the sounds or feeling the restraint in her body, she would hurt herself in small, controlled ways
just to force her mind onto something immediate and physical.

These habits narrowed her life, but they also deepened the very problem she was trying to escape.
The less Nadia entered open spaces, saw familiar people, or allowed herself to stay with the sensory
fragments long enough to speak about them, the more unreachable the event became in words. She had
come to believe that if she started describing it, she would lose control completely, and each
retreat confirmed that belief. Even in therapy she circled around the torture rather than through
it, offering isolated details and then falling silent, as though avoidance had taught the memory to
remain nonverbal. After cutting herself, she would sometimes feel briefly steadier, but afterward
she was even less able to explain what had overwhelmed her in the first place.

By the time she sought help, the strain was showing at work. She was still able to sit beside dying
patients and pray with families, but internally she was scanning constantly for uniforms, footsteps,
enclosed passages, and any moment of unwanted contact. Colleagues had noticed that she disappeared
from common areas and turned down shared lunches. She was frightened by how quickly her world was
shrinking and by the possibility that she might no longer be able to do the work that mattered most
to her. What finally brought her in was not only the intensity of the reactions, but the fear that
the torture had taken from her the very thing she relied on most: the capacity to make suffering
speakable. She said she wanted help finding words, and she was terrified that if she could not, the
sounds and sensations would go on ruling her life.
