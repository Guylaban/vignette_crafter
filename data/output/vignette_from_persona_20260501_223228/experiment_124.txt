At 24, Matiu had been working as a pharmacist for just over a year when his older cousin, who had
helped raise him in their Samoan American family, was killed after a night of heavy drinking. The
account Matiu gives of that night never comes in a clean line. He can say there was a call, that
people were shouting, that he ended up at the hospital and later at the funeral, but when he tries
to put the hours together he loses the sequence and goes blank. Some parts return with a crushing
stab of guilt—especially the thought that, as the one in the family with medical training, he should
somehow have prevented what happened or understood sooner how serious it was. At other times he
describes the same night in a flat, detached way that disturbs him, as if he is reciting something
that happened to somebody else.

In the months afterward, what settled in most deeply was not only grief but a conviction that his
life had been ruined. He had come to believe that the person he had been before—easygoing, social,
proud of finally becoming a pharmacist—was gone for good. Nothing felt meaningful in the way it once
had; even work, which had been a source of purpose, now seemed hollow. That sense that happiness had
been lost fed directly into how on edge he felt in his own body. He began living as if some further
blow was always about to land, shoulders tight, jaw set, pulse jumping before he had even worked out
what had caught his attention. When his body surged into alarm in ordinary moments, he took it as
further proof that the trauma had permanently damaged him, that his life really had been destroyed
rather than shaken.

His view of himself and his future also shaped the way he related to the memory. Because he was
certain he had failed someone he loved and that nothing worthwhile remained ahead of him,
remembering the event pulled him quickly toward guilt. The more he told himself that this death had
stripped life of meaning, the less he could hold the memory together as a full account; instead it
broke into jagged fragments or turned strangely numb. He does not remember and then conclude that
life is over so much as approach the memory already convinced it confirms that conclusion.

Those same beliefs have made the world feel full of loaded reminders. Since he sees the trauma as
the point at which happiness and meaning ended, he scans for anything that belongs to that world of
loss. Funerals are unbearable; when a patient at the pharmacy mentions an upcoming burial, he feels
a jolt of dread before he can think. Cemeteries and grief rituals feel contaminated, not simply sad.
The smell of alcohol is especially potent. At work, opening the counseling window for a customer
whose breath carries liquor can make his stomach drop and his chest tighten instantly. He has also
become sharply reactive to people behaving badly in public—customers arguing, someone being loud and
careless in the parking lot—because it fits with the way he now sees the world: reckless behavior
destroys lives, and one moment of it can take everything.

Once a trigger catches him, memory follows in the form it usually does now: incomplete, guilty, and
disjointed. The alcohol smell at the pharmacy can bring back the hospital corridor without a clear
beginning or end, just flashes and the certainty that he should have done more. A funeral
announcement can leave him with an image of standing beside his family, emotionally blunted and
ashamed of that blunting, unable to tell himself a whole story about what he was seeing or feeling.
His reactions also run in the other direction. Because the memory is so fractured and saturated with
guilt, he is left vulnerable to whatever resembles one piece of it; the gaps seem to make the
reminders spread. Not being able to organize what happened means that funerals, graveyards, and even
the sour smell of alcohol continue to stand in for the whole event, and the guilty fragments make
other people’s misbehavior feel intolerably charged.

Over time this has narrowed his life. In the pharmacy he keeps his interactions brisk and avoids
lingering near customers who smell of alcohol. He has stopped attending family graveside visits,
which has been noticed and quietly resented by relatives who see absence where he experiences sheer
overwhelm. What frightened him most by the time he sought help was how automatic everything had
become: his body launching into alarm before thought, ordinary scenes turning into reminders,
reminders collapsing him into guilt, and each episode seeming to confirm that he was no longer the
person he had been. He came in after realizing he was starting to dread going to work and had begun
to think that if this continued, there would be nothing in his life left that still felt like his
own.
