David is a 55-year-old fashion designer who came to treatment after years of insisting he had simply
become “difficult” with age. He grew up in a house where danger arrived through the person meant to
protect him. His caregiver’s physical abuse was unpredictable, often happening in stretches of
unnatural quiet that made him listen for footsteps, a shift in breathing, the touch of a hand on his
shoulder that could turn, without warning, into violence. What remained with him was not a single
clean account but broken sensory fragments and a bodily certainty that silence meant something bad
was about to happen. Even now he cannot tell a clear story of what happened from beginning to end.
At times he can describe an incident in a flat, detached way, as if he were talking about someone
else’s childhood, and then without warning a shard of memory brings a surge of guilt so strong that
he feels implicated in his own suffering.

As an adult, David built a life around beauty, control, and presentation. In his studio, where
assistants move around him pinning fabric and adjusting garments on models, he has become known for
flinching sharply if someone touches him from behind. He covers it with irritability or precision,
but inside the reaction feels immediate and absolute. He has come to believe that people cannot be
trusted and that something in him was permanently altered by what happened. The fact that he still
startles, still scans even his own apartment at night, has convinced him at times that he may be
going crazy. Those beliefs do not sit quietly in the background; they shape how he moves through the
world, leaving him on guard at home and especially unsettled by silence, which feels less peaceful
than ominous, as though it is the moment before something terrible begins again.

The memories and the meaning he makes of them feed each other. Because the abuse comes back in
disconnected pieces, David takes the gaps as evidence of damage: if he cannot form a coherent story,
he thinks, then his mind must be broken. When he recalls the past in an emotionless, almost foreign
way, that numbness frightens him as much as the content itself; it seems to confirm his fear that he
has been changed for the worse. And when guilt arrives during recall, he turns it inward and outward
at once, dwelling on what he must have done wrong while also circling for hours around what other
people did to him and how unprotected he was. The more he remembers in fragments, the more
mistrustful and self-condemning he becomes.

Over time David developed rituals to manage this. He avoids times of day and situations that he
associates with nightmares, delaying sleep with work, television, or pacing around the apartment
rather than lying in the dark when the quiet closes in. He ruminates constantly—sometimes accusing
himself, sometimes replaying the injuries done by others—because he feels that if he could only
think hard enough, he might finally make sense of it or assign blame correctly. These habits are
driven by his beliefs that people are dangerous, that he has been permanently damaged, and that his
reactions signal mental collapse. Yet the effect is the opposite of relief. By refusing the times
that bring dreams and by spending hours trapped in repetitive blame, he never arrives at a fuller
narrative; the memory remains jagged, numb in places and contaminated by guilt in others. Each
failed attempt to think his way through it leaves him more convinced that something is fundamentally
wrong with him.

His state of alarm also pulls the past closer. On nights when his apartment falls silent, he checks
locks, listens for sounds in the hallway, and feels watchful even in bed. After an unexpected touch
at work—a seamstress brushing his arm to get his attention, a model stepping too close backstage—he
can spend the rest of the day with old fragments pressing in, not as a complete recollection but as
bodily knowledge and flashes he cannot organize. That renewed contact with memory deepens his
conviction that he is unsafe and unstable. In turn, those convictions heighten his vigilance, making
silence more menacing and ordinary contact more startling.

By the time he sought help, the pattern had begun to cost him professionally and personally.
Assistants had started avoiding him because his sharp reactions to touch felt hostile, and his adult
daughter had told him that being in his apartment was exhausting because he was always listening,
always tense. What finally brought him in was a recent night when he sat awake until dawn to avoid
dreaming and found himself thinking, with unusual clarity, that he could not keep living as if the
danger were still in the room. He is most afraid that the damage is permanent and that his mind is
slipping beyond his control, but he is also hoping, cautiously, that if he can understand what
happens to him when the silence comes, the past may stop ruling every room he enters.
