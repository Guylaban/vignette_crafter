Ama was 23 and early in her first full-time role as an environmental scientist when her older
brother died unexpectedly. He had been driving home after a late shift, and when he stopped replying
to her texts she told herself he was busy or his phone had died. Hours later, after a delay that
stretched far beyond what felt normal, she got the call telling her there had been a crash and that
he had not survived. What stayed with her was not a clear, tellable story but a set of jagged
fragments: staring at her phone, the unbearable waiting, her mother’s voice breaking, the sense that
the world had split before she could understand what was happening. Since then, whenever anything
takes longer than expected, her whole body jolts before she can think. If a colleague is late back
from field sampling or a family member does not answer right away, she is immediately certain
something terrible is unfolding.

In the weeks after his death, Ama kept circling the same conclusions. She had come to believe that
somebody else would have stopped it, and that if she had been a better sister, sharper, more
responsible, somehow she would have done something too. She was certain the death said something
shameful about the sort of person she was, someone who missed signs and failed the people she loved.
She also could not get past how deeply unfair it felt that he was gone and nothing could undo it.
Those beliefs pulled her into hours of self-blame, replaying what she had texted, when she had
texted, whether she should have called sooner, and whether a different person would have acted
decisively enough to change the outcome.

The memory itself was difficult for her to hold in one piece. When she tried to describe the night,
she often stopped, saying she knew what happened but could not find words. At other moments she
began to approach it and then mentally shoved it away before she could fully recall it. Yet the
memory did not stay distant; it would seize her as if it were happening now, especially in moments
of waiting. A delayed email, a missed call, brake lights massed on the highway during her commute to
a wetlands site could suddenly make the original dread feel present rather than past. Because those
fragments came with such force, she increasingly avoided television, films, or news stories
involving accidents or sudden deaths, fearing they would pull her straight back into that night.

At work, where field schedules changed and teams often ran late returning from site visits, the
pattern tightened. If a truck was delayed in traffic or a coworker’s location update did not come
through on time, Ama’s chest would clench and her heart would race before she had formed a thought.
She was sleeping badly, lying awake scanning for signs that something else was about to go wrong. In
that exhausted, keyed-up state, she found herself starting arguments with her mother or friends over
minor things, later recognizing that the conflict gave her somewhere to put the fear and agitation.
The constant sense of danger also pushed her back into self-blame: if she was this alert now, she
thought, maybe she should have been this alert then; maybe her brother died because she had been the
kind of person who let herself be reassured.

Over time, the ways she tried to manage it seemed to make the whole experience harder to bear. The
more she ruminated about what she should have done, the more fixed her conclusions became that
another person would have coped better and that there was something fundamentally wrong with her.
Picking arguments left her ashamed afterward, which she folded into the same harsh view of her
character. Avoiding media and cutting off the memory before it fully unfolded meant the night
remained as disconnected flashes that still hit her with full force, impossible to place firmly in
the past. Because it never felt fully processed, each fresh surge of alarm seemed to confirm that
the danger was still close and unresolved.

By the time she sought help, Ama was frightened by how small her life had become. She dreaded
routine delays in the field and checked her phone compulsively whenever anyone was late. She was
worn down by broken sleep and by the way ordinary waiting could turn, in an instant, into certainty
that death was about to happen again. What finally brought her in was an argument at work after a
routine scheduling change left her shaking in the bathroom, unable to explain why she felt as if she
had been dropped back into the night her brother died. She said she wanted, more than anything, to
remember him without feeling ambushed by the moment of losing him, but she was afraid that if she
stopped blaming herself, she would be letting his death matter less.
