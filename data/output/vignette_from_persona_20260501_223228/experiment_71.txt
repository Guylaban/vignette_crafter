Raúl Méndez is a 70-year-old widowed welder who came to treatment after months of telling himself he
was too old to be “shaken by ghosts.” He grew up in a Latin American country during a period of
political violence, and in his thirties he was detained, beaten, and tortured by armed men who
accused him of helping the wrong people. What remains most vivid to him is not a coherent sequence
but fragments: a metal chair bolted to the floor, the crack of gunfire somewhere close outside the
building, hands forcing him down when he realized there was no way out. He remembers trying to read
the men’s faces, changing his tone, agreeing quickly, saying whatever might keep the next blow from
coming. Even now, when he tries to tell the story in order, he loses the thread and stops mid-
sentence, jaw tight, as if the memory refuses to stay in the past long enough to be described.

After immigrating to the United States, Raúl built a life around work. Welding suited him because he
could keep his head down and let the day be structured by metal, heat, and repetition. After his
wife died, the old memories became harder to hold off. At the fabrication shop, the sudden crack of
metal striking metal or any sound resembling a gunshot can make his whole body jolt. If he is boxed
in between steel racks or a coworker blocks the only open path, he feels the same trapped certainty
he felt during the torture, and the memory arrives as if it is happening right now. In those moments
he is no longer an older man in a workshop; he is back in the room with no options, flooded with the
same helpless fury. Violence on the news or a heated argument nearby can do the same, pulling up the
image of armed men and leaving him braced for attack.

These moments do not just bring back the past; they confirm what Raúl has come to believe about the
world and himself. He is certain that you can never know who will harm you, because the men who
tortured him had seemed ordinary until they were not. When he is startled by a loud bang at work or
feels hemmed in by bodies and machinery, his mind goes immediately to danger, as though threat has
already announced itself. At the same time, he berates himself for not having done more then. He
says he should have resisted better, should have prevented what happened to others, should have
acted differently, and he cannot forgive himself for what he sees as his failure and the way he
behaved under interrogation.

Because he believes danger can come from anyone and that his own actions helped bring the torture on
him, Raúl has organized his life around not provoking people and not letting his mind go near the
past. In the shop he becomes overly agreeable the moment a supervisor sounds irritated or a coworker
steps toward him too quickly; he says yes before he has even thought, eager to smooth things over.
He takes the long route rather than squeezing through narrow spaces, keeps himself near exits, and
works hard to push away any thought of detention or beatings. When an image forces itself in, he
tries to detach from it, going blank and distant until the feeling passes.

Those strategies bring short spells of relief, but they have narrowed his world. The more he forces
the memories down, the more abruptly they break through when something reminds him of the torture. A
sharp report in the shop, footage of street violence, even the bodily sense of having no options can
ignite a memory so immediate that he feels his hands clench with rage before he understands where he
is. That intensity has made the triggers feel even more dangerous to him; he now scans constantly
for loud sounds, blocked pathways, or men whose posture feels threatening, and ordinary moments are
increasingly hard to trust.

His coworkers have noticed that he startles, goes silent, or suddenly becomes almost excessively
compliant after a loud noise. He has begun turning down jobs that would put him in tighter
workspaces, and he leaves the break room if the television shows shootings or assaults. What finally
brought him in was an incident at the shop when a dropped steel bar made a cracking noise; he froze,
then erupted in anger he could not explain, and afterward could not clearly tell his foreman what
had happened. He was ashamed of that loss of control, frightened by how quickly the past took over,
and frightened too by the thought that at his age this may be how the rest of his life will remain.
What he says he wants most is simple: to be able to hear a bang, stand in a crowded workspace, and
know for certain that he is here, now, and not back in that room again.
