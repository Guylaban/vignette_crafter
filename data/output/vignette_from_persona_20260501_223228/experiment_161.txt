Martin is a 54-year-old civil servant who came to treatment months after leaving a violent
relationship with a male partner who had repeatedly assaulted him in private, usually inside the
apartment they had shared. What stayed with him most was not only being hit, but the feeling of
being pinned inside someone else’s control: being blocked in doorways, ordered where to stand,
watched when he tried to move away. On several occasions the assaults happened late at night after
an argument, when the apartment was quiet and he realized no one else would hear him. He remembers
the sharp, metallic taste that rose in his mouth during those episodes, and certain scenes return to
him as still images—the angle of the hallway light, his partner’s shoulders filling the doorway, the
bedroom seen in a kind of frozen frame. Yet when he tries on purpose to tell the story from
beginning to end, he often says it goes blank and feels strangely out of reach, as if the memory
will only come back on its own terms.

After the relationship ended, Martin expected that being away from his former partner would bring
relief. Instead, situations that carried even a trace of that old helplessness began to unsettle
him. At work in a government office, changes he could not control—a supervisor abruptly revising an
assignment, a meeting being moved without warning, a security door locking behind him—could provoke
an immediate inner jolt. Sleeping alone was also unexpectedly difficult. In hotel rooms on work
travel, or even in his own apartment on nights his current partner was away, the silence and
unfamiliarity made him feel exposed. Being alone with men could be particularly tense, especially if
a male colleague lingered after others had left a conference room. In those moments, he did not
simply feel nervous; the old scenes seemed to force their way in. A closed office door or the
sensation of being by himself with another man could bring back those photographic flashes of the
apartment and, with them, the taste in his mouth from the abuse.

Because of this, Martin has organized his life to avoid being alone, both with his thoughts and in
actual rooms. He stays late near other people’s desks rather than working by himself, volunteers for
collaborative tasks, and fills evenings with constant social contact so that there is always another
voice in the background. If his partner is out, he will sit in a café until closing or call friends
rather than spend time in the apartment by himself. He is aware this can look excessive, but to him
it makes sense: being alone in a room or building feels too close to the conditions in which the
abuse happened. In the same way, he tries to detach from the memories whenever they stir, pushing
them off before they can fully form. Yet this way of coping seems to make the memories stranger
rather than smaller. When he forces them away, he later finds he cannot deliberately retrieve what
happened, and then a chance reminder will bring back a sharply isolated image or that vivid taste
with alarming force.

Over time, that pattern has tightened. The more Martin arranges his days so he is never by himself,
the less able he feels to tolerate moments he cannot control, and ordinary situations begin to carry
more threat. At the office he now avoids being the last person left with a male coworker, sometimes
inventing an errand to step out into the hallway or leaving work unfinished so he can follow the
group out together. If travel is required, he dreads the hotel room more than the trip itself. He
keeps the television on, calls people repeatedly, and sometimes sleeps little because lying alone in
the dark seems to invite the return of those frozen images. The memories, arriving in flashes rather
than in a narrative he can hold onto, make the triggers feel even more potent; and because those
situations feel dangerous, he doubles down on staying surrounded by others and not letting himself
think.

This has begun to affect both his work and his relationship. Colleagues have noticed that he is
reluctant to take independent assignments, and his partner has become frustrated by how panicked
Martin gets at the idea of a night alone. What finally brought him to seek help was a recent
overnight work trip when he found himself sitting in a hotel lobby until nearly dawn rather than
going back to his room, shaken after being briefly alone in an elevator with another man. He says he
is frightened by how small his life has become and by the sense that these moments can still
overtake him without warning. More than anything, he wants to be able to be by himself without
feeling trapped, and to remember what happened without feeling as though the memory is controlling
him.
