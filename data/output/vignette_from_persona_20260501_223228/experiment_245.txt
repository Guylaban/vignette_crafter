Tane is a 53-year-old airline pilot who had spent most of his adult life priding himself on calm
judgment, disciplined routines, and the ability to function under pressure. During a period overseas
several years ago, he was detained and tortured. He does not recount it in a flowing story. What
comes back are fixed images: a metal door, a narrow room packed too close with bodies and heat, the
face of a guard leaning in while someone nearby cried, the sour smell of sweat and damp concrete. He
remembers men being humiliated and beaten for the smallest perceived infraction, and what stayed
with him most was not only the pain and fear but the sense that ordinary rules had vanished, that
people could behave with deliberate cruelty and call it justified. Even now, when he approaches the
memory, it arrives as stark snapshots rather than a narrative, and the smell comes with it so
vividly that for a moment he feels he is back inside that room. If he lets it go on too long, the
emotion surges so strongly that he has to shut it down.

In the months after he returned home to the United States, Tane found that certain moments in
ordinary life hit him with a force he could not explain to anyone around him. Crowded places were
the worst. Walking through an airport concourse, standing in a packed shuttle bus, or waiting in a
tightly bunched security line could abruptly make his chest tighten, not just from the press of
people but from the sense of being trapped among bodies again. Seeing someone cry, even briefly,
could stop him cold. What also set him off was witnessing people act badly in public—an angry
passenger berating a gate agent, someone shoving ahead in line, a stranger behaving with arrogant
disregard. In those moments, it was as if the world had tilted back toward the same lawless cruelty
he had seen during detention. He became sharply watchful, scanning faces and movements around him
for signs that someone might turn dangerous.

The triggers did not stay confined to the moment. A crying child in a terminal or a crowd closing in
at boarding would often pull up those visual fragments with brutal immediacy: the room, the faces,
the door. At times he could smell the air from captivity so distinctly that he would step away from
others and steady himself against a wall, embarrassed by how completely his mind had been overtaken.
Because the memory came in photographic flashes and quickly became overwhelming, he learned to cut
it off as fast as possible, but that left him braced and vigilant for the rest of the day. The
memory itself had come to feel like a warning signal. When those images and smells broke through, he
took them as evidence that danger was near and that he could not afford to relax.

Over time that sense of threat spread into his home and work life. Silence, especially unusual
silence, became difficult to bear. If the house was too still at night, he would feel a sick
certainty that something was about to happen. He made repeated rounds checking locks and windows,
sometimes returning to the same door minutes later despite knowing he had already secured it. He
watched other people closely, including strangers in terminals and, to his wife’s distress, even
neighbors or service workers who had done nothing overtly wrong. His threat monitoring also fed back
into what provoked him. In any crowded place he was no longer simply among people; he was studying
who looked agitated, who seemed disrespectful, who might suddenly lash out. That constant scanning
made public conflict and misbehavior stand out instantly, and once he noticed one crying person or
one pushing, angry passenger, the whole environment became charged.

As an airline pilot, he could not avoid airports, boarding areas, and tightly managed public spaces.
He began altering his routines in small but consequential ways—arriving through quieter entrances
when possible, lingering in restricted crew areas, choosing routes through terminals that minimized
contact with dense crowds. Even so, a gate area filling with impatient passengers or the sound of
someone crying nearby could set the whole sequence in motion: the flash of images, the remembered
smell, the conviction that he needed to watch everyone, the urge to get somewhere he could check an
exit. The more he lived this way, the more potent those cues became. He had come to expect that
crowds, tears, and public disrespect meant threat, and because he was already on guard for them, he
found them everywhere.

By the time he sought help, his world had narrowed despite the fact that he was still working. His
wife had grown frustrated with the nightly checking and with how absent he became in public, his
eyes always moving, his attention captured by who might be unsafe. What frightened Tane most was not
simply remembering what had happened, but the sense that the memory could be summoned by almost
anything and that once it arrived he was no longer fully in the present. He came to treatment after
realizing that even in the familiar silence of his own home, and even in the routine environment of
the airport where he had once felt most competent, he was living as if danger were about to break
through at any moment. He says he wants, more than anything, to be able to move through a crowd and
hear quiet without waiting for the next sign that something terrible is about to begin again.
