worker when she was thrown to the ground by police during a chaotic street operation near an intake
site she was visiting for work. She remembers the crush of bodies, bicycles swerving as people tried

hurt before anyone realized she was not a threat. What stayed with her was not only the force of it
but the helpless public humiliation of it. In the months after, she had come to believe that what
happened was profoundly unjust and that nothing meaningful had been done about it. She compared
herself harshly to an imagined steadier, more competent person who “would never have ended up
there,” and this self-judgment mixed with a painful sense that she no longer recognized herself.

Those beliefs began to shape what stood out as dangerous in daily life. At work, when police
officers appeared near the shelters or transit hubs where she met clients, Lina’s attention snapped
toward them before she even meant it to. In crowded waiting rooms or on busy sidewalks, especially
if cyclists threaded through the press of people, she felt her body tense as if the same scene were
gathering again. What frightened her was not just the reminder of the event but what it seemed to
prove: that the world could turn arbitrary and violent without warning, and that she was the sort of
person to whom this could happen. The sight of police, bicycles cutting past, or dense crowds could
pull the memory back with startling force—the terror she had felt then, the grief that followed, and
an emotional flood so strong she often had to stop what she was doing.

Because those moments brought back so much, Lina began organizing her life around not setting them
off. She changed routes to avoid heavily policed areas, delayed client visits if the sidewalks
outside were congested, and kept away from neighborhood festivals or markets where bicycles and
crowds were likely. When an image or bodily jolt of the assault broke through anyway, she tried hard
to force it out of mind, telling herself not to think about it. She also stopped saying yes to
things that might have made her feel light or hopeful—dinners with friends, small celebrations with
her husband, even quiet weekend plans—because any lift in mood felt fragile and somehow wrong, as if
happiness would only make the crash back into terror or grief worse. Some evenings she turned to
gambling for distraction and a sharp burst of feeling that briefly drowned everything else out.

These strategies did not settle her for long. The more Lina pushed the memories away, the more
abruptly they seemed to return: the officer’s presence over her, the panic in her chest, the
unbearable sense of loss afterward. After gambling late into the night or spending an evening grimly
numbing herself instead of resting, she slept poorly, lying awake keyed up and irritable the next
day. That edge showed at home and at work. She snapped more quickly at minor frustrations, and there
were moments when she acted in ways she privately knew were reckless, taking unnecessary risks in
transit or staying in tense situations longer than felt safe, as if part of her had given up on
caution mattering. Those states of agitation made the memories easier to trigger again; on little
sleep and already on edge, one glimpse of police near her office or one cyclist brushing too close
could send her right back into the same overwhelming recall.

The memories themselves deepened the meanings she drew from the event. Each return of that terror
and grief seemed to confirm that the violation was still unresolved, still unfair, still lodged
inside her life. When she had to cut a memory short because the emotion became too much, she took
that as evidence that she was no longer the person she used to be. And each time she avoided a
gathering, silenced a thought, or lost herself gambling, she later judged herself for it, comparing
herself again to someone stronger and more intact. That shame sharpened her irritability and
restless risk-taking, and it made the next trigger feel even more loaded, because now police,
cyclists, and crowds were not only reminders of what happened but reminders of what she felt she had
become since.

By the time Lina sought help, the pattern had narrowed her life considerably. Her work environment,

notice how often she withdrew from anything enjoyable. She came in after a week in which a crowded
client intake area, a police presence outside the building, and a cyclist weaving through the
entrance left her shaken enough that she had to stop mid-conversation and hide in an office. What
she fears most is that this is permanent—that the terror will keep overtaking her, that she will
remain angry, sleepless, and unlike herself. What she hopes, cautiously, is to be able to do her
