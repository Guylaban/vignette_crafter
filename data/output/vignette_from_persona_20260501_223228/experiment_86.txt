Elena Morales is a 79-year-old retired combat pilot who came to treatment long after the event she
still calls, simply, “that stop.” Widowed now and living alone, she had spent most of her life in
disciplined, high-stakes settings, and she describes herself as someone who once trusted training,
rank, and procedure. That changed during an encounter with police violence several years ago. She
had been walking home in a busy downtown corridor after an aviation veterans’ event, still wearing a
dark flight jacket. Patrol cars pulled up fast. The first thing that returns to her is not a full
sequence but a set of fixed images: the glare of lights on a windshield, a hand on a holster, the
angle of a shoulder driving her toward the car, pavement at eye level. Around those pictures comes
smell before story—hot rubber, exhaust, sweat, and a sharp metallic scent she cannot name without
her chest tightening. Even now she does not tell it as a smooth narrative; pieces arrive abruptly,
vivid and gone again.

In the weeks afterward, certain situations began to carry the force of the event itself. Crowded
places were the worst. In a grocery line or clinic waiting room, if bodies pressed too close, Elena
felt her system surge before she could think: her heart hammered, her muscles locked, and she was
suddenly unable to relax even when she could plainly see she was not in immediate danger. Sirens did
something similar. The sound did not just startle her; it seemed to pull those picture-like moments
directly into the present, along with the smell of exhaust, as if the scene were breaking through in
fragments. Seeing police officers was especially potent. A routine patrol car outside her apartment
or officers posted at a public building made her instantly search their belts, hands, and distance
from her, noticing weapons and hard objects in the environment with a speed she experiences as
automatic.

Because the memories come in flashes rather than a story, Elena has come to dread the conditions
that might let them in. At night she stays up until very late, often in the living room with the
television on low, telling herself that sleep is dangerous because the moment she lets go, the
pictures appear without warning. She is careful about clothing in a way that would once have
embarrassed her: she avoids wearing anything that resembles what she had on that day, especially
darker uniform-like jackets, because she cannot separate those clothes from the snapshots that
intrude. The more the fragments return, the more she organizes her routines around not giving them
any opening.

Her fear is not only about remembering. Elena lives as if danger can ignite at any second. She scans
every room she enters for exits, for heavy objects, for anything that could be used as a weapon, and
once that scanning starts she cannot settle. In the airport museum where she volunteers twice a
week, she now avoids family event days because the crowding and the visible security presence keep
her body braced for impact. She watches people’s hands, notices radios, batons, utility belts, and
the metal stanchions by the entrance. Under that level of alertness, the same sensory fragments are
more likely to break through—the hand on the holster, the smell, the slice of pavement—so that
vigilance seems to feed the very memories she is trying to prevent.

Over time, this has narrowed her life. She takes long detours to avoid police stations and city
blocks where sirens are common. If she cannot avoid going out, she arrives early to beat crowds and
chooses seats with a clear view of the door, constantly checking her surroundings for signs of
threat. These habits make sense to her because they promise a margin of control, yet they also keep
her attention fixed on exactly the cues that set her off. A passing siren, a cluster of people, the
sight of an officer near a crosswalk—each can spark a physical rush and pull up another fragment.
After a day spent scanning, she lies awake even later, tense and waiting, and the broken images
return again in the small hours.

What finally brought Elena in was an incident at the museum. A school group crowded the lobby just
as sirens passed outside and two uniformed officers entered to ask for directions. She felt herself
go cold and electric, started cataloging belts, radios, and possible weapons in the room, and then
was hit by the familiar snapshots and smell so strongly she had to lock herself in a staff restroom
until the episode passed. She is frightened by how quickly the present disappears when that happens.
What she wants, she says, is not to erase what occurred but to be in a crowded place, hear a siren,
or see police without feeling as though her body and mind are being dragged back into those few
terrible pictures.
