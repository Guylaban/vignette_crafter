Minh is a 41-year-old Vietnamese American dancer who came to treatment months after returning from
relief work in a war zone, where he had volunteered with a civilian aid team delivering food and
basic medical supplies. The event he returns to most often happened late in the afternoon, in that
dimming hour when the heat had not yet lifted and the air inside a damaged school building felt
close and stale. He had been helping move injured civilians into a corridor that had been judged
safer than the street outside. There was burnt plastic and concrete dust in the air, and on one of
the foreign contractors nearby he could smell alcohol, sharp and sour on the man’s breath, even in
the chaos. When shelling resumed, people surged toward the narrow exit. Minh remembers a moment in
which he pulled free from someone clutching at him and kept moving with the group. He survived, but
another person behind him did not. What has stayed with him is not only the terror of that corridor
but the conviction that, in the instant that mattered, he acted against the kind of man he believed
himself to be.

Since coming home, the time of day has become charged for him. In the late afternoon, especially
when rehearsal runs long and the studio light starts to change, he feels his body tighten before he
can explain why. The smell of alcohol on a colleague after a night out, or a burnt odor from
overheated stage equipment, can collapse the distance between then and now. He describes it as
having no warning: one second he is marking choreography, the next he is back in that corridor with
the same crush in his chest and the same certainty that something terrible is happening right now.
These moments do not feel like remembering. They feel immediate, as if the room around him has been
replaced. What follows just as quickly is shame. Even in the studio, with music playing and other
dancers around him, he is overtaken by the sense that he is reliving the worst thing he has ever
done.

Over time, Minh has come to believe that what happened revealed something unforgivable about him. He
says he acted in a way that violated his values, and he cannot make room for the fact that he had
been frightened and trapped; in his mind, that explanation sounds too much like excuse-making. He
has also started reading his life since the war through the same lens. If a smell sends him reeling
or the approach of late afternoon unsettles him, he takes that as proof that he is a lousy coper and
that he cannot rely on himself. Because he doubts himself so completely, he scans his surroundings
for signs that he might lose control again. He notices the faintest burnt smell before anyone else
does. He checks who has been drinking. He watches the clock as daylight shifts, bracing for the hour
he dreads. The more he does this, the more quickly those cues seize his attention and pull the
memory forward in full force.

When the memory comes, it does not remain a picture of the past. It becomes evidence in a case he is
constantly building against himself. In session he circles back to the same thought: if he can still
be thrown back there without warning, if he still feels as though it is happening now, then maybe
the event exposed the truth that he is weak and morally compromised. Each episode of reliving leaves
him more ashamed, and that shame hardens his belief that he is not the person he thought he was. He
has become certain that his reactions mean something damning about his character rather than
something understandable about what he lived through.

This has begun to shape his work and home life. Minh still dances professionally, but he has started
avoiding late rehearsals when he can, offering vague excuses to leave before the light changes. If
someone in the company comes in smelling of alcohol, he keeps his distance and struggles to focus on
partnering or timing. A burnt smell from lighting equipment has, more than once, made him step off
the floor abruptly and lock himself in a bathroom until the sensation passes. He is embarrassed that
his wife has noticed how the end of the day changes him; he becomes watchful and remote, as if
preparing for something he cannot name. What finally brought him to seek help was the fear that this
pattern was tightening around his whole life. He worries that he can no longer trust his own mind or
reactions, and he hopes, cautiously, that if he speaks about the event directly, he might find a way
to remember it without being pulled back into it and without feeling condemned by what he remembers.
