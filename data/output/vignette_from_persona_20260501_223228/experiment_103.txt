Arjun Mehta was 39 when his older sister died without warning. She had collapsed at home and was
gone before the ambulance arrived. In the first stunned days after the call, what lodged in him most
sharply was not only the fact of her death but the atmosphere around it: the funeral home, the smell
of flowers, the sight of mourners speaking in hushed voices about who had been lost and who was left
behind. He remembered standing near the casket feeling unreal, as if the scene were happening at a
distance and directly inside his body at the same time. Since then, grief-related settings have
carried a particular force for him. A funeral, a cemetery, even a conversation about someone losing
a parent or sibling can pull him back toward that first week.

Over the months that followed, Arjun tried to get on with work and family life. He is an interior
designer, and much of his day is spent meeting clients, visiting homes, and discussing personal
spaces with people who often disclose intimate details about marriages, illnesses, aging parents,
and deaths in the family. He has found that when a client casually mentions bereavement while
explaining why they want to redo a bedroom or clear out a relative’s belongings, his stomach
clenches before he has fully registered what was said. Around the anniversary of his sister’s death,
this reaction intensifies. He starts tracking the date weeks in advance and becomes preoccupied with
how bad it might be this year, running through images of himself falling apart at work, losing
control in front of clients, or getting trapped in the same helpless shock he felt when she died.

Those moments do not stay as thoughts for long. When he passes a cemetery while driving to a job
site or receives an invitation to a memorial service, he can suddenly feel as if the original loss
is happening again. His chest tightens, his arms go cold, and for a moment he is back in the funeral
home, braced for news that cannot be changed. He says his body “goes there” before his mind catches
up. By the time he realizes he is only hearing someone talk about a death or seeing headstones from
the road, he is already flooded and on edge. At home, his wife has noticed that after these episodes
he is quick to snap over small things, especially if she asks what is wrong when he is trying to
steady himself.

Because those reactions feel so immediate and uncontrollable, Arjun has built his life around trying
to stay ahead of them. As the anniversary approaches, he becomes absorbed in mentally rehearsing the
worst possible versions of the day, telling himself that if he imagines every way it could undo him,
he will be less blindsided. He avoids putting the date on the calendar, declines family plans that
might acknowledge it, and tries to keep himself busy with site visits and vendor calls so he does
not have to sit with any reminder of his sister. He has also started steering away from grief-
related events altogether. If he learns that a client meeting will include discussion of a recently
deceased relative, he will try to shorten the visit or redirect the conversation back to fabrics,
lighting, anything practical.

Yet these efforts have not given him the sense of control he wants. The more he rehearses
catastrophe, the more vivid and near it feels, and the more watchful he becomes for signs that he is
about to lose himself in it. Avoiding anniversaries has made the date feel more dangerous, not less;
each year it seems to arrive with greater dread. Keeping away from funerals and other reminders has
left him less able to tolerate the ordinary ways loss comes up in adult life, so even brief mention
of someone else’s bereavement can set off the same jolt in his body. Afterward he grows irritable,
ashamed of how harshly he has spoken, and even more determined to avoid anything that might trigger
another episode.

What finally brought Arjun to treatment was a scene at work this past year. During a consultation, a
client explained that she was renovating after her mother’s death. Before he could respond, he felt
himself drop into that old sense that the loss was happening now; his heart pounded, his hands
shook, and he had to excuse himself to the restroom. He later barked at an assistant who asked if he
was all right. He came home frightened by how little control he seemed to have and admitted to his
wife that he had been arranging his life around not being reminded of his sister. What frightens him
most is that grief has become something ambush-like and dangerous, waiting in dates, conversations,
and places he cannot fully avoid. What he hopes for, cautiously, is to remember his sister without
feeling as though he is being thrown back into the moment of losing her.
