than a sequence, and those pictures arrive with a force that feels more real than ordinary
remembering. He does not simply recall the fire; he feels dropped back inside it. What cuts deepest
is the shame that comes with those flashes: shame that he froze, shame that he noticed people
behaving selfishly and still thinks about it, shame that part of him believes he should have done
more.

In the weeks after the fire, Daniel returned to work because the store reopened in a temporary
location, but ordinary parts of retail life became loaded with danger. If a customer stood too close
behind him in an aisle, his body stiffened before he knew why. If he watched someone cut in line,
ignore store rules, or bark at a cashier, he felt a surge of alarm and disgust that seemed far
bigger than the moment. Handling certain coarse, woven fabrics in home décor brought back the
sensation of that shelf under his palm so sharply that the image of the stockroom door would snap
into his mind. These moments did not just remind him of the fire; they pulled up those vivid
snapshots with their shame so fast that he began to dread the store floor itself.

To get through shifts, Daniel started doing a quiet kind of bargaining with his own mind. When a
customer crowded him or acted badly and a picture from the fire burst up, he would tell himself it
had not really happened the way he remembered, or that it was not as bad as he made it out to be. He
tried to force the images down, pushing himself not to think about the fire, not to look too long at
the woven stock, not to let his mind go near the stockroom scene. On his worst days, after a memory
had come on with that humiliating vividness, he dug his nails into his arm in the employee bathroom
or snapped a rubber band against his wrist, using pain to interrupt what was in his head. Those
strategies made sense to him in the moment: if he could stop the thought, blunt it, or prove to
himself he was overreacting, maybe he could finish the shift and not fall apart in front of
customers.

Instead, the more he fought the memories, the stranger and more intrusive they seemed to become.

vivid the scenes felt, and the mismatch seemed to sharpen his shame rather than settle it. Even
hurting himself for a second’s relief made the next return of the memory feel abrupt and punishing,

boundaries, and noticing textures before he noticed anything else. The memory had taught him what to
look for, and what he looked for kept summoning the memory.

That pattern spread into his life outside work. In crowded checkout lines he angled his cart to keep
