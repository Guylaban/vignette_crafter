Elena Morales is a 74-year-old pharmacist who has been married for many years, and when she first
described the assault by a former romantic partner from earlier adulthood, she did so in images
rather than a story. What came back to her were a few fixed moments: his hand on the door, the sharp
sound of it slamming shut behind him, her body pinned and twisted in a position she can still feel,
and the humiliation of being looked at while she could not move the way she wanted. Even now, when
the memory comes, it is less a sequence than snapshots, and with them comes a bodily reenactment so
immediate that her shoulders draw in and her torso stiffens as if she is back in that exact
position. What is strongest in those moments is shame. She does not simply remember being hurt; she
relives the sense that something about her was exposed and diminished.

Over time, certain things came to carry the force of the assault. At work in the pharmacy, if a
stockroom door opens suddenly or a customer lets the front door slam, Elena’s whole body tightens
before she can think. At home she listens for the slightest sound that might mean someone is
entering, and even in her own kitchen she stays braced, as if danger could cross the threshold at
any moment. Comments about family can unsettle her in a different but equally immediate way. If
someone asks why she and her relatives are not closer, or remarks that family should be a place of
safety, she feels a jolt of dread and distrust; someone getting emotionally close to her has a
similar effect. In those moments she is not only on guard but pulled back toward the event itself,
as if the trigger and the memory have fused.

What frightens Elena is how quickly these cues become proof, in her mind, of what she has come to
believe about herself and the world. A slammed door or a well-meant question about family leaves her
thinking, again, that she is weak, that she is no longer capable in the ways that once defined her,
and that faith in people was a naïve mistake. She had once taken pride in being composed,
knowledgeable, and reliable in the pharmacy, someone others depended on. Now, when she notices
herself jump at a sound or recoil from warmth, she reads that reaction as evidence of damage. The
more on edge she feels, the more certain she becomes that she has been fundamentally altered for the
worse.

Those beliefs shape how she has tried to manage herself. Convinced that strong emotion will
overwhelm her and reveal her weakness, Elena works hard not to let herself feel too much of
anything. She keeps conversations practical, withdraws from people when they become too personal,
and avoids looking at or attending to her own body because bodily awareness so quickly evokes the
posture of the assault and the shame attached to it. Her watchfulness at home feeds these habits:
because she is already listening for entry sounds and feeling keyed up inside her own house,
retreating from others and from her own emotional life seems, to her, like the only way to stay
contained.

But these strategies have narrowed her world and deepened what she believes about herself. Avoiding
people has left her lonelier and more convinced that closeness is unsafe and that trust is gone for
good. Turning away from her body and her feelings has not made the past less powerful; instead, it
has made her feel less capable, as though even ordinary human experience is now more than she can
handle. When a colleague at the pharmacy tries to speak warmly to her after a difficult shift, Elena
often cuts the exchange short and later takes that withdrawal as more evidence that she is
diminished. The less she allows herself contact, the more comments about family or gestures of
emotional closeness stand out as threats the next time they occur.

Her vigilance also keeps the memory close. Because she is continually listening for doors and
scanning for signs of intrusion, the old snapshots are easily reactivated; and once they surface,
the bodily reenactment intensifies her sense of immediate danger. A door opening in the pharmacy can
bring back the image of the partner entering and closing off her exit; later that evening, alert at
home, she hears ordinary household sounds as if they belong to him. In this way, the memory teaches
her what to fear, and her fear teaches her where to look, until slamming doors, family remarks, and
emotional intimacy all feel increasingly charged.

By the time Elena sought help, her marriage had been strained by how much she pulled away from
closeness, and her workday had become organized around avoiding startle and exposure. She came in
after realizing that even inside her own home she no longer felt at rest, and that a life spent
guarded had begun to feel smaller than the one she once knew herself capable of living. What she
fears most is that this guarded, ashamed version of herself is now permanent. What she hopes, though
she says it cautiously, is that she might stop living as if every door opening means the past is
entering again.
