Daniel Weber, a 46-year-old graphic designer, came to treatment more than a year after an encounter
with police that he still could not tell in a straight line. He described being pulled over late one
evening while driving home from work after staying late on a deadline. What began as a traffic stop
escalated quickly. He remembered the flash of police lights in his rearview mirror, officers
shouting over one another, being ordered out of the car, and then being forced to the ground before
he understood what was happening. His cheek was pressed into the pavement; he saw blood on his own
hand and, in a fragment that stayed especially vivid, the torn skin and swelling on his wrist
afterward. What he could never reconcile was the sense that he had done nothing to deserve it. From
the start he had come to believe that what happened to him was profoundly unjust and that no one had
been held accountable, and this belief hardened into a broader conviction that the trauma had ruined
his life and drained it of meaning.

That sense of unfairness seemed to make the reminders of the event feel even more charged. Police
uniforms, a patrol car on the street, even seeing officers standing outside a coffee shop could jolt
him immediately. Visible injuries on other people had a similar effect: a cut on a coworker’s hand,
a bruised face in a news image, even the exposed scrape on a stranger’s knee could grip his
attention. He also complained of unwanted thoughts arriving on their own, as if his mind were
ambushing him. Because he was already carrying the belief that his life had been destroyed by
something that should never have happened, these reminders did not feel neutral or containable; they
landed as proof that the danger and the wrongness of it were still present.

When he was triggered, the memory did not come back as a narrative he could reflect on. It arrived
full force and without warning. He said that a glimpse of an officer’s badge or someone’s fresh
stitches could bring back the whole scene in an instant, and when that happened he became so
emotionally overwhelmed that he had to shut down the attempt to think or speak. In session he would
begin describing the stop, get as far as the command to step out of the car, and then lose the
thread, staring at the floor, unable to continue. His convictions about the trauma seemed to feed
this as well: because he experienced the event as the moment his life was destroyed, remembering it
felt less like recalling the past than being thrown back into the worst meaning of the present.

Over the months that followed, Daniel began living as though another sudden confrontation could
happen at any time. He was unable to relax even in places he knew were safe. In restaurants he
scanned for exits before he could settle into his chair; in his design studio he automatically noted
the back stairwell and loading door. His body remained tense and ready, shoulders tight, jaw set, as
though bracing had become permanent. Triggers pushed him into this state quickly, but his own
beliefs sustained it too: if life had already been wrecked once by an arbitrary abuse of authority,
he felt foolish letting his guard down now. The memory and the sense of current danger intensified
each other. Once the scene was activated in his mind, his body reacted as if he were back on the
pavement; and because his body was already on alert so much of the time, the memory seemed closer to
the surface, easier to ignite.

To manage this, Daniel increasingly organized his life around not giving his mind any room. After
work he disappeared into his phone, internet videos, and games for hours at a time, telling himself
that if he kept his attention occupied he would not have to see the scene again. He pulled back from
friends and avoided people whenever he could, preferring to sit alone in a dark room scrolling late
into the night rather than risk conversation that might touch the event or put him near strangers.
These habits brought immediate relief, but the relief never lasted. The more he trained himself to
escape into screens and stay away from people, the less practiced he became at tolerating the memory
when it broke through, and the more suddenly it seemed to hit. His world also became narrower and
more filled with the very cues he feared: online news feeds exposed him to images of injuries and
police footage, and going out less made every necessary outing feel more loaded, so that spotting an
officer near his office building could send his body surging before he even crossed the street.

By the time he sought help, the impact had reached both home and work. He was still producing enough
as a designer to get by, but he worked in isolation, avoided collaborative meetings when possible,
and spent breaks hiding with his phone rather than joining coworkers. At home, his wife had begun
telling him that he was present in body only, unreachable behind a screen and always watching the
room. What finally brought him in was the realization that he could no longer pass a police officer
or see an injured person without feeling instantly pulled back, flooded, and physically primed to
flee. He said he was afraid that this was all that remained of him now, but he still hoped there
might be some way to remember what happened without being overtaken by it.
