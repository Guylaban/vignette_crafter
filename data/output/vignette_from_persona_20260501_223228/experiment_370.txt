Liam is a 19-year-old White American man who works as a counselor, and when he speaks about the
illness that nearly killed him, he does so with a flatness that gives way abruptly to panic when the
details come too close. Last year he was hospitalized after a sudden, severe medical crisis that
required repeated blood draws, IV lines, and urgent procedures. He remembers the glare of hospital
lights on metal trays, the sight of needles being opened, the clear drip chamber hanging beside his
bed, and the sharp antiseptic smell that seemed to cling to everything. What has stayed with him is
not only fear of dying, but the helplessness of watching his own body fail while strangers moved
quickly around him, touching him, inserting lines, examining him, telling him to hold still while he
felt less and less certain that his body would keep him alive.

In the months afterward, those reminders kept breaking into ordinary life. Passing the health office
at work and catching the sterile smell of disinfectant could make his chest tighten before he had
fully registered why. If a client mentioned a medical procedure, or if he saw a syringe in a
training video, he felt his body react immediately, with a rush of heat, tension, and the sense that
something dangerous was happening now. Medical examinations became especially difficult. Even the
idea of being checked by a doctor made him brace as if he were about to be pinned down again.
Unexpected movement nearby could set him off in a similar way; if someone reached quickly across a
table, he jerked and turned before he could think, already in a state of alarm.

Those moments also began to shape what he believed about himself. Each time he saw a needle or
smelled something hospital-like and felt his body surge into panic, Liam took it as proof that his
body was still unsafe and fundamentally unreliable. He had come to believe that he could no longer
trust his body to tell the difference between danger and safety. What frightened him most was the
sense that his body had become an enemy, reacting on its own, dragging him back into terror even in
harmless situations. That belief spread beyond the reminders themselves. He described feeling that
nothing in his life carried much meaning anymore; the future felt thin and insubstantial compared
with how abruptly illness had shown him that his body could fail without warning.

Because of that, he began organizing his life around not setting anything off. At work as a
counselor, he quietly traded away tasks that might bring him near health screenings or medical
paperwork. If he heard an ambulance outside the building, he waited before stepping into the
hallway. He postponed routine physical care and avoided hospitals altogether unless there was no
choice. Even in therapy, when discussion started to move toward the hospitalization, he noticed
himself shutting it down internally afterward, refusing to think about what had been said once he
left the session. He told himself there was no point in opening it up if his body would only ambush
him again, and his conviction that his body could not be trusted made avoidance feel like common
sense rather than fear.

Sleep became another battleground. Liam started staying up very late most nights, scrolling or
watching videos until exhaustion overtook him. In his mind, going to sleep meant letting down his
guard; the quiet and stillness gave too much room for the hospital images, the smell, the sense of
lying trapped under medical care. The later he stayed up, the more keyed up and physically reactive
he felt, and the less rested he was the next day. That constant strain left him even more jumpy, so
small movements nearby startled him more easily, and ordinary medical cues hit him with greater
force. In that exhausted state, a glimpse of an IV pole in a clinic doorway or the antiseptic scent
from a cleaned room could feel almost indistinguishable from being back in the hospital.

As the months passed, the pattern tightened. The more intensely his body reacted, the more certain
he became that something in him remained damaged and unsafe, and the more carefully he tried to
avoid anything medical, including what came up in therapy. That narrowing of his world made the
reminders feel more potent rather than less. A routine examination seemed unbearable precisely
because he had stayed away so long. The smell of antiseptic did not register as a neutral odor; it
had become a signal that his body was about to betray him again. Even at work, where he is supposed
to help others sit with distress, he found himself counting exits, noticing who moved suddenly, and
dreading any conversation that veered toward illness.

He finally sought help more urgently after realizing that his life was shrinking around these fears.

felt present anymore. Liam was shaken by how hollow he sounded when he said that nothing felt
meaningful and by how reflexively he still treated his own body as an adversary. What he is most
afraid of is that this reaction means he will never feel safe inside himself again. What he hopes,
though he says it cautiously, is to be able to walk into a medical setting, smell the antiseptic
air, and not feel as if he is back in the moment when he thought he might die.
