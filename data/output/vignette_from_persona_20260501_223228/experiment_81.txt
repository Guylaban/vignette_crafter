Liam Weber was 21 when he came for treatment, a quiet young man who had returned from military
service carrying an experience he still could not tell straight through. During a convoy operation
overseas, an explosion struck near his vehicle. What remained with him was not a single coherent
story but flashes: the blast, the metallic pressure in his ears, a smear of smoke, shouted words he
could not piece back together, and above all the scorched, burnt smell that seemed to fuse itself to
the moment. He also remembered the chopping aircraft noise overhead afterward, and the way every
sudden bang became charged with the expectation that something catastrophic was about to happen
again. When he tried to explain what happened, he would stop, frown, and say he knew it had happened
but could not find the words for it.

In the months after returning home, Liam had come to believe that the world was fundamentally
dangerous and that his only chance of getting through a day was to stay on guard. He scanned sounds
automatically, especially any sharp bang or the distant thrum of a helicopter or plane, and his body
reacted before he had time to think. That sense of permanent threat narrowed his life. Although he
now worked as a bus driver, he approached each route as if danger might break into it without
warning. At crowded stops he watched passengers, exits, and sudden movements instead of settling
into the routine of the job. He had also begun avoiding crowded places outside work altogether,
turning down invitations to shopping centers or public events because being surrounded by people
felt unsafe in a way he could not easily justify to others.

Those beliefs also shaped what he did when he was alone. Because he was certain that letting his
mind go quiet would leave him exposed to whatever came back, he spent long hours on his phone,
gaming, or scrolling online late into the night, trying to keep himself mentally occupied. He had
stopped going into churches or other religious spaces as well; he would detour past them without
fully explaining why, only knowing they left him too exposed and unsettled. Beneath that was a more
despairing conviction he rarely said aloud: that he was no longer sure his life was worth living. He
did not describe an active plan, but the thought frightened him because it arrived with the same
flat certainty as his other conclusions about the world.

The memories themselves fed those conclusions. Because the event returned in disconnected pieces,
Liam could not place it safely in the past. Instead it replayed like a film that started without
warning: a burst of noise, the smell of burning, the aircraft sound overhead, and then a sickening
sense that he was back in it. Each replay seemed to confirm what he already feared—that nowhere was
truly safe and that he had to remain vigilant at all times. What frightened him most was not only
remembering but the fact that the memory felt unfinished and beyond his control, as if his mind were
still trying and failing to make sense of something too dangerous to hold.

That was part of why he kept reaching for distraction. When a memory began to gather, he would grab
his phone, start a game, or disappear into the internet for hours because it was the fastest way he
knew to stop the images from gaining momentum. Avoiding crowds helped for the same reason: fewer
noises, fewer jolts, fewer chances of a bang setting off the internal replay. Yet over time this
left the memory even less spoken and less organized. The more he pushed it away and replaced quiet
moments with screens, the more the event seemed to survive only as broken clips and sensory
fragments, still impossible to describe from beginning to end.

Nowhere was this clearer than at work. If a bus backfired nearby, if a passenger dropped something
with a sharp crack, or if diesel exhaust carried a burnt smell through the cab, Liam could feel the
film begin again. He would tighten at the wheel, lose the thread of the present, and afterward spend
his break staring at his phone to force the images down. Even the sound of helicopters over the city
could alter the rest of his shift. He hated how quickly this happened, and each episode left him
more convinced that the danger was real, current, and inescapable.

By the time he sought help, Liam’s world had become smaller and more exhausting than he wanted to
admit. He was still showing up to work, but much of his life outside those routes had collapsed into
avoidance and screen time. What finally brought him in was the realization that he could no longer
tell whether he was protecting himself or disappearing from his own life. He said he wanted, more
than anything, to be able to remember what happened without feeling trapped inside it, and he was
afraid that if nothing changed, the part of him that still cared whether his life was worth living
would go quiet for good.
