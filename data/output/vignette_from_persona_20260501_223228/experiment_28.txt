on his work pants and a bag of tools in the passenger seat. He remembered the red and blue lights
reflecting off the windshield and the abrupt shift from an ordinary drive to a scene that felt

behind him, and he became fixed on the sense that he could not see what was coming next. The worst
part, as he later described it, was not only the pain but the helplessness of being pinned down by
men who were supposed to keep people safe, while every second stretched and he did not know whether
it would stop there.

In the weeks afterward, Dara did not experience the aftermath as something finished. He had come to
feel that danger gathered in ordinary delays and pauses. If his wife took longer than expected to
answer a text, if a foreman did not return his call, if a police cruiser idled too long at an
intersection near the lumber yard, his body reacted as if something was already going wrong. He
found himself scanning rooms automatically and choosing seats in diners or waiting areas where his
back was against the wall and the door stayed in view. Even at home he rarely settled into the
couch. He said he could know, logically, that he was in his own living room with his wife nearby and
still feel unable to relax, as though some part of him was waiting to be caught off guard again.

That constant sense of threat began to shape what he did to get through the day. At work, where he
moved between unfinished houses and crowded supply stores, he started arranging his breaks around
where he could sit facing an entrance. If a delivery ran late or a supervisor said, “Give it a
minute,” his mind jumped immediately to the idea that something bad was unfolding out of sight. In
those moments he tried hard not to think about the roadside assault at all, cutting off images
before they formed and pushing himself back into measuring boards, loading trim, anything concrete.
When that did not work, he turned the blame inward, telling himself that if he had moved faster,
spoken more clearly, or kept his hands visible in exactly the right way, none of it would have
happened. Some nights he used drugs to get a break from the vigilance, because being high was the
only time his body stopped bracing for a few hours. His efforts made sense to him as ways to prevent
another shock or to shut down the spiraling expectation that something terrible was about to happen.

Over time, though, those habits tightened the fear rather than easing it. The more Dara cut off
thoughts of the incident, the less processed it seemed, and small interruptions began carrying the
same edge as that night on the pavement. Because he repeatedly blamed himself, he stayed convinced
there had been a hidden mistake in his behavior that he still needed to identify, which kept him
mentally reviewing the stop without reaching any relief. After using drugs, he often felt even less
steady the next day, more watchful, more dependent on sitting with his back protected and his eyes
on the room. What frightened him most was how quickly his mind filled in danger whenever anything
took too long: a delayed paycheck, his wife lingering at the grocery store, a client not answering
the door right away. He was certain these reactions were keeping him prepared, yet his world had
become smaller and more rigid.

The strain has started to show in his marriage and his work. His wife sees him angle his chair
toward the entrance in restaurants and go quiet when plans change unexpectedly. On jobs, coworkers
have noticed that he becomes tense and irritable during delays, pacing near windows or stepping

him she felt she was living beside someone always waiting for disaster. He agreed with her, though
