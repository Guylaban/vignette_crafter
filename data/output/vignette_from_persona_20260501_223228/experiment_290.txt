Moana Tui is a 46-year-old married forensic analyst who came to treatment more than a year after her
younger brother died suddenly in a violent accident. She had been the family member asked to
identify him. What stayed with her was not a full narrative of that day but a set of brutal
fragments: the fluorescent hum in the viewing room, the sound of her own breath turning ragged, and
the clipped voice of a staff member saying her brother’s name. In her mind, the memory returned as
still pictures rather than a continuous sequence—the edge of the sheet, his hand, the unnatural
color of his face. When it came back, she did not just see it; she sometimes heard the sounds from
that room again, as if they had been preserved intact. The force of it was so immediate that she had
to shut it down, turning away mid-thought or leaving the room because she felt flooded too quickly
to bear it.

In the months after his death, Moana had come to believe that something fundamental had ended. She
said her life had been destroyed by what happened, not in a dramatic sense but as a settled fact:
the future she recognized was gone. She no longer felt any sustaining trust in people, in life, or
in anything larger that might make suffering tolerable. Alongside that was a deadened conviction
that nothing in her life meant much anymore. Those conclusions shaped how the memory sat inside her.
Because she experienced the death as proof that life was empty and unsafe, the fragments of the
viewing room came back with the force of that meaning attached to them, each snapshot feeling less
like a past event than confirmation that everything had been permanently ruined. Even brief contact
with the memory became overwhelming.

Her work made it hard to keep distance from anything associated with death. As a forensic analyst,
she spent her days in labs and offices where case files, evidence logs, and matter-of-fact
discussions about bodies were ordinary. She began avoiding work whenever she could, taking sick
days, arriving late, and sometimes sitting in her car in the parking lot unable to make herself go
in. The sterile lighting and clipped procedural language at work were close enough to the atmosphere
of the identification room that she dreaded being pulled back into those frozen images and sounds.
At home she started staying up very late, scrolling aimlessly or watching television until the early
morning because sleep felt like surrendering control; in the quiet of bed, the pictures and the
remembered voices came fastest. She also began restricting her eating, describing it less as hunger
loss than as a way to keep herself tightly managed, numb, and contained.

These habits gradually tightened around her. By avoiding work and delaying sleep, Moana gave herself
fewer chances to feel steady or competent, and the long nights left her more frayed, more vulnerable
to the memory intruding without warning. Skipping meals added to the sense that she was running on
fumes. When the snapshots returned, they seemed sharper, and the sounds in her head more vivid, at
exactly the times she was most depleted. She would try to think about her brother and immediately
hit that same wall of emotion, as though the memory could only arrive at full intensity. The more
she organized her days around not going in, not sleeping, not eating, the less her life resembled
the one she had before his death, and this seemed to prove to her that the trauma had in fact
destroyed everything worth recognizing.

That conclusion fed the habits further. Because nothing felt meaningful anymore, work seemed
pointless; because she had lost faith in life itself, caring for her body felt irrelevant; because
she was certain her old life was gone, there seemed to be little reason to keep ordinary routines.
Her husband had started pleading with her to eat dinner with him or come to bed before dawn, and she
often withdrew from those conversations with a flat certainty that he could not understand what had
been broken. Missing work had begun to draw attention from supervisors, which only deepened her
sense that the life she had built was collapsing.

By the time she sought help, Moana was frightened by how small and barren her world had become. She
was not coming with much hope in recovery; hope itself felt like something she had lost. But she was
afraid that if nothing changed, she would lose her job, damage her marriage, and remain trapped with
those few unbearable pictures and sounds of her brother’s death as the only parts of him her mind
would allow her to keep.
