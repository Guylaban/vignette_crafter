ministry, visiting parish families in crisis, organizing meals, and sitting with people through
illness and loss. She had once understood herself as someone steady, warm, and deeply sustained by
faith. The rape happened eight months ago after an evening ministry meeting, when she stayed late to
lock up and finish paperwork. In the near-empty building, the quiet of the hallway had felt ordinary
at first. What remains with her now is not a continuous story but a series of fixed images: the
fluorescent light over the side entrance, a hand closing over her mouth, the pattern of the floor
seen sideways, her blouse twisted against her skin. When she tries to speak about it, she stalls and
says she knows exactly what happened but cannot get the words out in order. Each return of those
picture-like moments brings a rush of guilt so intense that she drops her eyes and says she should
have left earlier, should have known, should have done something different. Remembering it this way
has hardened into something broader: shame about her body, a sense that happiness has gone out of
her life for good, and a painful loss of trust not only in people but in the God she had relied on
for years.

Those meanings now shape how she lives with herself. Because she has come to believe that her body
is spoiled and that she deserves to suffer, she refuses herself ordinary comfort. She avoids
relaxing in the evenings with her husband, even on the couch in their own home, because any
softening of vigilance feels wrong and unsafe, almost like letting something happen again. She has
also begun to eat in a tightly controlled way, skipping meals and restricting portions with the
feeling that if she can master hunger, she can at least master something. There is a punishing
quality to this that she recognizes: on bad days she tells herself she should not be allowed ease.
The same beliefs have altered how she reads the world around her. Silence no longer feels peaceful;
it feels loaded, as if danger is gathering just outside awareness. If a colleague is late returning
from a home visit or a parishioner takes too long to answer a knock at the office door, Marisol’s
mind goes immediately to catastrophe. Even when she can state, rationally, that she is in a safe
place, she cannot relax into that knowledge.

Her memory of the assault intensifies this vigilance. In the ministry office, if the hallway
suddenly goes quiet after a burst of activity, one of those still images flashes into her mind and
her body tightens before she has fully understood why. Delays are especially potent because the
attack took shape in an ordinary in-between time, when she had expected to leave and instead was
trapped. When something runs longer than expected, she feels the same helpless dread rising. The
snapshots do not come with a full narrative she can tell herself through; they arrive as fragments,
and in the gap where words should be, threat fills in. Afterward she is often even harsher with
herself, as though the mere fact that she can still be thrown back into those moments proves she has
been permanently changed for the worse.

Over the months, Marisol has built her days around trying not to feel overwhelmed, but those efforts
have narrowed her life and kept the experience raw. At work she avoids sitting alone in a quiet room
between appointments and keeps herself moving through noisy, practical tasks. At home she stays busy
late into the evening folding laundry or cleaning the kitchen because being still feels dangerous.
She controls her eating most rigidly on days when the images are close to the surface, and the self-
punishing thoughts become louder after she remembers. Yet the constant tension leaves her scanning
for danger all the time, more reactive to silence and delay, less able to settle. The less she
allows herself rest, the more foreign and unreachable calm has become. And because she has not been
able to put the assault into words, the memory remains trapped in those stark pictures, returning
with the same guilt and bodily shame each time.

This has changed her marriage and her work. Her husband has begun to say she is physically present
but never at ease, always braced. In the parish, she still shows up for others, but she times visits
to avoid being alone in the building and feels ashamed that a ministry built on trust now unfolds
for her in a world that seems fundamentally unsafe. What finally brought her to treatment was a
recent afternoon when a client was late, the office fell silent, and Marisol felt certain something
terrible was happening; afterward she went home, could not eat, and sat rigid for hours, unable to
pray. She is seeking help because she is frightened that this miserable, watchful version of herself
is all that is left, and because some part of her still hopes she might recover a way to live in her
body, with other people, and with God that does not feel governed by danger.
