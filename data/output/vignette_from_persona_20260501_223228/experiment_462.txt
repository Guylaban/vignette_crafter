United States that, on paper, looked orderly and accomplished. Before the war, she had been visiting
family in the Middle East when fighting moved into their neighborhood. The memory she returned to
most often was not a single explosion but the tense, prolonged days of trying to stay quiet while
armed men moved through nearby streets and apartment buildings. She remembered hearing men’s voices
echoing in the stairwell and courtyard, not loud at first but close enough to make everyone freeze.

listening. Later, when she and her relatives tried to move between buildings, she had the sickening
sense of being tracked, of needing to keep her head down because eyes might be on them from a window
or rooftop. What stayed with her was the feeling that danger announced itself through sound and
watchfulness: a male voice in the wrong tone, footsteps behind her, the unmistakable sensation that
someone had seen her before she saw them.

In the months after she returned home, those cues did not remain in the war zone. They attached
themselves to ordinary moments. At the courthouse, if she heard a voice in the hallway that matched
the cadence or roughness of the men she had heard outside that apartment, she would stiffen before
she even knew why. Walking from the parking garage to her office, she would glance behind her
repeatedly, suddenly sure someone was following too closely. In elevators, in security lines, even
in the glass reflections of the courthouse lobby, she felt watched. These moments set off an
immediate state of alert. Her shoulders tightened, her breathing shortened, and her attention
narrowed to exits, footsteps, and any unexpected sound. She had begun waking in the night and
checking the locks, then checking the windows, fully awake and listening for proof that the house
was secure. Loud or sudden noises could keep her on edge for hours. Over time she came to feel safe
only in a very limited number of places—her bedroom with the door locked, her office with the blinds
positioned just so, and one corner seat in a conference room where no one could come up behind her.

That constant scanning changed how she understood herself and the world. Because she was always
braced, always listening, Nadia had come to believe that this was simply the truth of things: the
world was dangerous, and the people who moved through it safely were the ones who had not yet
learned that lesson. She was unsettled by how little upset it now took to throw her. A clerk
knocking unexpectedly on her office door or a raised voice in a neighboring courtroom could leave
her shaky and irritable, and she took that as evidence that she could no longer cope with ordinary
life. She often said she used to be a happy person, someone who laughed easily with her husband and
colleagues, but now misery felt like her baseline, as if some essential part of her had been
replaced by vigilance.

The more she lived in that state, the more potent the cues became. Because she was sleeping lightly
and waking to check whether she was safe, she moved through the next day already prepared for
danger, noticing voices, footsteps, and glances that others would miss. In the courthouse this had
become especially pronounced. If she heard men talking outside her office, she would stop working
and listen to determine whether they were lingering. When she crossed the parking structure after
work, she was so certain she might be followed that any car slowing near her or any echoing step
behind her seemed to confirm it. Her husband had started calling her name twice before she answered
because the first time, if he approached from another room, she would go rigid at the sound of his
voice. The circle had tightened quietly over time: the more she monitored for threat, the more often
she found it in voices, sounds, and the gaze of other people.

Professionally, the consequences were becoming harder to hide. Nadia still functioned at a high
level in court, but she avoided lingering in hallways, kept her office door shut, and timed her
departures to avoid walking alone with others behind her. Colleagues experienced her as distant. At
home, her husband had grown used to finding her awake at 2 a.m., checking the alarm panel and
peering through the blinds. What finally brought her in was not one dramatic collapse but the
recognition that her life had narrowed to a handful of places where she could exhale. She was
frightened that if she kept living this way, she would lose her marriage and eventually her footing
at work. More than anything, she wanted to know whether she could stop feeling hunted in ordinary
life, and whether the person she remembered as happy was still reachable.
