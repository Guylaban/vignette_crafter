Rohan Patel was 32 when he was mugged on his way home from an evening housekeeping shift at a
downtown hotel. He had finished late after turning over several rooms and taking out bags of
laundry, and he was walking the familiar route to the bus stop with his work keys and a small
envelope of cash from tips in his pocket. A man stepped out from between two parked cars and closed
the distance before Rohan had time to register what was happening. He remembers the sharp shove into
the side of a brick wall, the hand gripping his shirt, the demand to hand everything over, and the
blow that landed across his ribs when he hesitated. What has stayed with him is not only the fear
but the bodily certainty of it: the pressure in his chest as he was pinned, the hot pain along his
side, the sound of the stranger’s voice close to his face. He can picture the streetlight, the
stained cuff of the man’s sleeve, the smell of cigarette smoke. Yet when he tries to tell the story
out loud, he stalls. He says he knows exactly what happened, but when he reaches the worst part,
“the words just go.”

In the weeks that followed, Rohan had come to believe the assault revealed something fundamental
about other people. He was certain that people were not what they seemed, that someone could look
ordinary one second and turn dangerous the next. The city he had once navigated automatically now
felt predatory to him, as if threat were woven into everyday life. That belief made the memory feel
close and ready to strike at any moment. A guest stepping unexpectedly into a hallway at work, a man
lingering by the service entrance, even the scrape of shoes behind him on the sidewalk could pull
the scene back with startling clarity. When it did, he felt the same pain in his ribs and shoulder
as if his body were reliving the attack, and the detail of the memory only seemed to confirm what he
had concluded: the world was dangerous, and trusting appearances was how people got hurt.

The memory, in turn, kept hardening those conclusions. Rohan could not dismiss the mugging as bad
luck because it returned to him with such precision. He would be changing sheets in a hotel room or
pushing his cart down a corridor and suddenly see again the stained sleeve, the angle of the brick
wall against his cheek, the man’s face half-lit under the streetlamp. The physical pain came with it
so vividly that he felt foolish trying to reassure himself he was safe in the present. To him, the
fact that his body reacted this strongly meant the danger had never really passed. Each recurrence
left him more convinced that people could not be trusted and that safety was mostly an illusion
maintained until someone decided to take it away.

At work, this has become especially visible. Housekeeping requires him to move through back
hallways, elevators, loading areas, and parking-adjacent service corridors, and those in-between
spaces now feel to him too much like the street where he was attacked. If he hears footsteps behind
him while carrying linens, his whole body tightens before he turns around. When male guests speak
abruptly or stand too close to ask for towels, he watches their hands and scans for exits, already
bracing for something to happen. He does not describe this as anxiety so much as common sense; in
his mind, being wary is the only reasonable response once one has learned what people are capable
of. And because he keeps seeing the robbery in such exact, painful fragments, he feels he would be
reckless to let his guard down.

His wife has noticed that he is no longer the man who used to chat easily with neighbors or make
small jokes with hotel staff after a long shift. He has become curt with strangers and suspicious
even in ordinary interactions, questioning motives in a way that strains him. He says little about
the mugging at home, not because he has forgotten it but because the memory is too fully there and
too hard to put into language. Sometimes he starts to explain and then stops, pressing a hand to his
side as if the old blow has landed again. On those occasions what frightens him most is not only the
memory itself but what it proves to him about the world: that danger can emerge without warning from
a person who looked unremarkable moments before.

Rohan finally sought help after freezing in a hotel corridor when a guest came around a corner
unexpectedly and asked him for extra hangers. For a few seconds he could not answer. He felt the old
pain in his body, saw the mugger’s face with unbearable clarity, and afterward was shaken by how
completely the past had overtaken the present. He came to treatment exhausted by living as if threat
were always one step away, but also afraid that if he stopped viewing people with suspicion, he
would leave himself open to being hurt again. What he hopes for, cautiously, is not to forget what
happened, but to be able to remember it without feeling trapped inside it.
