Nadia is a 30-year-old Middle Eastern American woman who came to treatment after years of trying to
live inside a world she experiences as narrow and precarious. She grew up in a home where violence
between her parents could erupt without warning. Her clearest sensory impressions are not a tidy
story but flashes: her father’s arm moving too fast, a chair scraping hard across the floor, her
mother’s breath turning ragged, the slam of a door, the metallic click of locks afterward. As a
child she would freeze and listen from the hallway, straining to tell from movement and sound
whether the danger was about to get worse. Even now, when she tries to describe what she witnessed,
she says she knows exactly what happened but cannot find words for it. If she pushes herself to
talk, she becomes so flooded that she has to stop.

Those early experiences left Nadia with a settled conviction that people are not to be trusted, and
that she cannot rely on herself to do the right thing in a crisis. She has come to believe that when
something turns dangerous, she will misread it or freeze, just as she feels she did as a child. That
sense of mistrust and self-doubt keeps her body on alert. Sudden movements by other people,
especially a quick reach across a room or a sharp turn of the shoulders, put her immediately on
guard before she has time to think. She feels safe only in a very small number of places, mainly her
apartment and one back storage area at work where she can see the door. At night she repeatedly
checks the locks and windows, not once but over and over, because she does not trust either other
people or her own first judgment that things are secure.

Her work as a bartender brings these fears into daily life. In the bar, when customers stand
abruptly, swing an arm while talking, or move behind the counter unexpectedly, Nadia’s whole body
tightens. She scans exits and tracks where everyone is standing. Open spaces are particularly hard
for her; being out on the floor during a busy shift, exposed from several directions, feels
intolerably vulnerable, and she finds reasons to stay near enclosed corners or behind the bar.
Because she feels under threat so easily, she tries to manage it by mentally rehearsing worst-case
scenarios—what she would do if a customer became violent, if someone came through the door angry, if
she had to escape through the back. She also works hard to control her emotions, believing that if
she lets herself feel fear or panic, she will lose control completely.

These efforts are meant to keep her safe, but they also keep the trauma close. When Nadia rehearses
disaster in her head, the old scenes come back not as a coherent account but as bodily fragments and
overpowering emotion. A customer’s sudden movement at work can pull up an image of her father’s arm
or the sound of impact before she has words for what she is remembering. Because the memory arrives
in this raw, nonverbal way, it feels immediate and dangerous, which only confirms for her that the
threat is still everywhere. She has noticed that after a shift when she has been especially
watchful, the memories are harder to hold onto long enough to explain; she goes blank, overwhelmed,
and ashamed of not being able to say something so central to her own life.

Over time, the ways Nadia copes have deepened her doubts about herself. She interprets the fact that
she must rehearse every possible catastrophe as proof that she cannot rely on herself naturally. Her
strict effort to control emotion feels, in her mind, like evidence that she is only barely holding
together. Avoiding open spaces has made her life smaller; she turns down invitations, leaves crowded
places early, and chooses routes home that keep her near buildings and away from exposed stretches.
At work, coworkers have noticed she is most comfortable in restricted parts of the bar and can seem
distant when the room gets busy. Nadia reads this as further confirmation that she cannot trust
herself around people and cannot trust them either.

The memories also feed that same conclusion. Each time she cannot put the childhood violence into
words, she feels defective and uncertain, as though not being able to narrate it means she never
really understood what was happening and still would not know what to do now. And because the
memories are so overwhelming when they surface, she becomes even more vigilant for signs of danger,
more certain that safety exists only in a few tightly controlled places. By the time she sought
help, she was exhausted from living this way—checking locks repeatedly, bracing at sudden movements,
silently playing out disasters before every shift. What finally brought her in was the fear that her
world was shrinking to just a few safe zones, and the hope, still tentative, that she might learn
how to remember what happened without being swallowed by it and without feeling that danger is
waiting in every movement around her.
