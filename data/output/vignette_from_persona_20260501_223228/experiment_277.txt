Kwame Mensah is a 47-year-old park ranger who came to treatment after months of telling himself he
should be able to handle it on his own. He grew up in West Africa and, as a civilian during a period
of armed conflict, survived an attack in a rural area where brush had been set alight and people
fled through fields and trees. In the first session he spoke haltingly about the worst part: being
ordered at gunpoint to help men he feared, doing things he now experiences as a betrayal of the
person he believed himself to be, and then living when others around him did not. What remains most
vivid to him is the orange movement of flames along dry ground, the smell of smoke in open air, and
the feeling that danger could be hidden anywhere in a landscape that looked peaceful until it
suddenly was not.

For years he kept working outdoors, eventually becoming a park ranger in the United States, but the
setting that once felt meaningful now catches him off guard. A small campfire at a legal fire ring,
sunlight flashing like flame through dry grass, or even being corrected by a supervisor on trail
procedure can jolt him into the same constricted state. When visitors burn brush illegally or he
sees flames at the edge of woodland, he feels his chest tighten before he has fully registered what
he is looking at. On patrol in the woods, especially in isolated stretches that resemble the rural
terrain where the attack happened, he becomes unable to relax despite knowing, intellectually, that
he is in a protected park and not a war zone. If a coworker is late answering the radio, or a
maintenance crew takes longer than expected to check in, he quickly assumes something bad has
happened.

Those moments do not stop with fear. Being corrected by someone in authority can carry an immediate
sting of shame, as if criticism proves what he already suspects about himself: that he failed
morally and survived at others’ expense. Fire in the landscape and time alone in nature also pull
him toward memories of the people who died and toward the part of the event he cannot forgive in
himself. He has come to believe he is set apart from other people, not simply because they do not
know what happened, but because if they did, they would see him as contaminated by what he did to
stay alive. In turn, these beliefs make the world feel less safe. At night he lies awake replaying
who was lost and what he did, and the guilt keeps his body keyed up. Even minor delays begin to feel
ominous, as though disaster is always just out of sight.

Over time Kwame has organized his life around getting through these reactions. He has cut off most
contact with friends and family, ignoring calls rather than trying to explain himself. He drinks in
the evenings to blunt the agitation enough to get a few hours of sleep. Although his work brings him
into regular contact with law enforcement in the park, he changes his route when he sees police
uniforms, avoids joint assignments that involve armed officers, and keeps his distance from anything
that reminds him of weapons. When criticism, flames, or certain wooded areas set him off,
withdrawing and drinking feel like the quickest ways to get out of his own skin.

But these habits have narrowed his life and deepened what he believes about himself. The more
completely he isolates, the more convinced he becomes that he does not belong with other people.
Avoiding officers and anything linked to weapons leaves those sights feeling even more charged when
he cannot escape them. Drinking leaves him groggy and ashamed in the morning, and after poor sleep
he is even more watchful on the job, scanning the tree line and reacting strongly if someone is
delayed or if smoke appears in the distance. In that state, being in the park itself becomes more
loaded; ordinary natural settings increasingly feel like places where something terrible is about to
happen. He notices criticism more sharply too, bracing for it and hearing correction as accusation.

What finally brought him in was an incident at work when a controlled burn was being discussed and a
supervisor tersely corrected him in front of others. Kwame left his shift early, drank heavily that
night, and did not answer his phone for two days. He said he is frightened that he is becoming
impossible to work with and that if this continues he will lose the one part of his life that still
gives him structure. At the same time, he is hoping—carefully, almost reluctantly—that treatment
might help him sleep, stop living as if every delay means catastrophe, and face the woods he works
in without feeling that the past is waiting there for him.
