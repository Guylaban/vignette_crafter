Priya is a 23-year-old UX designer who came to treatment months after a car accident in which she
had been driving and seriously injured a pedestrian. She describes the moment with a painful
precision that has not softened with time: she had been turning through a busy downtown intersection
after work, tired, thinking about a deadline, when she saw movement too late and felt the sickening
impact. She remembers the pedestrian on the pavement, the unnatural stillness and then the sudden
crowd, someone shouting for an ambulance, and the glimpse of a young woman kneeling beside him. What
has stayed with her most is not only that she was the driver, but the ordinary setting of it — the
office district, the end-of-day traffic, people in work clothes crossing under streetlights — so
that parts of daily life now feel contaminated by that scene.

Since then, Priya has found that the workplace itself can set everything off. Her office is in a
similar downtown area, and even arriving at the building can tighten her chest: the parking garage,
the crosswalk outside, the sight of coworkers stepping into traffic with laptops and coffee cups can
pull her back toward the accident. She is especially thrown by reminders of the injured man and by
strangers who resemble him in age, build, or dress. On days when she notices someone outside the
office who looks like him, or catches herself picturing his body on the road, she starts mentally
circling the same question: how exactly could she have prevented it? She replays the light, the
angle of the turn, whether she should have left work later, braked sooner, looked twice. At the same
time, she tries to force the images away, telling herself not to think about it, and she has largely
stopped driving at all, refusing even to be a passenger if she can help it. Her husband now does
most of the transportation, and she pays for rideshares to avoid sitting in a car with the sense
that it could happen again.

Over the following months these habits hardened rather than eased things. The more Priya tried to
shut the thoughts down, the more abruptly they seemed to return when she was at work or passing
through the parts of the city that resembled the crash site. She can spend a lunch break trying not
to picture the victim, only to find that a man in similar clothes entering the elevator brings the
image back in full force. Her hours as a UX designer have become more complicated because commuting
to the office has become an ordeal; she often negotiates remote days or arrives visibly tense after
a rideshare route through downtown. In meetings she can lose the thread when her mind starts
calculating alternate timelines in which no one was hurt. She knows this rumination goes nowhere,
but she feels compelled by it, as if finding the precise missed moment might somehow make what
happened less unbearable.

Avoiding cars has also made the world feel narrower and, in an unexpected way, has left the triggers
more potent. Because she no longer drives and rarely rides with others, she has had less chance to
discover whether she can tolerate roads, intersections, and pedestrians without catastrophe.
Instead, each necessary trip feels exceptional and charged. If a rideshare slows for a crosswalk
near her office, she becomes rigid and stares straight ahead, trying to blank her mind; later that
same day she may be even more alert to anyone who resembles the injured man. The office has stopped
being just a workplace and become one of the places where she braces for reminders. What frightened
her most was how quickly an ordinary workday could collapse into mental replay, and she had come to
believe that if she let herself think about the accident freely, or got into a car casually, she
would either fall apart or prove she had learned nothing.

Priya finally sought help after her husband told her their life was shrinking around the accident.
He had begun arranging his own schedule around driving her, and she had turned down an in-person
design workshop because it required traveling across the city. She was ashamed of how much of her
day was now spent either trying not to think about the injured man or going over, again and again,
how she should have prevented his injuries. Sitting in the intake, she said she wanted to be able to
go to work without scanning every street corner for someone who looked like him, and to sit in a car
without feeling she was stepping back into the worst moment of her life. What she fears most is that
this means she is still dangerous; what she hopes, more quietly, is that she might someday move
through her own city without every reminder turning her back toward that intersection.
