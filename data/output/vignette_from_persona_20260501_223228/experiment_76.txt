turning before his mind caught up, the atmosphere of waiting for the next blast or burst of gunfire.
He had learned to scan constantly—windows, doorways, rooftops, the posture of strangers nearby—and
by the time he returned home, his body no longer seemed to know how to stand down.

In the months afterward, Mateo found that the sense of threat followed him into ordinary American
life with a force that embarrassed and frightened him. In the open-plan office where he worked as a
product manager, he sat angled toward the room so he could keep colleagues in view. If someone came
quickly around his desk or reached across him toward a monitor, his whole body snapped before he
could think; his shoulders clenched, his chest tightened, and he reacted as if he were about to be
hit. He was watchful in meetings, in elevators, in coffee lines, tracking other people’s movements
before he could focus on what they were saying. What frightened him most was how physical it felt.
He was constantly tense and ready, as if danger were already underway somewhere inside his muscles.

That persistent alarm gradually pushed him into habits he half knew made no sense and half felt he
could not do without. When the tension surged, Mateo would check his body repeatedly for signs that
something had happened to him—pressing at sore muscles, looking for injuries, monitoring his
breathing and heartbeat, trying to determine whether he was actually safe. He also began texting his
sister and a close friend over and over, asking versions of the same question: Did he sound okay,
did they think he was fine, was this just stress. On the worst nights, when the pressure in him felt
unbearable and he could not get his body to come down, he hurt himself physically to release it. He
did not describe this as wanting to die; to him it was a desperate way to interrupt the feeling that
he was braced for catastrophe every second.

Mateo checked his body, the more sensations he found—tightness, jolts, soreness, a racing pulse—and
each one became further proof that something was wrong. After he asked for reassurance, he might
feel steadier for a few minutes, but very quickly he was scanning again for the next sign that he
was not okay, and needing to ask all over again. Even the self-inflicted pain gave only a brief drop
in pressure before leaving him more on edge, more attentive to his body, more convinced that he had
to stay prepared. He had come to live in a narrowing loop in which his attempts to get control made
him more watchful, and that heightened watchfulness made the need to do something—anything—feel
urgent again.

At work, the consequences had become visible. During product reviews he lost track of conversations
because a coworker shifting suddenly in a chair could make him flinch hard enough to miss several
minutes. He lingered over Slack messages to colleagues he trusted, fishing indirectly for
reassurance that he was handling things normally. He took extra trips to the restroom to inspect
himself in the mirror and calm the sense that his body was signaling danger. Because he was single
and lived alone, evenings were often the hardest; there was no one physically present to tell him he
was safe, and the apartment’s small ordinary noises kept his body on alert.

By the time he sought help, Mateo was exhausted by how relentlessly vigilant he had become and
ashamed of the ways he had started trying to cope. He said he was tired of reacting to ordinary
movement as though he were back in a war zone, tired of needing other people to tell him he was
okay, tired of living as if his body were an unreliable warning system he had to monitor every
minute. What he feared most was that this was permanent—that he would always be the person who could
not relax in a room, who startled at a colleague passing behind him, who had to hurt himself or ask
for reassurance just to get through the night. What he hoped for, though he said it cautiously, was
to feel ordinary danger as ordinary again, and to be able to work, sleep, and inhabit his own body
without bracing for the next threat.
