Nabil is a 48-year-old fashion designer who sought treatment months after surviving a man-made
disaster at an industrial building where he had gone for a fabric sourcing meeting. He describes the
event in jagged, disconnected pieces rather than a single continuous memory: a hallway, a shouted
warning, the violent jolt of impact, the sudden confusion of people pushing toward an exit. What
stays with him most vividly is not a coherent sequence but sensory fragments that return with
disturbing force. When the memory breaks through, he can taste the metallic, dusty air from that day
so clearly that he stops and swallows as if it were in his mouth again, and the scene does not feel
remembered so much as immediate, as though it is happening in the present.

In the weeks afterward, those fragments began attaching themselves to ordinary sensations. A draft
of air conditioning across his forearms at the studio, the wrong coolness on his skin in a fitting
room, or a wave of unexplained nausea could abruptly pull the event into his mind. At other times
the memory appeared first, out of nowhere, and brought the rest with it: the taste, the bodily
sickness, the certainty of being back there. Because the memory arrived in disconnected pieces, he
never felt able to place it safely in the past, and small bodily sensations became potent warnings
that another engulfing episode was beginning.

These moments changed how he moved through the world. Nabil now enters every showroom, restaurant,
and client office by first locating exits and imagining how he would get out if something happened.
He says he cannot settle until he has mapped the doors, stairwells, and windows. When a sudden
intrusive image strikes, or when that particular chill touches his skin and nausea rises, his
attention narrows immediately to the environment around him. He scans faces, doorways, and corners
for signs that something is wrong, and in meetings he loses the thread of conversation because part
of him is still searching for danger. The same fractured, present-tense quality of the memory keeps
him watchful; because it feels unfinished and still happening, he is certain he cannot afford to
relax.

That vigilance has steadily shaped his daily habits. At work, where deadlines and fittings once
demanded creative concentration, he now keeps himself in constant motion, filling every open minute
with sketch revisions, fabric pulls, emails, and unnecessary tasks because being busy feels safer
than being still long enough for the memory to break through. Before bed he checks locks, doors, and
windows repeatedly, sometimes circling back several times even after telling himself he has already
looked. On the worst nights, when the memory arrives with the taste and bodily immediacy of the
disaster and he feels overwhelmed by dread, he has hurt himself physically to force a different
sensation and regain a sense of control. His threat monitoring feeds these behaviors directly: once
he has spent an evening scanning for risk and rehearsing escape, checking and overworking feel like
the only ways to keep catastrophe from catching him off guard.

Over time, those efforts have not brought relief so much as tightened the pattern. The more
relentlessly Nabil keeps busy, the less space he has to make sense of what happened, and the memory
remains broken into sharp, unintegrated pieces that ambush him. Repeatedly checking the apartment
and organizing escape routes gives him brief reassurance, but it also keeps the idea of ongoing
danger active, so that he notices every sound in the hallway and every shift in temperature against
his skin. Even at the studio, he positions himself near the exit during design reviews and is only
half available to his team, his concentration broken by scanning the room and monitoring his body
for nausea. When he cannot stop these episodes, the return of the memory feels even more immediate
and convincing.

By the time he came for help, the cost had become difficult to ignore. His staff had started
noticing that he drifted during consultations and interrupted meetings to move around the room or
check the door. At home, nights were consumed by locking rituals and long stretches of restless
alertness. Nabil is divorced and lives alone, and he described feeling ashamed of how much of his
day is organized around preventing another disaster. What finally brought him in was the fear that
he was disappearing into a life structured entirely by anticipation and reaction—that he would lose
his work, his focus, and any sense that the event was truly over. More than anything, he wants the
memory to feel like something that happened, not something that keeps happening, and he is
frightened by how completely his body and mind still act as if the danger is present.
