event before finally recounting being forcibly held in a locked interior room by someone she had
initially trusted in a work-related setting. What stayed with her most vividly was not only the fear
that she might be hurt, but the crushing sense that there were no options: the door would not open,
the space felt airless, and every attempt to think clearly collapsed into the realization that she
could not leave when she wanted to. She remembers straining her body against the door and wall until
pain shot through her neck, and now that same neck pain can pull her back into the moment with
alarming speed.

In the weeks that followed, the world narrowed around cues that resembled that helpless confinement.
At work, if a conference room door clicked shut during a project meeting, Marisol felt her body
react before her mind caught up; her shoulders tightened, her breathing shortened, and she scanned
for the exit even when colleagues were simply reviewing timelines. Riding an elevator in her office
building became intolerable. If she found herself in any locked or confined room, or even caught in
a situation where she felt she had no good options, she would experience an immediate surge of alarm
as though danger were present again. The neck pain was especially potent. A brief spasm at her desk
could make her jolt upright, certain for a second that something terrible was about to happen. She
began saying that she could not relax even in places she knew were safe, and she was unsettled by
how often she reacted as if under threat when nothing was actually wrong, including in her own
apartment at night.

Those same moments also fed the bleak conclusions she had drawn about herself and the world. Each
time she froze in a closed meeting room or panicked when the deadbolt clicked behind her at home,
Marisol took it as proof that the person she had been was gone. She used to think of herself as
capable and warm, but now she described herself as always miserable, as if happiness belonged to
someone she could no longer access. She had also come to believe that harm could come from anyone,
because she no longer trusted her ability to know who was safe. As that belief hardened, even
ordinary situations took on the emotional shape of entrapment; a delayed subway door, a long
checkout line, or a supervisor asking her to stay in a room to finish a discussion could all feel
charged before anything had actually happened.

Her sense that the world was fundamentally unsafe kept her body in a constant state of readiness.
Marisol said that even at home she checked locks repeatedly and listened for sounds in the hallway,
unable to settle into her couch or fall asleep without feeling on guard. Because she felt danger
could come from anywhere and that she was no longer really alive inside, she treated every enclosed
space and every stab of neck pain as something to monitor closely. The more she watched for these
signals, the more readily they seemed to appear. A small muscular ache after a day at the computer
became ominous; the hum of the bathroom fan with the door closed could suddenly feel like
confinement.

When this constant alarm surged, Marisol tried to manage it in ways that gave temporary mental
distance but left her more trapped afterward. She would tell herself the captivity had not really
happened the way she remembered, or that it was not that bad, especially after a false alarm left
her embarrassed at work. At other times she lay awake going over every detail, trying to make sense
of what she had missed, or she turned the whole event inward and dwelled on what she had done wrong.
Being so on edge seemed to demand explanation and control, but the more she overanalyzed and blamed
herself, the more convinced she became that she had lost something essential in herself and that the
world was full of hidden harm. Trying to talk herself out of the event did not calm her for long
either; instead it left her feeling strangely dead inside, as though she had to disconnect from her
own experience to get through the day.

That combination has tightened over time. On bad days, Marisol arrives at the office early to avoid
crowded elevators and asks for meeting-room doors to be left ajar, though she gives practical
excuses rather than admitting how frightened she feels. At home she stays vigilant long after she
has checked that she is alone. A flare of neck pain can set off a chain in which she braces for
danger, tells herself she is overreacting, then spends hours replaying the captivity and criticizing
herself for not having escaped sooner. By the time she sought help, what frightened her most was not
only the memories but the conviction that she would never feel safe anywhere again, not even in her
own home, and that the happy, decisive woman she had once been might be permanently out of reach.
