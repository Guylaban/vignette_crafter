memory does not come as a narrative she can hold at a distance; it arrives with the same terror,
wordless and immediate, and that strangeness has helped convince her that something in her has been
permanently altered for the worse.

In the weeks after the rape, Amina found that the things that pulled her back toward the assault
were oddly specific and difficult to explain to people around her. A child crying in the hallway of

her stomach turn instantly. Moments of uncertainty are just as potent: a late text from a parent
changing a lesson time, an unfamiliar voice in a house, not knowing who else is home. The sensation
of nausea itself has become a warning sign she dreads. Each time one of these moments hits, she has
come to believe it proves how fragile she now is. If a crying child or a brief spell of not knowing
what to expect can undo her so quickly, then in her mind it means she cannot handle even the
slightest upset.

Because she is so certain that thinking about the rape will overwhelm her, Amina has organized her
days around not giving it room. She repeatedly blames herself in long, punishing loops—rehearsing
what she wore, why she stayed late, whether she should have noticed danger sooner—as if finding the
mistake might make the event containable. She also steers away from anything that might make her
feel happy or hopeful, declining outings with her partner and brushing off small celebrations after
good teaching days because the lightness feels unsafe and undeserved. When friends or family edge
toward asking what happened, she changes the subject or leaves. These habits make sense to her as
protection; she believes that if she lets herself think directly about the assault, or lets herself
feel too much of anything, she will lose control.

Yet the way she avoids has left the memory even less speakable. The more she cuts off conversations
and turns from reminders, the less the experience feels like something she can place in words.
Instead it stays in disconnected pieces that rush back with full terror. After nights spent mentally
circling self-blaming questions, she often wakes already tense and nauseated, and ordinary
uncertainty the next day feels charged before anything has happened. In tutoring sessions she has
become acutely alert to sounds in the home, especially children crying in another room, and she
reads her own bodily reaction as further evidence that she is damaged beyond repair. Avoiding
hopeful moments has narrowed her life with her partner; he has begun to describe her as absent even
when she is beside him, and she privately fears he is seeing the ruined version of her that she
feels she has become.

At work, this has become concrete. Amina used to enjoy traveling between students’ homes and
adapting easily to family routines. Now she asks for as much information as possible in advance and
feels panicked when plans are uncertain. If she hears a child crying as she arrives, she sometimes
sits in her car until the lesson start time, fighting nausea and trying not to think. If a parent
casually mentions a neighborhood incident or asks a question that brushes against safety, she
becomes quiet and shut down for the rest of the session. Afterwards she may spend hours blaming
herself for reacting, which only deepens her conviction that she cannot cope.

By the time she sought help, Amina felt trapped by the very measures she had been using to get

effort to avoid being overwhelmed seemed to leave her more easily undone. She came because she is
frightened that this is permanent—that a child crying, a wave of nausea, or any moment of
uncertainty will always be enough to bring the terror back—and because, despite that fear, part of
her still hopes there might be a way to remember what happened without disappearing into it.
