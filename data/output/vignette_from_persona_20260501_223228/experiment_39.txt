For most of his career, Minh Tran had been the one people called in crisis. At 68, a widowed public
defender with a reputation for steadiness, he was used to walking into courthouse holding cells and
hearing frightened, angry stories without losing his bearings. That changed after his adult daughter
was killed during a home invasion six months ago. He was not there when it happened, but he arrived
soon afterward, and what has stayed with him is not a coherent sequence so much as shattered
fragments: the front door ajar, a lamp overturned, the sound of a neighbor crying in the hallway,
and, most piercingly, a short burst of his daughter’s voice on the 911 recording that police later
played for him. In his mind, the memories do not settle into a single account. They come in jagged
pieces, and when they do he hears sounds as if they are happening again—the panic in her voice, the
banging at the door, the operator asking questions no one could answer in time. Each return of the
memory is soaked in overwhelming grief.

In the first weeks after the funeral, Minh tried to manage those intrusions in the practical way he
approached everything else. He stopped watching the evening news and turned off legal dramas or
crime shows as soon as a break-in or assault appeared. He began eating constantly in the late
evenings, telling himself that a full stomach gave him something else to feel besides the hollow
drop that came when his daughter’s voice intruded. The more the memory arrived in those broken
sensory flashes, the more urgently he looked for ways to block it out. He could not bear anything on
television or in the newspaper that resembled what had happened to her, because even a brief story
about violence was enough to pull him back into those disconnected moments and the grief they
carried.

Those same fragments also left him feeling as if danger were immediate and unfinished. Because he
could not hold the event in his mind as something completed, he reacted to ordinary sounds as if
they signaled another attack. A knock in his apartment building, footsteps outside his door, voices
rising in the street at night could set off a surge of fear before he had even oriented himself. He
became preoccupied with locks and windows, checking them repeatedly before bed and again after
waking in the night, not because he thought through a specific threat each time but because his body
seemed convinced that someone could force their way in at any moment. What frightened him most was
how quickly he went from sitting in his armchair to standing breathless in the hallway, certain for
a second that something was wrong.

That sense of danger spread into his work. At the courthouse, Minh found himself scanning faces in
waiting areas and elevators, vigilant for men who seemed agitated, intoxicated, or unpredictable. He
had spent decades around distressed defendants and distraught families, but now he watched people
with a strained, suspicious attention that exhausted him. When a client raised his voice in an
interview room, Minh felt the old jolt of alarm and afterward checked his office door twice before
leaving. Because he felt perpetually on edge, he leaned harder on the habits that seemed to contain
it: more checking, more avoidance of media, more eating after work to settle himself. For an hour or
two these things dulled the fear, but they also kept his world organized around the possibility of
danger, so that every unvetted sound or unfamiliar person seemed to require even more vigilance.

The cycle tightened over the following months. Nights were the worst. After checking the deadbolt,
he would lie down, hear a creak in the building, and suddenly the broken pieces of the night of his
daughter’s death would flare up again, especially the sounds. He would get back out of bed to
inspect the windows, then stand in the kitchen eating cereal or leftover rice straight from the
container, trying to push the audio fragments out of his head. By morning he was ashamed of how
little control he had, yet the poor sleep and constant alertness made the memories easier to trigger
again the next day. Even at work, after reading case files involving assault or burglary, he would
avoid related coverage online and leave his office to make another round of checking the door, all
while the same clipped sounds replayed internally.

Lately, Minh has started to worry that he is becoming someone his daughter would not recognize: a
man who mistrusts strangers, startles at harmless noises, and spends his evenings circling his
apartment to make sure it is secure. The change has begun to affect his work; he has shortened
client meetings, avoided certain assignments when he can, and once snapped at an investigator who
came up behind him unexpectedly in the office. He finally sought help after finding himself frozen
at home one night, hand on the lock, hearing his daughter’s voice so vividly that he could not tell
for a moment whether it was memory or something happening outside his own door. He says he is afraid
that if this continues, he will never again feel that the world is anything but moments away from
taking another person he loves, and he hopes, cautiously, that treatment might help him remember his
daughter without being dragged back into the sound of losing her.
