Marisol is a 22-year-old accountant who came to treatment months after her four-year-old nephew was
rushed to the hospital with a sudden, life-threatening infection. She had been the one watching him
that afternoon in her sister’s apartment while her sister was at work. What she remembers most
clearly is not a sequence she can easily tell from beginning to end, but flashes that still feel
lodged in her body: his skin suddenly too hot under her hands, the unnatural quiet when he stopped
crying and went limp against her shoulder, the metallic, sour taste of stale coffee and bile in the
back of her throat as she waited for the ambulance and tried to keep him awake. Even now she says
she cannot really “tell the story right.” When she starts to approach it, her mind jerks away before
she can get to the worst part, and she is left with fragments rather than a complete account.

In the weeks after he survived and eventually recovered, Marisol found that certain moments pulled
those fragments back with alarming force. Silence became especially hard to bear. If a room went
unusually quiet, she would feel a rush of alarm before she even knew why, as if quiet itself meant
that something terrible had already started. At work in the accounting office, when the usual
tapping of keyboards stopped or a colleague took too long to answer from the next cubicle, her chest
tightened and her stomach flipped. Delays had a similar effect. If her sister did not text back
quickly, if a report took longer to load on her screen, if a call was placed on hold for too long,
she was immediately certain that something bad was unfolding somewhere out of sight. In those
moments her body reacted first—heart pounding, breath turning shallow, hands going cold—and the
sensation brought her right back toward the hospital day. The taste would return to her mouth so
vividly that she would swallow hard and look around in confusion, and then just as quickly she would
force the whole memory back down before it could fully form.

Because the memory came back in this broken, wordless way, Marisol had come to believe something
bleak about herself and her life. The fact that she could not put the event into words made it feel
less like something finished in the past and more like proof that she had never really escaped it.
When she shut the memory down before it was complete, she was left with the rawest pieces—her nephew
limp, the waiting, the taste in her mouth—and those pieces seemed to confirm what frightened her
most: that she could not stop bad things from happening. She said she used to be a happy person,
easy with friends and quick to laugh, but now she felt miserable in a steady, flattened way that she
could not shake. Around other people her age, especially coworkers talking casually about weekend
plans, she felt set apart, as though everyone else had been allowed to remain young while she had
learned too early that disaster could arrive without warning.

That sense of danger has narrowed her life. At the office she checks her phone repeatedly for
messages from family, and when a reply is delayed she can no longer focus on spreadsheets because
her mind has already leapt to the emergency room. If the floor around her falls silent, she feels
the same surge of dread and the same bodily jolt, followed by those sensory scraps of the day her
nephew nearly died. She tries to interrupt it by keeping background noise on, stepping to the
restroom, getting water, anything to avoid letting the memory gather enough shape to become fully
visible. The relief lasts only briefly. What remains is the conviction that she is always bracing
for the next catastrophe and that other people do not understand how quickly a normal afternoon can
turn.

Marisol finally sought help after a minor incident at work frightened her. A supervisor was late to
a scheduled meeting, and during the unexpected quiet in the conference room she became so
overwhelmed by the physical rush and the taste in her mouth that she had to leave abruptly and lock
herself in a bathroom stall. She was embarrassed that she could not explain what had happened; even
to herself, the experience still came more as sensations than words. What she fears most is that
this miserable, cut-off version of her is permanent, that she will always be the person waiting for
terrible news. What she hopes, though she says it cautiously, is to be able to remember what
happened without feeling that danger is happening all over again.
