Omar is a 41-year-old Middle Eastern American man who has spent the last several years working the
late shift at a fast food restaurant, most often at the counter and drive-through where the noise
comes in bursts—timers beeping, fryers hissing, customers raising their voices over the din. He
traces the beginning of his current difficulties to an evening about a year ago, near closing time,
when he witnessed a violent assault in the parking lot behind the restaurant. He had stepped outside
to take cardboard to the dumpster when he heard shouting: one man angrily correcting another, his
voice sharp and humiliating, followed by the sudden crack of something metal striking pavement and
then a burst of yelling. When Omar turned, he saw one man on the ground and another standing over
him, shouting as if every delay, every movement, were an insult. The scene unfolded in a rush of
noise and helplessness. What stayed with Omar was not only the image of the injured man, but the
atmosphere of danger arriving all at once through sound—raised voices, harsh correction, the
explosive clatter in the dark—at roughly the same late-evening hour he still works now.

In the weeks afterward, Omar noticed that certain moments seemed to drop him back into that parking
lot. If a manager corrected him in a clipped tone during the dinner rush, he felt his body tense
before he had fully registered the words. If a customer banged a fist on the counter or a tray
crashed in the kitchen, he would jerk upright, scanning before he knew what he was looking for. The
same time of night was especially difficult. As the shift moved toward closing and the restaurant
emptied out, he became increasingly uneasy, as though something bad was waiting in the narrowing
quiet. These triggers did not remain brief reminders for him; they sharpened his sense that danger
was close. Being criticized no longer felt like ordinary workplace feedback but like the first sign
that a situation could suddenly turn volatile, and loud noises convinced him he needed to be ready
immediately. By late evening, if an order was taking longer than expected or a coworker did not come
back from taking trash out right away, Omar was certain something had gone wrong.

Over months, that sense of threat narrowed his world. Omar had come to believe that he was safe only
in a very limited number of places: his apartment, his older sister’s living room, and a small
storage area at work where he could close the door for a moment if the front counter became too
chaotic. Everywhere else felt exposed. At work, delays became especially difficult. If the headset
went silent too long in the drive-through, if a delivery driver was late, if the manager disappeared
into the office for more than a few minutes, his mind filled in the gap with catastrophe. He would
glance repeatedly toward the doors and windows, listening for raised voices or a sudden impact.
Unexpected sounds carried a special force. A slammed freezer door or a dropped basket of utensils
could leave him rigid, heart pounding, eyes fixed on the source of the noise as if he needed to
determine immediately whether violence was about to break out.

This has begun to affect how he functions with other people. Coworkers have noticed that Omar
becomes stiff and watchful when corrected, even over small things like the order of the prep station
or the timing on fries. He does not argue; instead, he grows quiet and inward, as if bracing for
more. Near closing time he tries to stay inside and avoids taking trash out unless someone goes with
him. He has turned down extra evening shifts because the later it gets, the more he feels himself
waiting for the same kind of eruption he witnessed that night. He is single and spends most of his
time outside work either at home or with family, not because he describes himself as uninterested in
other parts of life, but because those are the places where his body loosens enough for him to
breathe normally.

By the time he came for help, Omar was exhausted by how small his safe world had become. What
finally pushed him to seek treatment was an incident at work when a supervisor sharply corrected him
during the late shift just as a metal pan crashed in the kitchen. He found himself frozen, staring
toward the back door, convinced for several seconds that something terrible was happening again. He
was shaken by how quickly his mind still goes there, especially when the clock approaches that hour.
What frightens him most is the feeling that he can no longer trust ordinary delays or ordinary
sounds—that at any moment he will miss the sign that something bad is beginning. What he hopes for,
quietly, is not to stop remembering what he witnessed, but to stop living as if it is always about
to happen again.
