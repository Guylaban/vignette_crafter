Nabil is a 56-year-old married man who works as a social media manager for a regional restaurant
group, spending most days monitoring feeds, drafting posts, and responding quickly to public
comments. He traces the start of his current difficulties to an encounter with police outside a
downtown protest nearly a year ago. He had not gone looking for trouble; he had stayed after work to
meet a friend for coffee and was caught in the churn of people being pushed off the street. He
remembers officers shouting contradictory commands, the crush of bodies, and being driven face-first
against the hood of a car while someone forced his jaw open as he gasped for air. What still comes
back most sharply is the metallic taste in his mouth—blood and pepper spray mingled together—so
vividly that when the memory breaks through he can taste it again. In those moments he is flooded
with rage. He also cannot bear to stay with the memory for long, cutting it off before he can recall
it fully, as if letting it continue would make the whole scene happen again.

In the weeks afterward, Nabil became preoccupied with what the incident meant. He had come to
believe that people were not what they seemed, especially those in authority, and that the world was
fundamentally dangerous in ways he had once tried to deny. What cut deepest was his conviction that
he had acted against his own values. He had yelled back, cursed, and in his panic shoved another man
who was trying to steady himself nearby; afterward he fixated on that moment with shame, telling
himself he had become someone he did not respect. From these conclusions, his habits began to
narrow. Because he felt that danger could come from ordinary places and hidden intentions, he
started avoiding television, films, news clips, and articles about protests or police encounters. He
withdrew from anything emotionally stirring more generally, not only upsetting material but even
family celebrations or sentimental movies, because strong feelings of any kind seemed capable of
opening something he no longer trusted himself to manage.

That same sense of violation and danger left him living as though threat were always close. Even in
his own home he could not fully relax; his shoulders stayed tight and he found himself listening for
movement outside. At restaurants, in stores, in his office building, he scanned entrances, watched
people’s hands, and would not settle until he had identified how to get out quickly. He was certain
that vigilance was the only reasonable response in a world where appearances could not be trusted.
At work this became especially concrete: when reviewing trending content he now keeps one browser
tab minimized and positioned near the exit of his open-plan office, sitting where he can see who
approaches from behind. If a video thumbnail shows flashing patrol lights or a crowd scene, his body
reacts before he can think. The taste returns to his mouth, anger surges, and he feels a jolt of
fear and horror that leaves him restless for hours, unable to come down even when he knows he is
physically safe.

As these reactions intensified, they fed back into what he believed. The fact that he could not
relax in harmless settings seemed to prove to him that the world really was dangerous. His constant
route-checking and scanning did not feel excessive to him; they felt like evidence that he had
finally seen reality clearly. And each time he noticed how quickly rage could overtake him, he took
it as further proof that the encounter had changed him into someone less decent, less restrained.

Over months, Nabil’s efforts to control this became more rigid. He stopped watching television with
his wife because he could not tolerate unexpected footage or even emotionally charged scenes. He
curbed dinners out unless he could choose the table and map the exits first. He tries to flatten his
whole inner life, avoiding not just distress but joy, tenderness, and excitement, because any
feeling seems too close to the ones he dreads. Yet this emotional shutting-down has not quieted the
memory. If anything, pushing it away before it is complete leaves it jagged and unfinished,
returning in sensory fragments—the taste, the pressure, the flash of rage—without context or
resolution. Afterward he is more watchful than ever, and more convinced that people conceal danger
and that he failed some basic test of character that day.

His wife urged him to seek help after he left a family gathering abruptly, insisting on checking
side doors and pacing outside because he could not calm himself. Nabil comes to treatment exhausted
by the effort of staying on guard and by how small his life has become. What frightens him most is
not only that the world is unsafe, but that he may never again trust his own reactions. What he
hopes, though he says it cautiously, is to be able to sit with his wife in an ordinary room and feel
like he is actually there instead of bracing for the next blow.
