Omar Haddad, a 40-year-old software engineer, came to treatment nearly a year after a major
earthquake struck during a workday in his downtown office building. He had been in a conference room
on the ninth floor when the first violent shudder ran through the walls. What stayed with him most
was not a single image but a jumble of sensory fragments: the metallic groan of the building, the
crash of ceiling panels, the shrill alarms, the sound of people shouting over one another, and the
feeling of the floor moving under him as if it had turned liquid. In his memory the sequence still
would not settle into order. He could not say clearly whether he ran before the lights went out or
after, whether he heard a colleague crying before the stairwell filled or once he was already
outside. When it came back, it did not feel like remembering. He heard the alarms in his head and
for a few moments the whole scene arrived at once, vivid enough that he felt he was back inside the
building.

In the weeks after the earthquake, Omar became preoccupied with what the experience said about him.
He had come to believe that someone else would have handled it better, that a steadier, more
competent man would not have been trapped in panic and confusion. As the intrusive moments kept
breaking through, he took them as proof that he had been permanently altered. He said he used to be
a happy person, easy with his wife and children, absorbed in his work, but now he felt miserable
most of the time and was increasingly certain that nothing good was ahead for him. Those conclusions
sharpened his sense of danger in ordinary life. He no longer entered a room casually; before he
could settle anywhere he needed to know where the exits were, how quickly he could get out, whether
anything overhead looked unstable. A coworker turning suddenly beside his desk or his son rushing up
behind him could make his whole body jolt as if another collapse were starting.

The memories themselves pushed much of what followed. Because they arrived with sound and bodily
immediacy, Omar began trying to outrun anything that might pull them closer. He avoided hospitals,
ambulances, and even routine physical examinations because the alarms, stretchers, and antiseptic
smell felt too close to the chaos after the quake, when he had stood outside among the injured
listening to sirens layered over screaming and car horns. When a memory broke through, he also tried
to force himself into “positive thoughts,” insisting that he should focus only on gratitude,
survival, and how much worse it could have been. The effort was rigid rather than soothing. He would
repeat reassuring phrases in his head while his body stayed tense, as though he were arguing with
something more powerful than words.

His vigilance fed the same pattern. At work, where open-plan movement had once barely registered, he
now tracked who was walking behind him and where the nearest stairwell was. If someone brushed his
shoulder unexpectedly while passing his chair, he reacted with a burst of alarm before he knew where
he was. That constant scanning kept the earthquake close. A slammed door, a rolling office chair,
even rapid footsteps in the corridor could pull back the soundscape of the disaster so abruptly that
he again heard alarms and collapsing material in his mind. The more he lived braced for danger, the
more easily the building seemed to reassemble itself around him.

These episodes deepened his view that his life had narrowed for good. Each time he startled at a
colleague’s movement or found himself mapping exits in a restaurant before he could join
conversation, he saw further evidence that he was no longer the man he had been. His attempts to
manage this left him feeling worse. Forcing positive thoughts made him feel false and privately
ashamed when misery returned unchanged. Avoiding medical settings brought temporary relief, but it
also strengthened his sense that he could not cope with reminders and that his world was becoming
smaller. He spent long stretches mentally circling the disaster and, at times, drifted into thoughts
about getting even with whoever had built or approved unsafe structures, fantasies that gave shape
to his anger without relieving it. By the end of those loops he felt more bitter and more certain
that there was no real future to recover.

His wife finally urged him to seek help after he recoiled when she touched him unexpectedly while he
was working late at home, then spent the evening checking exits in their apartment and insisting the
family review what to do if the building shook again. Omar presented to treatment exhausted,
ashamed, and frightened by how real the memories still felt. What frightened him most was the belief
that this was permanent—that he would remain a man who could not trust his surroundings, could not
bear reminders, and could no longer imagine happiness ahead. What he hoped for, cautiously, was not
to erase the earthquake but to stop living as though he were still inside it.
