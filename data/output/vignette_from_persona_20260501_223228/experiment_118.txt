Nadia Rahman, a 53-year-old married ship captain, grew up in a household where danger came from the
person meant to care for her. She described her mother’s beatings as abrupt and often preceded by
small sounds at night—the shift of floorboards outside her bedroom, a hand on the doorknob,
footsteps in the hallway that made her body go rigid before anything had even happened. No one
intervened. Neighbors, relatives, and adults in positions of authority all seemed to see enough to
know something was wrong, yet nothing changed. As an adult, Nadia had come to believe that the
people and institutions that should have protected her had failed her, and from that grew a broader
conviction that the world is fundamentally dangerous. She also spoke with grief about feeling cut
off from her community and culture, as though the place that should have held her had instead become
associated with silence and abandonment.

In the years since, her memories of the abuse have never settled into a coherent past. Often they
come as sharp visual fragments: the bedroom door opening, the angle of light in the hallway, the
outline of a raised arm. At other times she is flooded by the same terror in her chest and stomach
with no image attached, only the full force of dread as if something is about to happen. And
sometimes she can recount an incident in a flat, detached way, feeling almost nothing, “like it
happened to someone else.” These fractured forms of remembering seem to confirm what she already
believes: that danger can arrive without warning, that no one will help, and that what happened was
never truly contained in the past.

Nadia’s work keeps her in an environment that both suits and strains her. As a ship captain, she is
comfortable with responsibility, routines, and constant scanning of surroundings. But on overnight
watches, or when she is alone in her cabin listening to metal creaks and footsteps along the
passageway, her attention narrows immediately to whether someone is approaching. Even at home,
despite knowing she is safe with her husband, she listens for sounds that might mean someone is
entering the house and finds nighttime markedly worse. Because she sees the world as dangerous and
people as unreliable, she does not experience safety as something that can be trusted; she stays
braced, unable to relax, as if vigilance were the only thing standing between her and another
betrayal.

That stance has shaped what she does with her mind. Nadia spends long stretches dwelling on how the
abuse might have been prevented—what some adult should have noticed, what she might have said, what
would have happened if someone had stepped in sooner. She also finds herself mourning who she was
before the abuse, rehearsing an imagined earlier self who might have belonged more fully to family
and culture, and who might not have learned to live in a state of constant alert. At the same time,
she tries hard not to think about the trauma at all. The effort has a desperate logic to her: if the
world is dangerous and the past proves no one will protect her, then letting her guard down or
allowing memories in feels reckless.

The problem is that the more she pushes the memories away, the less predictable they become. On
ships, when she hears movement outside her cabin at night and tells herself not to think about
childhood, she is instead struck by a sudden wash of fear with no clear scene attached. At other
moments an image flashes up with unnerving vividness. She has noticed that trying to suppress these
experiences does not quiet them; it leaves them disjointed, either all feeling and no picture, or
all picture and no feeling. Afterward she can spend hours circling the same questions about
prevention and loss, trying to make sense of what came over her.

Her vigilance feeds the same pattern. Because she listens so closely for signs of entry and remains
tense even in safe places, ordinary nighttime sounds readily acquire the force of the past. Once
activated, her mind supplies fragments—the doorway, the hall light, the sense of waiting for
impact—or else only the bodily certainty of danger. Those episodes deepen her conviction that threat
is everywhere and that she can never afford to stop monitoring. In turn, she doubles down on mental
control: more scanning, more suppression, more backward searching for how it all could have been
prevented.

Over months and years this has narrowed her life. At work she keeps to herself more than she once
did, preferring the command structure of the bridge to casual closeness with crew. At home her
husband has learned that nights are difficult; she checks sounds others would ignore and rarely
settles fully into sleep. What finally brought her to treatment was not a new trauma but the
exhaustion of living as if the door were always about to open again. She said she is afraid that if
she stops watching, something terrible will happen, but she is equally afraid that if this
continues, she will remain cut off—from rest, from her marriage, and from any sense of belonging she
still hopes might be possible.
