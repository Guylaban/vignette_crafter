Amina is a 74-year-old African American woman who has spent most of her life doing agricultural
work, rising before dawn to tend rows of vegetables on a family-run farm outside town. She lives
with her partner, a steady man she describes as patient, though in recent years she has kept even
him at a careful distance. The rape happened late in the afternoon near the edge of a field after a
long workday, when the air smelled of damp soil and cut plants and the farm had gone strangely
still. What remains most vivid to her is not a full story she can tell straight through, but the
sudden silence in the open land, the smell of earth rising up around her, and the unbearable sense
afterward that her body had been violated and spoiled. Each year, as the date approaches, she feels
herself tightening days in advance, as if her body already knows what is coming.

In the months after the assault, the farm itself became difficult terrain. When Amina steps outside
in the early morning and catches the smell of wet dirt, she is pulled back into the worst parts of
what happened, and with that comes an immediate conviction that she is dirty. If the fields fall
quiet for even a moment, she feels exposed in a way that is hard to explain to anyone else; the
silence does not feel peaceful, only dangerous, and in it she hears again the helplessness of that
afternoon. Around the anniversary, these reactions sharpen. She has come to believe not only that
what happened ruined her body, but that there is something fundamentally stained about her now, and
on those days she can find herself thinking that life may no longer be worth much. The triggers do
not remain simple reminders for her; they seem to confirm what she already fears about herself.

Because of this, Amina has gradually narrowed her world. At work she avoids darker storage sheds and
any circumstance that feels too enclosed or too much like the place where she was overpowered. If
the crew breaks and she notices the smell of turned soil hanging thick in the air, she will busy
herself elsewhere or go inside under the pretense of needing water. She no longer tolerates casual
touch. A hand on her shoulder from a coworker or even her partner reaching for her in the kitchen
can make her recoil. When these moments happen, she tries to clamp down on everything by forcing
herself into bright, positive thoughts—telling herself to be grateful, to think of good things, to
push the ugliness away. The effort is desperate and rigid rather than soothing, a way of trying not
to feel what the smell, the silence, or the date on the calendar has stirred up.

Over time, this way of coping has deepened the very beliefs she is fighting. Avoiding touch has left
her more convinced that her body is shameful, as though closeness would expose something
contaminated in her. Steering clear of reminders on the farm has not made the fields feel ordinary
again; instead, the outdoor smells seem more loaded each season, and her fear of them starts earlier
as the anniversary nears. Forcing herself to think only positive thoughts gives her brief moments of
control, but when the intrusive sense of dirtiness returns anyway, she takes it as proof that even
her own mind cannot be trusted to right itself. Then the thought comes back with more force: maybe
life is not worth living if this is what she carries.

These appraisals now shape what she notices everywhere. Because she feels contaminated, she is
acutely alert to the smell of soil on her clothes and skin after work, as though the odor itself
reveals her defilement. Because she is ashamed of what happened to her body, silence has become hard
to bear; in quiet moments she feels trapped with what she believes her body signifies. She responds
by tightening her routines further—staying out of certain parts of the farm, keeping her partner
from touching her, answering dread with more insistence that she must stay positive—and the more she
does this, the more potent those same smells, quiet stretches, and anniversary days become.

What finally brought Amina to treatment was not a single new symptom but the realization that her
life had shrunk around these patterns. This past anniversary week, she could not make herself go out
to the fields for two days, and when her partner tried gently to comfort her, she jerked away and
then wept from shame. She told the clinician she is frightened by how often the thought comes that
life may not be worth living, though she also says, quietly, that she wants some part of herself
back. More than anything, she hopes to be able to walk onto the land she has worked for decades,
smell the earth, and not feel that it is telling her who she is.
