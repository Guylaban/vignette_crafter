transport run that turned chaotic in seconds. One vehicle ahead of hers was hit, and the order of
the day—predictable movement, drilled procedures, clear lines of command—collapsed into noise, dust,
shouted instructions, and bodies moving in different directions at once. She remembers the sickening
lightness in her head as she crouched behind metal, trying to track where the next threat might come
from while her body felt unsteady and unreal. What stayed with her was not only the attack itself
but the sense that once things became unpredictable, she was no longer able to trust her own
reactions.

After leaving the military, Megan took a job driving a city bus because the route maps and fixed
schedules initially felt safer than anything open-ended. Over time, though, the parts of the job she
could not control began to unsettle her. Traffic detours, passengers arguing, a rider standing up
suddenly before a stop, even a day when she felt briefly dizzy in the driver’s seat could jolt her
into the same state of alert she had known overseas. In those moments she found herself scanning
mirrors, sidewalks, and the fare box area for signs that something was about to go wrong. During
breaks she would choose a seat in the depot cafeteria only if she could put her back against the
wall and watch the whole room. The more stress built up in a shift, the more sharply her body
reacted to small reminders, and the more convinced she became that she was still living in a world
where danger could break through at any moment.

Those episodes did more than frighten her; they changed what she concluded about herself. When an
unpredictable change on her route or a spell of lightheadedness sent her into intense physical
alarm, Megan did not experience it as a temporary reaction. She had come to believe it proved that
she was no longer capable of doing the things that once defined her. She had been proud of being
steady under pressure in the military, someone other people could rely on. Now, when stress alone
could make her feel on edge and unready, it seemed to her like evidence that the trauma had ruined
the version of herself she valued. She would sit after a shift gripping the steering wheel after the
engine was off, thinking that her life had been destroyed by something she should have been strong
enough to withstand, and that very little in front of her felt meaningful anymore.

Those beliefs began to shape what she noticed and what set her off. Because Megan was certain she
was no longer capable and that her life had been fundamentally wrecked, unpredictability itself
started to feel intolerable. A schedule change that another driver might find annoying registered to
her as proof that she could not cope. If she felt even slightly dizzy or overloaded, she immediately
read it through that same lens—something was wrong with her, and something bad could follow. In that
way, stress and bodily sensations became more potent triggers because they seemed to confirm her
bleak view of herself and her future.

At the same time, living in a constant state of watchfulness kept deepening those conclusions.
Megan’s scanning never gave her relief; it only kept her attention fixed on possible threat. By the
end of a route she was exhausted from monitoring every movement near the bus and every shift in her
own body. She noticed that she was planning where to stand, where to sit, and how to keep exits in
view almost everywhere she went. To her, needing her back against a wall in diners, waiting rooms,
and the employee lounge felt humiliating. It reinforced her sense that she was diminished now, a
person whose world had narrowed to surveillance and control, and that nothing outside staying safe
carried much meaning.

That same vigilance also made her more sensitive to the very triggers she dreaded. Because she was
constantly checking her surroundings and her body, she caught every flutter of lightheadedness and
every rise in stress before it had a chance to pass on its own. An unexpected sound or change in
routine was not just unpleasant; it landed in a system already braced for threat. On the bus this
meant that ordinary unpredictability—a late passenger banging on the door, a supervisor calling in a
reroute, a confrontation at the back—hit her with immediate force. What frightened her most was how
quickly her body responded and how completely it seemed to take over, as if the danger were present
again.

By the time Megan sought help, she was dreading her shifts and spending her off-hours feeling
emptied out. She was still showing up for work, but the effort it took to get through a day left her
withdrawn from everyone else. What finally brought her in was the fear that if this continued, she
would lose the job she had chosen to give herself structure and proof she could still function. More
than anything, she was afraid that the trauma had permanently taken away her competence and any
sense that life could matter again, but a small part of her was still hoping someone could help her
stop living as if every ordinary moment were about to turn into the worst one.
