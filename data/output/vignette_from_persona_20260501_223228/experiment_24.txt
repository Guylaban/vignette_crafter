labor turned frightening and quick decisions were needed. She still worked part-time at a community
clinic, proud that even now her hands remained steady with newborns. The accident that changed her
life happened on an afternoon drive home after a long shift. Traffic had slowed near an intersection
when another car came through fast and struck the side of hers with enough force to spin it. For
several moments she was trapped inside the vehicle, the door jammed, the cabin tightening around her
while horns blared and then sirens grew louder. She remembered seeing, through shattered glass, the
twisted front ends of the cars and thinking with absolute certainty that she might die there before
anyone reached her. What stayed with her most vividly was not only the impact but the feeling of
being enclosed and unable to get out while emergency vehicles approached.

In the weeks after, those details returned with unusual force in ordinary places. At work, if a
patient room door clicked shut and she realized it was locked, her chest tightened and she became
acutely alert to the walls, the distance to the handle, the possibility of being trapped again. On
the drive to the clinic, passing a fresh collision on the roadside or even a crumpled car waiting
for tow service could flood her with the same sense of danger, and the sound of sirens made her
whole body jolt before she had time to think. These moments did not feel like memories to her so
much as warnings. She became superalert, scanning exits, watching traffic, listening for abrupt
sounds, and nights grew long because she could not settle into sleep; ordinary noises in the house
made her start sharply awake.

As these reactions persisted, Abena had come to judge herself harshly for the accident and its
aftermath. When she saw damaged cars or heard sirens and felt herself go rigid, she would think that
somebody else would not have gotten into this situation, that another person would have been wiser
or stronger and would not now be undone by something on the road. The locked exam rooms at the
clinic became especially painful for that reason: standing inside one, even briefly, she found
herself thinking not only about being trapped in the wreck but about how her life had narrowed since
then. She had begun to say, quietly and with shame, that her life had been destroyed by the trauma,
and that the crash had permanently damaged her body in ways age would not allow her to recover from.

Those beliefs gradually made the world feel even less navigable. Because she was certain her body
had been permanently damaged, every jump at a sound and every sleepless night seemed like proof that

in herself. When she was lying awake, listening for sounds outside, she would think that this was
what her life had become now—watching, bracing, waiting—and by morning she was more convinced that
the trauma had ruined what remained of her old age. In that state, even the possibility of a trigger
took on more force. A routine home visit required an elevator ride in a building with a heavy
interior door, and before she even stepped inside she was already imagining being enclosed and
unable to breathe steadily.

been trained to search for signs of another catastrophe. In clinic hallways she listened for

instantly aware of the lock, and because she was sleeping so poorly she had less capacity to
reassure herself that she was in a safe room and not inside the car again. Her fear sharpened the
triggers, and the triggers deepened her fear.

The same pattern fed her convictions about what the trauma meant. After a night of broken sleep, if

delivering babies, but she sometimes asked younger staff to take over when a room felt too enclosed
or when sirens outside the clinic made it hard for her to focus. She hated this loss of confidence,
especially in a role that had defined her for decades, and she compared herself bitterly to the
capable woman she had once been.

Abena finally agreed to seek help after frightening herself during a routine shift. A supply room
door stuck for only a moment, but in that instant she felt trapped, heard imagined sirens in her

on her calm. What she hopes for now is simple and painfully important to her: to sleep, to walk into
a room without scanning for escape, and to believe that the accident did not take the rest of her
life with it.
