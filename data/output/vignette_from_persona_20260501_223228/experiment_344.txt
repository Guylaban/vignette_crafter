by another soldier in the barracks after an evening training exercise. What stayed with her was not
a clear narrative but a set of stark visual fragments: the fluorescent light above the bunk, the
pattern of the cinderblock wall, his sleeve against her wrist, her own boots on the floor at the
wrong angle. When she tried to place the event in sequence, it felt strangely unreal, as if she were
describing something from a film or something that had happened to another woman who merely
resembled her. She would say that the memory “doesn’t even feel like mine,” and yet the images
arrived with a force that made her body tense before she had fully registered what she was seeing in
her mind.

In the weeks afterward, those image-heavy memories did more than frighten her; they altered what she
believed about herself. Because the assault came back as disconnected pictures rather than a story
she could hold onto, Linh kept circling the idea that she should have done something different in
one of those frozen frames. She had come to believe that somebody else would have stopped it, that
another woman would have fought harder, yelled sooner, understood what was happening earlier. The
unreality of the memory made this worse rather than better: if it barely felt real, she could not
make sense of why she had become so changed by it, and she began to think the person she was
now—withdrawn, distracted, ashamed—was unrecognisable compared with who she had been before. Around
other soldiers and even around other Southeast Asian service members on base, she felt cut off, as
if some link to belonging had been broken and she was standing outside her own life as well as
outside her community.

Those beliefs shaped what she did each day. Convinced that she had failed in a way other people
would not have, Linh stopped trying to sit with the memory directly and instead pushed herself
toward anything that might blunt it. In the dining facility she found herself eating long after she
was full, not out of hunger but because the act of chewing and the heaviness afterward gave her
something else to focus on. When thoughts of the assault surfaced during drills or while cleaning
her gear, she would force her mind onto other topics—lists, routines, anything available. Just as
often, though, she became trapped in the opposite habit, going back over the details again and
again, trying to locate the exact second she could have prevented it. To her, this overanalysing
felt like a duty: if she could just understand it properly, maybe she could prove she was not the
weak, altered person she now believed herself to be.

Her efforts to manage the memory ended up keeping it in the same broken form. The constant
redirection meant she never let the event settle into a fuller account, and the overeating left her
dulled but not relieved, so the images returned unchanged. Overanalysing only sharpened the
fragments that already tormented her—the wall, the light, the angle of her boots—until these
snapshots felt even more vivid and intrusive while the larger sequence remained inaccessible. The
memory stayed alien to her, all pictures and no ownership, and each time she failed to make it
cohere she was left again with the sense that what happened was too extreme to have happened to her
and yet had somehow remade her.

That cycle gradually narrowed her life. In her work as a soldier, the barracks and dining hall
became the places where she was most likely to confront the memory and then disappear into one of
her coping habits. She would eat quickly and excessively at meals with her unit, keeping
conversation minimal, or mentally flee into rote task lists when someone’s posture or a glimpse of a
bunk frame stirred one of the visual flashes. Later, alone, she would replay the event for hours,
emerging more certain that she was no longer the person her husband had married and less able to
feel connected to anyone around her. The more she relied on these strategies, the more changed and
separate she felt; the more changed and separate she felt, the more desperately she used them.

By the time she sought help, Linh was frightened by how distant she felt from herself. What finally
brought her in was not one dramatic crisis but the accumulating sense that she was living beside her
own life—eating to go numb, thinking in circles, and watching the assault in pictures that still did
not feel like her memory. She wanted, cautiously, to feel real again and to stop measuring herself
against the imagined person who “would have stopped it,” but she was afraid that if she looked at
what happened directly, it would confirm that the self she used to know was gone for good.
