Ramesh Patel is a 58-year-old widowed fisherman who came to treatment after months of feeling as
though the accident that nearly killed him had never really ended. He had spent most of his adult
life on the water, and the rhythms of work had once been steadying to him: early mornings at the
dock, the slap of nets, the practical talk of tides and weather. The accident happened driving home
from the harbor before dawn after an exhausting shift. On a quiet stretch of road, another car
crossed into his lane and struck him hard enough that he remembered the crushing metal, the sudden
silence after impact, and the certainty, in those seconds, that he was going to die there trapped in
the vehicle. He survived, but afterward he found that what stayed with him was not a finished story
he could tell. He would begin to describe it and stop, saying he knew exactly what had happened but
could not find words for it. When it came back, it did not feel like something over and done with;
it arrived with the same terror, as if he were back inside the wreck.

In the weeks after the crash, ordinary moments began pulling him straight into that state. Seeing
footage of cars smashed in accidents on television, overhearing conversations about someone losing a
loved one, even being approached by unfamiliar people at the marina or grocery store could make the
scene flare up in him with frightening immediacy. What unnerved him was how little distance there
was between the reminder and the memory: one glance at twisted metal or one mention of bereavement,
and he was no longer simply reminded of the crash but overtaken by it. Those moments also sharpened
what he had come to believe since the accident. When strangers tried to make harmless conversation,
he felt a hard distrust rise in him—people were not what they seemed, and safety could vanish
without warning. Stories about loss landed with particular force because he had begun to see himself
as a man fundamentally altered, someone who no longer knew who he was if he could be reduced so
quickly to fear and helplessness.

That change in how he understood himself gathered momentum over time. Ramesh had always thought of
himself as capable and physically dependable; his work demanded it. Since the crash, he had come to
believe he could no longer trust his own body. A jolt of tension, a pounding heart, a moment of
freezing in traffic all felt like proof that something in him had broken. That belief left him
persistently on guard. He scanned rooms, roads, and the dock for signs of danger, and even at home
he rarely settled. Silence was especially hard for him. If the house became unusually still at
night, he did not experience it as calm; he felt certain it meant something dangerous was about to
happen. The more watchful he became, the more his old sense of self receded. He described feeling
like a stranger to himself, a man defined now by vigilance and suspicion rather than by competence.

His appraisals also fed the way the memory lived in him. Because he believed his body was unreliable
and that he himself had changed beyond recognition, each involuntary recollection seemed to confirm
those conclusions. If remembering the crash flooded him with the same terror, then to him that meant
he was still damaged, still vulnerable, still not safe inside himself. And because he saw other
people as potentially deceptive or unsafe, being approached by strangers did not remain a neutral
social encounter; it became another opening through which the crash could return. He found himself
avoiding conversation at the fish market and keeping exchanges with customers abruptly brief, not
out of shyness but because any unfamiliar face could set off both his mistrust and the vivid sense
of being back at the roadside.

The loop tightened further through his constant sense of danger. Being on alert made him more
reactive to the very cues he feared. When he was already scanning his surroundings, the sight of a
damaged car on the highway, an unfamiliar person stepping toward him at the dock, or a quiet spell
in the house could all strike him with heightened force, making those triggers feel as if they were
everywhere. Once triggered, the memory arrived fully alive, and in those moments the terror itself
convinced him all over again that his body could not be trusted and that the world was not safe.
After nights spent listening to the silence in his house, waiting for disaster, he would wake
feeling even more certain that danger was close and that he had become the kind of man who could
never let his guard down.

By the time he sought help, the effects had spread across his daily life. At work he stayed away
from casual talk with other fishermen and buyers, choosing isolated tasks whenever he could. On the
drive to and from the harbor, he took longer routes to avoid busy roads where wrecked cars might be
visible. At home, even though he lived alone, he checked doors and windows repeatedly and sat tense
through long stretches of quiet. What finally brought him in was the realization that he was no
