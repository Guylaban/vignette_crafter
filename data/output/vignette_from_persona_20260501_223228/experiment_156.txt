Rafael is a 62-year-old married Latino man who has spent most of his adult life as a dancer, and he
still teaches and rehearses in a studio where bodies move close together and touch is part of the
work. The collision happened late one evening as he was driving home after class. Another car ran a
light and struck the driver’s side hard enough to spin his vehicle. What has stayed with him is not
a clear story but fragments that come back with force: the explosive crash of metal, the shriek of
brakes, a horn blaring somewhere too long, and the sensation that events had outrun his ability to
act. When he tries to describe it, he stalls out and says it is “like noise and impact, not words.”
In those moments of recall he feels a wave of shame that is hard for him to explain, as if there is
something humiliating in having been so exposed and overpowered.

In the months after the crash, Rafael found that entering any room had become a quiet tactical
exercise. At the studio, before he could focus on music or choreography, he was already noting the
exits, the door to the hall, the back stairwell, who was standing where. If a student came up behind
him and touched his shoulder unexpectedly, he jerked violently and felt a surge of alarm out of
proportion to the moment. He was often flooded with fear, anger, and shame afterward. He had come to
believe that bad things could happen without warning and that he was the kind of person they
happened to. That sense of helplessness and character-based blame made every space feel less safe;
scanning for ways out and bracing against sudden contact felt necessary, not excessive.

The heightened vigilance also seemed to sharpen the way the crash returned to him. When he was
already on edge in rehearsal, a squeal from the sound system or the scrape of a chair could bring
back the sound of the collision in his head with startling immediacy. The memory did not come as a
coherent narrative but as auditory fragments and bodily shock, leaving him tense and ashamed before
he had fully registered why. He noticed that the more keyed up he was—watching doors, flinching at
touch, feeling his body ready for danger—the more easily these sensory pieces broke through.

Because those intrusions felt unbearable and almost impossible to speak about, Rafael began managing
them the only ways he could think of. He tried hard not to think about the crash at all, cutting off
conversations and mentally shoving images away the instant they began. At night he stayed up very
late, lingering over television or sitting awake in the dark because sleep felt too unguarded and
because closing his eyes made the sounds more likely to come. Sometimes, when the memory pressed in,
he deliberately tried to blank out and “go somewhere else,” describing it as leaving his body just
enough not to feel the full force of it. These strategies gave him brief relief, but they also
seemed to leave the event even less put into words; what remained were the same raw sounds, the same
unspeakable fragments, and the same shame each time they returned.

Over time, these habits changed how Rafael understood himself. Because he was avoiding the memory
rather than making sense of it, he became more convinced that something in him had been permanently
altered. He had come to believe he would never feel normal emotions again, only some mixture of
fear, anger, and deadness. When he found himself staying up half the night or mentally disappearing
in the middle of distress, he took this not as a sign of struggle but as evidence of damage: proof,
in his mind, that he was weak, defective, the sort of man to whom terrible things happen and who
cannot stop them. The very methods he used to get through the day deepened his bleak view of
himself.

That view, in turn, kept the sense of threat close. If he was inherently vulnerable and the world
fundamentally uncontrollable, then of course he needed to watch every doorway and keep his body
ready. In class he has become more distant with students, stepping out of partnering demonstrations
when possible and positioning himself near the studio exit. His wife has noticed he lingers awake
long after she has gone to bed and seems elsewhere when she asks about the accident. Rafael finally
sought help after startling so intensely when a younger dancer adjusted his arm from behind that he
shouted and stopped rehearsal cold. What frightened him most was not only the reaction itself, but
the conviction that this was now his permanent self: a man who cannot speak the worst thing that
happened to him, cannot trust safety when it is present, and may never feel like himself again. He
says he is coming now because he wants, more than anything, to know whether the crash still has to
live in his body this way.
