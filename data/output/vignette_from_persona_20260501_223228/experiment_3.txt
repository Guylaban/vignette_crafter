confidence happened during a hurricane response on the Gulf Coast, when he was deployed to help
clear unstable structures after severe flooding. In the middle of a neighborhood already stripped
apart by wind and water, he was working near a damaged house when part of the structure gave way
without warning. People were shouting over the storm noise, someone yelled for everyone to move, and
the crack of collapsing timber came with such force that for a moment it sounded to him like an
explosion. He remembers dirty water, splintered boards, and the helplessness of not knowing whether
more of the house was about to come down. What remains most vivid is the suddenness of it and the
soundscape around it—loud crashes, raised voices, orders shouted across confusion.

In the months afterward, unpredictability became hard for him to tolerate. At work, if a situation
changed suddenly or someone shouted across a room, he would feel himself clamp down internally and
begin managing the distress any way he could. He started avoiding anything he thought might become
stressful, passing off certain field tasks when he could, staying away from noisy briefings, and
keeping himself constantly occupied with television, cleaning, or pointless jobs around the house so
his mind would never settle. In the evenings he would eat far past hunger, describing it less as
enjoyment than as a way to blunt the agitation after a day of bracing himself against sudden noises
and abrupt demands.

Those same cues also set off reactions that felt to Kwame as if his body had stopped waiting for his
mind. A slammed metal door at the depot, a supervisor barking an instruction, an unexpected change
in a routine assignment could make him jolt, tense, and scan the area before he had even registered
what he thought was happening. At times the response sharpened into brief flashback-like moments in
which he felt transported back into that flooded street, as if the collapse were happening again.
The triggers did not just frighten him in the present; they also tugged at the memory itself. A loud
noise or somebody shouting could pull up fragments—the cracked beam, muddy water, the sense of
impact—but as soon as more of it started to come, he would push it away before he could see the
whole thing.

That pattern has left him with a memory that feels both intrusive and unfinished. He says he
remembers pieces, never a complete sequence, and when those pieces break through he is flooded with
intense rage—rage at the chaos, at the lack of warning, at how quickly control vanished. Because the
memory comes in fragments, he remains on edge, convinced that danger can arrive before there is time
to understand it. And because his body so often reacts as if he is under threat, the memory itself
becomes harder to approach; he cuts it off midway, refusing to let it fully form because he expects
the physical surge and anger that come with it.

Over time this has tightened into a daily pattern. The more often he has false alarms and those
sudden bodily reactions, the more he organizes his life around preventing them. He avoids stressful
situations, keeps his attention filled every waking hour, and eats to settle himself after episodes
of alarm. Yet this way of coping has made him more sensitive to the very things he is trying to
manage. Because he is constantly watching for abrupt changes, shouting and loud noises stand out
immediately. Because he avoids ordinary stress, even minor unpredictability now feels potent. At
work in the bomb squad environment—where sharp commands and sudden noises are part of the day—he has
become quicker to flinch and quicker to interpret the atmosphere as dangerous, even when nothing is
actually wrong.

His efforts to push the memories away have also made the triggers feel broader and less predictable.
Since he never lets the memory run to completion, it returns as disconnected sensory pieces that
seem to latch onto whatever resembles the original chaos: a raised voice in the garage, a crash from
dropped equipment, a last-minute change in plans. Those moments can ignite his body before he has
words for what is happening, and once that surge starts he may spend the rest of the day distracting
himself and eating compulsively just to come back down.

Kwame finally agreed to seek help after his wife told him that home now feels organized around his

night so his mind never goes quiet, and has become more distant whenever she asks what happened
during the hurricane deployment. What frightens him most is that he no longer trusts his own

lets himself remember the event fully, the rage and panic will overtake him. What he hopes for,
cautiously, is to be able to hear a sudden noise or a shouted instruction without feeling as though
the disaster has started all over again.
