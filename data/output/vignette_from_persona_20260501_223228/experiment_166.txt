Arjun Patel is a 36-year-old accountant who came to treatment after years of telling himself that
what happened in his childhood should no longer have this much power over him. He grew up in a small
apartment where nights were unpredictable because of his father’s violence toward his mother. What
he remembers most clearly is not a single incident but a recurring scene: the sharp crash of metal
bowls or a slammed cabinet door from the kitchen, his mother’s breathless crying, his father’s
footsteps moving fast through the hallway, and the sense that the whole apartment had turned
dangerous in an instant. As a boy he would freeze in his room, listening for whether the sounds were
getting closer, certain that nothing he did could stop what was happening. No one outside the family
intervened, and in his mind the people responsible simply went on with their lives. Even now, he
says with bitterness, there were never any consequences.

He has great difficulty narrating those experiences in a coherent way. When he tries, his speech
stalls and he drifts into fragments—“the dishes,” “my mother against the counter,” “his shoes in the
hall”—as if the memory comes back as sensory pieces rather than a story. Remembering does not feel
like looking back. It brings the same terror into his body that he felt as a child, so quickly that
he often loses words and sits rigidly in the chair, jaw tight, arms braced on his knees. He
describes it as being overtaken without warning, as though something in the room has opened a
trapdoor beneath him.

That happens often in his daily life. Working as an accountant in a busy office, he is especially
shaken by abrupt domestic-sounding noises that echo the apartment of his childhood: a coworker
dropping a metal water bottle in the break room, a cabinet door banging in the office kitchen,
raised voices from behind a closed conference room door. In those moments his shoulders lock, his
chest tightens, and he scans before he is even fully aware of what he is looking for. He has learned
to sit where he can see the doorway and the hallway beyond it. He notices exits immediately in any
room and startles hard at sounds other people dismiss. What frightens him most is how instantly
these cues can pull him back into the old scene, and how little control he feels over the shift.

Over time, Arjun has come to believe that bad things happen because the people who cause harm are
not stopped, and that he is the kind of person to whom this can keep happening. That sense of
helplessness keeps his body in a near-constant state of readiness. He says he feels tense from the
moment he wakes up, as if danger could break through ordinary life at any second. The same beliefs
also shape how the memories return. Because he is certain there was no accountability and no
protection then, the past does not feel finished to him; it remains unresolved and current, making
the old scenes easier to re-enter and harder to place at a distance.

The reverse is also true. When a memory is triggered at work or at home, the terror in it seems to
confirm his bleakest conclusions. He does not come away thinking, that was only a reminder. He comes
away feeling once more that he is powerless, that safety is unreliable, and that the world allows
cruelty to pass unchecked. After one episode in the office kitchen, when a slammed microwave door
sent a shock through him so strong that his hands trembled over a spreadsheet, he spent the rest of
the day convinced he would never be a happy person again, only someone managing eruptions of dread.
The more these memories break in, the more fixed that view of himself has become.

His physical alarm then feeds the cycle further. When he is already wound tight and watchful, small
sounds are enough to set off full recollections; he says his body seems to remember before his mind
does. And after these surges—heart pounding, muscles clenched, eyes searching—he is left feeling
even more certain that he has no real protection against what might come next. The constant
vigilance makes the world feel dangerous, and that atmosphere of danger makes the old violence
easier to feel as if it is happening again.

By the time he sought help, Arjun was exhausted by living this way. His divorce had deepened his
conviction that he had lost whatever part of him was once light or hopeful, and he was ashamed of
how miserable and guarded he had become. He finally came in after realizing he was structuring his
entire workday around avoiding the break room and any closed-door conversations he could hear
through the wall. What he wants most is to stop feeling hijacked by noises and fragments he cannot
put into words. What he fears most is that the terror means the violence never really ended inside
him, and never will.
