Nadia Rahman was 51 when she finally came for help, a married hotel manager who had spent most of
her adult life priding herself on staying composed in emergencies. The incident that changed her
happened at work. During a renovation at the hotel, a cleaning chemical was mistakenly mixed with
another product in a poorly ventilated service corridor near a guest floor. Staff began coughing and
backing away; Nadia remembered the sharp, sterile smell hitting first, like something between bleach
and a hospital hallway, and then the burning in her throat and chest. An alarm sounded, people
shouted over one another, and for several minutes she was trying to direct guests and employees out
while fighting the sense that she could not get enough air. She was medically evaluated afterward
and told she had been exposed to toxic fumes. What remained with her most vividly was not only the
physical panic of that moment, but the smell and the sudden noise around it, which had come to mean
danger in a way they never had before.

In the weeks after, Nadia noticed that ordinary pressures at the hotel no longer felt ordinary. A
stressful check-in rush, a staffing problem, or an unexpected maintenance issue could set her body
off before she had fully registered why. If she walked past housekeeping carts and caught the
antiseptic scent of industrial cleaners, her heart kicked hard, her chest tightened, and she felt a
jolt of alarm as if the exposure were happening again. The workplace itself became charged; entering
back corridors, supply rooms, or any area with strong cleaning odors made her brace physically. Even
at home, if she smelled something medicinal or sharply disinfected, she felt the same surge in her
body. Loud or unexpected sounds had also become hard to bear. A dropped tray in the lobby or a
sudden bang from a service door made her jump and scan immediately, certain for a moment that
something dangerous had happened. The stress of the environment, the hotel setting, and especially
hospital-like smells all seemed to pull her into a state of threat before she could talk herself out
of it.

Over time, that constant sense that she needed to stay ready began to shape the way she moved
through her days and nights. Nadia had come to believe that letting her guard down was risky,
because danger might announce itself with a smell or a sudden noise and she would need to react
immediately. At bedtime she found it hard to fall asleep, lying still but inwardly listening, as if
some part of her was on duty. She would notice the hum of the house, a car door outside, the ice
maker in the kitchen, and remain alert instead of drifting off. At work she listened for abrupt
sounds in the back of house and paid close attention to the air whenever she entered a corridor,
searching for any chemical or antiseptic note. This vigilance made the triggers broader rather than
narrower: because she was already monitoring for smells, stress, and noise, she detected them
quickly and intensely, and more corners of the hotel began to feel contaminated with the possibility
of another exposure.

That tightening pattern became especially clear in the hotel. On busy weekends, when guest
complaints piled up and radios crackled constantly, Nadia would feel herself tense before she even
reached the operations office. If a cleaner’s smell drifted out from a recently serviced room, she
could feel heat rush through her body and her stomach drop. She began taking longer routes to avoid
certain service areas, lingering in the lobby where the air felt safer, and delegating tasks that
required going near storage spaces with concentrated cleaning products. When a banquet worker
dropped a metal pan one evening, the clang shot through her so strongly that her hands shook
afterward. Experiences like that left her even more convinced she had to stay alert at all times,
and the more alert she tried to be, the more the workplace, stress itself, and sharp antiseptic
odors stood out as warnings.

Her husband had noticed that she was sleeping poorly and coming home depleted, still keyed up by
sounds and smells from the day. Nadia herself was troubled by how much of her attention was now
spent anticipating the next jolt in her body. She sought treatment after a recent incident in which
a disinfectant smell in a guest-floor hallway made her freeze mid-step, heart pounding so hard she
had to hand off a conversation to an assistant manager. What frightened her most was the sense that
her world was shrinking around cues she could not reliably control: stress, the hotel itself, a
medicinal smell, a sudden noise. What she hoped for was simple and urgent—to be able to walk through
her own workplace, and to lie down at night, without feeling that she had to remain on guard for
disaster.
