When his nine-year-old daughter was admitted to the pediatric ICU with a sudden, life-threatening
infection, Arun had the experience of time breaking apart around him. He remembered the sharp
antiseptic smell that seemed to cling to every blanket and hallway, the sweet bite of hand sanitizer
and rubbing alcohol on the doctors’ hands, and the moment a nurse asked him to sit because he looked
faint. He had gone lightheaded beside the bed while monitors sounded around his daughter, and what
frightened him most was not only how sick she was but how helpless he felt standing there while
strangers made decisions that would determine whether she lived. In the weeks that followed, she
slowly recovered, but Arun did not feel as if the danger had ended. He kept returning in his mind to
the ICU, to the delays before she was diagnosed, to the staff members he felt had dismissed his
concern early on. He found himself dwelling on what other people had done to his family and, in
darker moments, imagining how he would make them answer for it. He knew these thoughts were harsh,
but they gave shape to his anger and made him feel less powerless.

Arun worked as a park ranger, a job that had always suited him because he valued steadiness,
physical competence, and being the person who knew what to do in a crisis. After his daughter came
home, he tried to throw himself back into his routine, checking trails, talking with visitors, and
helping with minor accidents or lost hikers. But he began pulling away from conversations that might
bring up hospitals or his daughter’s illness. If a coworker asked how she was doing, he would cut
the exchange short or leave the room. At home he discouraged relatives from “bringing it all back
up,” and his wife noticed that he became tense if anyone wanted to revisit those weeks, even to say
how grateful they were. The more he kept others from reminding him, the more his mind circled
privately around blame—what the first clinic missed, who failed to act fast enough, who should be
held responsible. Those hours of replaying and mentally arguing over what had been done to his child
seemed to keep the whole experience raw, so that ordinary sensations now struck him with the force
of the ICU.

In that way, his coping began feeding the very reactions he was trying to avoid. On duty, if he grew
dizzy in the summer heat or when he stood up too quickly after crouching to examine a trail marker,
he was abruptly back in the hospital chair with a nurse telling him not to fall. A visitor using
hand sanitizer at the ranger station, the smell of first-aid alcohol from a supply kit, or even the
sterile odor near a clinic attached to the park could make his chest tighten and his attention
narrow. He had come to expect these moments, almost to scan for them, because his private rehearsing
of the grievance left the hospital atmosphere vividly present in his mind. What startled him was how
little it took: a wave of lightheadedness, a trace of antiseptic, and he would feel his body react
before he had fully registered where he was.

Over months, this changed the way he moved through both work and family life. He lingered outside
the first-aid room rather than going in unless he had to. He delegated tasks that involved assisting
injured visitors whenever possible, telling himself someone else was closer or better placed. At
home, he left the kitchen when his wife cleaned surfaces with disinfectant, and he avoided sitting
with his daughter during routine follow-up discussions because the smell of the clinic and the
memory of almost losing her seemed too near. His wife was troubled not only by his irritability but
by the way his mind remained fixed on who had wronged them, as though he were still fighting the
hospital in his head. Arun understood that she wanted him to let it go; from his perspective,
letting it go felt like accepting that what happened to his daughter could happen again and no one
would answer for it.

By the time he sought help, he was exhausted by how quickly he could be pulled out of the present.
He was still functioning at work, but with increasing strain, and he worried that his avoidance of
certain duties would soon become visible to colleagues. What finally brought him in was a recent
episode at the ranger station when the smell of alcohol from a first-aid bottle and a brief spell of
dizziness left him shaken enough that he had to step outside in front of a junior coworker. He said
he was tired of feeling ambushed by sensations that seemed trivial to everyone else. At the same
time, he was afraid that if he stopped going over who was to blame, he would be abandoning his
daughter and the truth of how close she came to dying.
