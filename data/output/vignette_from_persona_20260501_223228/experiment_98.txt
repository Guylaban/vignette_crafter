Kwame Mensah, a 58-year-old Ghanaian American hairdresser, came to treatment nearly a year after he
was mugged closing his salon in the evening. He had stayed late finishing a client, swept up hair,
counted the register, and stepped into the alley behind the shop with the day’s cash in a small bag.
A man emerged from the shadows, unsteady and sharp-faced, reeking of alcohol. When Kwame hesitated,
trying to understand what was happening, the man snapped at him to hand it over and stepped closer
in a way that felt suddenly erratic and dangerous. What remained with Kwame was less a coherent
story than a set of stark, silent pictures: the glint of the man’s eyes, the dark mouth of the
alley, the bottle-sour smell on his breath, the accusatory force in his voice. He said he knew
exactly what happened, but when his wife asked him to describe it afterward, he found himself unable
to put it into words. Instead, the scene returned as if a film had started up inside him, playing
the same images again and again.

In the weeks that followed, those fragments seemed to attach themselves to moments in daily life.
The smell of alcohol on a client who came in after lunch could bring the alley back so vividly that
for a second he was no longer in the salon. If someone on the sidewalk outside the front windows
lingered too long, moved furtively, or watched the cash register, Kwame felt himself tighten and
scan for danger. What surprised him was how strongly criticism also began to affect him. If a
customer corrected him sharply about a haircut or complained that he had taken too much off, the
tone of being confronted jolted something loose, and the old visual sequence flashed up before he
could stop it. Because the memory came in pictures rather than words, sudden and repetitive, he
could not talk himself through it; he just felt captured by it.

Kwame responded by trying to force the whole thing out of his mind. When an image started, he would
clamp down on it and tell himself not to think about the robbery. He also found himself replaying
his own mistakes, dwelling on what he had done wrong that night—why he had taken the alley, why he
had paused, whether he should have seen the man sooner. The more those film-like memories intruded,
the more he turned them over afterward in self-blaming loops, as if finding the exact point of
failure might somehow protect him. At work he became more withdrawn. He shortened conversations with
clients, left the reception area to his junior colleague when possible, and avoided people he did
not know well, especially men who seemed watchful or unpredictable.

Over time these habits made his world narrower and his alarm quicker to rise. Avoiding people meant
he spent more of the workday on edge when he had to engage, with less of the ordinary ease that had
once anchored him in the salon. He watched customers for signs that they might turn aggressive or
suspicious, and because he was trying so hard not to think about the robbery, small cues seemed to
hit him even harder when they broke through. A whiff of whiskey from someone waiting for a trim, a
tense exchange in the chair, a stranger pacing outside the shop—each could set off the familiar
strip of images. Those images, in turn, sharpened the sense that danger was close by and made other
people’s behavior seem loaded and risky. By now, criticism from clients had become especially hard
to bear: a simple correction about sideburns or beard length could feel less like routine feedback
than the start of another threatening encounter, and afterward he would spend hours going over his
own failings.

His marriage had been affected by this shrinking. His wife experienced him as distant and hard to
reach, particularly because whenever she asked what was replaying in his mind he would go quiet and
say he did not want to talk about it. He was still working, but the salon no longer felt like a
place of easy familiarity. It felt exposed. He finally sought help after snapping at a regular
customer who had mildly criticized a haircut while another man in the waiting area smelled strongly
of liquor; Kwame was flooded with the old images and had to step into the back room to steady
himself. What frightened him most was that he seemed to be training himself to see threat everywhere
and losing the ease that had defined him for decades. What he hoped for, cautiously, was to be able
to stand in his own shop again without feeling that the alley was about to open up behind him.
