Kwame is a 22-year-old school principal who came to treatment carrying a childhood history of
physical abuse by the caregiver who raised him. When he speaks about those years, the story does not
unfold in a steady sequence. He remembers the atmosphere first: the sharp shift in the house when an
adult mood turned without warning, the way ordinary moments could become dangerous before he
understood why. He can point to certain scenes with painful clarity—an old family photograph on a
shelf, his younger self standing stiffly beside the adult who hurt him, the expression on his own
face already watchful—but when he tries to deliberately recount what happened across those years, he
loses the thread. He says the memories come as broken pieces, vivid beyond ordinary remembering, as
if some images are happening now while the rest remain unreachable.

In the years since, unpredictability has become especially potent for him. At school, where he
oversees staff and students, sudden changes in the day can affect him more than he thinks they
should. An unexpected conflict in a hallway, a meeting that shifts tone without warning, a teacher
appearing at his office door in visible distress can leave him braced before he has had time to
think. On mornings after a nightmare, he wakes with the feeling of the dream still clinging to him,
and the school building can seem charged from the moment he walks in. At those times, a trigger does
not bring back a full story so much as it pulls up disconnected flashes—sensory fragments, an image
of a hand, the feeling of waiting for something bad to happen. Those fragments arrive with a force
that feels more real than normal memory, and they quickly shape what he tells himself about who he
is now.

What frightens Kwame most is not only the past but what it seems to mean about him. When he feels
that rush inside, he has come to believe he may not be able to control his anger and could do
something terrible. He also worries that the abuse permanently changed him for the worse, that there
is something damaged in him that cannot be undone. Beneath both fears is a more familiar conviction
from childhood: that bad things happen and he cannot stop them. A trigger as small as waking from a
nightmare or noticing an old photo on his phone can bring those beliefs forward immediately, so that
the present moment feels like proof that he is still at the mercy of something dangerous inside him
or around him.

Those appraisals also shape the way he relates to the memories themselves. Because he dreads what he
might find if he gets too close to them, and because he fears the feelings they stir mean he is
losing control, he approaches the past in a strained, effortful way. He tries to pin it down
deliberately and becomes frustrated when he cannot produce a coherent account. The harder he pushes,
the more the memory seems to splinter. What comes instead are sharply lit fragments that feel
overpoweringly real, which only deepens his sense that something in him is wrong. Afterward he can
be left convinced that his mind is broken or that he is fundamentally less safe than other people.

The fragments themselves keep teaching him to expect danger. Because the memories arrive in pieces
and with such immediacy, old photographs have become difficult for him to tolerate; an image can
feel less like a reminder than like a portal into the original fear. Unpredictable situations carry
a similar charge, because the disjointed memories are organized around suddenness rather than
explanation. Even waking from a nightmare becomes a trigger for the day ahead, not just because of
the dream but because the residue of it tells him he is still vulnerable to being overtaken. He
starts scanning himself for signs of rising anger and scanning the environment for the next abrupt
shift, as if anticipating danger might finally let him prevent it.

Over time this has narrowed his life at work and at home. In the school setting he tries to keep
routines tightly controlled and finds himself disproportionately unsettled when plans change. He may
retreat to his office after an emotionally charged interaction, waiting for the sense of threat to
pass before seeing the next person. With his partner, he has struggled to explain why a bad night’s
sleep or an old picture can leave him distant and on edge. What finally brought him to seek help was
the realization that he was beginning to fear not just his memories but himself: after waking from
another nightmare and arriving at school already tense, he found himself preoccupied all day with
the thought that one unpredictable moment might push him past control. He is seeking treatment
because he wants to know whether the past has truly ruined him, and because he is terrified that if
he does not understand these reactions, they will keep ruling his life.
