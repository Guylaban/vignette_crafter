At 74, Minh Tran had spent most of his adult life in the United States, building a steady life with
his wife and working as a welder, but the memories that brought him to treatment began much earlier,
when he was a civilian in a war zone as a young man in Southeast Asia. He described one attack in
particular that has never settled into a clear story. He remembers the sound first: engines and cars
in the street, then noise, panic, people running, and the sudden sense that there was no safe place
to stand in the open. What remains with him is fractured. He can recall pieces of what happened, but
large parts feel missing, as if the middle has been torn out. At times the memory feels unreal even
to him, too extreme to belong to his own life, and at other times it feels strangely alien, as
though he is remembering something that happened to another man. Yet the gaps themselves have become
meaningful to him. Minh has come to believe that if he had acted differently, moved sooner, chosen
another place to stand, the event might not have unfolded as it did. The broken, dreamlike quality
of the memory has not softened it; instead it leaves him fixed on the conviction that what happened
was deeply unfair and that the people responsible were never held accountable.

Those beliefs have followed him for decades and shape how he moves through the present. He is
certain that nothing was ever made right, and that sense of unresolved injustice keeps the danger
feeling current rather than past. He lies awake for long stretches before sleep, feeling he must
stay alert, as if letting his guard down would be foolish. During the day he notices how quickly his
attention breaks apart, especially when there is noise around him. At the welding shop, the clatter
of vehicles outside or the rev of a car in the lot can pull him out of what he is doing in an
instant. He becomes watchful, scanning the space around him before he can return to the task in
front of him. What frightens him most is how easily ordinary sounds are no longer ordinary; because
he believes those who caused such harm were never stopped, part of him remains braced for danger to
appear again.

The triggers are now woven into daily life. Car sounds are the strongest. If a vehicle backfires
outside the shop or a car accelerates sharply on the street, the present loosens and the old scene
presses forward in fragments: noise, exposure, confusion, missing pieces that he cannot organize.
Sometimes even a spell of breathlessness, especially when he is exerting himself in a hot workspace
with a welding mask on, jolts him into the same state. News footage or related war media can do it
as well; a few seconds on television are enough to leave him unsettled for hours. Because these
moments so often stir up those strange, partial recollections, he has become increasingly careful
about what he allows himself to encounter. He changes the channel immediately, tells himself to
think about something else, and avoids open or exposed spaces whenever he can. At work he prefers
enclosed areas of the shop and dislikes stepping out into wide lots or open yards, where he feels
too visible and unprotected.

Minh also avoids talking about the event, including with his wife. Part of him believes there is no
point in talking because nothing can undo what happened and no one was ever made to answer for it.
Another part feels ashamed, because he still blames himself for the way he acted that day. When the
memory comes in jagged, disconnected pieces, he tries to redirect his mind quickly, pushing it aside
before it can gather force. But the less he says and the faster he turns away, the less coherent it
seems to become. The story remains patchy and unreal, and that incompleteness leaves more room for
self-blame and for the old sense that something terrible and unfinished is still hanging over him.
He notices that after he has spent a day dodging the subject, avoiding open areas, and forcing his
thoughts elsewhere, he is even more on edge by nightfall.

Over the past year the cycle has tightened. Poor sleep leaves him more watchful the next day, and in
that keyed-up state he catches every engine noise and every change in his breathing. Once triggered,
his concentration drops, his body stays tense, and he becomes more determined to shut the whole
thing down by not talking and by mentally steering away from it. His wife has told him he seems far
away, especially in the evenings when the television is on and he insists on avoiding any war-
related program without explaining why. He finally agreed to seek help after realizing he was
structuring more and more of his workday and home life around not being caught off guard. What he
fears is that if he stops avoiding it, the memory will become overwhelming; what he hopes, quietly,
is that it might finally begin to feel like something that happened in his life rather than
something broken, unfinished, and still waiting outside.
