mugged in a downtown parking structure after an evening school event. He had stayed late grading and
meeting with a parent, then walked to his car through a concrete stairwell crowded with people
leaving a nearby game. He remembers the press of bodies, the echoing noise, and then two men closing
in on him near his car while others moved past without stopping. One of them shoved him against the
door, demanded his wallet and phone, and yelled in his face. Daniel can still describe the metallic
taste that flooded his mouth at that moment, and the way everything became unnaturally vivid at
once—the smell of exhaust, the sound of footsteps and shouting, the cold car door at his back—as if
his mind had recorded the scene in one brutal, total impression. What has stayed with him almost as
strongly as the assault itself is his conviction that no one who should have helped actually did:
security never appeared, bystanders kept moving, and the police response afterward felt perfunctory.
He had come away certain that what happened was not only terrifying but profoundly unfair.

In the weeks that followed, Daniel found that the memory did not behave like a past event. It forced
itself into his mind without warning, and when it did, it felt completely real rather than
remembered. In the faculty parking lot, if students rushed past him at dismissal or if a classroom
door slammed, he could suddenly taste that same metallic tang and feel as though he were back
against the car. Riding in a car with his wife or a colleague became difficult because being
enclosed with others and unable to control the situation made the scene return with full force.
Crowded hallways between classes were especially potent; the compression of bodies and noise seemed
to pull the memory forward before he had time to think.

Those moments did more than bring back the attack. Each time he was jolted into reliving it, Daniel
found himself thinking again that the world was dangerous, that people could turn on you instantly,
and that nobody in charge would really protect you. A loud burst of laughter in the corridor or a
packed grocery aisle did not feel neutral to him anymore; they seemed like reminders of how exposed
he was and how easily order could collapse. The triggers had started to carry meaning. By the time
he noticed his heart racing in a crowd, he was already telling himself that this was exactly how bad
things begin.

As these beliefs hardened, the memories seemed to come even more readily. Daniel spent a great deal
of time turning over the unfairness of the robbery and the lack of consequence for the men who
attacked him. He was angry at the building security, angry at the police, angry that ordinary
routines like walking to his car had become contaminated. When his mind returned to those
conclusions—no one had protected him, nothing had been made right, the world was unsafe—the sensory
reliving was close behind, as if those thoughts opened the door to the scene all over again.

grabbed. He understood that this looked excessive to other people, but to him it felt like the only
sensible response to a memory that still arrived as if the assault were happening in the present.

exits, and reacted to movement, the more evidence he seemed to gather that danger was everywhere. He
had become convinced that safety was mostly an illusion and that institutions only looked protective
until something happened. His wife told him he was never really relaxed anymore, and Daniel agreed;
even at school, a place he used to experience as familiar and structured, he moved through the day
as if trouble might break out at any minute.

His vigilance also made ordinary settings feel like threats in advance. If he entered a crowded
restaurant, he was already monitoring the room so intensely that the noise, bodies, and sudden
gestures quickly became the very triggers he dreaded. Riding with others was similar: because he was
so alert to being trapped or surprised, every unexpected brake light, lane change, or raised voice
in the car primed the robbery scene to intrude. And each time the memory burst in again with its
full sensory force, it seemed to broaden the range of what could set him off next time.

Daniel finally sought help after leaving a school assembly abruptly when students stood and surged
toward the exits. A chair scraped loudly, someone brushed past him, and for a moment he was back in

quickly he lost his bearings and by how much his world had narrowed around crowds, noise, and shared
car rides. What frightened him most was the sense that his mind was teaching him to expect attack
everywhere; what he hopes for now is not to erase what happened, but to stop living as though it is
still happening.
