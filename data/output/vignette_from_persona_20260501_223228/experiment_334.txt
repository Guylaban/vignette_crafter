Nisha Patel was 32 when she came for treatment, a first-term City Council member who had built a
public identity around composure and accessibility. The event that changed that happened six months
earlier at a crowded evening town hall in a municipal building downtown. She had stayed late after
the formal meeting, speaking with residents in a narrow corridor outside the chamber, when an
explosive device detonated near the entrance. She remembered the violent concussion more than a
coherent sequence: the lights cutting out, plaster dust filling the air, the crush of bodies surging
toward the exits, people screaming and falling over one another in the dark. What stayed with her
most was the trapped feeling. For several seconds she could not tell where the nearest way out was,
and as she was pushed against the wall she became certain that if there were a second blast she
would die before she could get clear. Even now, enclosed public buildings, evening events, and any
room where she cannot immediately locate a door carry that same charge.

In the weeks after, Nisha had come to believe that the bombing revealed something fundamental rather
than exceptional: the world was dangerous, people could turn violent without warning, and safety
depended on never letting her guard down. She was certain that what had happened proved how quickly
ordinary civic life could become lethal. At night this conviction intensified. Darkness felt like
reduced information, fewer cues, more opportunity for danger to approach unseen. She found herself
thinking that she had to be especially careful because you never know what can happen next, and that
if she relaxed even briefly she would miss the sign that could save her.

Those beliefs began to organize her days. Before entering any council chamber, restaurant,
constituent office, or campaign venue, she now identified exits and secondary exits before she could
settle into her seat. If an event was in an unfamiliar building, she often declined it altogether,
telling staff she had a scheduling conflict when in fact she could not bear the thought of being
somewhere she had not already assessed for danger. During meetings she scanned faces in the
audience, vigilant for dangerous people, and tracked hands, bags, and sudden movements instead of
listening fully to whoever was speaking. She also started avoiding physical exertion: she stopped
taking the stairs to council offices, cut short walks between buildings, and avoided rushing,
because any pounding in her chest or shortness of breath made her feel exposed and less able to
react. In her mind these habits were not excessive but necessary; if the world was this dangerous,
being on guard all the time felt like the only rational way to move through it.

Over time, the habits that were meant to keep her safe made danger feel even closer. Because she was
constantly checking the room, every public appearance produced something to notice: a man lingering
near the back wall, an unfamiliar aide entering late, a door that seemed partially blocked, a sharp
sound from the hallway. The fact that she always had to map an escape route before she felt
comfortable meant she rarely felt comfortable at all. Avoiding unfamiliar places narrowed her world
until even routine invitations felt risky by default. Nights became the worst; after evening council
sessions she would sit awake in her apartment, listening for sounds in the corridor outside her
door, unable to feel safe enough to sleep. The more she lived this way, the more convinced she
became that her vigilance was justified. If she was this alert, she reasoned, it must be because
there was truly so much to watch for.

This had begun to alter her work in visible ways. Staff noticed that she insisted on arriving early
to every venue so she could inspect the layout and choose where to sit. She repositioned chairs in
community rooms so she would not have her back to an entrance. She turned down site visits in
unfamiliar neighborhoods unless she could be given detailed floor plans in advance. Constituents
experienced her as distracted; during one listening session she realized she had spent more time
monitoring a man in a heavy jacket near the door than hearing a resident describe a housing
complaint. She was ashamed of this, but what frightened her most was the idea that lowering her
guard would mean repeating the mistake she believed everyone had made before the blast—assuming
normalcy where there was none.

By the time she sought help, she was exhausted from living as if threat were always about to break
through. She still told herself that constant vigilance was the price of survival, yet her life had
become smaller and more governed by exits, lighting, and familiar rooms. She came in after canceling
an evening public forum because it had been moved at the last minute to an unfamiliar school
auditorium, and she found herself trembling in the car park, unable to go in. Nisha said she wanted
her life and her work back, but she was afraid that treatment might require her to stop being
careful. More than anything, she hoped to find a way to be in public again without feeling that
catastrophe was waiting just beyond the door.
