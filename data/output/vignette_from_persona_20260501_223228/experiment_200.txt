career around staying composed, responsive, and quick. The event that fractured that sense of
herself happened on an ordinary late afternoon drive home from work. She had been inching through
traffic after a long day, distracted for a split second as her phone lit up on the passenger seat
with work notifications she did not answer. When the line of cars ahead slowed, she braked too late
and struck a cyclist crossing in front of her lane. She still remembers the sickening thud and the
way time seemed to collapse around the sight of his body hitting the pavement. What has stayed with
her most sharply is the smell: hot rubber, engine heat, and a metallic, burnt odor that she says
comes back “like it’s in the room.” In the immediate aftermath she was coherent enough to call 911,
but later she found she could not describe the sequence without stopping mid-sentence, knowing
exactly what had happened and yet unable to find words for it.

In the weeks that followed, Mariam became preoccupied with what the accident said about her. She had
come to believe that a decent, careful person would not have done what she did, even by accident,
and this conviction spread into a broader mistrust of herself. She second-guessed small decisions at
work, reread posts before scheduling them, and hovered over routine approvals as if one careless
click could hurt someone again. She often thought that the person she had become was unrecognizable
to her and that something in her had been permanently damaged. Those beliefs seemed to shape the way
the memory returned: sometimes she would think about the crash and be flooded by the smell of hot
rubber and metal, and other times she would describe it flatly, almost with surprise at her own
numbness, as if the scene had happened to a stranger. Even that emotional deadness frightened her,
because to Mariam it seemed like proof that she was no longer the person she had been.

Her work environment kept confronting her with moments she could not fully predict. In the open-plan
office, coworkers often tapped her shoulder to ask about a campaign revision or a client comment,
and she would jolt violently, heart racing, turning with alarm before she knew where she was. A
sudden touch on the subway, a pedestrian stepping too close in the crosswalk outside her building,
even the abrupt movement of a cyclist passing her window could make her react as though danger were
already present. These false alarms were not random to her. She was certain she could no longer
trust herself to judge what was safe or what was happening around her, and that sense of being
fundamentally unreliable kept her on edge. The more she saw herself as someone who had already
caused catastrophic harm once, the more her body behaved as if another disaster were always about to
break open.

brakes in a parking garage or exhaust in city traffic could bring back the smell from the accident
so vividly that she felt transported to the roadside. At other times the recollection came stripped
of feeling, just visual fragments and facts, and that peculiar numbness did not calm her; it made
her more alarmed. She would think, if I can remember this without feeling anything, what else is
wrong with me? In those moments her fear, guilt, and shame surged together, and she became jumpier
for hours afterward, reacting to ordinary noises and unexpected touch as if they signaled fresh
threat.

That pattern also ran in the other direction. After a day spent braced against startle and scanning
her surroundings, the accident felt closer and easier to trigger. By evening, if she had been bumped
on the train twice or startled in the office, she was more likely to smell the crash again in her
mind when she closed her apartment door, or to sit on her couch unable to put any of it into words.
Her own constant alarm seemed to pull the memory nearer. And once she had been set off repeatedly,
she judged herself even more harshly: a normal person would have moved on, she thought; a
trustworthy person would not react like this. Each flare of fear and each reflexive jolt to
unexpected touch became, for Mariam, further evidence that she was altered for the worse.

shoulder taps and crowded commute that kept setting her off. She delayed decisions until colleagues
followed up twice, and one supervisor commented that she seemed hesitant in a role that used to suit
her confidence. Mariam heard this as confirmation that she was no longer herself. By the time she
came to treatment, what finally pushed her to ask for help was not only the persistence of the
memories, but the fear that her whole life was reorganizing around them. She was frightened that she
had become someone permanently dangerous, permanently diminished, and she wanted desperately to know
whether she could ever trust herself again.
