At 34, Michael had been a firefighter for most of his adult life, and before that he had spent
several months as part of a relief team in a war zone, deployed to assist with extraction and
emergency medical support after repeated shelling of civilian neighborhoods. What stayed with him
was not a single dramatic image so much as the constant lesson that safety could vanish in an
instant. He remembered entering damaged apartment blocks where families had gathered in stairwells
because they believed interior walls would protect them, only to find the exits blocked by debris
after the next blast. In crowded aid stations he had learned to register doors, windows,
bottlenecks, who was moving too quickly, who did not belong. Men who looked ordinary one moment
could become a threat the next. The most enduring imprint was that danger came without warning and
that being unprepared, even briefly, could cost lives.

When he returned home to the United States, his wife expected that the structure of his job and the
familiarity of home would help him settle. Instead, Michael found that ordinary settings now felt
exposed. He walked into restaurants and immediately mapped the exits before he registered who was
speaking to him. At the firehouse he chose the chair with the clearest line to the door and felt a
jolt of tension if someone stood behind him. Even at home he rarely relaxed fully; he checked the
locks repeatedly, noticed the sound of a car door outside, and kept a part of his attention tuned to
whether he could get his wife out quickly if something happened. Strangers unsettled him in ways he
found hard to explain. If someone approached too directly in a grocery store parking lot or lingered
near him in line at a coffee shop, his body reacted as if he were back in a place where an ordinary
approach could conceal catastrophic intent.

Over the months, he organized his life more and more tightly around staying ready. He began making
sure he was always near an exit, not as a preference but as a rule he felt he could not afford to
break. If his wife suggested a table in the middle of a restaurant, he would steer them to the edge
of the room without discussion; if that was not possible, he wanted to leave. He also stopped
exercising, though he had once relied on physical training as part of his identity as a firefighter.
Getting his heart rate up and his breathing hard made him feel exposed rather than strong, too close
to the bodily state of emergency. He had also started pushing away good moments with his wife
because warmth and ease felt unsafe, almost irresponsible. If he caught himself laughing on a day
off or feeling content on the couch beside her, he would shut it down internally, as though letting
himself feel positive emotion would mean letting his guard drop.

These habits did not calm him. They seemed instead to teach him, day after day, that danger was
always near. Because he was constantly positioning himself by doors, scanning buildings as soon as
he entered, and avoiding anything that made him feel physically open or emotionally off guard, the
world increasingly appeared to confirm his sense that threat was everywhere. Staying near exits kept
his attention fixed on escape. Avoiding exercise left him wary of his own arousal, so even minor
activation could feel like a sign that something was wrong. Pushing away positive feelings narrowed
his life to vigilance, and in that narrowed state strangers seemed more menacing, his own house less
reliably safe. By trying not to be caught unprepared, he had come to live as though he were already
under threat.

His work environment sharpened this pattern. At the station, where crews and civilians came and went
unpredictably, he found himself scanning every room on entry and tracking where people were standing
rather than listening to conversation. On calls he remained functional, but back at base he could
not settle into the ordinary banter that used to restore him between emergencies. His wife had begun
to notice that even at home he sat where he could see the front door and tensed when delivery
drivers or neighbors approached the porch. She experienced him as present but unreachable, always a
little braced, and arguments had begun over his insistence on leaving public places early or
refusing activities they used to enjoy together.

Michael finally sought help after his wife told him that home no longer felt like home with him
constantly on watch. He did not describe himself as afraid in a simple way; he was more certain that
relaxing was dangerous and that only constant readiness kept disaster at bay. What frightened him
most was how ordinary his world had become on the surface while, inside it, he felt perpetually
positioned for escape. He wants to be able to sit in his own living room without tracking exits and
to walk into a room without first deciding how to get out. More quietly, he wants to be able to feel
close to his wife again without experiencing that closeness as a risk.
