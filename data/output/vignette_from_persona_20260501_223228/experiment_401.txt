David is a 58-year-old dentist who came to treatment after more than a year of feeling as if his
life had narrowed into something tense, watchful, and unrecognizable. The trauma he returns to began
after he was pulled over driving home from his practice one evening. He describes it as an ordinary
stop that turned violent with a speed he still cannot fully comprehend. He remembers an officer
shouting at him to keep his hands visible, then another voice cutting across it, louder and closer,
and being yanked from the car before he understood what he had done wrong. He was forced onto the
pavement, his face turned to the side, his chest pinned down, and he felt sharp pain through his
shoulder and ribs. What stayed with him most vividly were the sounds—the barked commands, the radio
crackling, the metallic clatter near his ear—and the bodily pain of being pressed down while he
tried to say he was a doctor, that he was cooperating. No apology followed, and no meaningful
accountability came afterward. David had come to believe there was something about him that made
this happen, something in him that had marked him out to be treated that way, and at the same time
he was consumed by the conviction that it was deeply unjust and that nothing had been done about it.
He speaks of that contradiction with bitterness: he knows it was wrong, yet he still feels, at some
level, that he must have invited it.

In the months after, the memory did not settle into the past. It often came back in a way that felt
strange and disconnected, as if it belonged to somebody else and not to him. At other times it
arrived with brutal sensory force: he would hear the officer’s voice in his head with startling
clarity, or feel pain in his shoulder and side in the same places that had hurt on the pavement.
Those returns seemed to confirm his bleakest conclusions. When the sounds and pain came back so
vividly, he took it as proof that the incident had ruined his life and permanently altered him; the
fact that the memory felt alien only deepened his fear that something fundamental in him had been
broken.

His work as a dentist has become one of the main places where this pattern plays out. He still goes
to the office, but he is preoccupied with where people are in relation to him. If a new patient or
delivery person comes in unexpectedly, he feels a jolt of threat that far exceeds the situation,
especially when a stranger approaches him quickly in a hallway or waiting area. In meetings he
chooses the chair with the clearest view of the door and the easiest exit, and in restaurants after
work he does the same. He sleeps badly, often lying awake for hours, listening for sounds in the
house and mentally tracking where every person is. This persistent sense that danger could come from
ordinary strangers keeps him scanning faces, entrances, posture, tone of voice. That constant
vigilance leaves him exhausted and keyed up, and in that state the memory is easier to trigger; a
man raising his voice in the parking lot outside the clinic, or the clipped authority in a security
guard’s speech, can bring back the shouted commands and the pain in his body almost immediately. The
more often this happens, the more certain David becomes that the world is not safe and that the
police assault destroyed the life he used to have.

To manage this, David has steadily organized his days around avoidance. He tries not to feel much of
anything at all, not only fear and anger but also excitement or pleasure, because any strong emotion
seems capable of opening the door to the memory. He avoids stressful situations whenever he can,
delegates difficult conversations at the practice, leaves crowded places early, and turns down
social invitations from friends he once enjoyed seeing. When he cannot avoid being out, he checks
his surroundings constantly. These strategies make sense to him in the moment: if he stays numb, if
he keeps life small, if he notices every possible threat before it reaches him, perhaps he can
prevent the memory from taking over again. But the cost has been cumulative. The more he constricts
his life and monitors the environment, the more the trauma stays active in his mind; his efforts to
suppress it seem only to make the sounds and bodily pain return more abruptly, and each return
reinforces his view that he has been permanently damaged. His partner has told him he seems absent
even when sitting in the same room, always half-listening for something else, and David hears that
as further evidence that the trauma has already taken too much.

By the time he sought help, what finally frightened him was not only the intensity of the memories
but the feeling that he was disappearing behind them. He was still going to the dental office, still
functioning on the surface, but he felt increasingly certain that strangers were dangerous, that he
could not let himself relax, and that his life had been derailed beyond repair. He says he wants
help because he cannot keep living as if every room must be assessed for escape and every emotion
shut down. What he fears most is that this is now who he is, and what he hopes, cautiously, is that
the memory might someday feel like his past rather than an attack that keeps happening in the
present.
