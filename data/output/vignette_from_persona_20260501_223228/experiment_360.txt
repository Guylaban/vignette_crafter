Maya is a 21-year-old electrician apprentice who had been proud, before the crash, of how steady she
was with her hands and how quickly she learned on job sites. The accident happened late in the
afternoon when she was riding with a coworker to pick up materials. They were moving through an

remembers the violent sideways jolt, the burst of shattered glass, and the sickening sight of
another vehicle filling the window an instant before impact. Her body was thrown hard against the
door and seat belt, and for a few moments afterward she could not tell whether the shaking in her
arms meant she was badly hurt or only terrified. The other driver survived, but in the months that
followed Maya could not shake the conviction that what happened had been deeply unfair, that her
life had been split open by someone else’s recklessness and that nothing meaningful had been done
about it.

In the weeks afterward, certain moments took on a charged quality that felt out of proportion even
to her. Seeing roadside footage of cars crumpled after accidents, or even passing a fresh collision
on the highway, brought back the visual impression of a vehicle suddenly rushing toward her door.
Riding in a car with other people was especially difficult. She found herself bracing at
intersections, watching the angle of approaching cars, and tensing when someone else had control of
the wheel. Conversations about the future also became unexpectedly painful. When family asked about
where she saw herself in a year, or whether she wanted to finish her certification and move out on
her own, she felt herself go blank or irritable, as if being asked to imagine a future exposed
something fragile and uncertain.

What frightened Maya most in those moments was not only the memory of the crash but what it seemed
to prove about her. When she was trapped in a passenger seat or confronted with images of mangled
cars, she had come to believe she could not trust herself to do the right thing if danger came
again. The old confidence she had in her judgment felt gone. The shaking and weakness she remembered
from the collision had also become evidence, in her mind, that her body was unreliable now, liable
to freeze or fail when she needed it most. Even talking about future plans touched the same sore
point: if she could be blindsided once and if her own reactions could not be counted on, then
planning ahead felt naive. Each trigger seemed to confirm her sense that she was no longer someone
dependable, physically or mentally, and that the unfairness of the crash was still hanging over her
life unanswered.

trips long before they happened. Because she was certain she could not rely on herself or her body
if something went wrong in traffic, every ride with others felt more dangerous than it objectively
was. When coworkers casually talked over lunch about where they wanted to be in five years, she
would withdraw or make a joke to end the conversation; in her mind, looking too far ahead invited
the same kind of blindside, and she felt almost superstitious about it. The more unfair and
unresolved the crash seemed, the more sharply she noticed accident scenes, dented bumpers, and news
clips about collisions. Her attention kept finding reminders that other cars could come out of
nowhere, and each reminder landed on the same conclusion that she was not safe with her own body or
judgment.

Over months, this changed her life in ways that embarrassed her. She remained committed to the
trade, but transportation became a quiet ordeal built into the work itself. She sometimes rearranged
tasks to avoid riding with others, which drew comments from supervisors who saw her as less flexible
than before. At home, she shut down conversations with her parents when they asked about saving
money, future jobs, or whether she might want her own place. Being single had once felt incidental;
now it fit with her retreat from imagining a life unfolding normally. She hated that her world
seemed to narrow around traffic, accident images, and any demand to think ahead, yet she also felt
these reactions were the only sensible response to what had happened.

afterward she was shaken by how quickly her mind went back to the certainty that she would fail if
anything happened. She says she wants to feel like herself again at work and to be able to hear
ordinary talk about the future without feeling trapped. What she fears most is that the crash
revealed something permanent—that her body cannot be trusted, that her judgment will fail at the
worst moment, and that her life will keep organizing itself around one brutal, unfair afternoon.
