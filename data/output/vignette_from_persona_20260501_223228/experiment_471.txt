Matthias is a 52-year-old film director who came to treatment months after a life-threatening
accident on location that still feels to him less like something that happened in the past than
something waiting just off camera. During a night shoot in a remote industrial area, a lighting rig
failed and crashed as a vehicle was being repositioned. In the confusion that followed, power
dropped out across part of the set, leaving stretches of sudden darkness broken by harsh work
lights, shouted instructions, and the metallic blast of equipment hitting concrete. Matthias was
knocked down and briefly trapped amid cables and debris, convinced for several seconds that another
collapse was coming and that he might die before anyone reached him. What remains with him now is
not a coherent story but a sequence of sharp images: the angled glare of a work lamp, sparks, the
black gap beyond the set, a twisted stand descending into view. He says the memory is
extraordinarily clear and detailed, yet when he tries to describe it aloud he loses his words and
becomes frustrated, as if the event exists in pictures his mind can replay but not translate.

That quality of memory has worn on his sense of himself. Because the accident returns in these
vivid, mostly visual fragments that he cannot fully put into language, Matthias has come to believe
there is something fundamentally inadequate about him. He compares his current state to the
composed, decisive director he had always known himself to be and thinks, with shame, that his
reactions prove he is a lousy coper. At times he says he barely recognizes the man he has become:
someone derailed by a moment on his own set, unable even to give a straightforward account of it.

Those conclusions have shaped how he lives day to day. Convinced that his ongoing reactions mean
weakness rather than injury, he works hard to manage them privately. He distracts himself
relentlessly, burying himself in editing notes, streaming rough cuts late into the night, or
scrolling through production schedules whenever his mind drifts toward the accident. He also finds
himself circling the same unanswerable question—why this happened to him, why on a set he was
responsible for, why he was the one who cannot seem to move on. Most concretely, he avoids
circumstances that remind him of that night, especially darkness. He has started insisting on
overlit rooms at home and on set, delegating night exterior shoots when he can, lingering in his
trailer until others have checked locations first. The more he sees these maneuvers as necessary,
the more they seem to confirm his view that he is no longer capable in the way he once was.

At work, the sensory world of filmmaking has become a minefield. Unexpected bangs from dropped
equipment, a slammed stage door, or a sound cue played too loudly can make him jolt violently. He
scans constantly for what might go wrong, listening for changes in the room and tracking crew
movement with a vigilance he did not have before. Those reactions are tied not only to the set
environment but to the way the accident lives in his mind: a sudden metallic crash or the hum-and-
cut of power can call up the image of the rig falling so quickly that his body responds before he
has fully registered where he is. Once that happens, he becomes even more watchful, certain that if
he lets his guard down he will be caught off balance again.

That sense of threat feeds directly into further avoidance. If a sound check is chaotic or a
generator backfires, Matthias may abruptly step away under the pretense of taking a call, then
remain in brighter, quieter areas for the rest of the shift. He tells himself he is being practical,
but his world has narrowed. Crew members have noticed he is less available during complicated
setups, and his partner has noticed that he keeps lamps on throughout the apartment and changes the
subject when the accident comes up. His attempts to distract himself and stay away from reminders
have not softened the memory. If anything, the images intrude more forcefully in the spaces he is
trying to outrun, and because he never stays with them long enough to make them into a fuller
narrative, they remain jagged, sensory, and immediate.

Over time this pattern has tightened. The more Matthias avoids darkness, distracts himself, and
searches for a reason the accident happened, the more fragile and altered he feels; the more fragile
he feels, the more he monitors for danger and braces at ordinary sounds. He finally sought help
after startling so badly at a crashing prop case during a daytime production meeting that he knocked
over his chair in front of his crew. What frightened him most was not just the embarrassment, but
the thought that this was who he is now—someone unrecognizable to himself, always on guard, ruled by
images he cannot master. What he hopes, cautiously, is that he can find a way to speak about the
accident without being swallowed by it, and return to a set without feeling that catastrophe is
about to happen again.
