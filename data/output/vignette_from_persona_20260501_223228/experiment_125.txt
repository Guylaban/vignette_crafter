Kwame is a 46-year-old park ranger who came to treatment after months of telling his wife he was
only “stressed” and could handle it on his own. He had served in combat years earlier, and when he
describes the worst event, he does so with a flat, controlled voice that slips at the edges. His
unit had been moving through a dry area under aircraft overhead when the situation turned suddenly.
He remembers the chopping sound above them, the abrupt blasts, the smell in the air that mixed fuel,
hot dust, and something burned, and the moment he froze while another man closer to the line of fire
went down. What has stayed with him is not only terror but a settled conviction that he failed a
test that mattered: he did not do enough, fast enough, and something in him has been wrong ever
since.

In the years after coming home, Kwame built a life that seemed to fit someone who liked the outdoors
and structure. As a park ranger, he spends long stretches on trails, at entrance stations, and
around campgrounds, listening for changes in weather, watching visitors, and responding to problems
before they grow. But the sounds of helicopters or low aircraft passing over the park cut through
him immediately. On those days he feels uncomfortable in his own body before he can think clearly
about why, and sometimes, standing near a trailhead or ranger vehicle, he has the unnerving
sensation that things are becoming unreal, as if he is slightly outside himself. The moment that
happens, he goes on high alert. He starts tracking every sudden noise, watches visitors’ hands and
movements more closely, and scans tree lines, parked cars, and open ground as if danger might emerge
from anywhere.

These reactions do not feel random to him. Each time he catches himself flinching at aircraft noise
or feeling detached, it confirms what he has come to believe about himself: that he is permanently
changed for the worse and that ordinary people do not live like this. He tells himself he has to be
on guard all the time because he is no longer capable of relaxing safely. The trigger is frightening
not just because it startles him, but because it seems to prove that the war is still inside him,
ready to take over.

His beliefs about what happened also shape how quickly he is set off now. Because he is certain that
vigilance is necessary, he moves through the park already keyed up, listening for engines and loud
sounds and bracing against the possibility of feeling detached. A helicopter carrying tourists over
the canyon, or even aircraft he cannot yet see, can become the focus of his attention long before it
is overhead. He has come to expect these moments, and that expectation seems to make them easier to
trip.

Once he is in that state of alert, the memories come with unusual force. If he hears unexpected loud
sounds while on patrol, he does not simply remember the combat incident in outline; the memory
arrives with sharp detail, more vivid than most of the rest of his life. He can almost smell again
the fuel and scorched air from that day, and he describes those moments as more real than normal
memory, as if the past is pressing directly into the present. Watching other people closely for
possible harm and scanning his surroundings seem to pull him back toward the same sensory fragments,
as though his mind is searching the current scene for the old danger and finding the old images
instead.

Those memories, in turn, deepen the conclusions he has drawn about himself. When the smell and sound
come back so vividly, he is overwhelmed by guilt that he did not do more to stop what happened. He
reads the intensity of the memory as evidence that he has never truly left the event and never will.
To him, the fact that the past can feel this immediate means the damage is permanent.

His constant watchfulness has also begun to feed those same beliefs. At work he monitors visitors so
closely that he rarely feels at ease, even on routine days, and by the time he gets home he is
exhausted and irritable. He sees this exhaustion as proof that his mind cannot switch off and that
being on guard is now simply who he is. On the trail, if a sudden sound makes him tense and
detached, the reaction itself becomes another warning sign to him that he is not safe inside his own
body.

What finally brought Kwame in was an incident in the park when a helicopter passed low during a busy
afternoon and he lost his sense of the present for several seconds while speaking with a family
asking for directions. He recovered quickly, but he was shaken by how unreal everything had felt and
by how intensely the old smells and images rushed back afterward. He is afraid that he has become a
man who will always live as if an attack is about to happen, and that his wife is right when she
says he has been disappearing even while standing next to her. What he hopes, though he says it
cautiously, is not to erase the past but to stop living as if every sound overhead means he is back
there again.
