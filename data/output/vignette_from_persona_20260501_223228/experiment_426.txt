the smell of fuel and sweat, and a moment of intense bodily vulnerability when he was caught without
clothing and felt completely defenseless. Even in the office, when a motorbike revs in the street
below, he does not just remember the event; he hears the sound again in his head as if it is
happening now.

outside the house, or when he catches a related smell in a parking garage or at a gas station, Minh
feels his body tighten before he has fully registered why. The cue is immediately loaded with
danger. He becomes certain that he is not safe, even at home, and if a stranger comes too close in a
grocery line or on a sidewalk he experiences the approach as threatening rather than neutral. He has
also become wary of unfamiliar places because he cannot quickly judge exits, distances, or who may
be nearby. The triggers are potent partly because they pull up the sensory pieces of the combat
memory so abruptly; the smell or sound seems to bring the scene with it, and the more often this
happens, the more sensitized he has become to noticing those same cues everywhere around him.

Over time he began organizing his life to prevent that surge. He stopped watching war films, action
shows, and even news coverage if there might be footage or discussion that resembled combat. He
rarely goes out unless necessary, preferring routes and locations he already knows. He has also
given up exercise, including jogging outdoors, because being physically exposed and out in public
leaves him feeling too vulnerable and too easy to catch off guard. These choices make sense to him
in the moment: if he avoids the cue, he may avoid the memory, and if he avoids the memory, he may
avoid the sense that danger is present now. Yet the narrowing of his world has had consequences. His
wife has become frustrated that he declines invitations and errands, and at work he has resisted
coming into the office because commuting exposes him to traffic noise, strangers, and places he
cannot fully scan.

The avoidance has not quieted the trauma so much as made it more dominant. Because Minh turns away
from reminders and cuts off situations that might evoke the event, the memory remains largely
unspoken and unprocessed, returning in the same sensory form rather than as a narrative with a
beginning and end. He still experiences it as a replay, accompanied by sounds in his head from the
original event, and his inability to put the worst moments into words leaves him feeling that the
experience is unfinished and close. After a day of staying inside and monitoring the house, small
cues begin to stand out more sharply: a faint smell, an engine outside, a person passing the front
window. The more guarded he feels, the more readily these cues register as signs of immediate
threat, and once they do, the memory is pulled back into the foreground again.

to combat, and each detection seems to confirm that vigilance is necessary. The memories that follow
make going out feel even less possible, so he retreats further, and the retreat leaves him with
fewer experiences that compete with the old scene. Minh finally sought help after his wife told him
