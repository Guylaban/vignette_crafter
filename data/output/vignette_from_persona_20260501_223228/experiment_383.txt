music from a nearby parked car was playing the same love song he had mentioned in one of his
messages. She remembered the way he looked at her then, calm and certain, while she felt trapped in
the open parking lot with no clear place to go. Police were contacted after he followed her for part
of her drive home, but even after a report was filed, Linh did not experience their involvement as
restoring safety. The fact that he had already crossed so many boundaries made the world feel porous
to her, as if protection was more theoretical than real.

with the same sense of exposure. Certain songs were especially potent. If one came on in a grocery
store or drifted from a patient’s phone in the waiting room, she felt her body tense before she had
fully registered why. Related media content had a similar effect; news stories or television scenes
about stalking, harassment, or women being watched could leave her scanning the room and replaying
the sense that no one recognizes danger until it is already too late. Even the presence of police
could make her more uneasy rather than reassured. Seeing an officer near the hospital parking
structure next to her practice, or noticing a patrol car outside, brought back the period when
reports had been made and nothing in her lived experience had changed quickly enough to feel
protective. In those moments she became certain that other people’s assurances were irrelevant. Even
if her husband told her she was safe, or a colleague reminded her that the office had security
cameras, she could not make herself believe it.

That conviction shaped the way she moved through her days. At the dental practice, Linh began
choosing seats in the staff lounge only if she could watch the doorway. In consultations, if there

see the room. At restaurants she quietly steered her husband toward tables along the perimeter, and

with a solid wall behind her. She described this not as preference but necessity: if she sat exposed
in the middle of a room, she could not follow conversation because too much of her attention was

long she lingered after her last patient. Safety had become, in her mind, something she had to
engineer moment by moment because it could not be trusted to exist on its own.

highly professional, but she noticed herself tracking sounds in the hallway and feeling a surge of
vigilance whenever unfamiliar male voices entered reception. When patients left music playing on
their phones, she found herself listening for particular songs rather than concentrating fully on
charting. At home, if a film trailer or news segment touched on stalking, the rest of the evening
could be colored by the feeling that she needed to check locks again and reconsider where she would
sit in the living room. Her husband had become used to the way she scanned restaurants before
committing to a seat, though she felt ashamed that she could no longer accept his reassurance as
comforting.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was not a new assault, but the dawning realization that she
had organized nearly every environment around anticipated danger and still never felt safe. A song
playing unexpectedly in a café near her office recently left her so shaken that she abandoned lunch

is frightened that this is how her life will remain: always calculating sightlines, always
unconvinced by reassurance, always one reminder away from feeling exposed again. What she hopes for,
carefully and a little skeptically, is not to forget what happened but to stop living as though the
stalker still has access to every room she enters.
