Mele had built a long career as a curriculum designer, someone colleagues knew for order, language,
and calm judgment, which made it harder for her to explain why certain moments now undid her. At 66,
divorced and living alone, she came to treatment saying that what happened during her military
service still did not feel like a story she could tell from beginning to end. In the combat incident
she returns to most often, she had been in a position where decisions were being made over her head
while aircraft circled above, the chopping sound of helicopters cutting through confusion, shouted
commands, and the felt certainty that someone else held absolute power over whether she lived or
died. What stayed with her was not a coherent sequence so much as sharp pieces: the sound overhead,
the sense of being trapped inside someone else’s command, a few visual flashes that arrived without
context. She would start to describe it and stop, telling the therapist she knew what happened but
could not find words for it, and even while speaking she seemed unconvinced that it had really
happened to her, as if the memory belonged to another woman.

Over the years, the present kept collapsing into those fragments. A helicopter passing over her
neighborhood could make the event come to mind all at once, not as a deliberate recollection but as
something forced into awareness. Situations that carried even a mild power imbalance had a similar
effect. A routine meeting at work in which an administrator spoke in a firm, final tone could
suddenly bring back disconnected pieces of the combat scene, and because the memory arrived in
shards rather than in a narrative she could place in the past, it felt unreal and immediate at the
same time. Those moments left her shaken not only by what she remembered but by what the remembering
seemed to say about her. She had come to believe she could not trust herself to do the right thing
under pressure, and whenever she felt cornered by someone with authority, that old self-doubt surged
with the memory. The same triggers also tightened her conviction that she had to stay on guard all
the time; the world felt full of unnoticed openings through which danger might return.

That belief changed how she moved through ordinary life. In conference rooms at the educational
nonprofit where she worked, Mele began choosing seats nearest the door before anyone else arrived.
If a meeting felt tense or hierarchical, she tracked exits instead of content. She turned down
chances to present off-site and gradually avoided going out unless it felt necessary. She also tried
to keep a lid on strong emotion altogether. If she felt herself getting angry, tearful, or even
deeply moved, she shut it down quickly, as though any strong feeling might break the thin barrier
holding the past back. In her mind this made sense: if she could not fully trust her own judgment
and had to remain vigilant, then staying close to an exit and restricting where she went were the
safest ways to avoid being caught off guard again.

But the strategies that were meant to keep her steady narrowed her life and confirmed her worst
conclusions. Because she kept leaving restaurants early, declining invitations, and sitting on the
edge of every room, she felt more separate from other people, including coworkers who had once
depended on her easy presence. She noticed how alone she had become and took that isolation as proof
that she was fundamentally apart from others. At work, her preoccupation with where doors were and
who held authority in the room made power-laden situations stand out even more sharply, so the very
circumstances she feared became easier to detect everywhere. Avoiding strong feeling did not quiet
the combat memory either. If anything, the event stayed unsorted, preserved in fragmented pieces
because she never let herself remain with it long enough for words to come. The less she allowed
herself to feel, the more unreal and inexpressible the memory seemed, and the more easily a
helicopter overhead or a controlling tone of voice could pull it back into the present.

By the time she sought help, the pattern had become hard to ignore. A low-flying news helicopter
over her office one afternoon had derailed her for the rest of the day; she sat through a planning
session near the exit, barely able to follow discussion, convinced she needed to be ready to leave.
Driving home, she realized she had begun organizing her entire world around escape, restraint, and
watchfulness. What frightened her most was not only the memories themselves but the fear that she
would remain trapped in this half-spoken life, unable to trust her own responses and increasingly
cut off from others. What she hoped for, cautiously, was a way to put the pieces into words and stop
living as though danger were always about to enter the room.
