Nadia is a 50-year-old Middle Eastern American woman who came to treatment after years of trying to
keep herself tightly controlled. She works as a social worker in a city resettlement program,
helping newly arrived refugee families navigate housing, schools, and benefits. She rarely speaks at
length about her own flight to the United States, but in the first sessions she described leaving
her home country during a period of violent upheaval after men began stopping women in the street,
watching them, and making it clear that being noticed could become dangerous very quickly. What
stayed with her most was not a single blow or one dramatic scene as much as the constant sense that
danger could emerge from an ordinary moment—a stranger’s stare, footsteps behind her, the wrong
person recognizing her, the feeling of exposure when outside. Even now, she says, her body reacts
before her mind can catch up.

In the months and years after resettlement, Nadia found that daily life never fully felt ordinary
again. She startles into certainty that something is wrong when nothing identifiable is happening.
In grocery store aisles, in parking lots, and even walking from her office to her car after meeting
clients, she feels a jolt of alarm and immediately begins searching faces, exits, shadows, and
parked vehicles. At night she wakes abruptly, already alert, listening for movement in the apartment
and getting up to check locks and windows. She knows, in a rational sense, that she is in a safe
neighborhood and that no one is currently pursuing her, but she cannot relax into that knowledge.
What frightened her most was how quickly a neutral moment could become, in her body, proof that she
was in danger.

Because that sense of threat feels so immediate, Nadia has built her days around not provoking it.
She avoids situations likely to stir strong feeling, turning down social invitations, skipping
community events, and cutting short difficult conversations with colleagues when she feels herself
becoming activated. At work, if the waiting room grows crowded or emotionally charged, she finds
reasons to step into her office and close the door. She dresses in loose, concealing clothing that
does not draw attention, explaining that it is simply more comfortable, though she knows she is
trying to disappear into the background. She also checks her surroundings constantly: when entering
the agency building she scans the lobby, notes who is near the elevator, and chooses seats in
meetings that give her a clear view of the door. The strategies make sense to her because she is
certain that if she lets herself be visible, emotionally open, or off guard, she will be vulnerable
again.

Over time, though, the very habits meant to keep her safe have kept the danger feeling close.
Because she is always scanning, she notices every ambiguous sound and glance, and each small
uncertainty is taken up by her body as another warning. The loose clothing and effort not to be
noticed bring temporary relief, but they also confirm for her that being seen is unsafe. Avoiding
emotionally intense situations means she never has the chance to learn that she can feel fear,
grief, or anger without being overwhelmed or endangered, so even mild emotion begins to feel like
the start of a crisis. On nights when she wakes to check the apartment, the searching itself leaves
her more awake, more vigilant, and less able to settle, as if her nervous system has been reminded
to stay on duty. By morning she is exhausted but still tense, moving through her day braced for the
next false alarm.

This has narrowed her life in ways that are becoming harder to hide. Her adult children have told
her she seems distant and unreachable, and coworkers notice that although she is deeply competent
with clients, she keeps herself set apart and avoids informal gatherings after work. The irony is
painful to her: she spends her days helping other displaced people feel secure, while privately she
is still organizing her own life around the possibility of sudden danger. She finally sought help
after waking several times one night to check the hallway outside her apartment, then arriving at
work depleted and panicked when a client’s raised voice in the corridor made her heart race as if
she were back in the uncertainty she fled. She said she is afraid that if she keeps living this way,
she will disappear inside vigilance entirely. What she hopes for, cautiously, is not to forget what
happened, but to stop feeling that every ordinary moment is about to turn against her.
