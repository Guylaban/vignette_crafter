Mele was a 46-year-old Tongan American musician who had spent most of her adult life performing in
small ensembles, teaching voice lessons, and rehearsing in churches, studios, and community arts
spaces around her city. Before she became ill, she had understood her body as reliable and finely
trained; breath was part of her craft, something she could shape and trust. That changed when a
severe, life-threatening respiratory illness sent her to the hospital. She remembered the most
frightening moments with painful clarity: the sharp medicinal smell on the ward, the plastic dryness
of oxygen tubing, the panic of trying to pull in air and feeling that each breath stopped halfway.
During examinations she had to lie still while people leaned over her, and what stayed with her was
not only the fear of dying but the helplessness of having to wait for someone else to tell her
whether she would keep breathing.

In the months after she recovered medically, Mele found that her sense of danger had not receded.
She woke in the night abruptly and sat up to check whether she was breathing normally, listening to
the room and to her own chest before she could settle again. She often said that her body “went
there before my mind did”: a brief catch in her breath, a tight practice room, or the faint
antiseptic smell in a hallway could send a jolt through her before she had even named what was
happening. Gradually, safety narrowed. Home, especially her own bedroom and a single corner of the
living room, felt manageable; beyond that she felt exposed, as if any unfamiliar place might become
the site of another crisis.

That persistent state of alarm changed how she made sense of herself. Because she was so easily
startled awake, so dependent on checking, and so quickly overtaken by physical fear, she had come to
believe there was something fundamentally inadequate about her. She compared herself bitterly to the
person she had been before the illness and called herself “a lousy coper,” someone who should have
been stronger by now. The fact that her reactions seemed to erupt automatically only deepened her
self-doubt; if she could not trust herself to stay calm or to judge what was actually dangerous,
then she could not trust herself to do the right thing when it mattered.

Those beliefs fed directly into the way she dealt with the aftermath. When she felt weak or
uncertain, she went over counterfactual versions of her life for long stretches—how different things
would be if she had never gotten sick, whether she would still be taking more performance work,
whether her voice would feel like her own. At harsher moments she told herself she deserved to
suffer, as though her continuing fear were a kind of verdict on her character. Convinced that she
did not cope well and might make the wrong decision under pressure, she began avoiding medical cues
whenever she could: putting off routine physical examinations, feeling sick at the sound of
ambulances, and taking elaborate routes to avoid passing the hospital where she had been treated.

Her ongoing sense of threat also pushed those habits further. Because she remained so alert to
danger and trusted only a very limited set of places, avoiding hospitals and examinations felt to
Mele like common sense rather than fear. If her body could react before she even thought, then why
deliberately expose herself to the smells, rooms, and procedures that might set everything off? She
canceled appointments, delayed follow-up care, and asked her husband to handle practical tasks that
involved medical settings. The pattern had begun to affect her work. As a musician, she was
repeatedly confronted by the very thing she no longer trusted: breath. In rehearsals she sometimes
cut phrases short or stepped out when she noticed the slightest shortness of breath, and in older
performance venues, where there might be a musty or chemical smell backstage, she became tense
before going on. What frightened her most was not just the sensation itself, but the speed with
which her body seized on it, making ordinary breathlessness feel like a warning.

Over time, her watchfulness seemed to make those triggers more potent. Because she was constantly
scanning her breathing and waking to make sure she was safe, any small change in respiration stood
out immediately. Smells linked to the hospital hit her with unusual force, and the prospect of a
medical procedure felt unbearable long before it occurred. A simple exam room could feel charged
from the moment she imagined it.

By the time Mele sought help, her world had become smaller than she wanted to admit. She was still
teaching, but she turned down more performances, and her husband had begun urging her to get support
after another night of waking in panic and checking her breath repeatedly until dawn. She came in
feeling ashamed of how restricted she had become and doubtful that she could handle treatment, yet
also frightened by what would happen if she kept living this way. More than anything, she wanted to
trust her body enough to sing and sleep without bracing for catastrophe.
