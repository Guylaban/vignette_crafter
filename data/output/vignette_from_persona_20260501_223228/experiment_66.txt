Kwame is a 55-year-old African American man who has worked for years as a house painter, moving from
room to room with a radio on low and the smell of primer and thinner in the air. He came to
treatment after the sudden death of a loved one, a loss that had split his life into a clear before
and after. The death had unfolded in an emergency setting, and what remains most searing is not a
calm narrative but flashes: the ambulance lights, the sound of urgent voices, the sense that
everything was happening too fast while he could do nothing to stop it. Even now, when he tries to
speak about that day, he does not always get a full scene. Sometimes the feeling arrives first and
all at once—raw grief and panic with no clear picture attached—and at other times what comes are
stark visual fragments, like still photographs he cannot control.

In the weeks after the death, ordinary parts of life began to take on the charge of that day. Seeing
an ambulance on the street could make his whole body tighten before he had even fully registered
what he was looking at. At work, being on a job site became difficult in ways that embarrassed him.
If a coworker mentioned someone who had lost a spouse, parent, or child, Kwame would go quiet and
feel himself leave the conversation internally, as if he were already back in the emotional weather
of the loss. The workplace itself became a setting he dreaded; climbing ladders and painting trim in
clients’ homes, he found himself listening for sirens outside and scanning for signs that something
terrible was about to happen. Those moments did more than upset him. They left him feeling that his
life had been shattered beyond repair, and that other people were still moving through an intact
world from which he had been cut off.

As these reactions persisted, Kwame had come to believe that he was no longer the man he had been
before. He described feeling as if he did not know himself anymore: a man who used to work steadily,
talk easily, and return home tired but settled now moved through his days braced and estranged. That
sense of being altered fed his watchfulness. He was certain something in him had changed
permanently, and because of that he no longer trusted his surroundings, even in his own house.
Nights were the worst. Daylight offered distraction, but after dark he felt exposed, tense, and
unable to let down his guard. He checked doors, listened for sounds outside, and stayed keyed up
long after his wife had gone to bed.

His view of himself and his life also shaped the way the loss stayed in his mind. Because he saw the
event as having destroyed his life, any return of memory seemed to confirm it. When images surfaced,
they were not just recollections; they felt like proof that he was trapped in the worst moment of
his life. And when no image came, only the same crushing emotional force, that too reinforced his
sense that the loss had taken over from the inside, beyond his control. The more isolated he felt
from others, the less likely he was to talk, and the less he talked, the more the memories remained
unprocessed, returning as pictures, surges of grief, or sudden emotional collapses without warning.

A tightening pattern gradually took hold. When Kwame felt on guard at home or caught himself tensing
at night, the heightened alertness itself seemed to pull the trauma closer. In that state, his mind
became quick to latch onto ambulance sirens in the distance, conversations about bereavement, or
reminders at work that might otherwise have passed him by. Once activated, those cues brought back
the images and the unbearable grief, and the memories in turn made his body feel even more ready for
danger. After a bad night he would go to work already braced, and because he was scanning so hard
for threat, the workplace felt full of reminders. By the end of the day he would think, again, that
this was all his life was now: a man set apart, a man ruined by what happened.

Over time he withdrew socially, not because he had lost interest in people in a general way, but
because talk of illness, death, or family emergencies had become so potent that he could not stay
present for it. His wife noticed he was harder to reach and more restless after dark. He began to
fear not only the memories themselves but the places and moments that might call them up, and the
more that happened, the more convinced he became that nowhere was truly safe. He finally sought help
when he realized he was dreading both home at night and the job sites where he had spent most of his
adult life. What frightens him most is the thought that the loss has permanently destroyed who he
is. What he hopes, cautiously, is that he might learn how to be in his own home, hear an ambulance,
or stand at work without feeling pulled back into that day.
