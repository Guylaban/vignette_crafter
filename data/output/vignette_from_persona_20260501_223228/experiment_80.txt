phone call, the rush to the hospital, and then the ambulance bay where everything seemed to collapse

inward, the sense that disaster can happen in an instant. When she tries to sit down and
deliberately piece together the sequence of events, her mind seems to go blank around the worst
parts, as if the memory will not come when summoned. Yet at other times it returns all at once,
complete and uninvited, and with it she can smell again the antiseptic and gasoline-tainted air she
associates with that day.

Over the months after her husband’s death, those sensory fragments began to shape what caught her
attention in the world around her. Passing a wreck on the road or seeing footage of a traffic
collision on the local news could seize her immediately, not as a reminder in the ordinary sense but
as if the original scene had broken through into the present. Ambulance sirens became especially
potent. Even before she consciously registered what she was hearing, she would find herself bracing,
with the hospital smell already in mind and the image of a damaged car close behind it. The
anniversary of his death has become the worst point in the year; as it approaches, she feels as
though the memory is waiting just beneath the surface, and ordinary sights on the road begin to
carry a charge they do not have for other people.

These moments now set off a strong sense of immediate danger in Evelyn’s body. If an ambulance
passes her street, she becomes keyed up for hours, checking the windows and front door repeatedly
before she can sit down. If she sees cars clustered after even a minor fender-bender, she startles
at the slightest movement nearby, turning sharply as if someone were about to strike her or deliver
terrible news. Nights are hardest. On days when she has been exposed to sirens, accident scenes, or
the date on the calendar has forced the anniversary into focus, she has great difficulty falling
asleep and often gets back up to make sure the locks are secure. She knows on some level that a
locked door has nothing to do with what happened to her husband, but in the moment vigilance feels
like the only thing standing between her and another catastrophe.

control she has over it. She can spend an afternoon trying to tell her sister exactly what happened
and find she cannot reach the memory clearly, then later catch a faint medicinal odor in a clinic
waiting room and be thrown straight back into the ambulance entrance with her heart pounding. When
the recollection arrives in that abrupt, sensory way, she reacts as though the threat is current,
scanning the room, tensing at movement in her peripheral vision, and going home to check doors and
windows again. Her poor sleep has gradually deepened this sense that she is never fully off guard.

That constant alertness has narrowed her life. Since becoming unemployed after a series of absences
and sleepless nights made it hard to function reliably, she spends much of her time at home. Even
there, the threat does not settle. The more she watches for signs of danger and reacts sharply to
sounds or motion, the more quickly ambulances, crash scenes, and the anniversary date pull her
attention and feel impossible to escape. She has come to believe that the world gives no warning
before it takes away the person you love, and that if she relaxes even briefly she will be
blindsided again.

Evelyn finally sought help after this year’s anniversary, when an ambulance sped past the grocery
store parking lot and she froze beside her car, overwhelmed by the smell and image of the accident
as if she were back at the beginning of it. She went home without her groceries, checked the locks
over and over, and did not sleep that night. What she wants now is not to forget her husband. What
she is afraid of is that these sudden returns of the memory, and the constant sense of danger that
follows them, have become the shape of the rest of her life.
