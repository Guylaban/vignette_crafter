Evelyn is a 68-year-old retired American woman who came to treatment more than a year after her
husband died suddenly at home. They had been married for decades, and on the morning he collapsed
she had been in the next room, moving between ordinary chores, thinking they still had time for
breakfast and the errands they had planned. By the time she reached him, called for help, and waited
for the ambulance, she had already fixed on one thought that has never really loosened its grip: she
should have protected him and failed. She knows, in a factual sense, that she did not cause his
death, but what stays with her is the conviction that she missed something, should have noticed
sooner, should have done more, should somehow have prevented the moment that took him from her.

In the first weeks after the funeral, people came by, called, and invited her out, especially the
same neighbors and friends she and her husband had socialized with for years. Evelyn found she could
hardly bear it. Being with them sharpened her sense that she no longer belonged anywhere; their
couples’ routines, familiar gatherings, and easy references to shared history left her feeling cut
off and set apart, as if she had been pushed outside a life everyone else still knew how to inhabit.
That belief made avoiding people feel necessary, not simply preferable. She began declining
invitations, stopped answering some calls, and kept her days packed with tasks at home so she would
not have to sit with the loneliness or face people who reminded her of what she had lost. She tells
it as “keeping busy,” but the busyness has a driven quality to it, a way of filling every hour so
there is no room for stillness or conversation.

Those same beliefs have also kept her body in a state of strain. Because Evelyn has come to see
herself as someone who failed at the one moment that mattered most, she does not feel basically safe
even in her own house. The feeling is less a thought she chooses than a constant readiness in her
body: shoulders tight, chest drawn, attention keyed to any change in sound. She says she cannot
relax even when she knows she is in a safe place, and the mismatch troubles her. Part of what makes
it so hard to settle is the shame and guilt threaded through her grief; when those feelings rise,
they carry with them a sense that something terrible can happen without warning and that she cannot
trust herself to prevent it.

Over time, the physical tension has begun to feed the same harsh conclusions about herself. Evelyn
notices her body staying braced through the day and interprets that as evidence that she is no
longer like other people, that something in her has been permanently altered. When she lies awake
feeling on edge in a quiet house, she takes that persistent alertness as proof that she is alone in
a way other people cannot understand. The more frightened and keyed up she feels, the more convinced
she becomes that she belongs nowhere now—that she is separate, damaged, and living at a distance
from the world she once inhabited with her husband.

Her way of coping has tightened that pattern further. Since retiring, most of her life is organized
around home, and she has turned the house into the setting of near-constant activity: cleaning
drawers that do not need cleaning, sorting papers repeatedly, finding one more task before she
allows herself to sit down. If a former dinner companion spots her at the grocery store and starts
toward her, she will suddenly remember another aisle and leave quickly rather than risk
conversation. For brief moments, staying busy and avoiding people spare her the jolt of guilt and
the pain of feeling out of place. But the cost is that she remains tense all the time, as if she has
to keep herself moving to hold something worse at bay. When she does pause, the relief vanishes and
the bodily alarm rushes back—her muscles clenched, breathing shallow, emotions close to the surface.

Her marriage had defined much of her social world, and that world has narrowed dramatically. She
spends most days alone, not because she prefers solitude, but because being around people she once
knew with her husband intensifies the feeling that she has been left outside ordinary life.
Recently, her daughter told her gently that she seems unreachable, always occupied and never really
present. That comment stayed with Evelyn. She sought help after realizing she had begun refusing
even small family gatherings and dreading the possibility that this tense, cut-off version of her
might be permanent. What frightens her most is not only the memory of losing her husband, but the
thought that she failed him and has become someone who will never feel at home with other people
again. What she hopes, quietly, is that she might learn how to be still without feeling defenseless,
and how to be with others without the immediate certainty that she does not belong.
