of a long shift. She was working as a paramedic when her ambulance responded to a highway collision
in wet evening traffic. In the confusion of flashing lights, shouted directions, and vehicles edging
around the scene, Anna made a rapid movement with the stretcher as another responder repositioned a
patient. She has never been able to tell the sequence in a smooth line. What remains are a few fixed
images: the wheel of the stretcher jolting sideways, the patient’s body pitching, a cry, blood
suddenly where she had not expected it, and the look on the other medic’s face when everyone
understood that the patient had been seriously injured in the chaos. Even now she does not recount
the whole event so much as brace against those separate pictures. When she is asked about it, she
says the memory comes in “flashes,” and each flash hits with the same raw terror she felt on the
roadside.

In the weeks after, those fragments began arriving without warning. At work, the metallic rattle of
equipment being unfolded in the ambulance bay could bring up the snapshot of the stretcher wheel
catching. At home, a siren in the distance or rain tapping the windows could pull back the roadside
scene so abruptly that for a moment she felt she was there again, heart racing, body tensed for
catastrophe. Because remembering was not like thinking about the event but like being dropped back
into it, Anna started doing whatever she could to interrupt it. She poured herself drinks after
shift to dull the edge of the images, took medication to make herself feel less keyed up, kept the
television on or scrolled on her phone to force her attention elsewhere, and shut down any
conversation when her husband gently tried to ask how she was doing. Avoiding talk about the call
made sense to her; saying it aloud felt too close to seeing it.

Those efforts gave her brief relief, but they also narrowed her life. She became increasingly
watchful, especially after dark. Nights felt less safe than daytime, though she could not point to a
specific danger in the house. She checked windows, listened for sounds outside, and startled at
small noises. On shift she found herself scanning constantly—monitoring traffic around the rig, the
movements of coworkers, the placement of equipment—with a level of alertness that exhausted her but
never settled into confidence. The more on guard she was, the harder it became to focus. She lost
her place in routine charting, had to reread dispatch information, and found herself momentarily
blanking during ordinary tasks. In those strained, overalert moments, the memory seemed even more
likely to break through: a sudden picture of the patient falling, the same surge of terror, then
disappearance again before she could fully grasp it.

quiet herself, she slept poorly and woke in the night feeling exposed and unsafe, listening hard
into the dark. On days when she threw herself into distraction or cut off conversation, the event
stayed unprocessed and seemed to return in the same jagged form, not softened by time but preserved
as isolated, frightening moments. She had hoped that not talking and not dwelling would keep the
call contained; instead, the fragments arrived more abruptly, and each return left her more guarded.
That constant guardedness, in turn, made the world feel full of cues she had to monitor, and in that
heightened state the roadside images seemed always just beneath the surface.

changes the subject or leaves the room. At work, the ambulance bay has become a daily test of
endurance. The clang of a stretcher locking into place can make her chest tighten before she has
even understood why, and she has started lingering over minor tasks to avoid being the one to handle
equipment first on certain calls. She is ashamed of how much effort it takes to appear steady.

swallow her. What she hopes for, though she says it cautiously, is to be able to think about that
night without feeling hurled back into it, and to come home after dark without living as if another
disaster is already on its way.
