Arjun Patel, a 41-year-old park ranger, came to treatment after months of telling himself that what
happened should have stayed buried. During a backcountry patrol the previous year, he had been taken
and held captive overnight by armed men running an illegal operation on protected land. What stayed
with him were not a continuous set of memories but sharp, isolated images: a flashlight beam cutting
across his face, boots near his knees, a uniform-style jacket on one of the men, his own hands
bound, the sound of someone warning him not to look up. He remembered being watched while he was
forced to stay still, acutely aware of being seen and judged in his helplessness. When these moments
returned, he often felt pain again in his shoulders and wrists where he had been restrained. At
other times he could describe the same scenes in a flat, detached way that frightened him almost as
much, as if the ordeal had happened to somebody else.

In the weeks after he was released, the things that set him off began to organize themselves around
the captivity. Conversations about the military, strangers in uniform, and even ordinary moments of
feeling observed all seemed to pull him back toward those frozen pictures. At work, a law-
enforcement training discussion at the ranger station could suddenly bring back the image of the
jacket and the command in a man’s voice; when a visitor stared too long while asking for directions,
Arjun felt the old exposure and shame rush up before he had words for it. These same situations did
more than stir memory. They also activated what he had come to believe about himself: that he had
been degraded, that he was now set apart from other people, and that a stronger or smarter man would
never have ended up captive in the first place.

Those beliefs deepened each time the memories came back. The bodily pain and the abrupt return of
those snapshots made the event feel less like something over and more like evidence of what he was
now—humiliated, damaged, different from everyone around him. He found himself thinking that if the
memory could still seize his body so completely, then the captivity had marked him permanently. The
numb retellings led him to another conclusion he hated: that something essential in him had gone
cold. Over time he became increasingly certain that others, especially other men in uniform or
outdoorsmen he worked alongside, would see this weakness if they looked too closely. Because of
that, being seen itself started to feel dangerous, and the more isolated and ashamed he felt, the
more sharply he noticed military references, uniforms, and watchful eyes wherever he went.

Arjun tried to manage this in ways that initially felt practical. He began strictly controlling what
he ate, describing it as the one area where he could impose order and not be at someone else’s
mercy. He also became markedly compliant with supervisors, law enforcement partners, and even his
girlfriend, going along with what others wanted because yielding felt safer than risking disapproval
or confrontation. In private, he sometimes imagined revenge against the men who had held him, brief
fantasies that gave him a hard, energizing sense of power. But these strategies fed the very beliefs
he was trying to escape. Skipping meals left him feeling more separate and brittle; placating
everyone around him confirmed his sense that he was diminished; even the revenge fantasies ended
with him feeling ashamed of how consumed he still was by what had been done to him.

The strategies also kept the captivity close. Hunger made him physically tense and easier to startle
on patrol. Trying so hard to avoid conflict meant he scanned authority figures constantly, watching
for signs of danger in supervisors, police uniforms, or abrupt movements nearby. In the park, if a
coworker stepped into his peripheral vision unexpectedly, his body reacted before he could think, as
though he were back on the ground under guard. Once that jolt hit, the snapshots followed
immediately—the flashlight, the boots, the restraint—and with them the old pain. At other times the
sequence ran in the other direction: a memory would surge up first, and he would spend the rest of
the day hyperalert, unable to trust reassurance from anyone who told him he was safe.

This tightening pattern had begun to affect his work and relationship. Ranger duties that required
public contact became harder because being watched by visitors could trigger both the memory and the
conviction that people could see something broken in him. He would overaccommodate difficult park
guests, then go home angry, ashamed, and newly preoccupied with revenge. His girlfriend had started
noticing how little he ate and how quickly he flinched when she moved suddenly in the kitchen. He
finally sought help after a routine briefing involving military coordination left him briefly
feeling as if the captivity were happening again at work. What frightened Arjun most was not only
that he could no longer trust the feeling of safety, but that the ordeal had made him permanently
lesser in the eyes of others. What he hoped, cautiously, was that treatment might help him stop
living as though he were still being watched.
