Sione is a 51-year-old Pacific Islander man who has spent most of his adult life working as a
welder, someone used to heat, noise, and the disciplined control of heavy materials. He is married,
and before the accident he described himself as steady and practical, the kind of man who kept going
without much complaint. That changed after a life-threatening motor vehicle collision several months
ago. He had been driving home when another car crossed into his lane and hit him hard enough to trap
him in his seat. What stayed with him most was not only the impact itself but the sensory crush of
it: the shriek of metal, the oncoming sight of the other vehicle at the instant of collision, and
the sharp smell of alcohol when he realized the other driver had been drinking. He remembered being
pinned in an unnatural position, unable to move normally, with pain shooting through the same parts
of his body that had taken the force of the crash. In the ambulance and afterward, those details
seemed burned into him rather than filed away as something over.

Since then, when the memory comes back, it does not feel to Sione like remembering in the ordinary
sense. He says it is as if he is there again all at once. He does not just picture the crash; he
feels his body pulled back into the same twisted position he was trapped in that night, muscles
tightening as though the seatbelt is still cutting across him. At the same time, he feels pain flare
in the same areas that were injured in the collision, which leaves him bracing and wincing before he
has fully registered why. The experience is vivid across all his senses, with the sound, smell, and
visual image arriving together so completely that for a few moments he cannot hold onto the fact
that he is in the present. What frightens him most is how quickly his body seems to obey the memory,
as if the crash is still happening somewhere inside him.

Because the memory returns in that fully real way, the world around Sione has become crowded with
moments that can set it off. Seeing cars that have been in accidents on the roadside, even a
crumpled fender on his commute, can pull him straight back into the collision. The sight lands
first, and almost immediately the rest follows: his body draws up into that same trapped posture,
pain returns to the same spots, and he is gripped by the sense that impact is imminent. Car sounds
affect him in a similar way. At his welding job, vehicles move in and out of the yard all day, and
the sound of engines, brakes, or sudden metal-on-metal noise can make him freeze for a second at his
station, heart pounding, as though the crash is unfolding again rather than a truck passing outside
the shop. The smell of alcohol is especially potent because it was so bound up with the aftermath of
the collision; if a coworker comes in after lunch carrying beer on his breath, Sione feels the
memory rush in with a force that seems to take over his whole body.

Over time, these episodes have begun to shape how he lives. He still goes to work, but he has become
wary of the parking lot and distracted when traffic noise carries into the workshop. He times
errands to avoid busy roads and has his wife drive whenever she can, not because he has lost all
practical ability but because he is certain that one glimpse, one smell, or one sound could make the
crash seize him again before he can steady himself. At home, passing a wreck on the highway can
leave him quiet and physically tense for hours afterward, his wife noticing how his shoulders lock
and how he seems to disappear into something she cannot see. Sione came for help when he realized
these reactions were not easing with time and were beginning to narrow his life. He is afraid that
the memories have too much control over his body for him ever to feel safe in ordinary places again,
but he is also hoping that if he can understand why the crash still feels so present, he might be
able to return to work and to the road without feeling as though impact is always one second away.
