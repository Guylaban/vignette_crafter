For most of her life, Lien had taken pride in being steady with her hands and unflappable under
pressure. At seventy-six, she still worked as a mechanic in the family’s small auto shop, spending
long days around grease, concrete dust, and the damp outdoor smell that drifted in whenever the bay
doors were open. The stalking began last year after a man she had rebuffed at a local market started
appearing where she did: first in the parking lot, then across from the shop, then idling his car
near her street after dark. What has stayed with her most are not long, coherent memories but sharp
visual slices: his face half-hidden beyond the windshield, his shoes on the wet ground outside the
garage, the outline of him standing near her fence at dusk. When she tries to tell the story in
order, she loses the thread, falters, and abruptly says she would rather not continue. She can feel
herself shutting it down before she reaches the end, as if letting the whole memory unfold would be
too dangerous.

Over the months that followed, ordinary sensory moments began to pull those images forward without
warning. The smell of soil after rain, potted plants outside the shop, or the earthy odor that clung
to tires from muddy driveways could suddenly bring back one of those frozen pictures so vividly that
she felt she was looking at a photograph rather than remembering. Even stress at work could do it:
if a repair ran behind or a customer was impatient, her body tightened and the images came closer.
Strangely, feeling relaxed could also set it off. If she caught herself enjoying a quiet evening
with her husband or laughing over coffee before opening the shop, there was a jolt of alarm,
followed by a flash of him near the fence or outside the garage, as if her mind would not allow her
to let down her guard. Because the memories arrived in these fragments and she pushed them away
before they could fully connect, the stalking has remained for her a set of vivid scenes rather than
a finished event, and that unfinished quality seems to make the earthy outdoor smells and moments of
lowered tension feel especially dangerous.

Those intrusive fragments have left Lien feeling that danger is still nearby. She now scans
constantly, lifting her head at passing engines, checking mirrors, and turning to look behind her in
parking lots and while locking up the shop. At home she is no easier; she has come to feel on guard
even inside her own house, peering through the curtains and listening for movement in the yard. The
more those photographic images intrude, the more certain she becomes that she must stay watchful,
because to her they do not feel like old memories but like evidence that the threat has never really
passed. Yet that constant alertness also keeps the memory in circulation. When she is repeatedly
checking behind herself in the parts store or monitoring the driveway from her kitchen window, her
attention catches on anything that resembles the original scenes, and another visual fragment breaks
through, only to be cut off again when she tries to hold it long enough to describe it.

This pattern has narrowed her life. At the shop, where she once moved easily between the indoor bays
and the gravel outside, she now hesitates near the open doors if the air smells like wet earth. She
keeps an eye on the lot instead of focusing fully on repairs, and she startles herself by looking
over her shoulder when a customer approaches from behind. Her husband has noticed that even on good
days she rarely settles. If an afternoon feels calm, Lien starts monitoring herself for that calm,
almost waiting for the next sign that she has missed something. Being on guard makes the outdoor
smells, stress, and even brief moments of happiness feel loaded, and when she senses one of those
triggers she braces immediately, which seems to prime the next flash of memory before it fully
forms.

By the time she came for help, she was exhausted by the effort of staying prepared. She said the
worst part was not only feeling afraid in public but realizing she no longer felt safe in her own

her mind going blank or cutting away, and she wants one quiet day with her husband that does not end
with her checking the yard, the street, and the space behind her, certain that if she stops watching
for even a moment, he will be there.
