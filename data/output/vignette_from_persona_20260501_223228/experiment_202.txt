his way home from an evening shift. He had parked a few blocks from his apartment, still in partial
uniform, and was cutting through a dim stretch of sidewalk with his duffel over one shoulder when
two men approached him. What he remembered most clearly was not a full sequence but sharp,
disjointed fragments: the scrape of a shoe on concrete, one man stepping too close, the sudden
movement of a hand toward his chest, the metallic flash of something he still could not confidently
identify, his bag being yanked hard enough to twist him sideways. When he tried to describe the
assault in session, he kept stopping and restarting. He would get as far as “they came up on my
left” and then lose the thread, frowning as if the rest were sealed off behind something he could
sense but not reach. He said more than once that he “just can’t put it into words right,” and the
effort of trying seemed to leave him frustrated and vaguely ashamed.

In the days after the robbery, Liam gave statements to police and to his supervisor because his
Coast Guard ID had been in the stolen bag. Each time he was asked to recount what happened, he was
struck by how little of it felt like a normal memory. It did not come back as a coherent story with
a beginning, middle, and end. Instead there were isolated bits that arrived without order: the
pressure in his throat when he realized he could not immediately call for help, the smell of wet
pavement, his own hand on the strap of the bag, the image of one attacker’s jawline under a hood.
Other stretches were simply absent. He could tell there had been shouting, but could not reliably
say who said what. He knew he had ended up on the ground, but not exactly how. This patchwork
quality unnerved him, because he was used to being observant and precise in his work, and he could
feel himself searching for connections that never settled into place.

What was equally strange to him was that sometimes he could talk about the mugging with no feeling
at all. Sitting with his girlfriend in their kitchen or answering a routine question from a
superior, he would hear himself describing being robbed in a flat, distant voice, as if he were
reciting something that had happened to another man. He noticed the mismatch and found it
unsettling. He knew the event should feel frightening or enraging, yet at moments it seemed remote,
drained of emotion, and this emotional deadness made the whole thing harder for him to understand.
At other times a single fragment would surface—a hand grabbing his uniform, the instant of realizing
he was outnumbered—without any fuller context around it.

His work in the Coast Guard gave these difficulties a particular edge. He was accustomed to clear
reporting, chain of events, practical detail. On base, when colleagues spoke casually about theft in
the city or asked whether the police had found anything, he felt exposed by how incomplete his own
account remained. He could say the facts in outline, but the actual memory would not organize
itself. Driving in for duty before dawn, he sometimes found himself replaying disconnected images
rather than a narrative, almost as if his mind had stored snapshots instead of an experience. The
result was less a story he could tell than a set of sensory shards he carried around with him.

By the time he sought help, Liam was exhausted by this ongoing sense that the worst night of his
adult life existed in him only as broken pieces and blank spaces. His girlfriend had encouraged him

most was not only the robbery itself, but the persistence of this altered remembering—how often the
event felt both inaccessible and strangely impersonal. He wanted help making sense of what had
happened, and he hoped that if the memory could finally become something whole and sayable, it would
stop feeling like a series of disconnected scenes belonging to someone else.
