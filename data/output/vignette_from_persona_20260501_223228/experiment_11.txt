Claire Martin was 43 when a sudden life-threatening illness overturned the ordinary structure of her
life. Until then she had been working long shifts as a chef, moving quickly through heat, steam,
sharp timing, and the constant demands of a restaurant kitchen. She described the illness as
arriving with terrifying speed. What she remembered most vividly was not a single diagnosis
conversation but the bodily crisis itself: the sense that her body was collapsing from the inside
while people rushed around her, bright hospital lights glaring, chemical smells in the air, monitors
sounding, and staff speaking in urgent tones she could not fully follow. She had moments of
believing she might die there, unable to control what was happening to her body. Even now, when she
tries to tell it as a story, it can sound unreal to her, as if something that extreme could not
quite have happened to her, and yet when it returns it does not feel distant at all. It comes back
across all her senses at once, with an immediate grief that catches her off guard.

In the months after she survived, Claire expected relief, but instead she became preoccupied with
what the illness meant about her body and her future. She had come to believe that her body had been
permanently damaged and that it could no longer be trusted. More than the memory of being ill, what
frightened her was the sense that her own body had become an enemy, still reacting as though danger
were present even in safe places. She was certain she would never feel normal emotions again; what
had once been pleasure, affection, or anticipation now seemed flattened or unreachable. These
beliefs shaped how she lived. She began to numb herself deliberately, keeping her reactions blunted
and avoiding situations that might make her feel happy or hopeful because any lift in mood felt
fragile, almost foolish, in the face of what she thought had been irreversibly taken from her.

Those same beliefs also kept her on alert. A flutter in her chest, a wave of heat, fatigue after a
shift, even the press of her chef’s jacket against her skin could register as signs that something
catastrophic was happening again. Her body reacted before she had time to think; she would tense,
her breathing would change, and she would feel a surge of alarm before she could remind herself
where she was. Standing at the stove during dinner service, surrounded by steam and the metallic
smell of the kitchen, she could suddenly feel transported back into the sensory chaos of the
hospital. The more this happened, the more convinced she became that her body really was still in
danger and still damaged, which only deepened her sense that she could not trust herself to relax.

When these episodes came, the memory did not arrive as something finished and past. It overtook the
present. She would have flashes in which the hospital felt more real than the kitchen in front of
her, and afterward she would be shaken by overwhelming grief for the life she felt she had lost.
Because the memory was so intense and so hard to reconcile with ordinary reality, she found herself
thinking again that no one could go through that and come back intact. In that way the reliving
seemed to confirm her bleakest conclusions about herself: that normal feeling was gone for good,
that her body had not recovered, that safety was never more than temporary.

Claire responded by shrinking her life further. After work she stayed up very late, delaying sleep
because the quiet of night made the sensory reliving more likely and because going to bed meant
losing the distractions that helped her hold herself together. She stopped agreeing to small
positive plans with her partner—dinners out, a weekend away, even a movie she might once have
enjoyed—because hope felt dangerous and emotionally deadened distance felt safer. She told herself
she was sparing both of them disappointment, but the cost was a life increasingly organized around
getting through rather than living. The numbing brought a kind of temporary control, yet it also
left her more cut off from any evidence that she could still feel warmth or safety, which fed her
conviction that something essential had been permanently broken.

Over time this way of coping made her more brittle, not less. The later she stayed up, the harder it
became to settle her body, and the more raw and reactive she felt the next day in the restaurant.
The more she avoided anything hopeful or emotionally alive, the more unfamiliar normal feeling
became, until its absence itself seemed like proof she would never get it back. And because she
spent so much energy bracing and shutting down, even ordinary bodily sensations seemed sharper and
more threatening, making it easier for flashbacks to break through and harder for her to believe the
danger was over.

By the time Claire sought help, her world had narrowed to work, endurance, and vigilance. She was
still showing up in the kitchen, but she did so in a state of constant tension, unable to relax even
when she knew, intellectually, that she was safe. What finally brought her in was the fear that this
was now permanent—that she would go on living inside a body that kept sounding false alarms, unable
to feel close to her partner or present in her own life. What she most hoped for was modest but
urgent: not to erase what happened, but to stop feeling as if it were happening all over again.
