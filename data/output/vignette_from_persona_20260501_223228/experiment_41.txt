other people name and survive what frightened them. He worked as a psychologist in a community
mental health clinic, and part of what made his own assault so disorienting was that it happened in

What remained with him most sharply was not only the assault itself but the trapped feeling of
hearing the lock click, realizing the door would not open, and being forced to endure the presence

even passing remarks about sexual violence in trainings or case discussions could throw him back
into that room, because the language landed with the same invasive force as the experience it named.

In the weeks after, Kwame did not talk about what had happened. He had come to believe that
something fundamental had changed in how he appeared to other people, as if the assault had marked
him in a visible way. Sitting with colleagues, he found himself certain they could see it just by
looking at him. He also felt newly convinced that bad things could happen without warning and that
he would be powerless to stop them. Those beliefs did not stay abstract. Because he felt exposed and
helpless, being alone with men became charged almost immediately; an ordinary moment with a male
coworker in a consultation room no longer felt neutral. The same was true of confined spaces. A
closed office door, even in his own workplace, seemed to carry the meaning that he could be trapped
again.

That sense of permanent vulnerability began to shape his body and his attention. In sessions he
started choosing the chair with his back to the wall, scanning the room so he could see the door and
whoever entered. If he had to meet with a male patient or a male colleague, he positioned himself
carefully and stayed braced through the entire interaction. At night he struggled to fall asleep
because lying down and letting go felt dangerous; he told himself he needed to stay alert, even in
his apartment with the door locked. He could not relax in places he knew were objectively safe. What
frightened him most was the feeling that safety no longer counted for much, because he was sure he
could not stop another violation if it came. The more he lived in that state of readiness, the more
hopeless he became about the future, as if this watchfulness was now the only way he could exist.

His work environment kept pressing on the same injuries. In case conference, if a trainee mentioned
sexual assault, Kwame felt a jolt of dread before he had fully registered the words. He became
acutely aware of the men in the room and of whether the door was shut. When an office door clicked
closed during supervision, he would feel his body tense and his mind race ahead to danger. His
constant vigilance made these moments harder, not easier; because he was already scanning for
threat, comments about sexual violence, locked rooms, and being alone with men stood out immediately
and hit with full force. By then he was no longer reacting only to reminders of the assault itself
but to the whole network of conditions his mind had learned to treat as warnings.

Over time the vigilance and the bleakness fed each other. Each night spent awake, listening for
sounds in the hallway, left him more certain that his life had narrowed into mere survival. Each day
spent arranging chairs, monitoring exits, and enduring the strain of one-on-one meetings with men
deepened his conviction that he was helpless and visibly damaged. He began to think, with increasing
certainty, that he had no future beyond managing the next threat. Even when nothing happened, the
fact that he could not stand down seemed like proof that the world was unsafe and that he had been
altered beyond repair.

He came to treatment after nearly walking out of a multidisciplinary meeting when a male physician
casually referenced a case of sexual violence and another staff member shut the conference room
door. Kwame felt trapped, exposed, and suddenly sure everyone could see what had been done to him.
He was shaken by how quickly he lost his sense of the present and by the realization that his
professional world had become organized around avoiding the next moment of danger. What he fears

be alone with another man without feeling at risk. What he hopes, though he says it cautiously, is
that he might someday stop living as if the lock is always about to click.
