Markus Weber is a 56-year-old combat pilot who came to treatment after months of telling himself he
should be able to handle what happened. During a training flight the previous year, his aircraft
suffered a catastrophic mechanical failure on descent. He remembered the first loud bang inside the
cockpit, sharp and unnatural, followed by warning alarms and a violent shudder that threw his body
against the harness. In the seconds before impact, he became acutely aware of his own physical
sensations—lightheadedness, a sick rolling feeling in his stomach, the sense that his body was no
longer reliable. He survived the crash, but the experience seemed to split his life into before and
after. What stayed with him was not only the memory of the explosion-like sound and the
disorientation in his body, but the conviction that the world could become lethal without warning.

In the weeks afterward, those same sensations began to feel less like ordinary bodily states and
more like signals that danger was already underway. If he grew dizzy standing up too quickly, or
felt vaguely unwell after a poor night’s sleep, his whole system seemed to snap tight. A slammed
metal door on base or any bang resembling an explosion made him jolt before he could think. Around
other people, even small unexpected movements in the room pulled a reflexive reaction out of him, as
though he had to defend himself before he knew what he was seeing. He described living with his body
“revved up,” constantly tense, scanning, watchful, unable to relax even at home with his wife. The
dizziness and sick feeling frightened him not only because they resembled moments from the crash,
but because they seemed to confirm what he had come to believe: that the world is dangerous, that
the rules he trusted about skill and preparation no longer hold, and that he himself had become
somehow hollowed out by the experience.

Those beliefs hardened over time. Markus had come to feel that what was destroyed in the crash was
not just the aircraft, but his confidence in how life worked. He said there had always been risk in
aviation, but now risk felt omnipresent, stripped of meaning or fairness. He moved through his days
with a deadened quality that troubled him almost as much as the fear. He noticed that when dizziness
or a sudden bang set him off, what followed was not only alarm but a bleak, immediate thought—of
course this is what the world is, unsafe and ready to turn. In that state, even ordinary physical
discomfort took on the force of evidence.

He began organizing his life around not setting off that chain. He avoided hospitals, ambulances,
and physical examinations with unusual rigidity, even when he knew routine medical care would have
been sensible. He could not bear waiting rooms, the smell of antiseptic, or the suggestion that
someone might check his body closely; those situations made him too aware of feeling lightheaded or
ill. At work he stayed functional, but he narrowed his world outside the cockpit. He gave up dinners
out, stopped meeting friends for activities he had once enjoyed, and became strict and controlling
about what he ate, telling himself that if he kept his body tightly managed he might prevent the
sensations that sent him spiraling. The more on guard he felt, the more he relied on these routines.
If his body was going to stay primed for danger, he believed he had to compensate by controlling
every variable he could.

But these efforts tightened rather than relieved things. Restricting his eating left him physically
depleted at times, and the resulting lightheadedness only made the old fear more immediate. Avoiding
medical settings meant he never had the chance to learn that bodily sensations could be tolerated
without catastrophe, so every spell of dizziness remained potent. Pulling out of enjoyable parts of
life left him more emotionally flat, and that numbness fed his sense that something vital in him had
died in the crash. His wife had started commenting that he was present in the house but unreachable;
Markus heard the truth in that and felt ashamed, then withdrew further. He noticed, too, that the
smaller his world became, the more sharply he reacted to sounds and movement within it. A dropped
tool in the hangar, a cart clattering across concrete, someone stepping unexpectedly into his
peripheral vision—each seemed to prove again that he could not let his guard down.

By the time he sought help, Markus was exhausted by the discipline it took to manage each day and
frightened by how little of himself he could still feel. What finally brought him in was a recent
physical exam he postponed twice, then abandoned after seeing an ambulance pull up outside the
clinic and feeling instantly sick and lightheaded in the parking lot. He sat in his truck shaking,
unable to go inside, and realized his life was being run by efforts to avoid that moment. He says he
wants his marriage and ordinary life back, but what he fears most is that the crash revealed a
permanent truth—that the world is fundamentally dangerous, his body cannot be trusted, and the part
of him that once felt alive may not return.
