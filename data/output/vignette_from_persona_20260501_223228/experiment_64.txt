Samira Haddad, a 71-year-old Lebanese American woman, came to treatment months after witnessing a
violent assault a few blocks from the apartment where she worked as a nanny. She had been walking
one of the children home from a neighborhood library program when a man rushed at a young woman near
the corner store, shoved her to the pavement, and struck her while grabbing her bag. Samira
remembered freezing for a split second with the stroller in her hands, feeling the child begin to
cry while the woman screamed for help. Other people turned to look, but the man ran before anyone
stopped him. What had stayed with Samira was not only the woman on the ground but her own felt
hesitation: she had not moved toward the victim, had not shouted loudly enough, had only hurried the
child away and called for help once she was half a block down. In the weeks that followed, she
became fixed on the thought that she should have protected someone and failed. She had come to
believe that this moment revealed something shameful and permanent about her, that she was
inadequate when it mattered most, while the person responsible had simply disappeared and faced no
consequences.

Since then, Samira moved through the city as if danger were always a few feet behind her. On the
walk to the children’s school, she repeatedly turned her head to check the sidewalk behind her,
studied men approaching from the opposite direction, and scanned faces for any flicker of
aggression. At the playground she kept one eye on the children and the other on adults nearby,
watching closely for signs that someone might turn threatening without warning. This constant state
of alarm fed the meaning she made of the assault. Because she remained so certain that harm could
break out at any moment, she took her own freezing that day as proof that she could not cope,
telling herself that a more capable woman would have acted differently and that the world was one in
which innocent people were hurt while no one was held to account.

Her efforts to manage these feelings gradually narrowed her life. Samira stopped taking the route
past the corner store altogether, even when it was the most direct way to get the children to school
or home. She asked the parents she worked for if she could use different streets, and when they
suggested errands near that block she smiled and agreed to other tasks instead, too anxious to say
no directly. At home and at work she tried hard to push the images away whenever they surfaced,
interrupting herself with chores, prayers, or whatever someone else needed from her. The more she
felt she had failed that woman, the more she acted as though safety depended on never upsetting
anyone and never going near the place where her failure had been exposed.

Her vigilance also drove the same pattern. If she noticed footsteps behind her, she would cross the
street or detour an extra block with the stroller rather than pass the scene of the attack. If a
parent asked her to stay late or accompany the children somewhere unfamiliar, she found herself
agreeing automatically, believing it was safer to go along than risk conflict or disapproval. She
told herself she was being careful, but the suppression and avoidance never settled her for long.
Instead, each maneuver seemed to confirm that the danger was real and that she was the kind of
person who could not handle it directly.

Over time these habits deepened her self-judgment. Every street she avoided became, in her mind,
another sign of inadequacy. Each time she swallowed her preferences and went along with what others
wanted, she privately took it as evidence that she was weak. Trying not to think about the assault
did not erase it; it left the accusation untouched. She would lie awake replaying the woman’s cry
for help and thinking that the attacker was still somewhere living freely while she, who had done
nothing wrong, was the one arranging her whole life around that corner.

By the time she sought help, Samira’s world had become organized around watchfulness. During
sessions she sat angled toward the door and glanced over her shoulder in the waiting room. She was
still working as a nanny, but the job had become exhausting because every school pickup and park
visit felt like a security exercise. What finally brought her in was her fear that the children were
beginning to notice how often she checked behind them and steered them away from certain streets.
More than anything, she wanted relief from the conviction that she had failed another person in
need, and she was frightened that if anything dangerous ever happened again, she would freeze in
exactly the same way.
