When Kofi was a boy, he learned to read danger from ordinary sounds and smells. He grew up in a home
where his father drank heavily, and some of his clearest fragments are not full scenes so much as
jagged pieces: the sharp odor of alcohol on his father’s clothes, a door thrown open hard enough to
strike the wall, his mother trying to move them outside toward the edge of the yard where there were
trees and tall grass, as if getting into the open might calm things down. He cannot tell the story
in a smooth sequence. When he tries, the details slip away from him, and what comes instead is the
raw force of it in his body—sudden dread, the feeling that something terrible is about to
happen—without any complete picture to match it. What remains are disconnected scraps: the slam of a
door, the smell of liquor, a patch of brush and evening air. Those bits seem to have given the
trauma its pathways into the present, so that even now natural spaces, the sound of doors opening or
banging, and the smell of alcohol can strike him with an alarm he does not fully understand in the
moment.

For most of his life, Kofi managed by staying in motion. Now 79 and still working in a warehouse,
divorced and living alone, he has built his days around not being left with too much quiet. At work
he lingers near the loading crew after his shift should have ended, joining in break-room talk or
drifting toward any pocket of conversation rather than going straight home. He says being around
people keeps his mind from “going backwards.” When a bay door crashes shut in the warehouse, or when
he catches the sour smell of beer on a coworker’s breath, or when he has to walk the outside
perimeter of the building past a strip of weeds and trees, the reaction in him is immediate and old.
On those days he drinks more in the evening, finds reasons to call people or sit where there is
noise and company, and at times picks needless arguments, especially if he feels that familiar
pressure rising in his chest. Starting a quarrel gives him something concrete to focus on. It is
easier, in his mind, to be angry at someone in front of him than to sit with a fear that arrives
without a full memory attached.

Over the years those habits have not quieted the past so much as thickened it. Kofi describes that
the more he drinks and the more he keeps himself surrounded by people or stirred up in conflict, the
less able he is to deliberately remember what happened as a child. He has the sense that the memory
breaks apart further when he pushes it away in these ways. He can still be flooded by the emotional
force of it, but the event itself remains inaccessible, in pieces that do not join. That leaves him
vulnerable to being caught off guard by triggers because they seem to come from nowhere; a slammed
warehouse door or the smell of alcohol can hit him before he has words for why. In this way, his
attempts to manage the distress have also helped preserve the strange, ungraspable quality of the
memory.

The pattern has narrowed his life. Kofi avoids being by himself for long stretches, not out of
sociability exactly, but because solitude makes room for the sensations he cannot place. He stays
late around other workers, calls acquaintances just to hear another voice, and if an evening begins
to feel too still, he may drink or stir tension with someone close by. Yet those same choices leave
him more on edge. After nights of drinking or days marked by arguments, he notices he is jumpier the
next morning at work, more likely to flinch when the heavy doors roll open or slam shut, more
reactive to the smell of alcohol in the locker area, more uneasy when he passes the brush at the
edge of the lot. The strategies that promise relief seem to sensitize him further to the very cues
that set him off.

He finally sought help after a recent incident at the warehouse, when a loading door banged
unexpectedly and he found himself overcome by terror so intense that he had to sit down, though he
could not have said exactly what he was remembering. Later that week he started an argument with a
coworker who had been drinking after shift, then went home and drank alone until he fell asleep.
That frightened him more than he likes to admit. At his age, he says, he had believed he should have
outrun this long ago. Instead he has come to fear that the feeling will keep finding him in ordinary
places, and that if he ever slows down enough to face what happened, there may be more there than he
can bear. What he hopes for, cautiously, is to be able to hear a door slam, smell liquor, or walk
past a stand of trees without his body being pulled back into something he still cannot fully
remember.
