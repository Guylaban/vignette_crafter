Mele had been widowed for several years when she began a relationship she had hoped would bring
companionship into the later part of her life. Instead, the relationship became physically abusive.
She describes one night in particular as the point after which she no longer felt like herself:
during an argument in her apartment, her partner moved toward her abruptly, grabbed at her, and
struck her when she tried to pull away. What stayed with her was not only the pain but the
unbearable closeness of it—his face directly in hers, his hands on her body, the sense that she
could not read what would happen next until it was already happening. In the weeks after leaving
him, she found she could not shake the feeling that something fundamental in her had been altered.

By the time she came for treatment, Mele, a 67-year-old database administrator, spoke about herself
with quiet contempt. She had come to believe that her reactions since the abuse proved she was “a
lousy coper.” She compared herself unfavorably to the competent, methodical woman she had once known
herself to be at work and at home, and said she now felt defective, as if there were something wrong
with her as a person that had allowed this to happen and had left her unable to recover cleanly from
it. More than fear, what distressed her was the conviction that she no longer recognized herself.
She said she used to be direct, warm, and steady; now she felt estranged from her own personality,
as though the abuse had replaced it with someone smaller and more brittle.

Those beliefs began to shape how she moved through her days. Because she saw her ongoing reactions
as evidence of weakness and damage, she started trying to manage herself by pulling back from
situations that made her feel exposed. She avoided eye contact with other people, especially in the
office, keeping her attention fixed on her monitors or on a printed sheet of queries when colleagues
stopped by her desk. Looking directly at someone felt too revealing, as if they might see how shaken
and altered she was. She also became tense about physical contact of any kind. If a coworker brushed
past her in a narrow corridor or a church friend reached for a hug, she stiffened and stepped away
quickly, telling herself it was easier not to invite touch at all. Nights grew longer as well. Mele
delayed going to sleep, often staying up very late with the television on and her laptop open beside
her, because settling into bed meant being alone with herself, and she dreaded the recognition of
the person she had become.

Over months, these habits did not bring the relief she wanted. At work, her avoidance made her seem
distant in a department where she had once been known as reliable and approachable. She kept
conversations brief, rarely looked up in meetings, and arranged her day to minimize casual contact.
The more she did this, the more she noticed herself as awkward, changed, and unlike the woman she
believed she should be. Each small retreat seemed to confirm her worst view of herself: that she was
not coping, that there was something wrong with her, that the old Mele was disappearing. Staying up
late left her exhausted the next day, and in that exhaustion she judged herself even more harshly
for not being able simply to “get over it.” Avoiding touch and eye contact gave her temporary
control, but afterward she experienced that control as further proof that she was now the sort of
person who had to live defensively.

This reciprocal pattern had become especially visible in her daily environment. As a database
administrator, she spent much of her day solving problems in systems that once gave her a sense of
mastery. Now even routine interactions around those tasks carried strain. If a younger colleague
leaned in to look at her screen, she angled her chair away. If someone greeted her directly and
waited for her gaze, she dropped her eyes and answered without looking up. Later she would replay
these moments as evidence of personal failure. Rather than thinking, “I am protecting myself,” she
concluded, “This is who I am now,” and that conclusion made her more determined to keep her distance
the next time.

What finally brought Mele to seek help was the dawning fear that this withdrawn, ashamed version of
herself was becoming permanent. She was less frightened by any single reminder than by the
possibility that she had been permanently damaged by the abuse and would remain cut off from other
people for the rest of her life. At the same time, she still held a quiet hope that if she could
understand why she had come to live this way—avoiding others’ eyes, shrinking from touch, staying
awake to postpone the night—she might find some path back to a self she could recognize again.
