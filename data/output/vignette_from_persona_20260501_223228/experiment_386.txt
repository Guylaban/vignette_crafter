treatment months after the sudden death of his younger sister, Ama, with whom he had spoken almost
every day since his divorce. She died unexpectedly at home after collapsing while they were on the
phone together. He still remembered the abrupt change in her breathing, the thud he thought he
heard, and then the silence while he shouted her name from hundreds of miles away, helpless and
disbelieving. What made the loss so potent in his daily life was how ordinary details from that
afternoon had become charged: a ringing phone on the trading floor, a dropped line, a pause in
someone’s voice. At work, when several desk phones began sounding at once and market alarms cut
through the room, he could suddenly feel as if he were back in that call, bracing for the moment her
voice disappeared.

In the weeks after her death, Kwame found that the memory did not come to him as a coherent account.
Instead, fragments arrived without warning—the sound of breath catching, the imagined noise of her
falling, the dead air on the line—and then vanished before he could make sense of them. When he
tried on purpose to remember exactly what had happened, he would hit a blank stretch and become
frustrated that so much felt missing. Because the picture was incomplete and inaccessible when he
wanted it, he spent long stretches turning over why this had happened to him, replaying the call and
searching for some explanation he might have missed. Just as often, he fought to push the whole
thing away, telling himself not to think about it, and he began steering clear of anything that
reminded him of Ama, including her favorite music and family conversations about her.

Those same broken pieces of memory also carried a sense of immediate danger. A phone ringing sharply
during the trading day, or a client suddenly going silent on a call, could bring on a flashback-like
moment in which he felt he was reliving the call and not standing at his desk. He would be flooded
with fear, anger, and guilt in an instant, as though the loss were happening again and he had once
more failed to do anything useful. The more these fragments erupted, the more he felt unsafe inside
his own mind.

That sense of threat began shaping how he lived. Kwame had always worked in a fast, high-stakes
environment, but since Ama’s death he noticed himself leaning harder into reckless trades and
impulsive decisions that felt uncharacteristically dangerous, as if the jolt of risk could drown out
the internal pressure. When the fear and guilt rose, he tried even more forcefully not to think
about the call, and he avoided grief reminders with increasing rigidity because he believed any
opening would unleash the same unbearable surge. He told himself it was better to stay moving, stay
sharp, stay away from anything that could pull him under.

Over time, those strategies tightened the problem. The harder he tried to suppress thoughts of the
call and avoid reminders of his sister, the less able he became to hold the memory together
deliberately. What remained were more disconnected flashes, arriving out of nowhere and leaving him
with the same missing middle he could never fill in. He would then return to brooding over why it
happened and why he had been the one on the phone, only to clamp down again when the feelings became
too much. In this way, his efforts to manage the loss seemed to leave the memory even more
fragmented and elusive.

The threat he felt also seemed to scramble memory further. In moments of intense fear or guilt, he
could no longer retrieve a steady narrative at all; he was left with isolated sensory pieces and a
conviction that something terrible was about to happen again. Then, because the memory was still
incomplete, he redoubled his rumination and avoidance, which only made the next eruption feel more
sudden and overpowering. The cycle became most visible at work: a burst of ringing phones on the
trading floor could trigger a flash of Ama’s last moments, followed by a reckless trade, then hours
of trying not to think, and later that night another futile attempt to piece together what exactly
had happened.

By the time he sought help, Kwame’s world had narrowed. He was avoiding family contact because any
mention of Ama could break through his control, and colleagues had begun to comment on his erratic
appetite for risk. What finally brought him in was not only the intensity of the flashbacks but the
fear that he was becoming dangerous to himself in quieter ways—through the trades he was making, the
strain he was putting on his work, and the unrelenting belief that if he stopped suppressing the
memory, it would swallow him completely. What he wanted, cautiously, was to be able to remember his
sister without feeling as if he were back on that phone call, trapped in the moment he lost her.
