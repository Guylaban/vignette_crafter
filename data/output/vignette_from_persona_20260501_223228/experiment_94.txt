Nabil is a 55-year-old farmer who came to treatment after months of telling his wife that he was
“fine” and then finding, more and more often, that he was not. Last summer, near dusk, he was
loading tools into his truck at the edge of his property when a man he did not know approached him,
asking a question in a way that at first seemed ordinary. Nabil remembers the man standing too
close, the sharp smell of sweat in the heat, and the instant his body registered danger before his
mind caught up. The assault itself does not come back to him as a clear sequence. What he can
describe are disconnected pieces: the man’s shoulder turning, a burst of pounding in his own chest,
dirt under one knee, breath and body odor at close range, the sense of being overpowered and then
suddenly alone. When he tries to tell it in order, he loses the thread and stops, frustrated, saying
it sounds like something that happened to someone else.

In the weeks afterward, those fragments did not settle into a coherent memory. Instead they stayed
jagged and estranged from him, as if he were trying to account for an incident he had only half
witnessed. He would start to explain to his brother what happened and then drift into silence,
unable to connect one moment to the next. Because the memory felt alien and unfinished, he kept
returning to it privately, circling around the same questions and imagining what he should have done
differently. Those hours often turned into fantasies of getting even with the man who attacked him.
He knew he did not even have a clear plan or, really, a clear picture of the assailant, but the urge
to think about revenge gave shape to an experience that otherwise felt broken and beyond his grasp.

Nabil’s days on the farm became the place where the trauma kept pressing itself into the present.
Physical labor would make his heart pound, and that sensation alone could jolt him into the same
alarm he had felt during the assault. On hot afternoons, the smell of sweat—his own, a hired hand’s,
a customer’s—could stop him short. If someone came onto the property and seemed evasive, watched him
too closely, or moved in a way he read as suspicious, his attention narrowed immediately. In those
moments he did not experience himself as a man having a memory; he felt as though danger were
happening again. To manage that state, he would throw himself harder into work, staying out in the
fields longer than necessary, fixing equipment that could have waited, keeping his hands busy so he
would not have to sit with the pounding in his chest or the images that would not line up. At other
times the same triggers pushed him into long, bitter mental rehearsals about tracking down the
assailant and making things right himself. He also became careful around the date of the attack,
finding reasons to schedule deliveries or extra chores so that he would not have to notice the
anniversary approaching.

Over months, these strategies hardened into routine. Nabil told himself that overworking was
practical, the kind of response a man with land and responsibilities should have, but his wife
noticed that he was using the farm to outrun his own mind. He stayed outside until after dark, came
in exhausted, and was less available to her than he had been before. When conversation turned toward
the assault, he either stalled out because he could not explain it clearly or veered into angry
speculation about revenge. The more suspicious he became of unfamiliar people on the property, the
more he kept himself occupied, and the less space there was for anything except work and the private
replaying of what had happened.

By the time he sought help, the anniversary was nearing again, and he had started dreading ordinary
parts of his day. A laborer wiping sweat from his neck, the thud of his own heartbeat after lifting
feed sacks, a stranger pausing too long at the gate could shift him instantly into that same
internal state. What frightened Nabil most was not only the assault itself, but the sense that he no
longer owned the memory of it or knew what he might do with the anger it stirred. He came in hoping,
cautiously, that he might be able to tell the story without losing it halfway through, and afraid
that if he could not, he would spend the rest of his life working and thinking in circles around one
unfinished moment.
