When Minh Tran was 34, what began as several weeks of unexplained chest tightness and exhaustion
turned into an emergency hospitalization for a severe cardiac infection that doctors told him could
have killed him if he had come in later. He remembers the fluorescent brightness of the ICU, the
rhythm of alarms that seemed to pause too long before someone appeared, and the helpless, watchful
stretch of time while staff moved quickly around him without explaining much. What stayed with him
most was the feeling that his body had become unpredictable and dangerous, as if something
catastrophic had been developing inside him while he was still trying to work and act normal. Before
that illness, Minh had thought of himself as competent, contained, the person who solved problems
for clients and teammates as a UX designer. During the admission, dependent on machines and other
people’s timing, he felt stripped of that identity.

In the months after discharge, he found that what had happened settled into a harsh judgment about
himself. Minh had come to believe there was something fundamentally wrong with him, not just
medically but as a person. He spoke about “failing” at something as basic as staying healthy, and he
was embarrassed by how frightened he had been in the hospital. The fear was quickly folded into a
conviction that he was weak. When his wife tried to remind him that anyone would have been terrified
after nearly dying, he dismissed it; to him, the fact that he still thought about the monitors and
waiting meant he lacked some basic sturdiness other people had. Alongside this was a deadened
quality he found hard to explain. He told the therapist he often felt “flat” or “not really there,”
as though the illness had hollowed him out from the inside.

Those beliefs colored the way he now read danger. Because he felt his body had already betrayed him
once and because he saw himself as weak and defective, small disruptions easily took on catastrophic
meaning. If his physician’s office put him on hold too long, or if test results were not posted when
expected, Minh was certain something bad was being withheld. A late email from a clinic could leave
him pacing the apartment, muscles rigid, unable to settle. He no longer trusted reassurance. Even
when his wife sat with him and pointed out that the cardiologist had said he was recovering well, he
could not take in the idea of safety; it felt naïve, even reckless. His body stayed braced as if for
another emergency, shoulders tight, jaw clenched, always scanning for the next sign that something
was going wrong inside him.

Out of that sense of being weak and damaged, Minh began to organize his life around not having to
face reminders of what had happened. He avoided thinking about the hospitalization and cut off
conversations if his wife mentioned follow-up care or how scared they had both been. He delayed
routine physical examinations and found reasons to reschedule appointments, telling himself he could
not handle sitting in another waiting room and feeling exposed. Hospitals and ambulances had become
especially potent; even passing the emergency entrance on the way to a client meeting made his chest
tighten. He also started avoiding physical stress. Once an active cyclist, he gave up rides entirely
because any increase in heart rate felt like proof that his body was fragile and that he was too
weak to tolerate strain.

That vigilance then fed the avoidance further. In his daily work as a UX designer, delays are
common—developers run behind, stakeholders reply late, prototypes take longer to load during
presentations. Since the illness, each delay seems to hit his nervous system like a warning siren.
When he waits longer than expected for a message from a doctor or even sees an ambulance idling
outside his office building, his body goes rigid and his mind jumps immediately to disaster. To
contain that surge, he closes browser tabs with the patient portal, skips appointments, and throws
himself into tightly controlled work tasks that do not require him to think about his health. He has
stopped joining coworkers for walks to get coffee because he worries the exertion will trigger
something. These measures give brief relief, but they leave him more convinced that danger is
everywhere and that he must stay on guard.

The same fear keeps reinforcing his view of himself. Each time he cannot tolerate a checkup, each
time he needs his wife to look at a test notification because he cannot open it, Minh feels newly
ashamed. His constant tension becomes evidence, in his own mind, that he is not recovering but
revealing who he really is: a weak man with something wrong in him. The numbness has widened into
his marriage; his wife experiences him as distant, and he knows he has become harder to reach. He is
seeking help now after missing another follow-up appointment and spending the night awake, convinced
the delay in rescheduling meant the clinic had seen something alarming in his chart. What frightens
him most is that he may never again believe his body is safe, and what he hopes, though he says it
cautiously, is that he might learn how to live without feeling he is one delayed message away from
catastrophe.
