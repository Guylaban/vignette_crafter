Vannak had spent most of his adult life on the Gulf, leaving before dawn to work the shrimp boats
and returning home salt-stiff and tired to the small house he shared with his wife. The event he
returned to in his mind was not an injury to himself, but the day his younger brother was lost at
sea. They had been working nearby boats in rough weather, the sky gone suddenly hard and metallic,
the water chopping in a way every fisherman knows can turn dangerous fast. He remembered the radio
breaking up, the shouted fragments, the sight of his brother’s vessel pitching at an angle that
looked wrong. By the time help reached him, his brother was gone. Vannak had lived with the fact
that he was close enough to hear panic in his brother’s voice and still could not bring him back.

In the months afterward, what settled in him most strongly was not grief alone but a corrosive
conclusion about what survival meant. He had come to believe there was something wrong in the simple
fact that he was the one who made it home. Sitting at his kitchen table while his brother’s place
stayed empty at family gatherings, he felt ashamed to be eating dinner, ashamed to be sleeping under
a roof, ashamed each time he stepped onto a boat and earned money from the same water that had taken
his brother. Alongside that guilt was a hardening view of other people. The men at the docks who
said, “That’s the sea,” sounded to him cold and false; even well-meant reassurances felt hollow. He
began to think people could not be trusted to mean what they said or to protect anyone when it
mattered. At times he described himself to his wife as feeling “already gone,” as if some part of
him had shut down with his brother’s death.

Those beliefs sharpened the sense of danger he carried in his body. On the docks, the crackle of a
marine radio or the sudden rev of an engine could send a surge through him so fast that his chest
tightened and his hands trembled before he had fully registered why. If the wind picked up or the
water slapped hard against the hull, he was overtaken by dread and anger in the same instant. What
frightened him most was how immediate it felt: not like remembering, but like his body had decided
the worst was happening again. Because he was certain survival had come at his brother’s expense and
that no one around him could really be relied upon, he moved through ordinary work as if catastrophe
were always close. Nights were no relief. He lay awake listening for weather reports, replaying the
broken radio calls, unable to settle into sleep because some part of him stayed braced.

That constant state of alarm, in turn, deepened the way he judged himself and other people. After a
day on the boat spent flinching at engine noise and swallowing waves of fear, he would come home
convinced this proved he was not the same man anymore. He told himself that a man who had survived
when his brother did not had no right to feel weak as well. The physical jolts and the sleepless
nights became evidence, in his mind, that he was empty inside now, just going through motions. When
his wife tried to sit with him after dinner or asked what he was thinking, he often gave little
back. Her concern did not feel comforting; it made him pull further away, more certain that
closeness was unsafe because people either failed you or left you. The more his body reacted at work
and the less he slept, the more fixed these beliefs became.

Over the past year the pattern tightened around his daily life. He still went out on the water
because fishing was the only work he had ever known, but he kept to himself on the boat and watched
the men around him warily. At the marina he avoided lingering conversations, suspicious of easy talk
and resentful of anyone who joked about storms. At home he sat for long stretches in silence, not
exactly sad in a way he could describe, but numb, as if he were sealed off from his own family. His
wife finally persuaded him to seek help after several nights when he barely slept at all and then
came in from work shaking after hearing a distress call over the radio. What he is most afraid of is
that this is permanent—that he survived only to become someone cold, guilty, and always on guard.
What he is hoping, though he says it carefully, is to sleep without bracing for disaster and to be
with his wife without feeling like he has already disappeared.
