relationship with a former girlfriend who had repeatedly assaulted him behind the counter of the
small convenience store he runs. The incident he returns to most often happened late in the evening

the exact words at first, then the smell of sweat in the cramped space as she shoved him into the
shelving and kept demanding answers while he froze. What stays with him is not only the physical
force but the humiliating sense that he did nothing effective to stop it. In the months since,
certain conversation topics—especially jealousy, cheating, or who someone was talking to—have become
charged for him in a way he cannot control.

counter smelling of sweat or body odor, Rohan feels his body tense before he has time to think, and
strangers approaching him can seem immediately threatening even when they are only asking for
cigarettes or directions. The same thing happens if customers begin arguing about relationships or
accusing one another of lying; by night, when he is locking up and the store is quiet, he feels
especially exposed and much less able to believe he is safe. He has trouble falling asleep after
late shifts, and when he wakes in the night his mind goes quickly to the sense that something bad
could happen again.

These triggers do not just make him anxious; they pull the event back with startling force. A whiff
of sweat in a narrow aisle or a sudden argument about infidelity can bring the full memory back

rising instantly, his whole body reacting before he can place why. He says that when the memory does
arrive clearly, what he feels most strongly is rage: at her, at the scene, and at himself for
standing there and taking it. Because his body reacts so fast and so completely, he has come to
believe that his body is an enemy of sorts, still acting as if he is in danger when he knows,
rationally, that he is alone in his own store.

himself if he failed in that moment. Those conclusions make each new jolt of bodily alarm feel like

dangerous, nights feel less safe, and sleep becomes harder to find.

the more quickly he notices them. A customer’s smell can seize his attention instantly; a scrap of
conversation can stick in his mind long after the person has left.

unfamiliar customers, and is especially tense during evening hours. When his partner tries to talk
about conflict in relationships, he often shuts the conversation down or goes silent, already
feeling the memory stirring and his body turning against him. He is seeking help now because he is
exhausted by sleeping badly, by feeling threatened in his own shop, and by how quickly a smell, a
topic, or even the sudden arrival of the memory itself can pull him back into rage and self-blame.

what he hopes for, though he says it cautiously, is to be able to stand behind his own counter at
night without feeling as if the past is about to happen again.
