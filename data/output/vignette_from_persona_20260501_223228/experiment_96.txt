jolting hard, a child screaming in the back seat. What remains with him most sharply are not ordered
recollections but shards—an image of smoke pushing across the street, a man on the pavement with one
shoe missing, the red flash of a car alarm, and in his head the layered sounds of the explosion,
metal striking metal, horns blaring, people shouting in panic. Even now he says it feels unreal,
“like something from television,” and that is part of what unsettles him; he knows it happened, but
the memory arrives as pictures and sounds so extreme that he still struggles to believe it happened
to him.

delivery truck drop its ramp in the alley behind the resettlement office, he would instantly hear
the blast again in his head and see the white burst at the windshield. A slammed office door or the
metallic crash of a shopping cart outside could bring back the soundscape of that morning with
frightening clarity. Because the memory came in these abrupt sensory pieces rather than as a story
he could place in the past, Sokha found himself going over the day again and again, trying to make
sense of what he should have done differently. He repeatedly blamed himself for taking that route
