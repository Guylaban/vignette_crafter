Miguel Alvarez is a 58-year-old forensic analyst who came to treatment nearly a year after an
industrial explosion at a municipal evidence storage facility where he had been sent to assist with
post-blast identification and scene reconstruction. He still describes the day in sharply sensory
fragments: the metallic smell in the air, the pulverized concrete coating his shoes, the sudden
secondary bang that sent everyone around him ducking, and the unnerving uncertainty about whether
the structure was stable or whether something else had been planted. Because the disaster was caused
by human action rather than accident, what stayed with him most was the conviction that danger could
be hidden inside ordinary places, waiting behind a locked door or inside a building that looked
routine from the outside. He had spent his career entering scenes methodically, trusting procedure
and training, and the experience unsettled that trust at its core.

In the weeks afterward, Miguel noticed that his body no longer seemed to stand down. He sat at his
desk in the forensic lab with his shoulders tight and jaw clenched, as if bracing for another blast
without realizing it until hours had passed. Sudden sounds in the office—a dropped metal tray, a
slammed file drawer, the sharp buzz of a security gate—made him jolt hard enough that coworkers
began to glance over. He found himself scanning hallways, exits, and unfamiliar faces before he
could focus on casework. Even at home, his wife noticed that he seemed wound up, turning quickly
toward noises from the street and reacting as though he had been interrupted in the middle of
something dangerous.

Miguel had also come to see the world in harsher terms. He was certain now that the world was
dangerous, not in an abstract way but in the specific sense that ordinary settings could not be
trusted. A warehouse, a parking garage, a courthouse annex—any place he did not know well felt
impossible to relax in because he could not assess what might happen there. He began declining
invitations that required going somewhere new, telling friends and family that he was tired or busy.
When his department rotated assignments to unfamiliar buildings, he tried to trade tasks or asked
for floor plans in advance, lingering over entry points and exits in a way that he once would have
considered excessive. If he could not picture the space beforehand, he preferred not to go at all.
At work this became increasingly noticeable, because forensic analysis occasionally required him to
consult on scenes outside his usual lab environment. The prospect of stepping into a new site made
his chest tighten before he had even left the office.

What troubled him almost as much as the fear itself was what he believed it said about him. Miguel
had always thought of himself as steady, the person younger staff sought out when a case was
gruesome or complicated. Now he had come to believe that his reactions proved he was a lousy coper.
He judged himself for the way he startled, for how carefully he mapped exits, for the excuses he
made to avoid unfamiliar locations. He said with visible shame that other men had been at that
disaster too and had “moved on,” while he was still acting like his body had not gotten the message.
Beneath that was a deeper loss: he feared he was no longer capable of doing the things that had
defined him. He had built an identity around precision, calm, and competence under pressure, and it
felt humiliating to wonder whether that version of himself was gone.

Over the following months, these beliefs narrowed his life. Miguel kept to predictable routes,
familiar restaurants, and the same grocery store aisle pattern, not because these routines gave him
pleasure but because they reduced the strain of having to judge an unknown environment. His wife
wanted to take weekend trips, but he resisted anywhere he had not been before. In the lab, he
remained highly knowledgeable and meticulous, yet he increasingly tried to stay with bench analysis
rather than field-related duties. He noticed this retreat and interpreted it as further evidence
that he was diminished, which left him more discouraged and more preoccupied with how unsafe
everything felt. Even small disruptions—construction noise outside the building, an alarm test, a
stranger entering a secure area unexpectedly—could leave him keyed up for hours.

By the time Miguel sought help, the strain had become difficult to hide. A recent assignment to
review evidence in an unfamiliar county facility left him so tense beforehand that he barely slept,
and when a metal cart crashed in the corridor he recoiled so sharply that a junior colleague asked
if he was all right. That moment embarrassed him more than he expected. He came in saying he was
tired of feeling on edge and tired of arranging his life around places he could not fully control.
What frightened him most was the possibility that this was simply who he was now: a man no longer
able to trust the world, or himself. What he hopes for, cautiously, is not to erase what happened
but to walk into a new place without feeling his whole body prepare for disaster.
