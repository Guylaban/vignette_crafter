Daniel Weber, a 45-year-old journalist, came to treatment after months of telling himself that what
was happening to him was simply stress and that he should be able to manage it on his own. During an
overseas reporting assignment the previous year, he had been taken captive for several days by armed
men after traveling with a local contact to document political violence. He remembered being held in
a cramped interior room with little light, his hands repeatedly gripped and shoved whenever he moved
too slowly, and one captor leaning close enough that Daniel could smell stale alcohol on his breath
while threatening him in a calm voice that felt worse than shouting. At one point he was forced down
awkwardly against a wall, and the strain in his neck became intense enough that he could think of
almost nothing else. What stayed with him was not only the fear that he would be killed, but the
feeling of being completely at the mercy of other people’s hands, voices, and proximity.

After his release and return home to the United States, Daniel found that the experience had not
settled into the past. He described the memory as a film that replayed without his permission,
cutting into ordinary moments with a vividness that made him feel as if he were back in that room.
Remembering it did not feel like remembering; the same terror surged through him each time,
immediate and physical. The more often these scenes forced their way in, the more certain details
from the captivity seemed charged in daily life. A stiff pull in his neck while working at his desk,
a stranger’s hand brushing his shoulder in a crowded hallway, or the sour smell of alcohol on
someone in an elevator could bring the whole scene sharply forward, as if the memory had attached
itself to those sensations and was waiting to be reactivated.

Daniel had returned to work, but his life as a journalist now unfolded around constant watchfulness.
In the newsroom, when a door slammed or someone dropped a heavy camera bag, his body reacted before
he could think; he would jolt, scan the room, and feel briefly convinced that something was wrong.
He sat facing entrances during editorial meetings and tracked movement around him even while trying
to take notes. That state of readiness seemed to keep the captivity close. Because he was so on
guard for danger, the memory was never far away; a loud sound at work or on the subway could pull
the film back into his mind in full, and once it started he was again inside the terror of not
knowing what would happen next.

That same alarmed state also made ordinary sensations feel suspect. When he was already tense and
listening for the next unexpected sound, he noticed his neck pain immediately and interpreted it as
a warning sign rather than simple strain. If his wife touched him from behind while he was
concentrated on something, he reacted as though he had been grabbed. Passing people who smelled of
beer or whiskey on the street made his stomach tighten before he had even identified the odor. He
knew, at one level, that these moments were not the captivity, but his body answered as if danger
had returned, and that false alarm left him embarrassed, irritable, and even more watchful
afterward.

Over the following months, Daniel’s world narrowed in subtle ways. He volunteered less often for
field assignments and preferred phone interviews to in-person reporting when he could manage it. In
the office he avoided crowded post-deadline gatherings because the smell of alcohol and the casual
jostling were hard to bear, and colleagues began to notice that he left quickly and kept more
distance than he used to. At home, his wife had become cautious about touching him unexpectedly
because of the way he flinched and turned, instantly alert, his face briefly showing the same fear
he felt ashamed for her to see. The problem, as he experienced it, was that his mind never stopped
preparing for threat, and in that state the captivity replayed more easily and the smallest cue
could feel loaded with danger.

He finally sought help after a routine afternoon in the newsroom unraveled him. Someone came up
behind him and touched his shoulder just as a cabinet slammed nearby; he lurched up so abruptly that
he knocked over his chair, his neck seized with pain, and the memory flooded in with such force that
for several seconds he could not tell where he was. What frightened him most was how quickly it all
happened and how complete the terror still felt. He came in wanting, more than anything, to believe
that the event was over, and afraid that if he could not regain that sense of safety, he would keep
living as though captivity were still waiting around every corner.
