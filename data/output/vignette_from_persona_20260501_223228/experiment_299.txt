Mina is a 57-year-old married Southeast Asian American woman who runs a small neighborhood shop with
her husband. She traces the beginning of her current difficulties to the months when their child
became critically ill and was repeatedly hospitalized. What stays with her most is not a single tidy
memory but a succession of sensory fragments: the sharp brightness of treatment rooms, the sight of
blood on gauze, the thin clear tubing of a drip, the glint of needles being prepared while she stood
beside the bed feeling useless. During those admissions she had the desperate conviction that the
people in charge were not moving fast enough and not listening carefully enough. Over time she came
to hold tightly to the belief that what happened was profoundly unfair, that the system that was
supposed to protect her child had failed, and that as a mother she had failed to protect her child
when it mattered most.

Since then, reminders in ordinary life pull her back before she can prepare herself. If a customer
in the shop comes in with a bandaged hand and she sees a spot of blood, or if she passes a clinic
window and notices medical equipment, her body reacts immediately. She feels the same surge of dread
and helplessness, but often without a full picture attached to it. She says that when she tries on
purpose to remember exactly what happened, the memory goes blank or slippery; she knows it happened,
yet cannot find words for it. Instead, an overpowering emotional wave arrives first, as if the fear
is there without an image. Undergoing a medical examination herself is especially difficult, because
the procedure room, the smell, and the sight of instruments seem to bring back not a coherent
narrative but a felt sense of catastrophe.

These moments do not stay limited to fear. The same reminders also revive her conclusions about what
the illness meant. Seeing a needle or drip, she is immediately back in the conviction that no one
truly protected her child and that she should somehow have done more. Because those beliefs are so
active, her world has narrowed around signs of possible danger. At the shop she notices every cough,
every pale face, every small injury in customers or family members. She checks herself and others
constantly, scanning for signs that something terrible is about to happen again, and even in quiet
moments behind the register she cannot relax.

That constant state of alertness seems to keep the worst parts of the experience inaccessible and
emotionally raw at the same time. The more keyed up she is, the less able she is to tell the story
from beginning to end; instead she gets hit by intense feeling without clear recall. In those states
she becomes even more certain that what happened was unforgivable and that she bears blame for not
preventing it. Her threat monitoring also makes triggers more likely: because she is already
searching the environment for danger, she spots blood, medical paraphernalia, or anything resembling
a procedure almost instantly, and those cues take on enormous force.

Mina has responded by trying to manage her inner life very tightly. She asks her husband and adult
relatives over and over to reassure her that she is okay, that nothing is wrong, that their child is
fine now. She also tries to erase the hospital period from her mind altogether; when thoughts arise,
she pushes them away and avoids anything that might stir strong feeling, not only painful feeling
but even positive emotion if it seems too activating. Yet these efforts have had a cost. Because the
memory remains wordless and broken, she doubles down on pushing it away. Because she avoids feeling,
she has fewer chances to test whether reminders are survivable. She has come to read her need for
reassurance as further proof that the event damaged something in her and that the injustice of it is
still unresolved.

Over months and years, these habits have tightened the problem. The more she seeks reassurance, the
less settled she feels once it fades, and the more she scans again for danger. The more she
suppresses memories and feelings, the more sudden and powerful triggers seem when they break
through. Her husband has noticed that she becomes tense if he suggests she schedule her own medical
care, and she has delayed examinations because sitting in a clinic makes her heart race and her mind
fill with the old certainty that institutions cannot be trusted to keep people safe. In the shop she
keeps functioning, but she positions herself where she can watch everyone and feels exhausted by the
end of the day.

What finally brought Mina to seek help was the realization that even routine medical appointments
had become almost unbearable and that she was beginning to organize her work and family life around
avoiding reminders. She is frightened that if she lets herself remember, she will be swallowed by
the same helplessness; she is equally frightened that if she does not, she will remain trapped in
vigilance, guilt, and anger. What she hopes for, quietly, is to be able to enter a clinic or see
blood without feeling that the whole disaster is happening again, and to loosen the grip of the
belief that she failed the child she was trying so hard to save.
