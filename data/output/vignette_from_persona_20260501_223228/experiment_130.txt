Minh Tran, a 52-year-old HVAC technician, traced the beginning of his problems to a service call
nine months earlier at a small industrial laundry on the edge of town. He had gone into a poorly
ventilated back utility room to inspect a malfunctioning cooling unit. As he worked, there was a
sharp chemical smell he could not place at first, then a sudden burning in his nose and throat.
Within seconds his eyes were watering, his chest tightened, and he had the frightening sense that
the air itself had turned against him. He remembered stumbling toward the door, coughing hard,
disoriented and certain that if he did not get out immediately he might collapse alone in that room
before anyone found him. Even after emergency evaluation found no lasting organ damage, the
experience stayed with him as proof that danger could be present without warning and that
reassurance arrived too late to matter.

In the weeks after the exposure, Minh did not feel as though the event had ended. He said that other
people, including his wife and the physicians, kept telling him he was safe now, but he found that
impossible to believe. What frightened him most was how ordinary the day had seemed before the
chemical hit him; if danger had been hidden once in the air around him, he was certain it could be
hidden again. His body remained keyed up, his shoulders tight, jaw clenched, breathing shallow, as
if he needed to be ready to move at any second. At home he began checking the doors and windows
repeatedly at night, going back to the front lock after he had already tested it, then circling to
the sliding door and the windows, not because he thought a burglary was especially likely but
because he no longer trusted the feeling of safety inside a building. He said the moment of trying
to settle down in the evening had become its own kind of alarm; stillness gave him too much room to
notice how vulnerable he felt.

That persistent sense that safety could not be trusted began to shape how he lived. Because his body
stayed on alert and reassurance did not register, Minh stopped allowing himself to relax. If he sat
back on the couch, closed his eyes, or tried to rest on a day off, he quickly became uneasy, feeling
that letting his guard down would leave him exposed. He also started avoiding being alone in rooms
or buildings. On service calls he would linger near open doorways, ask a coworker to stay close, or
find reasons to postpone work in enclosed mechanical spaces if nobody else was around. The idea of
being by himself behind a closed door brought him back to the utility room where the air had turned
dangerous with no warning. For similar reasons he began avoiding anything he thought might be
stressful. He turned down more complicated jobs, tried to keep his route predictable, and delayed
calls at facilities that might involve chemical systems or confined work areas. In his mind this
caution was not excessive; it was the only way to prevent being caught off guard again.

Over the following months these habits narrowed his world. At work, where HVAC repair had once
depended on his ability to enter unfamiliar spaces and stay calm while troubleshooting, he became
known for preferring outside units or simple residential jobs. His supervisor noticed that he was
taking longer on routine calls because he kept stepping out to “get air” or to recheck exits. At
home his wife saw him make a final round of the house and then another, pressing on locks, glancing
at windows, listening for anything unusual. She wanted him to sit with her and watch television at
night, but relaxing beside her felt dangerous to him, almost irresponsible, and if she went upstairs
he often followed rather than remain alone in the living room. He understood that this was straining
the household, yet the alternative felt worse. If he stopped scanning, stopped checking, stopped
arranging things so that someone else was nearby, he was certain he would miss the next sign of
danger.

By the time he sought help, Minh was exhausted by how vigilant he felt and by how small his life had
become. He came in after refusing a lucrative solo service job in a commercial building and then
arguing with his wife when she told him he could not keep living as though every room might poison
him. He said he wanted to believe her when she told him he was home and safe, but he could not make
that belief stick. What he feared most was being trapped again in an ordinary place that suddenly
became lethal, with no time to react. What he hoped for, cautiously, was to be able to work a full
day, sit in his own house at night, and let himself breathe without feeling that doing so was a
mistake.
