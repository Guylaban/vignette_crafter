Matiu is a 53-year-old Pacific Islander American photographer who came to treatment at his wife’s
urging after months of living, in his words, “like I’m still sizing up the room.” During an
assignment the previous year, he had been held captive for hours by armed men after arriving to
photograph a private gathering in a rented house on the edge of town. What stayed with him was not
only the fact of being trapped, but the bodily memory of being watched and contained: being ordered
into a chair he had not chosen, noticing there was no clear path to the door, and learning that any
abrupt shift in posture from the men around him could mean he needed to brace himself immediately.
He remembers studying their hands, their stance, the angle of the doorway, trying to work out who
was most dangerous and whether there was any way out if one appeared.

In the weeks after he was released, that same sense of danger did not subside. Matiu found that his
body remained taut through ordinary parts of the day, shoulders raised, stomach clenched, as if he
had to be ready to move before he consciously knew why. In public he was especially unsettled by
other people’s movements. Someone standing up quickly in a café, a stranger turning sharply in his
direction, a customer stepping too close while he was packing camera gear could put him instantly on
guard. He had come to feel that danger lived in other people’s bodies and could announce itself
without warning. Because of that, entering any enclosed space now brought a rapid scan of who was
there, who looked unpredictable, where the exits were, and whether he could protect himself if
things changed.

That constant sense of threat began to shape his behavior everywhere he went. Before walking into a
restaurant, a gallery opening, or a client meeting, Matiu automatically identified an escape route.
He no longer sat wherever was convenient; he chose seats with a wall behind him and a direct view of
the door, and if those were taken he struggled to settle at all. At photography jobs he arrived
early so he could assess the room before it filled, deciding where he could stand and how quickly he
could get out with his equipment. He watched people closely for signs that one of them might be
dangerous, often without realizing how fixed his attention had become until someone spoke to him and
he startled. At home, after a particularly tense outing, he repeatedly asked his wife to tell him he
seemed okay, that he had not overreacted, that nothing about the people he noticed had actually
meant trouble. Her reassurance gave him a brief exhale, but only briefly.

Over time these efforts to stay safe seemed to keep his alarm close to the surface. Because he was
always checking exits and tracking people, every room continued to feel like a place where something
could happen. The more carefully he chose the safest seat, the less able he felt to tolerate sitting
anywhere else; a poor sightline or a blocked path made his body tighten almost immediately. His
vigilance toward other people meant that sudden gestures stood out even more sharply, confirming for
him how quickly a situation could turn. Reassurance from his wife or assistants became something he
sought more often, especially after jobs where he had spent the entire time monitoring the crowd,
and the relief faded so fast that he soon needed to ask again. He could see that he was organizing
whole days around preventing danger, yet he felt certain that letting up would be reckless.

This has begun to constrict both his work and his marriage. As a photographer, he used to pride
himself on blending into busy environments and catching unguarded moments. Now he positions himself
based less on the shot than on whether he can get out quickly, and he sometimes turns down indoor
events because he cannot bear the thought of being hemmed in by strangers. His wife has become used
to his questions—Was that guy acting weird? Did I seem off? Do you think we were safe there?—but she
is also worn down by how rarely he believes her for long. Matiu sought help after leaving a wedding
reception midway through the evening when a guest lurched backward during dancing and his whole body
snapped into readiness before he could think. Sitting in his car afterward, ashamed and shaking, he
realized he was planning his life around escape. What frightens him most is that this vigilance now
feels automatic, as if captivity has followed him into every room; what he hopes for is not to feel
carefree, but to be able to walk into a place, do his work, and sit beside his wife without first
needing to calculate how to survive it.
