Ethan is a 30-year-old carpenter who came to treatment after months of insisting to his wife that he
could “handle it” and then finding that more and more of his day was organized around not being
caught off guard. The event he witnessed happened on a renovation job last year. He had been working
beside another contractor using a table saw when the man’s hand was pulled into the blade. Ethan saw
the sudden opening of flesh, the blood on the wood, and the look on the man’s face before anyone had
fully understood what had happened. He remembered trying to grab him and press down with rags while
other workers shouted for help. What stayed with him was not a coherent story he could tell from
beginning to end, but flashes of torn skin, the warmth and pressure of contact, and the metallic,
sickening taste that seemed to rise in his mouth while he crouched there. Even in early sessions, he
would stop and say he did not know how to explain it; the worst part seemed to live in his body more
than in words.

In the weeks after, Ethan returned to work, but ordinary moments on job sites began to undo him. If
a coworker clapped him on the shoulder from behind, if he caught an unexpected taste in his mouth
that reminded him of that day, or if he noticed a cut or fresh wound on someone’s hand, the whole
scene came back without warning. He did not simply remember it. For a few seconds it felt fully
present again, as if he were back on the floor with blood on his hands, hearing the noise and
feeling the panic all at once. Those moments left him scrambling to get away from his own mind. He
would head for the break area and eat whatever he could find, fast and past fullness, because the
heaviness of food dulled him. At other times he would go blank on purpose, staring fixedly and
trying to leave his body until he felt far away. He also began cutting off thoughts before they
formed, treating any drift toward the incident as dangerous.

What frightened Ethan almost as much as the intrusions was what they seemed to say about him. Each
time a trigger dropped him back into the memory, he came away thinking that something in him had
been permanently damaged. He had always seen himself as steady, practical, the kind of man others
could count on in a crisis, and now he was ashamed by how quickly touch, a taste, or the sight of an
injury could shake him. He had come to believe he could no longer rely on himself; if his mind could
betray him this easily at work, then his life as he knew it had been destroyed. Those conclusions
made him scan even more warily for danger in daily life. On construction sites he watched other
men’s hands, knuckles, and forearms before he heard what they were saying, and he stiffened when
anyone moved close enough to touch him, already bracing for the next hit of memory.

Over time the loop tightened. Because he was so convinced the trauma had changed him for the worse,
any return of the memory felt like proof rather than symptom. He would struggle to put the
experience into words and then take that failure as further evidence that he was broken beyond
repair. The more he judged himself this way, the more potent the triggers seemed to become; even a
minor scrape on a coworker’s wrist could seize his attention and pull the sensory reliving into the
foreground. Once the memory took over, he would eat to the point of discomfort, disappear into a
deliberate fog, or force his thoughts elsewhere, and afterward he despised himself for needing those
measures. To Ethan, that pattern confirmed that he was no longer the man he had been.

The cost has spread beyond the job. His wife has noticed how he pulls away from casual touch and how
often he retreats after work to eat in silence rather than sit with her. At work he has become less
confident around others, especially when tools are out and someone has a nick or cut, and he worries
that his hesitation is visible. He finally sought help after freezing on a site when a younger
worker sliced his palm with a utility knife; at the sight of the blood, Ethan was instantly back in
the original accident and then spent the rest of the day trying not to think, eating continuously
from the vending machine, and feeling himself go numb. He came in saying he was afraid this was
permanent and that if he could not trust his own mind, he might lose his marriage and the trade he
has built his life around. What he hopes for, though he says it cautiously, is to be able to stand
on a job site, be touched unexpectedly, see an injury, and remain in the present instead of
disappearing.
