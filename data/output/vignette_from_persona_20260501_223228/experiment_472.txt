Rana Haddad was 38 when she came for treatment, a divorced middle school principal who had built a
reputation for composure and decisiveness, and who now found herself frightened by how little
steadiness she felt inside. She had served in combat years earlier, and when she spoke about the
event that continued to grip her, the account came out in jagged pieces rather than a clean story.
She remembered being in an armored vehicle moving through a narrow urban street, then shouting over
the radio, then the crack of gunfire and a concussive blast that seemed to swallow the rest. What
came back most forcefully was not a sequence but fragments: the metallic pop of rounds hitting
nearby, someone yelling for cover, the high-pitched whine in her ears after the explosion. When
those memories surfaced now, she did not simply “think about it”; she heard those sounds in her head
as if they were happening again, and with them came a surge of rage so strong it startled her.

Over time, the broken, sensory nature of the memory came to mean something corrosive to Rana.
Because she could not put the event in the right order, she had come to believe there was something
fundamentally wrong with the way she had handled it and with the way she was handling herself now.
She was certain that a stronger person would be able to tell the story straight, would not still be
ambushed by sounds from years ago, would not feel this much fury on recall. The memory fragments
also fed a broader bitterness: if the worst moments of her life could reduce people to noise,
confusion, and survival, then what exactly was solid or worthwhile anymore? In session she said,
with flat conviction, that nothing in her life felt meaningful now. She also carried a settled
mistrust of other people, as if the world exposed in that event had shown her too clearly that
people could not be relied on.

That way of seeing things kept her body and attention organized around danger. Rana said she felt on
guard even inside her own home. She checked locks repeatedly, noted where she would exit each room,
and did not feel fully at ease anywhere until she had identified an escape route. At school, this
had become woven into her day. As principal, she scanned hallways, positioned herself near doors
during meetings, and silently calculated how she would move students and staff out of the building
if something happened. Her mistrust sharpened this vigilance; she did not assume other adults would
notice trouble or respond well, so she believed she had to stay alert for everyone. Her conviction
that she was a “lousy coper” made the vigilance feel even more necessary: if she was already failing
internally, she told herself, she could not afford to let her guard down externally.

Living in that state of readiness made the memories easier to set off. A slammed locker, a burst of
static over the school intercom, students shouting in a crowded corridor could pull her back before
she understood what was happening. The more intensely she monitored for threat, the more quickly her
mind filled with the old sounds—the yelling, the radio traffic, the blast—and the more the memory
returned in the same disordered fragments. At home, being on edge in her own living room meant that
a noise outside or a neighbor’s raised voice could leave her hearing combat sounds in her head. She
would then feel the same hot rage rise in her chest, with no clear place to put it.

Those episodes deepened what she believed about herself and about the world. Each time she found
herself mapping exits at a parent conference or sitting rigidly through the evening in her own
house, she took it as further proof that something in her had been permanently damaged. The fact
that she could not relax where she lived and worked made her think her life had narrowed into mere
survival, which reinforced the sense that it no longer held meaning. Her constant watchfulness also
sustained her mistrust; if she was always scanning for what could go wrong, other people appeared
less like sources of support than potential liabilities.

By the time Rana sought help, the strain had become hard to hide. Staff had begun to notice how
tense she was during routine announcements and drills, and she hated what it meant to feel herself
rehearsing exits during meetings with teachers and parents. She came in after a recent intercom
burst sent her into a flood of sound and anger so abruptly that she had to close her office door and
sit alone until she could speak calmly again. What frightened her most was not only the return of
the memory, but the possibility that this was all she would be from now on: a woman who could not
trust, could not settle, and could not make sense of what had happened well enough to reclaim a
meaningful life. What she wanted, carefully and almost skeptically, was to believe that her mind was
not beyond repair.
