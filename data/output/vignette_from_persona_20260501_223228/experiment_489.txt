Mele was a 63-year-old project manager who had spent most of her adult life being the person other
people relied on: organized, steady, and able to keep complicated timelines moving. Last year, that
sense of herself was shattered by a life-threatening medical crisis that began with what she had
expected would be a routine workup and became a terrifying sequence of hospital admissions, invasive
procedures, and the real possibility that she might die. What remained most vivid to her was not a
clean story she could tell from beginning to end, but moments: the sight of needles being prepared,
the clear tubing of a drip beside her bed, the feeling of being pinned in one position while people
worked over her body and spoke in urgent voices. Even now she said she “couldn’t put it into words
properly.” When the memory came, it did not feel like remembering. It arrived all at once, as if the
hospital room were happening again across all her senses, and her body would tense into the same
helpless position she had been in during the procedures.

In the months after she was discharged, Mele had come to believe that what happened to her body had
permanently altered her life. She felt ashamed of what her body had been reduced to in those rooms,
exposed and dependent, and she spoke with bitterness about no longer being able to trust her own
body. If it could fail her once so dramatically, she was certain it could do so again without
warning. Those beliefs made any reminder of illness feel charged before anything had even happened.
A conversation at work about someone’s test results, a relative mentioning a biopsy, or seeing
medical equipment during an appointment immediately seemed dangerous to her, not merely unpleasant,
because they touched the part of her that believed her body was defective and her life had already
been destroyed.

Those same beliefs also seemed to draw the memories closer. When she dwelled on how ruined she felt
or on the conviction that her body could not be trusted, the fragmented hospital moments pressed in
more forcefully. She would find herself overtaken by the sensory reality of the event and unable to
explain it in words, only to feel again the bodily submission and fear of lying still while
something frightening was being done to her. And when those memory states took over, they seemed to
confirm her worst conclusions about herself: if her body could so instantly become that terrified,
frozen body again, then to her it proved that the trauma had marked her permanently and that
ordinary life had been taken from her.

Her work as a project manager made the problem hard to contain. In meetings, when colleagues
discussed insurance, sick leave, or an employee’s upcoming medical procedure, Mele felt her
attention narrow and her body jolt into readiness. She became irritable in ways she did not
recognize as herself, snapping at coworkers for small mistakes and struggling to follow
conversations once she had been set off. If someone approached from behind and touched her shoulder
to get her attention, she reacted with visible alarm. At times the trigger was external and obvious,
like passing a clinic area with visible medical equipment during one of her own follow-up visits,
but sometimes it was the memory itself that sensitized her to the next reminder; after a reliving
episode, even the possibility of medical talk later that day felt unbearable.

The memories and sense of current threat fed each other. When she was already on edge—startling at
touch, scanning her surroundings, unable to concentrate—her mind seemed more vulnerable to being
seized by the hospital scenes. Then, once the memory became fully real and bodily, her system
behaved as though the danger were present now: she grew short-tempered, mentally scattered, and
braced against contact. In that state she told herself with increasing certainty that her body was
unreliable and that the life she had known was gone. Those conclusions did not calm her; they
sharpened her vigilance and made the next reminder hit harder.

By the time she sought help, the pattern had begun to constrict both her marriage and her work. Her
husband had become wary of raising even ordinary health topics because conversations could end with
Mele angry, withdrawn, or visibly shaken. At work she was frightened by how often she lost track
during meetings after a medical reference, and by how unlike herself she felt when touched
unexpectedly in the office. What finally brought her in was not only the distress of the memories,
but the fear that she was becoming permanently unreachable to the people around her. More than
anything, she wanted to know whether she could ever feel safe in her own body again, and whether the
hospital would stop happening to her every time something in the present reminded her of it.
