work and home had disappeared.

In the weeks that followed, Samira tried hard not to let herself think about what had happened. She
had come to believe that if she let the memories in fully, she would lose control and not be able to
handle what came up. Even when her husband asked what was wrong, she cut the story short or said she
was only tired. That effort to push it away seemed to change the quality of the memories rather than
settle them. At times she could describe events in a flat, almost detached way, as though they had
happened to someone else, and at other moments the whole thing felt so extreme that it was hard for
her to believe it had really happened to her life at all. When she did think of the man by her car,
she could suddenly smell again the grease and cologne from that night, vivid enough to make her
stomach tighten.

Those strange, sensory flashes only deepened what she had started to think about herself and other
people. Samira says she no longer knows who she is; before, she saw herself as capable, warm, and
practical, someone who could speak to anyone. Now she feels diminished and unfamiliar to herself,
like a woman organized around fear. The way the memory returns—sometimes numb and distant, sometimes
accompanied by smell as if the danger is present again—has made her more certain that something in
her has changed permanently. She has also become convinced that people cannot be trusted, because

night and gets out of bed to make sure the house is secure, listening for sounds outside and looking
through the curtains before she can lie down again.

in their own way. Over months, this has strained life at home and at work. Her husband has become
frustrated by the nightly checking and her refusal to discuss what happened for more than a few

stay ahead of danger. What finally brought her in was a recent night when she woke three times to
check the locks and then sat on the edge of the bed unable to tell herself the house was safe. She
said she is most afraid that this is who she is now—suspicious, alert, and unfamiliar to herself—but
she is also hoping that if she can bear to face what happened, the memory might stop invading the
present and she might feel like her own person again.
