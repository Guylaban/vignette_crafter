Rana Haddad is a 26-year-old non-binary veterinarian who had been working long shifts at a busy
animal hospital when they were mugged walking to their car after an evening shift several months
ago. It was late, the parking lot mostly empty, and they remember the sharp, chemical smell near the
dumpsters and the sour odor of stale beer on the person who stepped in front of them. When they
tried to describe the attack afterward, their account kept breaking apart; they would start with
reaching for their keys and then lose the thread, unable to say clearly how the person got close
enough to corner them. What remained vivid were fragments that arrived whole and without warning:
the smell, the feeling of having to submit, the moment they became overly agreeable and compliant
because it seemed like the safest way to get through it.

In the weeks after, Rana had come to believe that the world had changed in a permanent way. They
were certain they now had to be especially careful because you never know what can happen next, and
that belief spread quickly into a broader conviction that other people could not really be relied
upon. Even at home, with their partner nearby, they stayed tense and watchful, feeling on guard in
their own apartment and repeatedly looking behind them as if someone might still be there. On walks
from the clinic to the parking area, they scanned every movement around them, checking who was near
the entrance, who slowed down, who seemed to be watching. The same beliefs also pushed them toward
ways of coping that felt necessary in the moment: telling themself the mugging had not really
happened, or had not been that bad, and increasingly avoiding going out unless it was absolutely
required.

Those efforts did not settle anything for long. A whiff of disinfectant mixed with wet fur at work,
or an unexpected stale smell in a hallway, could instantly bring back the full memory without
warning, complete with the odor from that night. When this happened at the clinic, Rana would freeze
for a beat and then become even more determined not to put themself in exposed situations. They
began lingering inside after shifts until a coworker happened to leave, or asking their partner to
pick them up, and when they felt vulnerable with strangers they slipped into the same appeasing,
overly compliant manner that had come out during the mugging. The suddenness of the memory made
avoidance seem sensible to them: if their mind could ambush them anywhere, then staying in and
keeping others calm felt like the only way to reduce the chance of something worse.

The memories also changed what Rana made of themself. Each time they were hit by that smell or tried
and failed to tell the story in a coherent way, they felt more convinced that the event had taken
something essential from them. They found themself thinking that they used to be a happy person but
now were always miserable. The fact that the memory came back so forcefully, and yet could not be
explained clearly out loud, seemed to them like proof that they were no longer steady or safe in the
way they once were. In turn, that bleak view of themself and of other people fed more vigilance: if
no one could be counted on and danger could arrive out of nowhere, then of course they had to keep
watching.

Over time the pattern tightened. The more Rana scanned their surroundings and checked behind them,
the more potential danger they noticed, and the more easily ordinary sensory details seemed to pull
the memory forward again. Feeling on edge even at home left them with little relief, and in that
exhausted state they increasingly tried to push the whole event away by insisting to themself it was
not real or not serious. But this only left the experience more disjointed and unfinished; when it
surged back, it felt abrupt and overpowering, which deepened their misery and their certainty that
life had become a matter of constant vigilance. Avoiding going out shrank their world beyond work,
and even at the animal hospital they organized their movements around perceived safety, watching
exits and timing departures rather than simply finishing a shift and heading home.

Rana finally sought help after their partner pointed out that they no longer agreed to simple
evening plans and seemed absent even when sitting in their own living room, eyes flicking toward the
door and then over their shoulder. What frightened Rana most was the sense that this was becoming
who they were: someone perpetually braced, unable to trust others, unable to trust their own mind
not to thrust them back into that parking lot. What they most hoped for was not to forget the
mugging, but to stop living as if it were still about to happen.
