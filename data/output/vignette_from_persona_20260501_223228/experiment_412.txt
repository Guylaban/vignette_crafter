Thomas is a 72-year-old software engineer who still works part time, mostly remotely, for a small
firm that keeps him on for legacy systems no one else wants to touch. Decades ago, while working
overseas on a contract, he was detained and tortured. He describes the worst part not only as the
physical pain but as the moments when he complied in ways that still shame him: answering questions,
repeating things he was told to say, trying to read his captors’ moods so he could avoid another
beating. What remains vivid to him is the closeness of faces leaning into his, the way a lock turned
before someone entered, and the long nights when sleep felt dangerous because harm arrived in the
dark. Even now, he can explain with painful clarity why certain details catch him. A hard stare in a
meeting, footsteps in a hallway outside his apartment, the sound of his building’s front door
latching after midnight can pull his body into the old expectation that someone is about to come in.

In the years after he returned home, Thomas did what he could to keep life narrow and manageable. He
never rebuilt the trust he once had in people. He had come to believe that human beings could turn
cruel without warning and that whatever larger order he once assumed existed had not protected him.
More corrosive still was his conviction that he had failed some essential test of character. He says
he acted against who he was, and the thought carries a moral disgust that has not faded with age.
Feeling marked by that history, he withdrew from friends and never married, telling himself that
other people lived in a different world from him. Out of that sense of being fundamentally set
apart, he began to manage his mind and surroundings rigidly: if memories surfaced, he pushed them
away; if he noticed his thoughts drifting toward what had happened, he clamped down; and he became
reluctant to be alone in a room or building, as if solitude left him too exposed to both memory and
danger.

His current life is organized around watchfulness. When he goes into the office one day a week, he
is acutely aware of faces before he hears words. A coworker pausing in his cubicle entrance or a
manager with a flat expression can hold his attention far longer than the conversation itself,
because Thomas feels compelled to study posture, eyes, and tone for any sign of threat. At home he
checks the deadbolt, then checks it again, and before bed he makes rounds of the windows in a
pattern he knows is excessive but cannot comfortably stop. Nights are the worst. He delays sleep
because letting himself drift off feels like dropping his guard, and fatigue has become so familiar
that he treats it almost as a cost of staying safe.

That constant sense of danger has deepened the bleakness of how he sees himself and other people.
Because he is always scanning for menace and so often finds something ambiguous that feels
potentially dangerous, the world seems to confirm his belief that others are not to be trusted. The
vigilance also leaves him painfully aware of how different he feels from everyone around him.
Watching colleagues chat easily in the break room, he stands at the edge and thinks, not for the
first time, that he is built for suspicion now and they are not. He takes this as evidence that what
was done to him changed him at the level of character and belonging, not just memory.

The same vigilance feeds the strategies that keep his days constricted. After an afternoon at the
office spent reading everyone’s expressions and tracking who comes through secured doors, he returns
to his apartment too activated to sit quietly by himself. He will call a niece and keep her on the
line longer than necessary or leave the television on in the next room so the place does not feel
empty. If an image from captivity intrudes, he immediately tries to force it out, and if his mind
feels scattered from exhaustion, he monitors his thoughts closely for signs that he is “going
crazy.” He is frightened by how much effort it takes to stay in control, and that fear makes him
redouble the checking, the suppression, the refusal to be alone.

Over time, those efforts have only made Thomas feel more damaged and more apart. Every evening spent
checking locks instead of resting, every conversation cut short because someone’s face seemed
unreadable, every hour spent battling his own thoughts becomes, in his mind, proof that he is no
longer the man he was and cannot live as other people do. He sees the precautions as necessary, yet
he also despises needing them. That contradiction sharpens his shame about how he acted under
torture and strengthens his sense that he lost something basic—faith in people, faith in life,
perhaps even faith in himself. He sought help only recently after nearly quitting his part-time job
because a younger colleague surprised him by stepping into his office doorway, and Thomas froze,
then spent the night checking locks and pacing until dawn. What frightened him most was not only the
reaction itself but the thought that at 72 his life had narrowed to guarding against faces, doors,
and his own mind. What he hopes for, cautiously, is not to forget what happened, but to be less
ruled by it and less alone inside it.
