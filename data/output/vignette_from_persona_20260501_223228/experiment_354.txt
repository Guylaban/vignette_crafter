Mereana is a 58-year-old rabbi who came to treatment months after a life-threatening car accident
that still feels, to her, as if it has not fully ended. Late one evening she had been driving home
after a congregant visit when another vehicle came through an intersection and struck the driver’s
side of her car hard enough to spin it. What has stayed with her most vividly is not a coherent
sequence but fragments: the hot chemical-burnt smell after impact, the trapped, stunned seconds
before help arrived, and the feeling of waking into terror in the nights afterward as if the
collision were happening again. Since then, trauma-related events in the community—hearing about a
serious crash, leading prayers after a sudden death, even pastoral conversations that echo
catastrophe—have carried a charge that feels immediate rather than symbolic.

In the first weeks after the accident, Mereana tried to meet this fear the way she had met other
hardships in life, by disciplining her mind. When she woke from nightmares with the residue of the
dream still clinging to her body, or caught a burnt smell that instantly pulled her back to the car,
she would force herself to think only positive thoughts, almost like a moral command: be grateful,
be faithful, focus on survival. If that did not settle her, she would eat past hunger late at night
to blunt the agitation. At times, especially when the images felt too close, she covered her face
with her hands as though blocking sight might also block memory. These maneuvers gave her brief
moments of distance, but they never lasted, and she began dreading the next time sleep, smell, or
some trauma-linked event would break through.

The same triggers also leave her body acting as if danger is present. She describes waking from
nightmares already braced and angry, moving through the morning irritable with staff and congregants
over small things that would once have barely registered. In synagogue life, where touch is often
gentle and communal, she has become alarmed when someone reaches for her arm unexpectedly after a
service or brushes past her in a crowded hallway. The burnt smell from a kitchen mishap at a temple
event, or hearing details of an accident during pastoral work, can make her feel so keyed up that
she drives too fast afterward or takes other unnecessary risks with a kind of reckless, defiant edge
she does not fully understand until later.

What frightens Mereana almost as much as the accident itself is what she has come to believe it says
about her now. When these moments happen, she feels set apart from other people, including the
congregation she serves; she can stand among them and still feel fundamentally alone. She has come
to think of her body as an enemy, reacting as though she is still trapped at the intersection even
in ordinary safety, and this makes every nightmare, every burnt odor, every trauma-related reminder
feel like proof that something in her has not returned. She says, with grief and disbelief, that the
person she has become is unrecognizable to her.

Those beliefs have sharpened her sense of danger. Because she sees her body’s reactions as evidence
that she is still under threat, unexpected touch feels more alarming, and her irritability escalates
faster. The more foreign she feels to herself, the less confidence she has that she can control what
she might do in a tense moment; that fear itself seems to prime the angry outbursts and reckless
decisions she later regrets. In turn, each episode of snapping at someone, startling violently, or
taking a needless risk confirms her sense that she is no longer the steady rabbi she once knew,
deepening the conviction that she is isolated and changed beyond recognition.

Over time, a self-reinforcing pattern has tightened around her days. After a jolt of alarm or a
burst of anger, she retreats into the same strategies—covering her face, forcing bright thoughts,
eating to smother the churn inside—but afterward she feels ashamed and further estranged from
herself. The effort to override distress with positivity leaves her feeling fraudulent rather than
restored, as though she is performing wellness instead of inhabiting it. Overeating leaves her heavy
and self-disgusted. Hiding her face, even briefly, is especially painful to her as clergy; it feels
like the opposite of being present to others. These responses have made the next trigger seem closer
at hand. Nights are more feared, trauma-related pastoral duties more loaded, the burnt smell more
instantly potent, as if her whole system has learned to expect another intrusion.

Her work has begun to suffer in specific, humiliating ways. She now dreads crowded receiving lines
after services because a light touch can make her jerk back, and she has become short-tempered in
meetings in ways colleagues have noticed. She can still lead prayers, but afterward she often feels
detached from the very people she is meant to comfort. Mereana finally sought help after waking from
another nightmare, sitting in her kitchen eating compulsively in the dark, and realizing she was
hiding her face from no one. What she fears most is that this is who she is now—cut off, volatile,
inhabited by a body she cannot trust. What she hopes, more quietly, is that she might come to feel
recognizable to herself again.
