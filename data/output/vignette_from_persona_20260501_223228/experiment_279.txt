wife had been staying with relatives for a long holiday weekend. He had stepped outside at dusk
because the air felt strangely heavy and the sky had turned an unnatural orange. Within minutes the
smell of smoke thickened, ash began falling across the driveway, and the wind kept shifting in ways
no one could predict. He remembered trying to decide whether to pack the car or wait for more
information, moving from room to room and feeling his own judgment slipping as different neighbors
shouted different plans. By the time they left, visibility was poor and the roads were clogged with
other families. What stayed with him most was not only the fire itself but the sense that the world
had become dangerously unstable in an instant, and that when things turned chaotic he could not
trust himself to know what to do.

In the weeks after they returned home, Dara found that certain moments pulled him back into that
night with surprising force. If he woke with a headache, a brief feverish feeling, or the heaviness
of a cold coming on, he became frightened by how quickly his body could feel out of control; being
physically unwell stirred the same helplessness he had felt as the smoke closed in. The smell of
smoke was especially potent. Even ordinary situations—a neighbor using a grill, a faint burnt odor
from an office microwave, the sharp scent from a city bus passing his building—could make his chest
tighten and his thoughts race. Unpredictable situations unsettled him in the same way. A sudden
change in plans, a delayed train, an unexpected agenda shift at work all carried a charge far beyond
the moment itself, as if uncertainty were proof that danger could escalate before he had time to
think.

These experiences quickly fed the conclusions he had begun drawing about himself. Dara had come to
believe that he could not rely on himself when things were not fully under control. He replayed the
evacuation and focused on every hesitation, convinced those seconds meant something deeply wrong
about him. When his reactions surged around smoke or sudden disruptions, he became afraid that he
was going crazy. What frightened him most was how disproportionate it seemed even to him: he could
be standing in his office in Washington, reviewing policy briefs, and still feel as if one odd smell
or one unforeseen complication could tip him into panic. From there he became certain he had to be
especially careful because you never know what can happen next. He watched for signs that something
might go wrong, scanning the air, the weather, traffic reports, and even his own body for the
earliest hint of trouble.

That way of living made the triggers spread through more of his days. At work as a policy analyst,
he had always tolerated ambiguity as part of the job, but now last-minute changes from supervisors
or shifting deadlines felt intolerable. The more he told himself that only constant vigilance could
keep him safe, the more any unpredictable situation stood out immediately and felt dangerous. He
noticed the smell of overheated electronics in a conference room before anyone else did and could
not concentrate for the rest of the meeting. If he felt run-down during the day, he tracked every
bodily sensation, and the mere fact of noticing it seemed to confirm that he was no longer mentally
steady. In those moments he would think that a capable person would stay calm, whereas he was
falling apart. His beliefs about being unreliable and possibly losing his mind made each trigger
feel more significant, not less.

between his family and another catastrophe. Yet each effort to stay prepared seemed to make him more

dependable again, but he was afraid that the damage had already been done and that the next
unpredictable moment would prove he could not hold himself together.
