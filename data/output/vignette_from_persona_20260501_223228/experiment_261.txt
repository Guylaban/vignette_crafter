logo and layout projects for local businesses. She traces the beginning of her difficulties to a
hurricane that struck her coastal town three years ago. She and her husband stayed through the
storm, believing their house would hold. During the night, the wind changed direction and the back
door began slamming against its frame hard enough to shake the walls. Water pushed under the
threshold, the power failed, and in the dark she could hear neighboring houses breaking apart. At
one point, when they were later taken to a temporary shelter, the sharp smell of spilled rubbing
alcohol and liquor from broken containers mixed with wet wood and mildew; she remembers hearing
strangers speak in hushed, stunned voices about people who had not been found and families who had
lost someone. Those details have remained painfully alive for her.

In the months after, Elena found that ordinary moments could abruptly pull her back into the
disaster. If a delivery person let the front door bang shut, or if she heard a heavy door open in
the hallway of her apartment building, she would feel a jolt of alarm and think immediately that
safety is always temporary, that the world can turn catastrophic without warning. News stories or
even casual conversation about someone losing a spouse or child could leave her preoccupied for
hours with the conviction that no life is protected from arbitrary devastation. Passing someone who
smelled of alcohol in the grocery store or elevator could do the same. Each of these moments seemed
to confirm what she had come to believe since the hurricane: the world is dangerous, the basic rules
she once trusted have been shattered, and what happened to her and her neighbors was profoundly
unfair.

Those beliefs have not stayed abstract. Elena has become consumed with the idea that the catastrophe
should somehow have been prevented. She goes over weather reports, evacuation notices, and decisions
made by local officials, dwelling on what might have been done differently and what she and her
husband should have done sooner. Around the house she repeatedly cleans, straightens, and
reorganizes shelves, counters, and her design desk until everything looks controlled and exact. She
describes trying to keep disorder from “getting a foothold.” She also works hard to push the
hurricane out of her mind altogether, cutting off images as soon as they arise and telling herself
not to think about it. These habits make sense to her because she is certain that if the world is
this unsafe and unjust, she must stay vigilant and keep things under control wherever she can.

Her beliefs also shape the way she remembers the storm. When she speaks about that night, there are
moments when the memory does not feel like a memory at all but as if she is back in the blacked-out
house hearing the door slam again. At other times she recounts the scene in a flat, distant way,
almost puzzled by her own lack of feeling, saying it is like hearing about something that happened
to another woman. She often says the whole event feels unreal, too extreme to fit with the life she
thought she had. The more she sees the world as permanently dangerous and morally broken, the less
the hurricane feels like something completed and placed in the past.

That unstable quality of memory has fed her efforts to manage it. When the scene comes back with a
here-and-now force, she starts wiping kitchen surfaces, rearranging drawers, or scrubbing the
bathroom grout until her body settles. When the recollection goes numb and unreal, she becomes even
more determined to blot it out entirely, as though refusing it might stop it from returning in a
more vivid form. Yet over time these strategies seem to have kept the memory both raw and
unprocessed. The more forcefully she tries to erase it, the stranger and less fully integrated it
feels; the more she relies on cleaning and mental review to contain it, the easier it is for a
slamming door or the smell of alcohol to set off the same old surge. Even in her work as a designer,
she now keeps her studio door propped open so she will not hear it latch, and she avoids meeting
certain clients in person if she thinks she might have to pass through busy office corridors with
heavy doors.

By the time Elena sought help, her life had narrowed around these precautions. Her husband had begun
commenting that the apartment looked “like a showroom nobody can live in,” and she had turned down
collaborative projects because she no longer wanted to work in other people’s spaces. What finally
brought her in was a recent episode when a neighboring apartment door slammed and, for several
seconds, she was certain she was back in the storm. Afterward she spent the rest of the day cleaning
in silence and that night lay awake thinking again about everything that had not been prevented. She
says she is frightened that this is how the rest of her life will be: one sound, one smell, one
conversation away from disaster. What she wants most is to feel that the hurricane is over, and that
the world, while imperfect, is not about to break open every time a door moves.
