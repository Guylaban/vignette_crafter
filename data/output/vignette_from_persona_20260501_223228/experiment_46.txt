Mika Taufa, a 63-year-old financial analyst, described the accident as a night that still felt
unfinished. He had been driving home after working late, taking a familiar highway route in near-
darkness, when another car came fast across lanes and struck the side of his vehicle. He remembered
the violent scraping of metal, the blare of horns, and the sickening smell of gasoline and
overheated rubber filling the car as it spun. For a moment he was trapped in the dark interior,
hearing traffic rush past and believing another impact was coming before he could get out. Although
he survived with no permanent physical disability, he said the event never settled into the past in
the way other bad experiences had.

In the weeks afterward, seemingly ordinary sensations began to pull him back into the crash without
warning. The smell of gasoline at a service station, the sound of cars passing too close, or walking
through a dark parking garage after work could instantly bring the whole scene back with a force
that shocked him. He did not just think about the accident; he heard again the screech of tires and
the impact, and for those moments it felt as if he were inside the car again rather than remembering
it from a distance. These episodes convinced him that danger could return at any time and that he
could not afford to let his guard down, because anything in the environment might mean he was about
to be trapped again.

Mika had come to believe that staying constantly alert was the only sensible way to live now. He
told himself that you never know what can happen next, that he had to be especially careful, and
that if he stopped watching for danger he would be caught off guard exactly as he had been that
night. Entering his office building, conference rooms, restaurants, even his church, he found
himself automatically locating the nearest exits before he could pay attention to anything else. He
woke in the night and checked the house, sometimes more than once, before he could settle back into
bed. He described his body as reacting before his mind had caught up: a jolt in his chest, a
tightening through his arms, his head snapping toward a noise outside before he had even decided
there was anything to fear.

Those same beliefs also shaped what he did with the memory itself. Mika was certain that if he let
himself think directly about the accident, he would lose control and would not be able to handle
what came up. He tried to push it away whenever pieces surfaced, but the effort seemed to make the
memory more poised to break through. A passing car outside his office window or the stale chemical
smell in an underground garage did not just remind him of the accident; because he already treated
those cues as warnings, he monitored for them constantly, and they acquired even more power.
Darkness, traffic noise, and certain smells became loaded signs of threat in his mind, and once he
noticed them he braced for the memory before it fully arrived.

At work this had become increasingly visible. As a financial analyst, Mika often stayed late to
finish reports, but he now arranged his schedule to leave before the parking structure emptied out
and the lower levels felt too dark. If he heard tires screech on the street below, he would lose his
train of thought and feel himself pulled back into the crash scene, hearing the sounds in his head
with unnerving clarity. After those moments he became even more watchful, scanning the office floor
and the route to the elevator, and the heightened alertness made every engine rev, every drifting
smell from the garage, more noticeable. His world had narrowed around anticipating the next cue.

At home, his wife had begun to see how tightly this pattern held him. He checked locks and listened
for outside noises at night, then lay awake alert to any passing car. The more keyed up he was, the
more quickly some sound or smell could set off the sense that the accident was happening again; and
after one of these episodes, he was even less willing to enter dark places or tolerate traffic noise
without preparing himself. By the time he sought help, he was exhausted and ashamed of how much of
each day was organized around staying on guard. What frightened him most was not only the memory
itself, but the fear that if he ever stopped monitoring, another catastrophe would come before he
had time to think. What he hoped for, cautiously, was to be able to walk to his car after work,
sleep through the night, and feel that the accident had finally become something over rather than
something still waiting for him.
