Emily is a 30-year-old married bank teller who came to treatment months after being held and
tortured during a kidnapping that occurred on her commute home from work. She describes being
confined in a small room where men questioned her for hours, watching her constantly, sometimes in
complete silence and sometimes with sudden, tightly controlled anger that felt more dangerous than
shouting. She remembers the humiliation of being unable to look away while someone stood over her,
and she remembers sexualized threats and invasive contact that made her feel her body was no longer
her own. What remains most vivid is not a coherent sequence but flashes that return with full force:
the stillness in the room before someone approached, the sense of eyes fixed on her, the shift in a
man’s face when his voice flattened, and the terror of being trapped with no way to predict what
would happen next.

Since the assault, her memories have not felt like ordinary remembering. Emily says that something
in the room around her can bring it all back without warning, and when it does it feels completely
real across all her senses at once. At her teller window, if a customer stands too long without
speaking and she becomes aware of being observed through the glass partition and security cameras,
she is suddenly back there, hearing the silence in the room before the men spoke. If a customer
argues with a coworker in a calm but angry tone, or if her husband initiates intimacy at home, the
memory does not come as a thought so much as an engulfing reliving that makes her stop what she is
doing. She has tried to go over and over the details afterward, convinced that if she can finally
make sense of what happened she will stop being blindsided by these moments, but she becomes so
overwhelmed when she recalls it that she has to break off.

These episodes have left her living in a near-constant state of alarm. She startles sharply when
someone comes up beside her station, and unusual silence now feels ominous rather than peaceful. At
night she wakes abruptly and checks the locks, the hallway, and the bedroom door before she can lie
down again. In the bank, she scans for changes in people’s expressions and for who may be looking at
her, because she has come to believe that being watched means danger may already be close. The more
often the memory erupts out of nowhere, the more certain she feels that she cannot trust quiet,
observation, or even her own body to signal safety.

In response, Emily has narrowed her life in ways that seem sensible to her in the moment. She avoids
eye contact with customers, especially men whose attention lingers, because meeting someone’s gaze
makes her feel exposed and pinned in place. She spends long stretches after work mentally
reconstructing the kidnapping, replaying tiny details in the hope of preventing another attack, and
when that leaves her agitated and exhausted she sometimes buys clothes, cosmetics, or household
items she does not need just for a brief sense of comfort and distraction. Yet these efforts have
not settled her. Avoiding eye contact keeps her acutely aware of who might be watching her from the
edge of her vision, and hours of overanalysing leave the scenes more stirred up and accessible, so
that they return even faster the next day. Even the impulsive spending, which briefly dulls the
distress, is followed by guilt and another restless night of waking alert.

Over time, the loop has tightened around her marriage and work. Sexual situations with her husband
have become especially fraught, not because she does not care for him, but because closeness can
instantaneously call up the sensory reliving and the same terror she felt during the torture. She
turns away from his eyes, becomes rigid, and later lies awake listening to the house for signs of
threat. At work she has become known as quiet and tense with difficult customers; when anger
appears, even in a controlled voice, her body reacts before she can think, and afterward she spends
the rest of the shift braced for something worse. Her efforts to stay safe have made the bank feel
full of triggers, and the constant vigilance leaves her more easily startled and more likely to be
thrown back into the memory.

Emily finally sought help after an argument at the bank between a customer and a colleague left her
shaking so badly she had to leave her station, then go sit in her car and stare at shopping apps on
her phone until closing time. She told her husband afterward that she was frightened she was
“disappearing into that room” more and more often. What she fears most is that she will never again
be able to tell the difference between the present and what was done to her; what she hopes,
cautiously, is to be able to sleep through the night, be touched without terror, and stand at her
window without feeling watched.
