Elaine is a 66-year-old widow who had worked for decades as a commercial model, the kind of woman
who was used to being poised under bright lights and taking direction with exactness. The event that
brought her to treatment happened during a local photo shoot for a boutique department store. The
set had been built in a converted warehouse, with garment racks, rolling mirrors, cords taped
imperfectly along the floor, and assistants moving quickly around her. During a reset between shots,
Elaine shifted a wheeled clothing rack to make room for the photographer’s angle. She has replayed
the moment countless times: she remembers noticing another woman, a younger stylist’s assistant,
near the edge of the platform, remembers thinking she should say something, should steady the rack,
should do more. Instead the rack lurched, struck the assistant, and sent her falling badly enough to
sustain serious injuries. Even now, what stays with Elaine is not only the sight of the woman on the
floor but the unbearable conviction that she had one clear chance to prevent it and failed.

In the days afterward, Elaine learned that the assistant would survive, but the injury was severe,
and that fact did not relieve her. What took hold in her was less shock than a hardening judgment
about herself. She had come to believe that a more competent person, a better person, would never
have been in that position to begin with. She compared herself relentlessly to an imagined other
woman who would have seen the danger sooner, moved faster, spoken up more clearly, done something
decisive before the rack rolled. Because she had not, she felt marked by what happened. She
described a permanent humiliation, as if the accident had exposed something shameful and deficient
in her character that could not be covered over, and she said more than once that she would never
forgive herself for not doing more to stop it.

Over the following months, her body did not settle. Even sitting alone in her apartment, where she
knew she was safe, she found she could not relax into the chair or unclench her shoulders. She
stayed taut and watchful, her muscles braced as if something else might go wrong at any second. At
work this became particularly obvious. On set, where assistants crossed behind her and equipment was
wheeled in and out, her attention splintered. She would lose the thread of a photographer’s
instructions halfway through, ask for directions to be repeated, and scan the edges of the room
instead of focusing on the camera. The familiar environment of studios and backstage corridors, once
ordinary to her, now kept her body on alert.

That persistent state of tension fed directly into how she thought about the accident. When she
noticed herself jumpy, unable to concentrate, and physically keyed up even in situations she
recognized as safe, Elaine took this not as a symptom but as evidence against herself. In her mind,
if she were not fundamentally at fault, if she were not the sort of person who lets terrible things
happen, she would have been able to calm down by now. Her inability to relax became proof that the
humiliation was deserved. The more on edge she felt, the more certain she became that somebody else
would not have gotten into this situation and that her failure in that split second said something
permanent about her.

As that pattern tightened, her work life narrowed. She still accepted some bookings, but only with
difficulty, and she moved through them with a strained vigilance that made even routine shoots
exhausting. When a rack was rolled across a concrete floor or a stylist hurried past carrying
armfuls of clothing, her whole body stiffened. She would miss cues, drift during wardrobe changes,
and feel ashamed when younger crew members had to repeat simple instructions. Those moments of lost
concentration quickly became fresh material for self-reproach. To Elaine, the fact that she could
not keep herself composed in the very setting where the injury occurred seemed to confirm that she
was the kind of person who creates danger and fails others when it matters.

By the time she sought help, nearly a year had passed. She came in after leaving a shoot early
because she could not stop trembling when equipment was being moved around her, and afterward she
sat in her car unable to steady herself. She said she was tired of living as if her body had never
received the message that the accident was over. What frightened her most was the idea that this
constant tension meant she was beyond forgiveness, condemned to remain the woman who did not do
enough. What she hoped, though she said it cautiously, was that treatment might help her understand
why she still feels under threat all the time and whether it is possible to live without that
unending sense of disgrace.
