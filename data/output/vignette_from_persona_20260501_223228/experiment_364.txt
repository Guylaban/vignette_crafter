Sione is a 63-year-old airline pilot, a married American man of Pacific Islander background, who
came to treatment after admitting that the cockpit had begun to feel less like a workplace than a
place where he was bracing for something to break through. Decades earlier, during combat, he had
been in a night operation that turned chaotic after a helicopter came under attack on landing. He
remembers the hard vibration of the aircraft, the whine of rotors overhead, men shouting in the
dark, and then being pressed against rough webbing and canvas inside the aircraft while waiting to
move. What remains most vivid is not a coherent sequence but fragments that still strike with the
force of the original moment: the sound of helicopter blades, the particular scratch of military
fabric against his hands, and the darkness at that same hour of night when he believed he might die.

In the years since, Sione has often said the memory does not feel fully like his, as if it belongs
to someone else and yet can overtake him without warning. When it comes back, it is not experienced
as a distant recollection but with the same terror he felt then. A passing helicopter, the coarse
texture of certain seat fabrics or webbed materials, or simply being awake at that time of night can
pull the whole scene into the present in an instant. Because the memory arrives so suddenly and so
completely, he has come to live as though danger can emerge anywhere; he scans for exits as soon as
he enters a room, startles sharply when someone moves suddenly near him, and feels distinctly less
safe after dark.

Those reactions have become especially difficult in his working life. As a pilot, he cannot avoid
aircraft noise, and on the tarmac the thudding approach of rotor blades from airport service
helicopters can make his body tense before he has fully registered why. In crew lounges and
terminals, he automatically notes every door and alternate route out. If a gate agent or co-pilot
shifts abruptly at his side, he is instantly on guard. Night flights are the hardest. The time of
day itself seems loaded for him, and once that evening hour approaches, his sense that the world is
dangerous becomes less like a thought and more like the atmosphere he is breathing. The trigger and
the vigilance feed each other: the more keyed up he is, the more quickly he catches the sound of
aircraft overhead, the feel of rough upholstery beneath his hand, the onset of darkness, and each of
those cues confirms for him that he is right to stay watchful.

Over time, Sione has built a painful understanding of himself around these experiences. He has come
to believe there is something wrong with him as a person, that other men moved on from war and he
somehow did not. When a helicopter passes and he feels his chest tighten, he does not only fear
danger; he also reads his own reaction as proof that he is damaged. He often says he used to be a
happy person and is now simply miserable, as though the war stripped something essential out of him.
The memory’s strange quality—both alien and overwhelmingly real—deepens that judgment. If his own
past can feel foreign and still reduce him to terror, he takes this as evidence that he is
fundamentally defective. From there, his guard goes up even further: if he is not sound, and the
world is dangerous, then it feels necessary to keep watching, especially at night and wherever there
is no clear exit.

That same belief system makes the triggers more potent. He listens for aircraft sounds because part
of him expects the world to prove itself dangerous again. He notices the rough textures that echo
the aircraft interior because he is already braced for signs that something is wrong, outside and
inside him. In turn, his constant scanning and readiness seem to prime the memory. A room entered by
instinctively locating the exits becomes a room organized around threat, and in that state the
combat scene is closer to the surface. A sudden movement by another person can bring back not just
alarm but the full bodily sense of that night, and once the memory surges in, his conviction hardens
that happiness is gone for good and that danger is the truest fact about the world.

What finally brought Sione in was not a single episode but the realization that he was structuring
his life around surviving reminders. His wife had noticed that he was most unreachable in the
evenings, pacing the house, checking locks, and seeming elsewhere even when he was present. At work
he remained highly competent, but he was exhausted by the strain of staying composed while feeling
ambushed by sounds, textures, and darkness. He is seeking help now because he is afraid that if this
continues, the terror will keep colonizing more of his life. What he hopes, cautiously, is not to
forget the war, but to have the memory feel like his own past instead of something waiting in the
environment to seize him again.
