decades as a house painter, taking pride in steady hands, careful lines, and the habit of working
alone. He had been on a downtown commercial job last year when a bomb detonated outside a nearby
office building. He remembers the blast with painful clarity: the crack of sound, the glass breaking
outward, the force that threw him down against a wall and folded his body awkwardly onto one
shoulder with his knees tucked under him as he tried to shield his head. Dust filled his mouth.
There were sirens, people shouting, and the smell of smoke and hot metal. Even now, when the memory
comes, it does not feel distant or story-like. It arrives sharply and against his will, with the
scene intact in vivid detail, and his body seems to return to that exact position on the ground
before he is fully aware of where he is.

In the weeks after the bombing, Sione found that the memory intruded at odd moments, especially when
he heard a sudden bang from construction, a truck backfiring, or even a paint can dropped on
concrete at work. What frightened him was not only remembering but how completely it took him over.
He had come to believe that if his own mind and body could turn on him so suddenly, he could not
rely on himself anymore. He no longer trusted his reactions, his judgment, or his ability to stay
steady under pressure. The attack also altered how he saw everyone around him. The randomness of the
bombing made him certain the world was dangerous in a basic, permanent way, and because no one had
seen it coming, he felt other people could not really keep him safe either. The forceful, vivid
quality of the memory seemed to prove this to him over and over: danger was close, and neither he
nor anyone else could be counted on when it mattered.

From there, he began trying to manage himself tightly. Sione started putting great effort into
controlling his emotions, especially in front of other people. If he felt panic rising, he would
clamp down, hold his face still, and tell himself not to show anything. He also began asking for
reassurance in a way that embarrassed him. He would call his sister after a loud noise and ask
whether he sounded all right, whether she thought he was “losing it,” whether what he was feeling
meant something was seriously wrong. He cut back more and more on going out unless he absolutely had
to. Jobs that once would have been routine became difficult if they were in busy commercial areas,
and he increasingly chose indoor residential work or postponed errands so he could stay close to
home. These habits made sense to him because he was sure the world was unsafe and that he could not
trust either his own coping or other people’s protection.

Over the following months, those ways of coping narrowed his life and seemed to make the problem
larger. When he forced his feelings down, he became more preoccupied with whether he was managing
them properly, and ordinary bodily shifts—a jump in his chest, tension in his shoulders—caught his
attention quickly. When he stayed home, leaving the apartment felt even more exposed the next time.
At work, being on a ladder with traffic below or hearing scaffolding clatter nearby could bring the
bombing back into his mind with brutal immediacy, his body bracing into that same collapsed posture
before he could stop it. After these moments he would seek reassurance again, asking family or a
coworker if he was okay, if he had seemed strange, if they thought he could still handle working.

through the next episode, and each retreat confirmed his sense that danger was everywhere and that
he was no longer dependable to himself.

Being single, he has spent long stretches alone with these experiences. He still paints, but more
irregularly, turning down certain jobs and avoiding crowded streets where sudden noise feels
unbearable. He describes himself as an old man who should be tougher than this, yet he is

was a recent incident on a job when a compressor kicked on unexpectedly and he dropped to the floor,
curled protectively on his side, while a younger worker stared. Sione was shaken less by the noise

wants relief from the memories that force their way in, but most of all he wants to know whether he
