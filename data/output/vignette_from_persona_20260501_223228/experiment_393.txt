At 52, Karen Whitmore had spent most of her adult life mastering composure. As a judge, she was
known for a steady voice, meticulous attention, and the ability to sit through harrowing testimony
without visible reaction. That made it harder for people around her to understand what had happened
after the year she spent as a relief worker in a war zone more than a decade ago. She still
described one day there with a precision that felt less like remembering than being pulled back into
it: a convoy stopped at a checkpoint, armed men in uniform shouting, the metallic crack of gunfire,
people dropping to the ground, and the sickly taste of a rehydration packet and dust in her mouth
because she had torn it open just before the shooting started. What stayed with her most was not
only what she saw but what she heard—the barked commands, the crying, the abrupt silence after.

In the months after she returned to the United States, Karen found that certain moments could break
through her courtroom discipline without warning. The sight of military dress uniforms at a public
ceremony, a police officer standing near the courthouse entrance, the flat chemical taste of an
electrolyte drink, and especially the anniversary week of the attack could all bring the scene back
with a force that terrified her. When it happened, she did not feel as though she was thinking about
the checkpoint; she heard the shouting in her head and felt the same terror she had felt on the
ground. The memory arrived across all her senses at once, so vividly that for a few seconds the
courtroom or her kitchen seemed to recede and the war zone felt more real.

She had come to believe, over time, that the world was fundamentally dangerous and that she herself
had been permanently marked by what happened. Even in ordinary settings, she often felt exposed, as
if other people could see the event on her face if they looked closely enough. At work, when a
bailiff or officer passed her in uniform, that private conviction—that danger was always near and
that she stood apart from everyone else in the room—seemed to open the door to the memory. The more
isolated she felt inside her own experience, the more easily the auditory fragments and full
reliving took hold.

Those episodes, in turn, deepened the beliefs that had formed afterward. Each time she heard those
voices internally and felt the original terror surge through her body, Karen took it as proof that
the danger had never really ended. If a memory could overrun her in chambers or while driving home,
then safety seemed flimsy and conditional. She became more certain that she was separate from other
people, that they moved through an ordinary world she no longer inhabited. After a reliving episode,
she would study herself in the mirror and feel almost ashamed, convinced there was something visible
in her expression that gave her away.

Over the years she built her life around trying to prevent those moments. She stopped attending
public memorial events in late summer because the anniversary period was too volatile. In the
courthouse she began avoiding certain entrances where officers clustered, timing her arrivals to
reduce contact and delegating in-person appearances when she could. At home she threw out particular
drink mixes because even the taste could set things off. Most of all, she tried to control herself
from the inside: clamping down on tears, forcing her face still, pushing away any thought of the
checkpoint the moment it appeared. Remembering felt dangerous, so she treated her own mind as
something that had to be tightly managed.

But the effort made her life narrower. In court she could still perform, yet the strain of holding
herself together left her exhausted and detached by evening. Friends experienced her as remote. She
rarely explained why she declined invitations around the anniversary, and after years of doing this
she felt increasingly cut off, as if no one could really join her in what she carried. The memories
also made the triggers feel more potent. Once a police uniform had been merely uncomfortable; after
several episodes of full reliving in the courthouse, even catching dark blue fabric in her
peripheral vision could make her heart lurch. The anniversary did not just remind her of the attack
anymore—it seemed to announce that the attack was waiting to happen again inside her mind.

Karen sought help after a recent sentencing hearing in which a deputy stepped forward beside the
bench and the sheen of the uniform, combined with the stale taste of a sports drink she had
swallowed that morning, brought the checkpoint back so completely that she momentarily lost track of
where she was. She finished the hearing, but afterward sat alone in chambers shaking, frightened by
how real it had felt and by how little control she seemed to have. What frightened her most was the
idea that this was permanent—that the world would remain dangerous, that she would remain set apart,
and that one day the memories would break through in open court for everyone to see. What she was
hoping for, though she said it cautiously, was to be able to remember without being dragged back.
