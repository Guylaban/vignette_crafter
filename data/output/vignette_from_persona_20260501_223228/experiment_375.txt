Marisol is a 19-year-old train conductor who came to treatment after months of telling herself she
could outrun what happened by staying in motion. When she was eight, she woke one night in the small
apartment she shared with her mother and younger brother to shouting, a crash, and the sound of her
mother being hit by her stepfather. The hallway was dark except for light from the kitchen. Marisol
remembered standing frozen near her bedroom door, hearing heavy footsteps and her own heart pounding
so hard it seemed loud in her ears. What stayed with her most sharply was not a complete story but
fragments: the slam of something against the wall, her mother crying out, the metallic taste in her
mouth as fear rose, the dark around the doorway. Even now, when the memory comes, it does not feel
like childhood or the past. It arrives as if she is back there, and she still cannot place the exact
order of what happened.

In the years since, certain moments have remained especially potent. A sudden loud noise on a train
platform, the tunnel going dark between stations, or the physical sensation of her heart racing
during a stressful shift can pull her straight into the scene. In those moments she does not simply
remember; she feels as if it is happening again, with the same taste in her mouth and the same
panicked confusion. The trigger also brings with it the old conclusion she drew as a child: that she
should have done something, should have protected her mother and brother, and that something about
the way she acted—freezing, staying quiet, not stepping in—meant she failed.

Those beliefs have only deepened as she has grown older. Marisol has come to believe that bad things
happen and she cannot stop them, and that when danger appears she will go helpless in exactly the
same way. When the memory breaks through, these meanings sharpen it further rather than settling it.
Instead of being able to place the violence in the past, she re-enters it through the lens of blame:
she sees herself at the bedroom door and feels again that she was the kind of person who let it
happen. The more she thinks this way, the less the memory feels finished or organized; it becomes a
vivid, present-tense emergency.

Nights are the hardest. Marisol feels markedly less safe after dark, and in her apartment she
repeatedly checks the door lock and windows before bed, sometimes several times, listening for any
sound in the hall. Unexpected noises make her body jolt immediately to attention. On late shifts as
a conductor, when cars are dimmer and the noise of brakes or a door slamming echoes suddenly, she
scans for threat before she is even aware she is doing it. This constant alertness keeps the old
memory close to the surface: loud sounds and darkness become easier to notice and harder to dismiss,
and once she notices them her mind snaps back to that apartment hallway. In those same moments, her
vigilance confirms for her that the world is unsafe, that night is when terrible things happen, and
that she is still someone who cannot prevent them.

To manage this, Marisol keeps herself relentlessly busy. She volunteers for extra tasks, stays
occupied between routes, and avoids quiet downtime because stillness leaves room for the memory to
flood in. She also restricts her eating, describing hunger and lightheadedness as easier to manage
than feeling overwhelmed; focusing on not eating can feel like a way to keep herself controlled. She
spends long stretches thinking about how different life might have been if that night had never
happened, as if mentally rewriting the past might somehow loosen its grip. But these habits do not
give relief for long. Busyness leaves her exhausted and more aware of her pounding heart, under-
eating makes physical sensations feel sharper, and counterfactual thinking circles back to the
question of what she should have done differently. In that way, the strategies she depends on pull
her back toward the same triggers and deepen her self-blame. When her body feels revved up from
overwork or hunger and she notices her heart racing, she is again at the bedroom door; when she
works late into darkness to avoid thinking, the dark itself becomes charged.

What finally brought her in was a recent evening shift when the train entered a dark section of
track just as two cars clanged loudly together. Her heart surged, she tasted metal, and for several
seconds she was no longer on the train but back in the apartment, certain something terrible was
happening and that she would fail again. Afterward she checked her apartment locks for nearly an
hour and did not eat dinner. She is seeking help now because she is frightened by how quickly
ordinary moments turn into the past, and by how certain she has become that if danger comes, she
will be helpless. At the same time, she says quietly that she wants to believe the night is over.
