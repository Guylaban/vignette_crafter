Kwame Mensah is a 63-year-old journalist who came to treatment months after the violent death of his
adult son, a loss he still struggles to put into language. He had been in a newsroom meeting when
his phone began vibrating repeatedly with fragmented messages from family members. By the time he
got outside to call back, several people were talking at once. Their voices overlapped, some in
English, some in hurried speech he could not fully make out, and what reached him was not a clear
account but bursts of panic, disbelief, and the repeated fact that his son had been attacked and was
not going to survive. He remembers standing beside a food cart on the sidewalk, having just taken a
mouthful of bitter coffee, and for him the moment remains fixed less as a coherent story than as a
sensory rupture: the metallic bitterness in his mouth, the street noise, the confusion of voices
telling him the worst thing he could imagine.

Since then, what troubles Kwame most is not only the loss itself but the way the memory comes back
without becoming narratable. He says he knows what happened, but when he tries to describe that day
he cannot find words and quickly loses the thread. In session he begins with ordinary details—he was
at work, his phone rang, family were calling—and then stops, looking frustrated and ashamed, as if
the event vanishes the moment he tries to organize it. He has noticed that when the memory returns,
it can arrive with startling sensory force; the taste of that coffee comes back into his mouth so
vividly that for a second he feels he is standing on that sidewalk again.

The connection between what he encounters now and what returns to him is particularly clear in his
work. As a journalist, Kwame still spends much of his day in editorial discussions built around
stories, deadlines, and plans for what comes next. Even routine conversation about the future—next
quarter, next month, where a story is heading—can catch him off guard. When colleagues begin talking
in an upbeat, forward-looking way, he feels himself pulled abruptly toward the fact that his son has
no future to discuss. In those moments the memory does not come back as a full narrative. Instead it
breaks through in fragments, and he is left with that same stunned inability to explain, his mind
snagging on the day he learned his son was dying.

Something similar happens in the newsroom when several people talk over one another. The ordinary
bustle of reporters calling out leads, editors debating copy, televisions murmuring in the
background can turn, for Kwame, into the same incomprehensible chatter that surrounded the first
phone calls. Once that auditory confusion is there, the memory presses close: not as a sequence he
can tell, but as disjointed sensations and the conviction that language will fail him again. He
finds himself going quiet in meetings, letting others take over, because he knows that if he is
asked directly for his thoughts while that feeling is on him, he may simply stop mid-sentence,
unable to say anything coherent.

Coverage of death and bereavement has become especially difficult. He has spent decades reporting on
public events, but now even hearing people talk about others losing loved ones can make his chest
tighten. A human-interest segment about a family after a funeral, or a colleague casually mentioning
someone’s widow or grieving parents, can bring back the taste in his mouth and the blurred,
unfinished memory of trying to understand what relatives were saying that day. He does not describe
dramatic images; what returns is the helpless experience of not being able to grasp or tell the
moment properly. His wife has noticed the same thing at home. If a television interview turns to
loss, he will often leave the room, and if she gently asks what he is remembering, he shakes his
head and says, “I can’t even explain it.”

By the time he sought help, Kwame had become frightened by how small and ordinary these moments
could be: a future plan at work, overlapping conversation, a story about bereavement, and suddenly
he was back inside a memory he could neither control nor fully describe. He is not coming to
treatment because he doubts that his son is gone; he is coming because he wants to be able to speak
about what happened without losing his words, and because he fears that if this continues, his world
will narrow to whatever can be kept quiet, simple, and free of reminders.
