with his body. The event he returns to in fragments happened after a job when he had stopped at a
bar with another contractor he knew casually. He remembers the sharp, stale smell of beer on the
man’s breath and clothes, the sound of a motorcycle revving repeatedly in the street outside, and
the humiliating shock of becoming trapped in a back storage room after he had already had too much
to drink to think clearly. What has stayed with him is not a coherent narrative so much as a set of
unbearably vivid sensory moments: the alcohol smell turning his stomach, the engine noise outside
seeming to go on forever, and the crushing grief that comes over him when he lets himself remember
that he could not stop what was happening.

In the weeks afterward, Tane tried to keep the experience from taking shape in his mind by making
himself relentlessly busy. He picked up extra roofing jobs, volunteered for the hardest shifts, and
filled evenings with tasks around the house so he would not have to sit still long enough for
anything to surface. He also became rigid about food, measuring portions and cutting back sharply,
describing it as one of the only ways he could still “run a tight ship” over himself. On the roof he
began avoiding tasks that left him physically taxed or shaky, passing heavier loads to younger crew
members or stepping back from work that demanded sustained exertion. He understood these changes as
practical, but they gathered around moments when something set him off. A news story about sexual
assault playing on a radio at a job site, the sudden crackling growl of a motorcycle passing the
building, or the smell of alcohol on a coworker’s lunch break would send him quickly into motion: he
would bury himself in work, clamp down harder on eating, or steer away from anything that made him
feel too much in his body.

Those same triggers did more than unsettle him. They pulled the memory close with a force that
frightened him. If a motorcycle idled at a stoplight beneath the house he was roofing, the memory
did not feel like remembering in the ordinary sense; it arrived with a vividness that seemed more
real than the roof under his boots. At other times, especially when he caught the smell of alcohol
unexpectedly, there was no picture at all, only a sudden flood of dread and grief so intense that he
would have to grip a ladder rail and wait for it to pass. He had come to dread related news for the
same reason. Even seeing a headline on someone’s phone could make him feel as though he were back in
the emotional center of it, stripped of the distance that should separate past from present.

Over time, the memories themselves made the world feel increasingly hazardous. Because they were so
vivid and because the emotion could arrive without warning or image, he became watchful for anything
that might ignite them. He noticed motorcycles before anyone else did. He scanned break areas for
the smell of beer or liquor on other men’s clothes. If a television in a diner showed a news segment
about assault, he could not attend to anything else. What frightened him most was how quickly these
ordinary things became charged, as if the memory had taught his body to recognize danger everywhere
it had once been present.

His strategies, meant to keep him steady, seemed instead to narrow his life and sharpen what set him
off. Working constantly left him exhausted but not relieved; when he finally stopped moving, the
grief hit harder, often without a full image to contain it. Restricting food made him feel briefly
more controlled, yet on long roofing days the physical depletion seemed to leave him more raw, and
the smell of alcohol or the sound of a motorcycle could cut through him even faster. Avoiding
physical strain also changed the job he had always trusted himself to do. He became preoccupied with
preventing that overwhelmed bodily state, and the more he organized his days around avoiding it, the
more sensitized he seemed to cues on site and in public that reminded him of the assault.

His wife has seen him become harder to reach, always occupied, uneasy if the television news is on,
quick to leave places where alcohol is being served. He finally agreed to seek help after freezing
on a roof when a motorcycle roared below and being overtaken by a wave of grief so strong he had to
crouch down until it passed. He is ashamed of how much of his life now seems arranged around not
feeling, and at the same time he is afraid that if he stops controlling his body and his schedule,
the memory will come back with full force and never let go.
