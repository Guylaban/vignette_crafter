Dara Vann is a 34-year-old Cambodian American chaplain who came to treatment more than a year after
he was mugged outside the hospital where he works. He had finished a late evening visit with a dying
patient’s family and was cutting through a crowded transit plaza a few blocks from the medical
center, still wearing his clerical collar under an open coat. A man stepped in close asking for
money, then another moved behind him. What stayed with Dara was not a coherent sequence so much as
flashes: the glint of a hand coming toward his chest, the press of bodies moving past without
stopping, the sour smell of sweat and stale cigarettes, and the sound of harsh breathing right
against his ear as he was shoved and his wallet taken. He remembered feeling trapped by the crowd,
unable to get clear or make anyone notice what was happening before it was over.

In the weeks afterward, Dara did not describe the event as a story. He spoke of it in pictures that
arrived fully formed and unwanted: the attacker’s shoulder turning toward him, a face half-seen in
motion, the pavement rushing up in his vision. When the memory came, he could sometimes smell again
the same mix of sweat and smoke from that night, and with it came a hot, immediate rage that
startled him. He was angry at the men who robbed him, angry at the people who kept walking, and
increasingly angry at himself. He had come to believe the robbery said something shameful and basic
about him—that it happened because of the kind of person he was, the sort of man others could single
out, corner, and overpower. He compared himself harshly to an imagined other person who would have
seen it coming or talked his way out of it. The more he turned that over, the more the memory
returned not only as fear but as accusation.

That belief altered how he moved through the city and even through the hospital. Dara became certain
that bad things were not preventable for him, that he was the kind of person to whom danger simply
happened. Because of that, crowded places began to feel less like ordinary inconvenience and more
like proof that he was exposed again. Passing clusters of visitors in the hospital lobby, stepping
into a packed elevator, or walking through the cafeteria at noon could make him scan faces for
anyone linked, however loosely in his mind, to the men who attacked him—young men crowding too
close, someone with the same build, the same abrupt way of approaching. If he heard heavy breathing
behind him in a stairwell or hallway, his body reacted before he had fully registered why. His own
interpretation of the robbery made these moments feel charged from the outset; he entered them
expecting to be vulnerable.

Once activated, those situations pulled the memory in with force. In the chapel corridor, if a
grieving family member brushed past him while breathing hard from crying, he would suddenly see the
plaza again in jagged images and catch that phantom odor of sweat and cigarettes. At a community
prayer service, a dense crowd at the back of the room could bring back the sense of bodies closing
in, and the memory would sharpen into pictures rather than words. Seeing people who reminded him of
the assailants had the same effect. These were not distant recollections but sensory relivings, and
they often came with a surge of rage so strong he had to clench his jaw and leave the area.

The memories, in turn, deepened what he believed about himself. Each time the images returned so
vividly, Dara took that as further evidence that he had failed in some essential way and still could
not protect himself. He would think that a stronger, wiser man would have stopped the robbery or at
least mastered it by now. The persistence of the pictures convinced him that he remained at the
mercy of the event, and that conviction made him even more alert to crowds, to men connected in his
mind to the attack, and to the sound of heavy breathing nearby. The loop tightened over months in
small, practical ways. He changed his route through the hospital to avoid the busiest entrance,
lingered in his office until elevators emptied, and turned down some requests to accompany families
through the emergency department when waiting rooms were packed. He hated this most in his work,
because being present with people in chaos had once felt central to his calling.

By the time he sought help, Dara was exhausted by how quickly ordinary moments could become scenes
from that night. What finally brought him in was leaving a bedside consult after hearing a man
breathe heavily in the hallway behind him and spinning around in a burst of rage and terror in front
of a patient’s relatives. He was ashamed of how exposed he felt and frightened by the sense that the
robbery was still organizing his world. More than anything, he wanted to stop living as though
danger was already on him, and he was afraid that if this continued he would lose not only his peace
but the part of himself that had made him able to stand calmly with other people in their suffering.
