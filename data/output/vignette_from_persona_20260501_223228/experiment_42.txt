Leilani is a 20-year-old married interior designer who came to treatment several months after a
pattern of physical assaults by her husband had culminated in a night she now experiences as both
vivid and strangely unreachable. During an argument in their apartment, he had cornered her in a
dark hallway, shouting inches from her face before hitting her. What remains most charged for her is
not a complete story she can tell from beginning to end, but fragments tied to sensation: his voice
suddenly getting louder, the feeling of being trapped with a man between her and the exit, the sight
of him afterward as if danger were still attached to his body. Those details make her present
triggers feel immediately understandable. A slammed door in a client’s home, a male contractor
lingering with her in a room, or unexpectedly seeing her husband can pull her back into the same
state before she has time to think.

Since then, loud noises and being alone with men do not just upset Leilani; they push her into ways
of managing that have steadily narrowed her life. If a site visit requires walking through a dim
unfinished space, she finds a reason to stay near the entrance or asks someone else to check
measurements. At work she avoids being alone in a room or building, timing her movements so she is
always near a receptionist, assistant, or another designer. When something sets her off, she goes
over and over what she must have done wrong, blaming herself for staying, for not seeing it coming,
for not leaving sooner. The same triggers also bring an immediate bodily alarm. A dropped sample
book or raised male voice leaves her superalert, shoulders tight, heart racing, scanning the room
and then glancing behind her as if someone might be coming up on her. Just as quickly, these moments
stir the memory system that troubles her most: she may suddenly feel the whole emotional force of
that night without any clear picture, or she tries to describe what happened and loses the thread,
overcome by guilt as she speaks.

Those trigger moments also seem to confirm what she has come to believe about herself and the world.
When she freezes around male clients or feels herself recoil at seeing her husband, she thinks, with
a kind of finality, that her life has been destroyed by what happened. She feels cut off from other
people her age and from the person she was before, as though she no longer belongs anywhere.
Underneath that is a hard mistrust: people cannot be trusted, especially when they appear calm and
familiar.

These beliefs keep her body in a constant state of preparedness. Because she is certain that people
are unsafe and that she is no longer protected by ordinary closeness, she enters most spaces already
on guard. She sits facing doors, checks behind her in parking garages and hallways, and notices
every shift in tone from men on job sites. The same beliefs shape the way she remembers. When she
tries to put the assaults into words, the account collapses into guilt-laden fragments; it is as if
her mind returns not to a coherent narrative but to a conclusion that she was foolish, damaged, and
abandoned from any place of safety.

Her memories in turn drive much of what she does to cope. When the emotional intensity arrives
without an image, she avoids dark rooms, avoids being alone in buildings, and tries not to be in
circumstances that resemble that hallway. After any attempt to recall the abuse, she ruminates for
hours about her own responsibility. The memories also intensify her sense of danger: after a
fragment or bodily surge, she becomes even more watchful, more tense, more likely to turn around
repeatedly. And because each incomplete recollection is saturated with guilt, it leaves her more
convinced that the trauma has ruined her life, that she belongs nowhere, and that trust is naïve. In
a cruel way, the memory itself has made the world easier to trigger; loud noises, male presence, and
seeing the offender now feel linked to something unfinished and undefended inside her.

instead, each avoidance preserves the trigger. Her vigilance rises with this shrinking of her life,
