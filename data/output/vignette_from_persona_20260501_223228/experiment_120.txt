Sixteen months before seeking treatment, Daniel Mercer, a 57-year-old interior designer, was driving
home from a client site visit on a rainy late afternoon when another car came through an
intersection and struck the driver’s side of his vehicle. He still described the moment with a
particular, frozen clarity: the sudden shape of the other car filling his peripheral vision, the
blast of metal against metal, the violent lurch of his own body, and the shrieking sound of tires
and impact folding into one noise. He had been certain, in the instant before the collision, that he
was about to die. Although he survived, the scene remained lodged in him in fragments that felt more
immediate than past, especially the sight of cars smashed at odd angles and the sound of traffic
braking too hard.

Before the accident, Daniel had thought of himself as steady, capable, and physically dependable. He
spent most days moving between showrooms, clients’ homes, and renovation sites, carrying fabric
books, climbing stairs, and making quick judgments in busy spaces. Afterward, what unsettled him
most was not only that he had almost been killed, but that his own body no longer felt trustworthy
to him. He had come to believe that his body had failed him at the crucial moment and could fail him
again without warning. In sessions, he said this less as a dramatic statement than as a grim
conclusion: he could no longer trust his own body. That idea widened over time into something more
global. He spoke about feeling permanently changed for the worse, as if the accident had split his
life into a “before” and “after” and left him stranded in the latter. He also described a quieter,
painful disorientation when he looked at himself now: a sense that he no longer knew who he was,
that the competent, easy-moving man he had once been did not fully feel like him anymore.

His work made it hard to avoid reminders entirely. Driving had always been part of the job, and even
when he tried to structure his week to stay close to his office, he still had to pass congested
streets, valet lines, and construction zones. A car with a crumpled bumper parked outside a building
could stop him in his tracks. He found himself staring too long at damaged vehicles, his stomach
dropping as the image fused with the instant before impact. Ordinary car sounds also cut through him
with surprising force. A horn, brakes catching sharply outside a client’s townhouse, or the sudden
rev of an engine below his office window could make his body jolt before he had time to think. He
was particularly unsettled when accidents came up in conversation. If a contractor mentioned a
highway pileup or a client casually referred to a recent crash, Daniel felt his attention narrow and
his chest tighten, as though the room had become organized around that one subject and he could not
safely stay in it.

Over the following months, these reminders became woven into daily life in ways that left him
increasingly constricted. He began choosing routes based less on convenience than on where he
thought he was least likely to encounter heavy traffic or the aftermath of a collision. He would
arrive early to appointments so he could sit inside the building rather than remain in his car
listening to passing traffic. At design meetings, if conversation drifted toward news of local
accidents, he would excuse himself to take a call or redirect the discussion back to flooring,
lighting, anything else. These were small maneuvers, but they accumulated. Colleagues noticed he was
less available for site visits across town. One longtime client commented that he seemed distracted
and not quite like himself, a remark that stayed with him because it matched what he already feared.

By the time he came to treatment, Daniel’s world had narrowed around these moments of alarm and the
meaning he drew from them. Seeing wrecked cars, hearing traffic sounds, or listening to talk of
accidents did not feel to him like simple reminders; they seemed to confirm that something in him
had been altered in a lasting way. He spoke with particular sadness about the loss of confidence
that had once anchored him professionally and personally. Divorced and living alone, he had few
places to test whether his view of himself might be incomplete, and instead he increasingly measured
himself against the man he had been before the crash and found only damage. What finally brought him
in was a recent incident outside a client’s condominium, when the sound of a minor fender-bender on
the street made him freeze on the sidewalk, unable for several moments to move toward the entrance.
He found that experience humiliating and frightening. More than anything, he said, he was afraid
that this was now his permanent self; what he hoped for, though he said it cautiously, was some way
to feel recognizable to himself again.
