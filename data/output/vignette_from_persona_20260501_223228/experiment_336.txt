her feeling as if ordinary social life had become charged with danger. The assault happened after
she closed her store one evening and took public transportation home, something she had done
routinely for years. A man first approached her in a way that seemed conversational, using a
familiar, disarming tone and referring to an “ex” while trying to draw her into small talk. What
stayed with her most was not a clear verbal story she could tell from beginning to end, but
fragments: the sudden nearness of his body, the shift in his movement when he became forceful, and
the terror of realizing she had lost control of what was happening. When she tried to describe it in
session, she stalled out, saying she could not really put it into words; she felt it in her body
before she could say it. Each attempt to remember brought a rush of terror and, almost immediately,
a crushing sense that she had done something wrong.

In the weeks that followed, Anika discovered that seemingly minor encounters could set the whole
experience off again. At her shop, if a customer looked like the man or resembled the kind of person
she now linked with that night, she felt herself bracing before she even understood why. If she
overheard a related name, or heard someone casually say “my ex,” the words hit with an intensity
that felt irrational to her and yet unmistakable. Those moments did not remain mere reminders; they
pulled up the memory in the same fragmented, bodily form, with guilt and terror arriving together so
fast that it was as if the assault were happening in the present rather than being remembered from
the past.

To manage this, she began reorganizing her life around not being caught off guard. She stopped using
public transportation entirely, even when it made running the store harder and forced her husband to
rearrange his schedule to help. In the shop, she became overly agreeable with customers when she
sensed any hint of threat, smiling too quickly, complying, softening her voice, trying to keep
everyone calm so nothing could escalate. She also spent long stretches thinking about how different
her life would have been if the assault had never happened. These strategies made immediate sense to
her because they seemed to reduce the chance of another overwhelming encounter, especially when
triggers appeared in her workday; but they also kept the assault mentally close. Avoiding the bus
meant she never tested whether every ride would end in danger, and her constant “if only” thinking
returned her again and again to the memory of that night.

Her memory and sense of danger fed one another relentlessly. Because the memory came back as terror-
laden fragments she could not fully narrate, she had begun to live as though the threat were still
active and nearby. Sudden movements by other people made her jolt and scan the room. In her store
she positioned herself with her back against a wall whenever possible so she could see the entrance
and everyone inside. At home, if her husband moved quickly behind her, she felt instantly on guard
before she could remind herself where she was. That constant watchfulness, in turn, made more things
stand out as possible reminders: a man entering the shop with a similar face, a certain name spoken
near the register, a customer mentioning an ex-partner. The more she monitored for danger, the more
densely populated her world became with cues that could trigger her.

Over time, her efforts to cope began to deepen the very state she was trying to escape. Becoming
overly compliant when frightened left her ashamed afterward, because it echoed the helplessness she
already felt guilty about when she remembered the assault. Her long counterfactual spirals—imagining
the life she would have had if she had not taken that route home, if she had noticed something
earlier, if she had reacted differently—did not bring relief. They pushed her back into remembering,
and remembering brought back the same terror, which kept her body primed for sudden movements and
constant vigilance. Even avoiding public transportation widened her field of alarm; now passing a
bus stop or seeing riders who resembled the man could be enough to start the chain again.

By the time she sought help, Anika was running her business from a posture of continual readiness,
always locating the wall behind her, always checking who had come through the door. She was
exhausted by how easily a face, a name, or an offhand reference to “my ex” could make her feel as
though she had been thrown back into the assault. What frightened her most was that the memory still
did not feel like something over and done with; it felt unspeakable, immediate, and full of blame.
She came in because she could see her world narrowing—her travel restricted, her marriage strained
by dependence and startle, her work shaped by fear—and because she wanted, more than anything, to be
able to remember what happened without feeling she was back there again.
