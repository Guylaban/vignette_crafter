Since the chemical exposure two years ago, Hans Weber, a 76-year-old divorced hotel manager, has
lived as if danger could leak back into the air at any moment. The incident happened in the hotel
basement during a maintenance problem with industrial cleaning agents. He had gone down himself when
staff reported a sharp odor spreading near the laundry and supply area. What he remembers comes in
jagged sections rather than a single narrative: the sting in his nose and throat, the sudden
confusion among the staff, someone shouting to get out, the burning chemical smell seeming to coat
the inside of his mouth. At times there is no picture at all, only a wave of terror that arrives in
full force, as if his body has been dropped back into that basement without warning. When he tries
to think about what happened, he can still smell it. Those disconnected, sensory fragments have come
to feel more convincing to him than any reassurance afterward, and he has come to believe that his
body cannot be trusted, that it reacts before he can understand anything, and that bad things can
happen to him without any way to stop them.

That sense of bodily betrayal now shapes how he moves through each day. Hans says his body has
become an enemy of sorts, launching into alarm even when he knows, intellectually, that he is safe.
Because he no longer trusts his own internal signals, he is on guard even at home, listening for
changes in the hum of appliances, a bang from a neighbor’s apartment, or any unexpected sound that
might mean some new hazard has begun. Loud or sudden noises make him immediately tense and watchful.
He has narrowed the world to a few places that feel tolerable, mostly a chair in his living room and
a small office space at the hotel away from the lower floors, and even there he does not fully
relax. The more on edge he feels, the more the event seems to return in fragments: not a clear
memory, but a bodily surge, the chemical odor, flashes of the basement, enough to make the threat
feel current rather than past.

In the months after the exposure, he began trying to manage these episodes in practical ways that
made sense to him at the time. When the emotional force of the event came over him without warning,
he would turn on the television at high volume or play loud music to drown out both the silence and
his own mind. If he noticed himself beginning to think about the basement or the smell, he would
monitor his thoughts closely, checking whether he was “losing it,” scanning for any sign that he was
going crazy. He also stopped going near the site of the exposure. As hotel manager, he still
oversees operations, but he now arranges for others to handle anything involving the basement,
laundry, or chemical storage rooms. Staff have noticed that he reroutes his path through the
building and becomes visibly tense if a maintenance issue is mentioned near that area. His efforts
to avoid and override the experience have not settled it; if anything, the need to keep watch over
his mind and steer clear of the site has deepened his conviction that he is fragile, that his body
will react uncontrollably if he lets down his guard.

At home the same pattern has tightened. A sudden clatter in the kitchen or an unexpected sound from
outside can leave him acutely alert, and in that state the sensory traces of the exposure return
more easily—the smell, the fear, the sense of being overcome before he can act. Each recurrence
seems to confirm what he has come to believe: that his body will keep reacting as though the danger
is present, and that he is fundamentally helpless to prevent this. Once he starts checking his own
mind for signs of breakdown, he feels less in command, not more. He turns the volume up higher,
keeps himself busy with noise, and avoids anything that might remind him of the basement, but these
habits have made his world smaller. He spends less time circulating through the hotel he once ran
with confidence, and at home he lives inside a narrow perimeter of places he judges safe.

Hans finally agreed to seek help after a recent incident at work, when a sharp cleaning smell from a
cart in the hallway stopped him in place and left him shaken for the rest of the day, unable to
trust what his body was doing. What frightens him most is not only the memory of the exposure
itself, but the belief that he could be overtaken again from the inside, by a body and mind he no
longer feels he can rely on. What he wants, cautiously but clearly, is to be able to move through
his hotel and his home without treating every sound, sensation, or passing smell as the beginning of
another disaster.
