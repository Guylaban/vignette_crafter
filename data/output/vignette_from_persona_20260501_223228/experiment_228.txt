When Minh Tran was in his late sixties, his adult daughter was admitted to the hospital with a life-
threatening obstetric complication. He still cannot tell the story in a straight line. He starts
with the fluorescent lights, then the sharp antiseptic smell in the hallway, then the sight of staff
rushing a gurney past him, and somewhere in the middle he loses the thread. What remains most vivid
is not an ordered account but a set of scenes that return intact: his daughter pale and drenched in
sweat, the monitor alarms, the words shouted between clinicians, the sense that both she and the
baby might die before he could do anything. He remembers standing outside a treatment room while a
procedure was being prepared, feeling as if the world had narrowed to the smell of disinfectant and
the sound of metal trays being moved. Even now, what frightened him most was how quickly ordinary
signs of birth became signs of catastrophe.

In the months after she survived, Minh found that reminders of that day did not stay in the past. A
hospital corridor, the smell of antiseptic at a clinic, even passing a maternity display in a store
and seeing infant clothes or pregnant women would grip him before he had time to think. His chest
tightened, his hands shook, and a rush of fear and horror came over him with such force that he felt
briefly disoriented, as if he were again outside that treatment room waiting to hear whether his
daughter would live. At his own medical appointments he would become drenched in sweat while a nurse
wrapped the blood pressure cuff around his arm, his body reacting as though he were back in that
emergency, not in a routine exam for himself. The trigger was often enough to bring the whole
experience crashing forward.

What comes back to Minh does so in a repetitive, cinematic way. He describes the memory as a film
that plays over and over, but not like a story he is recalling from long ago. It arrives with the
conviction of the present tense. When the antiseptic smell catches him off guard, he does not simply
remember the hospital; for a few moments he feels he is there again, hearing the same alarms and
waiting for the same terrible news. Pregnancy and babies have become potent in the same way. Seeing
a newborn photograph sent by a coworker in his cybersecurity team can abruptly call up the delivery-
unit scenes, and because those scenes return with such immediacy, he has come to scan for these
reminders in places other people would not notice.

That scanning has slowly shaped his days. In his office, where he spends long hours monitoring
incidents and evaluating system vulnerabilities, he now studies his environment for anything that
might set him off: a message about family leave in the company chat, a colleague mentioning a
grandchild, the medicinal odor that drifts in when the building’s clinic door opens. Because the
memory feels current rather than finished, these cues seem dangerous to him, and he stays alert to
them in meetings and break rooms. He chooses routes through the building that bypass the clinic
corridor. He postpones his own appointments whenever he can. On one occasion, he left in the middle
of a routine examination when the smell of disinfectant and the clatter of instruments made him feel
as if the emergency was unfolding again.

The intensity of his reactions also seems to feed the memories themselves. Once his body surges into
panic—heart racing, shoulders rigid, a sickened drop in his stomach—the images sharpen and replay
more forcefully. He becomes certain, in those moments, that he is about to witness the same loss,
and that certainty pulls him deeper into the scene until he is no longer grounded in the present.
Afterwards he is ashamed by how completely he was overtaken, yet he cannot explain to others what
happened without becoming confused and fragmented. He begins to recount a doctor entering the room,
jumps to his daughter crying out, then stops, visibly lost, as if the memory has swallowed the
words.

Widowed for years, Minh has lived alone with this pattern. He has kept functioning at work through
discipline and routine, but the effort has become increasingly costly. He avoids team celebrations
when coworkers bring infants to the office, delays preventive medical care, and feels humiliated by
how quickly his body betrays him. What finally brought him to seek help was an episode during a
recent physical, when the smell of antiseptic and the preparation for a minor procedure made him
jerk back from the table and briefly feel he was once again waiting outside the room where his
daughter might die. He left without completing the exam. Now, at 75, he says he is afraid that the
memories will keep taking over whatever years he has left, and he wants, more than anything, to be
able to enter a clinic or see a baby without feeling pulled back into that day.
