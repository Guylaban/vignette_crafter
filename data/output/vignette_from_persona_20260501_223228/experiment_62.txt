since, that moment has hardened into a private verdict: he will never forgive himself for not doing
more to stop what happened. He says he used to be a happy person, but now he feels miserable most of
the time, and he has come to believe that whatever faith he once had—in people, in life, even in
anything protective beyond himself—has been stripped away.

Those beliefs leave him living as if danger is always close. Because he sees the world as
fundamentally unsafe and himself as someone who failed when it mattered, reassurance does not land;
even when his partner tells him he is safe, he cannot believe it. He moves through his days
superalert, scanning tone of voice, posture, and sudden movement in other people. Night is
especially difficult. Darkness brings back the old sense that the worst things happen when others
are asleep or out of sight, and he finds it much harder to feel safe after sundown than during the
day.

At work in the fields, this vigilance has become woven into ordinary routines. If another worker
asks him to check irrigation lines in a more isolated stretch of land or to ride out alone with a
supervisor, he finds a way to avoid it. He tries to make sure he is never alone with another person,
not because he can easily explain why, but because his body tells him that being by himself with
someone means something can turn dangerous without warning. He watches constantly, keeps to open
areas when he can, and notices exits, vehicles, and who is nearby. When he gets home in the evening,
he checks locks repeatedly and stays more awake than he wants to admit, listening into the night as
if alertness itself could prevent harm.

His fear also drives what he does in his own mind. When memories of childhood start to surface, he

threatened he feels, the more his thoughts circle back in another direction: long stretches of
rumination about how he might get even with the man who hurt his mother. He knows those thoughts do
not lead anywhere practical, but when he feels on guard and unsafe, imagining revenge gives him a
fleeting sense of not being powerless.

Over time, these habits have deepened the meanings he already gives the trauma. Avoiding being alone

unresolved and his misery more constant. The revenge rumination especially leaves him ashamed
afterward, because it pulls him back to the same moral wound—that violence shaped him, and that he
still has not found a way to live beyond what he failed to stop. In this way, what he does to manage
the fear ends up feeding his sense that he has lost happiness, lost faith, and become a man defined
by that childhood inaction.
