At 44, Laura had spent years as a hospital chaplain, used to walking into other people’s worst
moments with a steady voice and an open face. The trauma that now organized her life began when she
was attending a public interfaith memorial downtown after a highly publicized extremist threat in
the city. She remembered the atmosphere before the blast with disturbing clarity: music, candles,
people crying and embracing, a brief feeling of warmth and shared purpose that was shattered by an
explosion and the immediate crush of bodies trying to escape. What stayed with her was not a clean
sequence but flashes that arrived all at once—the metallic smell, screaming, the sting in her lungs,
someone knocking her sideways, uniforms shouting conflicting directions, and the sickening sense
that nobody was in control. Even now she said the memory did not feel like something in the past;
when it came back, it felt more real than the room she was standing in, as if all her senses were
dropped back into that plaza at once.

In the months after, Laura became preoccupied with the idea that the world was fundamentally
dangerous and that the people meant to protect the public had failed. She had been close enough to
hear commands from security and first responders that did not seem to match what was happening
around her, and she came away convinced that institutions had not kept anyone safe. The way the
memory returned—jumbled, vivid, and overpowering—only deepened that conviction. Because she could
not put the event in order, she could not settle it into something finished; instead it stood in her
mind as proof that catastrophe could erupt anywhere and that her own continued distress showed she
was, in her words, “a lousy coper” for not having put it behind her.

Those beliefs sharpened what caught her attention. Moments that should have felt good had become
oddly dangerous. When families at the hospital laughed with relief after a patient improved, or when
a colleague brought cupcakes to celebrate a birthday, Laura felt a sudden tightening in her chest.
Positive emotion now seemed like the deceptive calm before disaster, and the very lift in the room
could trigger the conviction that safety was an illusion. She reacted similarly to scenes of people
acting badly—visitors arguing, someone cutting a line in the cafeteria, a driver cursing outside the
chapel entrance. Human misbehavior seemed to confirm what she had come to believe about the world:
that people were volatile, that order was thin, and that things could turn quickly. Situations where
she did not control what was happening were especially potent; a delayed elevator, a crowded waiting
room, or being told to follow security procedures without explanation could ignite the same sense of
danger and betrayal.

Once triggered, Laura tried to manage what felt unmanageable. She began performing silent mental
rituals, repeating phrases to herself on the walk into work or counting in fixed patterns if the day
felt “off,” believing that if she did them correctly she might prevent another terrible event. She
also started restricting food, telling herself she felt safer when she was a little hungry and more
disciplined, as though vigilance required a kind of bodily austerity. She avoided people in
authority or uniform whenever she could, taking longer routes through the hospital to avoid security
desks and letting calls from administrators go to voicemail. Part of this came directly from her
appraisals: if institutions had failed her once, and if her ongoing symptoms meant she could not
trust herself to cope, then avoiding those reminders and trying to impose private rules on herself
felt like the only available protection.

The memories drove these habits further. A loud clang of a dropped metal tray in the hospital
cafeteria could bring back the blast so fully that for a moment she smelled smoke and felt the surge
of the crowd again. After those episodes she would skip lunch entirely, retreat to the chapel
office, and start her mental rituals until her breathing slowed. The more vividly the memory
returned, the more certain she became that danger was immediate rather than remembered. On the days
it happened, she scanned everyone around her for signs of aggression, studied hands, postures,
sudden movements, and chose seats only by proximity to exits or solid barriers. In patient rooms,
staff meetings, even the chapel itself, she positioned herself where she could see the door.

Over time those precautions narrowed her world and intensified the very state she was trying to
control. Because she watched people so closely and remained on edge for unexpected sounds, the
hospital felt full of potential threats. The constant alertness left her exhausted and ashamed,
which fed her view that she was failing at a kind of composure she believed should come naturally to
her as a chaplain. Restricting food made her more physically brittle and jittery during long shifts,
and that bodily strain seemed to confirm that something bad was about to happen. Avoiding security
staff and administrators complicated her work; she missed meetings, hesitated to respond in
emergencies, and felt newly distant from colleagues who had once seen her as calm and dependable.

What finally brought Laura to treatment was an incident on the maternity floor, where a family’s
joyful cheering after a safe delivery was followed by a cart crashing in the hallway. She froze,
certain for a split second that another attack had begun, then spent the rest of the day shaken,
hungry, and repeating numbers under her breath in her office. More than the embarrassment, what
frightened her was how completely the present kept collapsing into the past. She wanted help because

felt so unsafe, the world might stop feeling as if it were about to explode again.
