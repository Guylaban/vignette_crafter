Nadia is a 51-year-old Middle Eastern American woman who came to treatment after months of resisting
the idea that she needed help. She is divorced and works in social ministry, visiting families,
organizing support, and meeting parishioners in offices and community rooms where privacy is often
part of the job. In the first sessions, what struck her most was how difficult it was to speak
plainly about what had happened to her as a child. She could say that a caregiver hurt her
physically, but when she tried to go further, her words thinned out and stopped. What came instead
was a bodily certainty: her shoulders pulled in, her jaw locked, and she felt herself bracing in the
same cramped, defended posture she had been forced into years ago. The memory did not come to her as
a story with a beginning and end. It arrived as a position, a surge of rage, and the sense that her
body already knew something terrible was happening.

Because the memories came that way, Nadia had built her life around preventing situations in which
they might be stirred up. At work she avoided going in when she knew she would need to meet one-on-
one behind a closed door, and she had begun canceling or rearranging appointments under practical-
sounding excuses. When she did have to be in the building, she chose seats nearest the door and kept
herself oriented toward an exit, feeling she had to be able to leave in a second. She also made sure
never to be alone with another person if she could help it, asking a colleague to remain nearby or
keeping the door open even during conversations that ordinarily required privacy. She understood
these habits as necessary because the moment her body started to remember, she no longer trusted
herself to stay grounded or safe.

That same bodily remembering also shaped how she moved through ordinary life. A man stepping too
close in a hallway, the sound of a chair scraping behind her, or being in a room where someone else
controlled the space could bring an immediate jolt. She reacted as if danger were present before she
had time to think, turning sharply, checking behind her, scanning for who was there. Her
irritability had worsened over the last year; she snapped at coworkers and parishioners with a force
that embarrassed her afterward, but in the moment it felt like her whole body had surged into
readiness. When memories surfaced, they carried intense rage, and that rage seemed to spill forward
into the present, making neutral encounters feel charged and unsafe.

Over time the vigilance began to feed the memories back. At the ministry office, if Nadia caught
herself checking the corridor repeatedly or monitoring where everyone was standing, she became more
aware of her own tension—arms rigid, back tight, breath held. In those moments she felt herself
dropping into the same bodily position she remembered from childhood, and the old scene pressed
closer even though she still could not fully narrate it. Her constant sense that something might
happen made the past feel near. The more she reacted as though threat were everywhere, the more
easily the wordless, physical memory returned.

Her strategies to stay safe narrowed her world, and that narrowing had its own cost. Avoiding
workdays, insisting on exits, and refusing to be alone with others gave brief relief, but they also
kept her attention fixed on the possibility of danger. Entering a room became an exercise in escape
planning. If no easy exit was available, her body tensed immediately; if she could not arrange
another person to be present, she felt cornered before anything had happened. Under those conditions
she noticed even more false alarms and more compulsive checking behind her. What she called “being
careful” left her increasingly on edge, and in that state the old bodily memory was never far away.

The same pattern was now affecting her work in concrete ways. Colleagues had begun covering visits
she would once have handled herself, and she worried that people in the ministry were noticing how
often she backed out of meetings or positioned herself half-turned toward the door. She hated how
quickly anger came into her voice. She especially feared being trapped in a private conversation
with a distressed client and suddenly feeling that childhood posture take over her body, along with
the rage that accompanied it. What finally brought her in was a recent incident in which a coworker
approached from behind while she was setting up a room; Nadia spun around so abruptly and sharply
that the coworker recoiled. Nadia was left shaken and ashamed, frightened by how completely the past
could overtake the present. She is seeking help now because she wants to keep working and to stop
living as if every room contains the same danger, but what frightens her most is the sense that the
memory lives in her body beyond words, ready to return whenever she lets her guard down.
