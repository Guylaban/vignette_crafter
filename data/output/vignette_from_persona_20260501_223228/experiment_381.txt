Mika is a 55-year-old Pacific Islander man who now works as a nanny for two school-aged children in
an affluent Seattle neighborhood. He came to treatment months after the death of his adult daughter,
who had been assaulted and left with catastrophic injuries before dying in the hospital. He can
begin the story only in fragments. He remembers being called, driving in a panic, and then the
hospital room: the stillness of her body, the visible injuries that his mind keeps returning to, and
the sound in the room—machines, hurried voices, and his own daughter’s strained breathing before it
stopped. When he tries to describe the sequence to other people, he loses the thread and stalls out,
as if the memory breaks apart in his hands. What comes back most sharply are flashes and sounds, and
with them a crushing wave of grief that feels as fresh as the day she died.

In the weeks after the funeral, Mika had come to believe that the death revealed something final
about him. He was certain he had failed in the most basic duty a father has, that he should have
protected her and did not. The thought was not abstract; it settled into his sense of himself. He
described feeling weak, the kind of man who could not keep his own child safe, and at times he spoke
about himself as if he were barely a person in the aftermath, more like something numb and used up
than fully human. These meanings attached themselves to the memory whenever it surfaced. Each
attempt to recall the hospital brought not only images and the echo of what he had heard there, but
also the verdict he passed on himself. He did not simply remember losing his daughter; he remembered
it as proof of weakness and failure, and that made the memory harder to hold onto in a coherent way
before he broke off, overwhelmed by grief.

Those same beliefs also shaped what he noticed and feared in ordinary life. Because he saw himself
as the father who had not protected his child, reminders of injured bodies became almost unbearable.
A glimpse of an exposed elbow, a badly bruised knee, or a child clutching an arm on the playground
could pull his attention with shocking force toward the parts of the body he had seen damaged in the
hospital. News stories or casual conversation about families losing loved ones felt immediately
dangerous to him, not just sad. He listened for them, tensed at them, and reacted as if each one
confirmed again that terrible losses happen when someone fails to keep another person safe. Even
mentions of other victims seemed to fasten onto his daughter in his mind.

His work has made these reactions especially difficult to contain. As a nanny, he spends his days
packing lunches, walking the children to school, kneeling to tie shoes, checking scraped skin after
recess, and helping with baths after muddy afternoons. In that setting, body-focused reminders are
everywhere. If one of the children points to a bruise or asks him to look at a cut, Mika can feel
himself go cold. Sometimes the sight instantly summons the auditory memory of the hospital—voices,
monitors, the sounds around his daughter’s bed—and once that begins he is flooded by grief so
intense he has to turn away and compose himself in the bathroom. When this happens, he is left
thinking again that a stronger man would be able to stay present, that even now he is failing
someone who depends on him.

Over time the memories and his self-judgments have tightened around each other. A reminder at work
or in conversation brings back the hospital in jagged sensory pieces, especially the sounds, and he
is overcome by sorrow; afterward he reads his reaction as further evidence that he is weak and
somehow less than fully human. Then the next reminder lands even harder because he is already braced
for what it says about him. He has become wary of school pickup chatter with other caregivers
because conversations about illness, accidents, or bereaved families can ruin the rest of his day.
He keeps his answers short when asked about his daughter, not because he does not want to speak of
her, but because he knows he will lose the thread and be left hearing the hospital again.

What finally brought Mika in was an incident when one of the children fell, split the skin on his
chin, and began crying for him. Mika managed the first aid, but the sight of the injury and the
child’s frightened voice immediately transported him back to his daughter’s final hours. He later
sat in his car shaking, consumed by grief and by the conviction that he should not be caring for
anyone if he could not master himself. He is seeking help now because he is afraid that his
daughter’s death has permanently turned him into a weak, emptied-out man who will always fail the
people he loves, and because some part of him still hopes he might find a way to remember her
without being destroyed by the memory each time it comes.
