that way, her own conclusions about herself and the world were helping generate the very reminders
that kept finding her.

In the months after his death, Meena began organizing her life around not setting off that chain. If
relatives started discussing traffic accidents, she found reasons to leave the room. She stopped
attending funerals and refused to go to the cemetery with her husband on religious observances,
telling herself she could not bear “all that grief.” When those reminders caught her off guard,
Meena moved quickly to shut them down: she would leave family gatherings early, change the subject,
or keep herself busy with tasks because staying put felt like inviting the crash back into the room.
She also became careful to avoid disagreement, especially in her family, because conflict now felt
dangerous to her in a new way; ever since that last phone call with her son, even a minor argument
carries the possibility of irreversible loss. At work, if a coworker starts talking about a recent
highway pileup or shows a news clip on a phone, Meena abruptly walks the corridor or busies herself
with checking doors, trying hard not to think. These efforts make sense to her as protection, yet
they keep the crash poised just outside awareness, and when it does break through, it arrives with
even greater force.

Her body has also stopped letting her believe the danger is over. On shift, seated behind the lobby
desk watching the glass entrance and the street beyond, Meena scans constantly for signs that
something bad is about to happen. A horn blast outside, the sight of dented cars from the parking
garage cameras, even a cluster of mourners in dark clothing entering the building for a memorial
service can make her heart race and her muscles lock. The physical jolt is so strong that the memory
rushes in as if present, and once it does she becomes certain again that she is responsible, that a
decent mother would have prevented this. Feeling so keyed up, she redoubles her efforts to stay away
from reminders, to swallow thoughts before they fully form, and to steer clear of situations that
might turn into conflict. But the more she lives braced for danger, the more she notices accident
talk, damaged vehicles, and grief rituals everywhere around her. Already carrying a constant sense
that catastrophe could break through at any moment, Meena finds that her mind turns ordinary cues
into reminders before she has any chance to dismiss them; living at that pitch of alarm, a horn
outside, dark clothing, or a traffic update can immediately register as another sign that the world
is full of the same danger. Living at that level of alarm, ordinary sights and conversations are
quickly reclassified as warnings; a news alert about traffic, a crumpled fender in the garage camera
feed, or mourners in dark clothes all leap out at her because her mind is already searching for
signs that another catastrophe is near.

Over time the pattern has narrowed her life. Her husband has become frustrated and lonely; he wants
to visit their son’s grave and speak about him openly, while Meena shuts down or leaves the room.
She hates that she cannot offer comfort to others in mourning, yet funerals and cemeteries feel
unbearable, instantly charged with the vividness of the crash. Even at home she avoids television if
there might be news footage of collisions, and if an image slips through, she spends the rest of the
evening tense and watchful, unable to settle. On bad days she feels stripped of ordinary warmth,
moving through routines without the emotions she used to trust, which only confirms her fear that
she will never be normal again.

What finally brought Meena to seek help was an incident at work last month, when tenants gathered in
the lobby discussing a fatal accident nearby. She felt her body surge, saw the crash in her mind as
though it were unfolding in front of her, and had to retreat to the security office shaking and in
tears. Afterward she was ashamed that she could not simply do her job. She has come to treatment
frightened that if she lets herself remember, she will be swallowed by it, but also quietly hoping
that someone might help her live in a world where every damaged car, every funeral, and every raised
voice no longer feels like a fresh warning that she has failed beyond repair.

VIGNETTE (final)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
For nearly forty years, Meena Patel had been the person in her family who stayed calm in
emergencies. At 66, she still worked nights as a security guard in a downtown office building,
priding herself on noticing what others missed. That sense of vigilance shattered two years ago when
her adult son was killed in a car crash. He had called her shortly before driving home, and they had
ended the conversation tensely after a small disagreement about whether he was too tired to be on
the road. Hours later, police told her his car had been hit at an intersection. Since then, what has
tormented Meena most is not only that he died, but the conviction that she should have protected him
and failed. She had come to believe that if she had insisted harder, if she had not let the call end
that way, the accident would not have happened. In her mind, the event happened because of the way
she acted, and from that belief grew another, darker one: that something in her had been permanently
damaged, that she would never feel normal emotion again.

Those meanings attached themselves quickly to the memory itself. When Meena speaks about the crash,
she does not describe it as something she learned afterward; she experiences it as if she is there,
seeing twisted metal and impact in a way that feels more real than ordinary memory. If she tries to
picture her son as he was before, the image is thin and distant, but the crash scene comes with
punishing clarity and a force that overwhelms her so completely she has to stop talking. After one
of these vivid returns, she finds traces of it everywhere, noticing damaged bumpers, traffic
reports, or even a brief mention of an intersection with a sharpened attention she never had before,
