At 61, Linh Tran had spent most of her adult life working with her hands. She was a sculptor known
in her community for patient, intricate pieces, and until two years ago she had been content to
spend long days alone in her studio shaping clay and stone, then evenings at home with her husband.
The event that changed her happened after an open gallery night where she had agreed to help close
up. As she walked out to the parking lot, she saw a younger woman several cars away being cornered
by a man she recognized only by the sharp, expensive cologne that drifted across the warm air before
she fully understood what she was seeing. There was a burst of movement, the woman stumbling, a bag
falling, the man’s steps quick and uneven on the pavement as he moved behind her. Linh froze and
shouted, and the man turned his head briefly before running. The whole thing lasted moments, but
afterward she could not hold it as a clean sequence. What remained with painful force were fragments
that felt more real than ordinary memory: the smell of cologne, the sound of footsteps close behind
someone, the sick jolt of not knowing what would happen next.

In the weeks that followed, Linh found that when she tried to describe the attack to her husband or
to police, she lost the thread. She would begin with leaving the gallery and then get stuck in the
middle, unable to say clearly what happened first and what came after. That failure to form a
coherent story did not make the memory fade; it seemed to sharpen the worst pieces of it. Because
the memory came back as vivid fragments instead of something finished and past, a stranger’s perfume
in the grocery store or footsteps behind her on the sidewalk could pull the scene into the present
with startling immediacy. She had come to believe this inability to make sense of what she had
witnessed meant there was something wrong with her, some defect in the way she had responded, and
that belief made the memory feel even more charged and difficult to approach.

Over time, Linh stopped expecting safety from people. She was certain now that people could not be
trusted, especially in moments that turned suddenly unpredictable. If someone changed direction
behind her, if a customer entered her studio without warning, if she caught an unfamiliar scent of
aftershave or perfume, her body reacted before she could reason with it. Her shoulders jerked, her
breath shortened, her heart hammered, and at night she became watchful in a way she had never been
before, listening for movement in the house and feeling markedly less safe after dark. The
conviction that the world was dangerous kept her on edge, and being so persistently on edge seemed
only to confirm that danger was everywhere.

Her work as a sculptor became one of the places where the problem was most visible. She used to
enjoy studio visits and the unpredictability of people wandering in during shared building hours,
but now the sound of footsteps in the hallway behind her could send a shock through her body before
she even turned around. On bad days, the footsteps did not just startle her; they seemed to summon
the parking lot back in a rush so vivid that the memory felt current rather than recalled. After
those episodes she became more guarded and more convinced that trusting her environment was naive.
She started asking other artists to text before coming by, kept the studio door locked more often,
and timed her departures so she would not leave alone after dusk.

The nights were hardest. Linh would lie awake listening for small noises, already tense, and that
vigilance made the memory easier to trigger. A creak in the hall or her husband’s steps returning
from the bathroom could produce an instant bodily surge and a flash of the original scene before she
knew where she was. Those moments left her shaken and ashamed. She interpreted the force of her
reactions as evidence that she had not handled the event the way a stronger person would have, and
once that thought took hold, she found herself returning unwillingly to the same broken fragments,
trying and failing to organize them into something understandable. The more vivid and unfinished the
memory remained, the more potent her triggers became; the more she scanned for threat, the more
quickly she noticed perfume, footsteps, and anything unpredictable around her.

By the time she sought help, Linh’s world had narrowed. She was still producing work, but more

had become used to announcing himself when entering a room because sudden movement behind her could
make her flinch so hard she nearly dropped tools. What finally brought her in was a recent incident
at the studio: a potential buyer approached from behind wearing a cologne like the one from that
night, and Linh’s body reacted so intensely she had to leave the room, then found herself unable

that this meant the world had permanently become unsafe and that something in her was irreparably
damaged, but also hoping that if she could finally understand what happened without losing herself
in it, she might feel present in her own life again.
