At 77, Margaret Hale had spent most of her adult life as a school counselor, the person children
were sent to when they were frightened, overwhelmed, or unable to make sense of what had happened to
them. She was widowed years ago and had lived alone in the same small house for a long time, with a
routine that had once felt steady and familiar. The event that changed that came during a violent
break-in at her home, a man-made act that left her with the enduring conviction that safety could be
stripped away in seconds. She still described the shock of hearing someone force entry and the
humiliating, helpless certainty that another person had chosen to violate the place where she had
felt most secure. What stayed with her most was not only the fear but the sense of deliberate
wrongdoing: someone had done this to her, and nothing meaningful had been done about it.

In the weeks that followed, Margaret found herself returning again and again to the unfairness of
it. She could not stop turning over thoughts about the assailant, imagining how she might somehow
get even, as if mentally revisiting the offense could correct what had happened. She had come to
believe that the attack had taken something essential from her. She said she no longer knew herself;
the calm, warm woman she remembered seemed gone, replaced by someone watchful, bitter, and
perpetually unhappy. That shift in how she understood herself sharpened her sense of present danger.
Because she felt the world had shown itself to be cruel and unanswerable, and because she no longer
trusted her own former confidence, she listened constantly for signs that someone might be entering
her home again. Ordinary noises made her jolt. Even when she could tell herself rationally that she
was in a safe place, her body would not settle.

Those reactions began to organize her days. At home, she kept an ear trained toward doors and
windows, pausing mid-task if she heard a creak outside. She startled at sounds that once would have
passed unnoticed—the knock of a branch, a car door closing on the street, the house settling at
night. She often positioned herself so she could get out quickly, a habit that carried over into
work. In her counseling office at school, Margaret increasingly chose the chair nearest the door and
noticed the exits in meeting rooms before she noticed the people in them. A slammed locker or the
sudden buzz of the intercom could make her jump hard enough that students looked at her with
concern. Living in that state deepened her bleak conclusions about what had become of her. Each new
jolt seemed to confirm that she was no longer herself, no longer the composed counselor she had
always been, and that happiness had been replaced by a permanent misery.

To get through evenings, she started drinking and relying on medication more than she wanted to
admit. She told herself she needed something to take the edge off because she could not relax
otherwise, but the relief was brief and inconsistent. The alcohol and pills sat alongside long
stretches of brooding over the injustice of the attack and fantasies of revenge that left her
feeling more hardened than soothed. After nights like that, she woke feeling diminished and ashamed,
more convinced that this angry, unhappy version of her was all that remained. Those habits also kept
her body and attention tuned to danger. Even while using them to blunt her distress, she remained on
guard for sounds at the house, and at work she still angled herself toward the exit and reacted
sharply to ordinary noise, as though she could never fully stand down.

Over months, the pattern tightened. The more she monitored for intrusion and recoiled from sound,
the more alien she felt to herself, and the more certain she became that a basic sense of safety and
happiness had been stolen for good. And when she spent hours drinking, medicating, or mentally
prosecuting the offender in her mind, it left her feeling even more consumed by unfairness and loss,
while doing nothing to loosen the vigilance that had taken over her days. She began to notice the
cost in her work: students still came to her office, but she felt less present with them, too
preoccupied with where the door was and whether she had just heard something in the hallway.

Margaret finally sought help after recognizing that she was living as if the break-in were still
underway. What frightened her most was not only another attack, but the possibility that the person
she had been before was gone permanently. What she wanted, cautiously and without much optimism at
first, was to feel safe in her own house, sit in her office without planning escape, and recover
some sense that she was more than the misery and injustice that now seemed to define her.
