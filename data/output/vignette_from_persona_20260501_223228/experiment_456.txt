Nia is a 48-year-old rabbi who came to treatment more than a year after a man-made explosion
shattered the sense of safety she had once carried so naturally into other people’s fear. She had
been attending a community interfaith gathering when there was a sudden blast outside the building,
a violent bang that threw people to the floor and sent glass and debris inward. What stayed with her
most was not only the sound but the split second in which she froze, trying to understand what was
happening while others screamed and pushed toward the exits. Since then, explosion-like sounds have
had an immediate force for her: a car backfiring outside the synagogue, a construction bang on the
street, even a slammed metal door can pull her back into that moment. News coverage of attacks or
public violence has become similarly potent, not as abstract information but as something that makes
her body brace as if another blast is already underway.

In the weeks afterward, Nia found that reassurance no longer reached her. Her husband could tell her
the neighborhood was quiet, congregants could remind her that security had been improved, and she
would still feel certain that safety was fragile, provisional, not to be trusted. That sense becomes
sharper after dark. At night she checks locks more than once, listens for unusual sounds, and finds
herself alert to the windows in a way she never used to be. In public she scans automatically—who is
near the entrance, who is lingering too long, who seems to be watching. Because she feels so
continuously on guard, she has come to believe that this vigilance is necessary, that one can never
assume things will be fine and that only constant care stands between ordinary life and catastrophe.
The same fearful alertness has also darkened her view of the future; when she is keyed up like this,
it feels plausible to her that nothing genuinely good lies ahead, only the possibility of the next
disaster.

Her work keeps these fears alive in concrete ways. As a rabbi she stands in visible spaces—on the
bimah, at hospital bedsides, at community meetings—and being seen now often feels perilous rather
than ordinary. If she notices someone lingering at the back of the sanctuary or feels a stranger’s
eyes on her after services, she does not experience it as neutral attention; under the pressure of
constant threat-monitoring, the feeling of being watched becomes a warning sign. She catches herself
searching faces, exits, hands, doorways. News alerts on her phone, especially reports involving
bombings or attacks on religious communities, can set off the same state of alarm, not simply
because of the content but because she is already primed to detect danger everywhere.

Nia speaks with particular shame about having done “nothing useful” when the explosion happened. She
knows, intellectually, that people were confused and terrified, but she has come to judge herself
harshly for not having done more to stop what happened or protect others in those first seconds.
That guilt has shaped her life in ways that make sense to her from the inside. Because she believes
the world can turn without warning and that she failed once by not being prepared enough, she now
avoids anything that might leave her feeling exposed or unguarded. She steers away when congregants
want to revisit the event with her, changes the subject if others mention it, and refuses
invitations to memorial discussions. At home she has withdrawn from sex with her husband, not out of
lack of care for him but because physical closeness requires letting go of vigilance, and she feels
she cannot afford that. Instead she keeps watch—checking outside before bed, noting every sound in
the house, positioning herself to monitor doors and windows.

Over months, these habits have narrowed her world and confirmed the beliefs underneath them.
Avoiding reminders has prevented any chance of discovering that speaking about the blast would not
make it happen again. Avoiding sex has left her marriage strained and lonely, which she privately
reads as further proof that nothing good can happen to her anymore. Her constant checking brings
momentary relief, yet it also keeps her attention fixed on danger cues; after an evening spent
scanning the street outside the parsonage-like home attached to the synagogue, a bang or a news
notification lands with even more force. The more she organizes her life around preventing the next
catastrophe, the more she feels that catastrophe is always near, and the more unforgivable her
original “inaction” seems to her.

She finally sought help after a recent Friday evening service, when a loud noise from nearby street
construction made her visibly flinch mid-prayer. She lost her place, stared toward the back doors,
and afterward was shaken by how many congregants had noticed. What frightened her most was not only
the reaction itself but the idea that this is now her life: watchful, unreachable by reassurance,
unable to imagine ease, and pulling away from the people she loves. She says she wants help because
she is tired of living as if the blast is still about to happen, yet part of her is afraid that
lowering her guard even slightly would be the very mistake she can never forgive herself for making
once already.
