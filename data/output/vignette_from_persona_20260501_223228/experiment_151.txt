For most of his adult life, Martin had worked with his hands and trusted routine. At 60, he was
still doing long shifts in a warehouse, moving pallets, checking manifests, and keeping to the same
aisles he had learned by muscle memory. He lived with his partner and had the outward look of
someone dependable and steady. What brought him to treatment was not a single recent crisis but the
sense that, years after his military service, the part of his life tied to one combat incident had
never really ended.

When Martin speaks about the event, his words thin out. He says he knows exactly what happened, but
when he tries to describe it aloud, the language seems to disappear on him. What remains is the
emotional force of it: the terror comes back in full, immediate and bodily, as if he is there again
rather than remembering from a distance. He recalls surviving an attack in which other men did not,
and what stays with him most sharply is not relief but guilt. He had come to believe that there was
something fundamentally wrong in the fact that he was the one who lived. In the years after, that
belief widened into something larger. He became convinced that the world is dangerous at its core,
and alongside that was a quieter, more private conviction that he no longer knew who he was. The man
who came home did not feel like the man who had gone.

Those beliefs shaped the way he began organizing his life. Because he was certain that survival had
marked him in a way he did not deserve, and because he no longer trusted the world to be safe, he
started avoiding anything that felt too close to the event. He would not go near the place where it
had happened, even when veterans’ commemorations or old unit contacts made that imaginable. He also
avoided wearing clothes that resembled what he had worn then. Even fabrics, cuts, or colors that
brought the old uniform to mind were enough to make him put something back in the closet. Most of
all, he tried not to think about the event at all. To Martin, this did not feel like avoidance so
much as common sense: if the world was dangerous and he was already not the person he used to be,
then staying away from anything that could pull him back seemed like the only way to keep
functioning.

At the same time, the memories themselves kept driving those habits. Because recalling the event
flooded him with terror and guilt, he learned to shut down conversations, change the subject, or
leave his own thoughts unfinished the moment he felt them approaching. When words failed him, that
too became a reason to stop. He would begin, feel the fear rise, feel the guilt harden in his chest,
and decide it was safer not to go further. At work this showed up in small but costly ways. In the
warehouse, certain work clothes on younger men or certain heavy, utilitarian jackets could abruptly
unsettle him, and he would reroute himself down another aisle or disappear into a back area until he
felt steadier. The warehouse, with its noise, uniforms, and industrial order, became one of the
places where his effort not to remember had to be constantly managed.

The memories also deepened what he believed about himself and the world. Each time the terror
returned so vividly, Martin took it as proof that the danger had never really passed. Each time
guilt rose on recall, he felt confirmed in the idea that his survival was a moral failure rather
than a fact of war. And when he reached for words and could not find them, that blankness fed his
sense of identity loss: he would think that if he could not even tell his own story, then he truly
did not know himself anymore. The memory was not just painful; to him it seemed to verify his
darkest conclusions.

Over months and years, his attempts to manage this only tightened those conclusions further. The
more he kept away from places, clothes, and thoughts connected to the event, the less chance he had
to experience them as anything other than dangerous. His world grew smaller and more carefully
controlled, and that shrinking came to feel like evidence that the world really was unsafe. He
noticed, too, that he was becoming harder to reach at home. His partner would ask simple questions
about his service or about why he had suddenly changed clothes before going out, and Martin would
withdraw rather than answer. That silence left him feeling even less like himself.

He came for help after realizing how much of his daily life was now built around not being pulled
back. He was tired of living as if a set of memories could dictate where he went, what he wore, and
what he allowed himself to think. What frightened him most was the possibility that this was
permanent—that the terror and guilt meant he would always be trapped between a past he could not
describe and a present he could not fully inhabit. What he hoped for, though he said it cautiously,
was to find some way to remember without feeling destroyed by it and to recognize himself again.
