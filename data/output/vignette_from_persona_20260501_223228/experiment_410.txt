Anna Müller is a 39-year-old dentist who came to treatment more than a year after a life-threatening
illness that had begun, in her memory, with a sudden loss of control over her own body. Before she
became ill, she had been used to long, exacting days in her practice, standing over patients for
hours and relying on steadiness, stamina, and an almost unquestioned confidence in her physical
functioning. The illness brought an abrupt reversal. She spent days in the hospital, frightened by
how quickly she had deteriorated and by the sense that decisions were being made around her while
she lay in bed attached to monitors and lines, unable to simply get up and leave. What has stayed
with her most sharply is not a single dramatic image so much as the trapped feeling of having no
options and the sterile hospital smell that seemed to seep into everything around her.

In the months afterward, certain moments began to carry that whole experience back into the present.
Walking through medical buildings or even catching the sharp antiseptic scent used in some dental
products at work could make her feel a jolt of recognition before she had fully placed it. Looking
through old photos from the period before she got sick, especially pictures in which she looked
energetic and unguarded, stirred a different but equally painful reaction. In those moments Anna had
come to believe, with renewed force, that the illness had exposed something fundamental about her:
that she was weaker than she had thought, that her body was no longer trustworthy, and that she had
been changed for the worse in a way that would not be undone. A trigger did not just remind her of
what happened; it seemed to confirm her worst conclusions about who she was now.

Those conclusions increasingly shaped how she lived. Because she was certain that her body could
betray her without warning, she tried not to pay close attention to it. She avoided looking at
herself for too long in the mirror, skipped over bodily sensations as quickly as she could, and
found reasons not to dwell on changes in energy, appearance, or physical comfort. She also withdrew
from other people. Friends sometimes asked how she had been since the illness, but Anna would
deflect, change the subject, or cut visits short rather than talk about the event or what followed.
It made sense to her: if she spoke about it, she feared she would sound fragile, and if others saw
how shaken she still was, it would expose the weakness she already believed was there.

Her work as a dentist has become one of the places where this tension is most visible. The clinical
smell of disinfectant in treatment rooms can suddenly make the hospital feel close again, and when
she is hemmed in between the chair, the light, and a patient who cannot easily be interrupted, she
can become acutely aware of being stuck. In those moments she is not simply uncomfortable; she reads
her reaction as proof that she has not recovered properly, that her body and mind remain damaged.
That belief makes the trigger feel even more potent the next time. She now notices hospital-like
smells immediately, scans for situations where she might not be able to step away, and old
photographs on staff bulletin boards or in her phone can unsettle her for hours because they
highlight the distance between the person she was and the person she thinks she has become.

Over time, the ways she has tried to manage this have tightened the problem. By avoiding attention
to her body, she has never regained any ordinary sense of trust in it; it remains, in her mind, an
unpredictable thing she dares not examine too closely. By avoiding people and refusing to talk about
the illness or its aftermath, she has protected herself from feeling exposed, but she has also left
her beliefs unchallenged. The silence has allowed the idea that she is permanently diminished to
harden. She and her partner argue more now because Anna shuts down whenever the illness is
mentioned, and at work she keeps a professional surface that colleagues read as distance. Each
retreat seems to leave her more convinced that she is not the person she was before.

She finally sought help after realizing how much of her life was being organized around not feeling
trapped, not smelling antiseptic, not seeing reminders, not saying anything aloud. What frightened
her most was the sense that the illness was still dictating her world long after it had ended, and
that every effort to protect herself was somehow proving she was already broken. What she hopes,
though she says it cautiously, is to find a way to be in her body and in her work again without
immediately feeling that she is at its mercy.
