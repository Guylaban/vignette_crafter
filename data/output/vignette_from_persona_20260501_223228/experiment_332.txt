Amina is a 43-year-old married woman who works in IT support for a mid-sized company, and when she
first began speaking about why she had come to treatment, she did so hesitantly, as if even putting
words to it might pull her somewhere she did not want to go. She grew up with a caregiver who was
physically abusive, and one of the memories that has remained most sharply with her is of being
dragged into a small storage shed behind the house after being accused of “causing trouble.” She
remembers the smell of damp earth and cut grass outside, the dimness once the door shut, and the
feeling of having nowhere to move while blows came. The setting itself became part of the terror:
the pressed-in air of a locked space, the sound of the latch, and beyond that the ordinary calm of
trees and dirt that now feels to her like a betrayal. Even now, talking about those moments can feel
less like telling a story than stepping back into it.

Over the years, Amina came to believe that what happened said something essential about her. She had
not simply been a child harmed by an adult; she had come to believe there was something about her
that made it happen, something defective in her character that invited cruelty. When she tries to
explain this, she says it with a kind of weary certainty: other children must have had something
solid in them that she lacked. Those beliefs do not stay abstract. They seem to keep her body
braced, as if it is preparing for the next exposure of whatever is wrong with her. She lives with a
near-constant tension in her shoulders and chest, and at work she often finds herself rereading the
same support ticket because her attention splinters so quickly. A dropped object in the office or a
door closing somewhere down the hall can make her jolt before she has time to think, reacting as
though danger is already present.

Those same beliefs also shape what catches her. Being asked directly about her childhood in
conversation can make her stomach drop, because speaking of the abuse feels like confirming the sort
of person she fears herself to be. A locked conference room during a troubleshooting meeting can
suddenly feel intolerable, not only because of the enclosed space itself but because it stirs the
old conviction that she is once again trapped in something meant for her. Even family outings that
should be relaxing have become difficult. If her husband suggests a walk on a wooded trail or a
picnic in a park, Amina notices the smell of soil and greenery almost immediately, and with it comes
the sense that she is back near the shed, back in a place where bad things happen because of who she
is.

Once her body has gone on alert, it seems to deepen the same harsh conclusions about herself. She
notices how quickly she startles, how hard it is to focus, how often she reacts before she can judge
whether anything is actually wrong, and she reads these responses as further proof that there is
something wrong with her as a person. In her mind, a normal person would be able to sit in a room
with the door closed, discuss difficult history, or walk through a natural setting without coming
apart inside. Each false alarm becomes evidence. The more keyed up she feels, the more defective she
believes herself to be, and that belief makes the next surge of tension easier to ignite.

Her sense of danger also narrows her world in concrete ways. In the IT department, she has started
avoiding the small back server room unless someone else is with her, even though part of her job is
to troubleshoot equipment there. If a manager wants to shut the door to talk through a problem, she
subtly shifts to keep it cracked open or finds an excuse to step out. During team meetings, if
conversation turns personal and someone asks about her childhood or family, her mind goes blank and
her body tightens so fast that she can barely track what is being said next. Outdoors, she scans
automatically, her muscles set and her attention pulled toward escape routes, until the trees and
enclosed paths begin to feel charged. By the time she reacts, she is often already convinced that
the place itself is unsafe.

What finally brought Amina to seek help was not one dramatic episode but the accumulation of these
moments. Her husband had begun to notice how often she refused weekend plans, and at work she was
worried that her concentration was slipping enough for others to see it. More unsettling to her was
the feeling that her world was organizing itself around tension, startle, and the need to stay away
from anything that might set her off. She came in frightened that if she keeps living this way, she
will only confirm what she has long believed about herself. At the same time, she is quietly hoping
for the possibility that talking about what happened might not always feel like being trapped inside
it again.
