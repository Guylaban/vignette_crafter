Minh Tran was 33 when he came to treatment, a married physical therapist who had built much of his
identity around the body’s capacity to heal. That sense of confidence fractured eight months earlier
in a highway pileup on his commute home. Traffic had slowed suddenly in heavy rain, and he
remembered the violent crack of metal behind him before his own car was thrown forward. What stayed
with him most vividly was the sound: brakes screaming, repeated impacts, glass breaking somewhere
close to his ear. For a few seconds he was trapped by the seat belt, disoriented and certain another
car was about to hit him. He remembered trying to move and feeling a sharp surge of pain through his
torso and shoulder, with a sickening thought that his body had failed him at the exact moment he
needed it to respond.

In the weeks after, Minh returned to work at the rehabilitation clinic, but the trauma stayed close.
Loud noises in the gym—a dropped weight, a metal stool scraping, a door slamming—hit him before he
could think, and his whole body jerked. On mornings after nightmares, he woke with the residue still
clinging to him, as if he were already braced for impact before his feet touched the floor. Unwanted
thoughts of the crash arrived while he was documenting notes or guiding a patient through transfers.
In those moments he became acutely aware of danger in the room, noticing the metal edge of exercise
equipment, scissors on a counter, even a cane positioned awkwardly against a wall, while his
concentration narrowed and he lost track of what a patient had just said. The more jumpy he felt,
the more easily a sudden sound or a half-remembered image seemed to pull the accident back into the
present.

Those same moments also revived what Minh had come to believe about himself since the crash. When he
woke from a nightmare with his chest tight and shoulder aching, or when an intrusive image struck
after a loud clang, he took it as proof that the accident had left lasting damage. He had begun to
think his body was unreliable now, no longer something he could trust to respond correctly under
pressure. As a physical therapist, this belief carried particular humiliation. He found himself
privately wondering how he could reassure injured patients when he no longer believed in his own
physical safety. What frightened him most was not only that harm had happened once, but that you
could never really know who or what would hurt you next.

These beliefs began shaping his days. Because he felt physically vulnerable and mistrusted his own
body, Minh started cleaning and organizing compulsively at home and in his treatment space,
straightening resistance bands, wiping counters repeatedly, aligning supplies with unusual
precision. He described it less as preference than as a way to feel some control over a world that
no longer felt predictable. For the same reason, he mentally rehearsed worst-case scenarios: a
patient falling during gait training, someone aggressive entering the clinic, another crash at an
intersection. Before entering restaurants or even the waiting room, he scanned for exits and for
objects that could be used as weapons if something went wrong. He knew this sounded excessive, but
to him preparedness felt safer than being caught off guard again.

Over time, those habits seemed to deepen the problem. Constantly checking the environment kept his
attention fixed on possible threat, so that he noticed danger cues everywhere and startled even more
readily. Rehearsing catastrophic possibilities made it harder to focus on the person in front of
him; his mind was often split between the present and some imagined emergency. At work, patients had
begun to ask him to repeat instructions because he lost his train of thought. His wife noticed that
at home he rarely relaxed through a movie or meal, his eyes moving to the kitchen knives, the
balcony door, the sound of a car outside. Each time he caught himself reacting this way, it seemed
to confirm his fear that his body and mind had been permanently altered by the accident.

That confirmation loop had become especially painful in the month before intake. After a barbell
crashed to the floor in the clinic, Minh startled so hard he knocked a clipboard from his hands, and
for the rest of the day he was flooded with images of twisted cars and the certainty that he would
not be able to protect himself if something happened again. Since then he had been dreading sleep
because of the nightmares, and dreading work because any loud noise could set the whole sequence
off. He came to therapy at his wife’s urging, ashamed that he was cleaning, scanning, and mentally
preparing almost constantly, but more ashamed of how plausible it all still felt. What he wanted
most was to trust his body again; what he feared was that the accident had taught him something
final and true—that safety is fragile, harm can come from anywhere, and he will never stop bracing
for it.
