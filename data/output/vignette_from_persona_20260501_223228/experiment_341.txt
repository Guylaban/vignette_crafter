David Mercer, a 51-year-old school principal, traced the beginning of his difficulties to the months
when a sudden life-threatening illness took over his life. He had been used to moving briskly
through hallways, settling parent complaints, stepping into classrooms without hesitation. The
illness interrupted that identity with a series of hospital admissions, repeated examinations, and
the sharp, clean smell of antiseptic that seemed to cling to everything. He remembered lying rigid
during procedures, his body fixed in a position he could not easily shift, waiting for staff to tell
him what would happen next. What remained most vivid was not a clear, orderly narrative of the
illness but a sensory imprint: antiseptic in the air, the medicinal scent of alcohol on skin and
gloves, and the dread that accompanied being examined while wondering whether his body was failing
him.

In the months after he recovered medically, those sensory cues began intruding into ordinary life.
Passing a custodian using cleaning products in the school nurse’s office, catching the smell of
alcohol-based sanitizer in the front office, or needing even a routine medical examination could
abruptly pull him into the emotional force of the illness. Often there was no full picture in his
mind, only a surge of fear and helplessness that seemed to arrive all at once. At other times, if he
tried deliberately to recall what had happened in the hospital, he found the memory oddly
inaccessible, as though the event would not come when summoned but only when triggered. In those
moments his body would tense and settle into the same constrained posture he had held during
procedures, as if he were back on the examination table.

These episodes shook David’s sense of who he was. Each time the smell of antiseptic or alcohol hit
him and he felt himself freeze, he took it as proof that his body was still unreliable and that he
could not count on himself to stay steady. He had come to believe that the person he had become was
unrecognizable to him: not the calm administrator others saw, but someone internally braced for
collapse. The fact that the memories came as bodily states and raw emotion rather than a coherent
story only deepened that conclusion. If he could not even call up the experience deliberately, but
was instead ambushed by it, that seemed to him further evidence that he was no longer in command of
himself.

Because he no longer trusted his own reactions, David began trying to manage every interaction in
advance. Before staff meetings, parent conferences, or even brief conversations with teachers, he
silently rehearsed what he planned to say, as if careful scripting might prevent him from being
caught off guard by a wavering voice or visible distress. He also started avoiding medical settings
altogether, putting off examinations and taking long routes to avoid the hospital campus near the
school district office. When possible, he delegated tasks that might bring him near the school
nurse’s suite if the smell of sanitizer was strong. Outside of work, he withdrew almost completely,
ignoring calls from friends and family and keeping his evenings empty. In his mind, this retreat
made sense: if he kept life tightly controlled and stayed away from reminders, he might avoid the
sickening bodily pull of the memories and the humiliation of feeling unlike himself.

Instead, the habits narrowed his world and seemed to confirm his worst beliefs. The more he avoided
hospitals, ambulances, and examinations, the more charged medical cues became; even a routine
checkup felt intolerable because he had had so little contact with anything that resembled care. The
more he isolated, the less he had any experience of himself as capable or recognizable in
relationship with other people. Rehearsing every conversation before speaking gave brief relief, but
it also kept alive the idea that he could not rely on himself spontaneously. He noticed that on days
when he had spent extra time mentally scripting staff interactions, he was even more alert to the
smell of hand sanitizer in the office or disinfectant in the hall, as if constant preparation left
him scanning for the next sign that something was wrong.

At work this became increasingly visible. David still arrived early and kept his tie straight, but
he avoided impromptu visits to classrooms and asked his assistant principal to handle a student
injury because the mention of an ambulance made his chest tighten. If he caught the smell of alcohol
wipes outside the nurse’s office, he would feel a rush of dread before he could place why, then
spend the rest of the day inwardly preoccupied, trying and failing to put the illness into words.
Those moments left him certain that his body could betray him without warning. He found himself
thinking that a man who needed to script every exchange and cut himself off from the people who knew
him best was not the man he had been.

He finally sought help after canceling yet another medical appointment and realizing he was more
frightened of a routine examination than he had ever been of difficult disciplinary hearings or
public conflict at school. What alarmed him most was not only the fear itself but the sense that his
life was being organized around escaping smells, procedures, and the internal jolts they set off. He
said he wanted to know whether he could become someone recognizable to himself again, and whether it
might be possible to face his own body without feeling that it was an enemy waiting to turn on him.
