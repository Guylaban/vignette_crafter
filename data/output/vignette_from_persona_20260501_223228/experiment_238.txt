Markus Weber is a 49-year-old city council member who came to treatment after months of telling his
wife that he was “fine” and then startling so violently in bed when she reached for his shoulder
that she began sleeping more cautiously beside him. He had served in combat years earlier, and when
he speaks about the event he still does so in fragments. He remembers being in a convoy and then
being on the ground after an explosion, with sound dropping in and out and pieces of the scene
coming back without order: dust, shouting, a body too close to him, the sense that he was about to
die. What remains most vivid is the terror. When he tries to explain the sequence clearly, he loses
the thread, stops mid-sentence, and looks as if he is back there rather than describing it from a
distance. The gaps in his memory have taken on meaning for him. Because he can only recall parts of
what happened and cannot form a complete account, he has come to believe that the world is
fundamentally dangerous and that his own body cannot be trusted, as if it knows he is under threat
before his mind does.

That belief has shaped the way he lives now. Markus is certain that safety is always provisional,
something people say to comfort themselves but not something that can be relied on. Even in familiar
settings, including council chambers and public meetings he has attended for years, he feels he has
to stay on guard. He scans the room automatically, tracks movement in the doorway, and notices who
is behind him. When colleagues or constituents reach out to greet him with a hand on the arm or
shoulder, he reacts with immediate alarm, his whole body jerking before he can think. The reaction
itself frightens him. He has come to see these bodily surges as further proof that his body is
working against him, still acting as though the danger is present, and each episode leaves him more
convinced that the world really is as unsafe as he feels it to be.

The memories and the threat responses feed each other. At night, when he lies awake unable to settle
into sleep, bits of the combat scene intrude more forcefully: the missing pieces remain missing, but
the terror arrives intact. On those nights he can feel his body bracing in the dark, listening for
sounds in the house, unable to accept his wife’s reassurance that they are safe at home. And after
days when he has been especially keyed up—after a crowded council session, or an unexpected touch in
a hallway—the memory becomes even less coherent when he tries to think about it, more like shards
than a story, with the same original fear rushing back and cutting off his ability to speak.

Over time Markus has developed ways of managing that he experiences as necessary. When images or
bodily fear begin to rise, he turns on loud television or music to drown out his own thoughts. He
also monitors his mind closely, watching for any sign that he might be “going crazy,” as he puts it,
especially when he feels himself losing the thread while remembering. He avoids physical contact
whenever he can, sidestepping embraces at public events and keeping a careful distance even from
people he likes. In the moment these habits give him something to do, but they also keep him from
staying with the memory long enough for it to become clearer. The event remains patchy and
unspeakable, and because it remains that way, it continues to return charged with terror rather than
as something finished and past.

Those same strategies have deepened his sense that something is wrong with him. The more he
orchestrates his day around preventing contact, controlling noise, and checking his own mind for
signs of collapse, the more he experiences himself as a man whose body is unpredictable and whose
surroundings require constant vigilance. In his work this has become increasingly visible. He now
prefers the edge seat in meetings, keeps people out of his personal space during constituent events,
and leaves some receptions early because the press of bodies feels intolerable. At home, his wife
has experienced him as distant and tense, especially because even ordinary touch can set off an
alarmed reaction he cannot hide.

What finally brought him in was not a single flashback or a single bad night, but the recognition
that his life had narrowed around staying prepared. He was exhausted from poor sleep, ashamed of
recoiling from touch, and increasingly unable to give a straightforward account of the combat event
without feeling overwhelmed by the same terror. What frightens him most is the idea that this
constant watchfulness means he will never really feel safe again; what he hopes, more quietly, is
that he might learn how to be at home in his body and with his wife without bracing for danger.
