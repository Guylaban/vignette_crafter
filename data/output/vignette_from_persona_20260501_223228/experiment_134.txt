Nadia Haddad is a 52-year-old project manager who came to treatment months after surviving a
terrorist bombing in a downtown transit corridor. She had been walking back from a client meeting
when the first blast threw glass and dust across the street. What she remembers most clearly is the
confusion of people running in opposite directions, the sight of armed police flooding the area
within minutes, and the low, chopping noise of helicopters circling overhead as she crouched behind
a concrete barrier, certain there might be another explosion. She saw an injured man collapse near a
bus stop and, for a few seconds that have stayed sharply fixed in her mind, she could not tell
whether she should help him, run, or make herself invisible. By the time she got home to her husband
that night, she was shaken but trying to present herself as practical and composed, the same
competent woman who had always managed crises at work and in the family.

In the weeks that followed, Nadia found that certain moments in ordinary life seemed to pull her
straight back into that afternoon. A helicopter passing low over the city would stop her in place,
her body going tight before she had even fully registered the sound. The presence of police,
especially when she encountered officers posted outside office buildings or directing traffic near
her train stop, made her feel exposed and unsteady. News footage or even brief glimpses of violence
on a screen could leave her staring, cold and ashamed at how quickly she was overtaken. Each time
this happened, Nadia came away with the same painful conclusion: that something essential in her had
been altered. She had come to believe she used to be a happy person and now was simply miserable,
and these reactions seemed to prove it to her over and over again. What frightened her was not only
the surge of fear itself, but the conviction that she no longer recognized the woman having it.

That sense of estrangement grew most noticeably in her work life. As a project manager, she spends
her days coordinating timelines, leading meetings, and moving between a glass-fronted office
building and client sites in the city. She used to take pride in being calm under pressure and easy
with people. Now, when police officers were visible near the building entrance after a security
alert or public event, she would pause outside, collecting herself and wondering whether everyone
passing by could see the change in her face. On days when helicopters hovered over nearby
construction or traffic incidents, she would lose her train of thought in meetings and retreat into
silence. These moments did not just unsettle her; they shaped how she understood herself. She became
convinced that others could see what had happened to her just by looking at her, as though the
bombing had marked her publicly. In her mind, a startled glance from a coworker or a routine “are
you okay?” carried the sting of confirmation.

At home, Nadia tried to explain to her husband that she felt as though the event had taken more than
her sense of safety. She said she did not know herself anymore. Before the attack, she had seen
herself as warm, capable, and optimistic, someone who could absorb difficulty without becoming
defined by it. Now a police siren in the distance or footage of an attack replayed online could turn
the whole day gray. When that happened, she would study her own reactions with bitterness, telling
herself that a happy person would not live like this and that a stronger person would have put it
behind her. The more often she was caught off guard by helicopters overhead, by visible law
enforcement, or by reminders of violence, the more fixed these judgments became. The triggers were
no longer just reminders of danger; to Nadia they had become evidence that her old self was gone.

By the time she sought help, the turning point had come at work. A helicopter had been circling
during a meeting with senior leadership, and she felt her attention drain from the discussion until
she could think only of that day downtown, the crush of noise, the armed officers, the sense that
catastrophe was unfolding in public view. Afterward she went to the restroom and looked at herself
in the mirror with the familiar thought that everyone must be able to see it on her. She later told
the clinician that what she fears most is not only being frightened again, but living the rest of
her life as this diminished version of herself—miserable, altered, and visible in her suffering.
What she hopes for, though she says it cautiously, is to feel like Nadia again rather than a woman
permanently organized around what happened that day.
