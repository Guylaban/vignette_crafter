Linh Tran was 54 when she came to treatment, a divorced Vietnamese American woman who had spent most
of her adult life working as an electrician, proud of being steady-handed and practical in job sites
where she was often the only woman. The event she returned to in the first sessions was not an
accident at work but a life-threatening illness that had unfolded in a hospital room two years
earlier. She described waking one night unable to draw a full breath, hearing the harsh, laboring
sound of her own breathing fill the room while staff moved quickly around her bed. What stayed with
her most vividly was the sterile bite of antiseptic in the air and the animal panic of listening to
herself fight for oxygen, certain for several minutes that she was about to die. Even now, when she
talked about it, she seemed to brace in her chair as if her body were remembering before she had
finished the sentence.

In the months after she was discharged, those sensory details began to appear in ordinary life with
an intensity that surprised her. The smell of disinfectant in a clinic hallway, a whiff of
antiseptic cleaner in a grocery store restroom, or hearing someone nearby breathe heavily could stop
her cold. At work, if she was in a cramped utility room with a coworker exerting himself and
breathing hard, her chest would tighten and her body would go rigid before she could tell herself
where she really was. Around the anniversary of the hospitalization, she became especially watchful
and short-tempered, already tense from the moment she woke. She said her body seemed to “jump first
and ask questions later,” with pounding heart, tight muscles, and a sense of danger arriving ahead
of thought.

Because those reminders hit so fast and so physically, Linh had developed ways of getting through
them that made sense to her in the moment. When a trigger caught her, she would mentally step away
from it and tell herself it had happened to someone else, as if the woman in the hospital bed were
not really her. She also spent long stretches thinking about how different her life would have been
if the illness had never happened—how she might still feel solid, how she would not scan her body
this way, how she could walk into a medical building without dread. And she began avoiding medical
cues wherever possible: postponing routine physical exams, taking long detours if an ambulance was
parked outside a building, refusing to accompany a friend to the hospital after surgery because she
knew the smell alone would undo her.

Those strategies, however, seemed to keep the threat close. The more Linh distanced herself from the
memory by treating it as if it belonged to someone else, the more abruptly reminders seemed to break
through as raw sensations rather than something placed in the past. Her “what if” thinking also left
her dwelling on the illness as the dividing line in her life, which kept the anniversary looming
larger each year. Avoiding hospitals and examinations narrowed her world in concrete ways: she
ignored follow-up appointments, and when a contractor’s office was temporarily relocated near a
medical complex, she found herself dreading the workday before it had even begun. She was
increasingly tense all over, shoulders drawn up, jaw set, as if bracing against another emergency.

That constant bodily alertness pushed her further into the same habits. Since her body reacted
before her mind could catch up, she trusted avoidance more and more, telling herself it was safer
not to give reminders a chance. If she heard heavy breathing on a ladder behind her, she would
abruptly leave the area or shut down emotionally until the feeling passed. On bad days she slipped
so quickly into acting as though the whole hospitalization had happened to some other woman that she
frightened herself afterward with how unreal everything had felt. She could see the cost in her work
and daily life: she had become less willing to take jobs in schools or public buildings where a
clinic might be nearby, and she kept conversations shallow when friends asked about her health
because she did not want to feel herself leave the room again.

By the time she sought help, the approach of the next anniversary had made the whole pattern feel
tighter and harder to escape. She had recently missed another physical examination after smelling
antiseptic in the waiting area parking garage and turning her truck around before she got out. What
frightened her most was not just the reminders themselves but how instantly her body still behaved
as if death were imminent, and how much of her life was now organized around not setting that off.
She said she wanted help because she was tired of living as though a hospital room were waiting
behind every smell and sound, and because some part of her still hoped she could be in her own life
again instead of watching it happen from a distance.
