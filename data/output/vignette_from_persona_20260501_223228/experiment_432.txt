captivity during which he was tortured. In the first sessions he did not offer a long, orderly
account, but the fragments were stark and consistent: being trapped in a confined room, held down in
painful positions, and hurt repeatedly until the memory of it lived as much in his body as in words.
What stayed with him most sharply was not only the fear, but the humiliation of being completely at
someone else’s mercy. Certain sounds from that time seemed to have fused with the experience. A baby
crying could cut through him with shocking force because the high, distressed pitch seemed to pull
him straight back into that room. Bus rides were almost as bad; being enclosed with strangers,
unable to control who came near him or when he could get off, carried the same trapped, exposed
feeling. Even moments of emotional openness with his wife could catch him off guard, because feeling
vulnerable in any form had come to seem dangerously close to the helplessness he had endured.

In the months afterward, Daniel found that these moments did not just remind him of what had
happened in a vague way. When a baby cried in a grocery store or on the dock, the memory hit with a
physical immediacy that frightened and infuriated him. He would feel his body tense into the same
position he had been forced into during the torture, as if his muscles were reenacting it before he
had time to think. The pain returned in the same places where he had been injured, and with it came
a surge of intense rage that felt hotter than fear. On buses he sometimes had to grip the seat
because it seemed, for a few seconds, that he was no longer in public transport at all but back
inside the event, his body already bracing for what came next.

Those memories changed how he moved through the world. Because recall came with such vivid bodily
pain and a sense of being pinned again, Daniel began treating ordinary spaces as if danger might
erupt in them at any moment. He sat with his back against the wall in diners, waiting rooms, and the
galley on the boat, scanning the room so no one could approach from behind. At home he listened for
small noises at night, alert for any sound that might mean someone was entering. He noticed tools,
knives, hooks, and any heavy object that could be used as a weapon before he noticed much else. He
understood that other people did not live like this, but to him it felt like the only sensible
response; once his body could be thrown back into the torture so completely, he was certain he could
not afford to relax.

off by a crewmate’s raised voice or by hearing a baby cry from a nearby marina. Even bus riding
became harder over time because he boarded already braced, already searching for exits and for what
could be used against him, and the sense of exposure made every jolt or unexpected sound feel
loaded. The more he listened at home for someone entering, the more a harmless creak in the house
could leave him rigid, body pulled back into that old position before he fully knew why.

His vigilance also narrowed his life in practical ways. He avoided taking the bus whenever he could,
even when it made getting to the harbor harder. On the boat he chose spots where he could see the
whole deck, and crewmates had begun to notice that he was edgy if someone came up behind him
unexpectedly. His wife saw a different version of the same pattern at home. If a conversation became
emotionally close and he felt exposed, he might shut down or pull away, not because he did not want
her near but because vulnerability itself had started to feel like a trigger. Afterward he would
often be more on edge in the house, checking locks again, listening for sounds, and sleeping in a
position that let him face the door.

By the time he came for help, Daniel was exhausted by living as if he were still about to be
overpowered. He was angry at how quickly a baby’s cry, a bus, or even the feeling of opening up to
his wife could throw him into pain and rage. What frightened him most was how instantly his body
seemed to obey the past, and how completely that made the present feel unsafe. He said he wanted to
be able to work, ride where he needed to ride, and sit in his own home without listening for an
intruder, but underneath that was a more private hope: that he might stop feeling as though
helplessness was always one moment away.
