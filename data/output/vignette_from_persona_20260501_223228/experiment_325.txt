had a claim on her. The worst moments were not dramatic in a cinematic sense; that was part of what

for whether he had found her again.

What she has struggled to explain in the months since is that the memory does not sit in the past in
an ordinary way. When she talks about those weeks, Linh often pauses and says it sounds exaggerated
even to her, “like something that happened to somebody else,” because the extremity of living under
constant watch still feels unreal. Yet when it comes back, it does not return as a distant
narrative. She feels as though she is back inside it immediately, with the same surge of terror she

moments in the present feel charged with danger. On the trading floor, a burst of shouting or a
slammed door can pull her straight into that older fear before she has even oriented herself.

race to where she can exit. Seeing a cheerful family in a restaurant window or in the lobby of her
building can unsettle her in a way she finds hard to justify, because it throws her against a
painful sense of distance from normal life; the contrast makes the stalking feel both bizarre and
vividly present, as if everyone else belongs to a safer world she somehow lost access to.

Because the memory arrives with such immediacy, Linh has become increasingly preoccupied with

to step into a stairwell to steady herself after a sudden noise sends her heart racing. At home she
is more likely to decline social plans if they involve crowded restaurants or drinking. Her partner
has tried to reassure her, but Linh has come to dread the look of concern on his face, as though
even that confirms how much the stalking still governs her.

sidewalk outside a bar, a weekend street full of families—without feeling hijacked by terror. What
she hoped for, tentatively, was not to erase what happened but to have it become unmistakably past.
