unflappable. She was a senior lawyer in a downtown firm, still working full time, known for her
precision and composure in negotiations. The assault happened eight months before she came to

way he looked at her, appraising and entitled, as if her age, her profession, and her clear
resistance meant nothing. She remembered hearing the sharp scrape of his shoes and the metallic slam

collapse before she could get away.

In the weeks afterward, those details became painfully alive in ordinary moments. In the office
garage, a sudden echoing footstep or the clang of the elevator door could send a rush of fear
through her before she had time to think. If she noticed a man’s eyes lingering on her body in a
courthouse hallway or in the lobby outside her office, she felt the same wave of horror and shame
she had felt during the assault. Even her own heart racing could become a cue; once she hurried up
the stairs to a deposition, felt the pounding in her chest, and was abruptly overtaken by the
certainty that something terrible was about to happen. Sounds, sexualized attention, and the
sensation inside her own body all came to mean danger, and in those moments she was flooded with
intense fear, anger, and disgust. At night this was worse. If her husband was late coming home from
the grocery store or took longer than expected walking the dog, her mind leapt immediately to
catastrophe, and the dark seemed to confirm that safety had gone.

Linh had come to believe that the assault revealed something enduring and terrible about the world.
She was certain now that the world was dangerous in a way she had once underestimated, and she
increasingly felt she had to stay on guard all the time. More disturbing to her was the conviction
that she could no longer trust her own body. During the attack, her racing heart and bodily panic
had seemed to betray her, and afterward any pounding in her chest felt like proof that her body
would again fail to protect her. Because she saw her body as unreliable and the world as
fundamentally unsafe, the same cues became even more charged: a slammed car door in the parking
garage, a stranger’s appraising glance, the thud in her chest after climbing stairs. These were no
longer just reminders of what had happened; to Linh they were signs that danger was present now.

That belief changed how she moved through her days. She started scanning constantly in courtrooms,
elevators, and parking lots, noticing every sound behind her and every look from a man across the
room. At the firm she began asking an associate to walk her to her car, though she framed it as
convenience. She avoided client dinners where alcohol and flirtation sometimes blurred boundaries,
and she timed her departures so she would not have to cross the garage after dark. The more she
watched for threat, the more quickly she detected it. A passing noise at work would make her body
tense; the tension and pounding in her chest would catch her attention immediately; and that
sensation itself would become another warning that she was not safe. When her husband lingered in
another room at night or took longer than expected to answer his phone, she felt herself spiraling
into dread, listening for sounds in the house and unable to settle.

ran late. At home she insisted on checking locks repeatedly after dark and felt ashamed of how
dependent she had become on her husband’s presence to feel even somewhat secure. What frightened her

afterward sat in her car convinced that neither her experience nor her body could be trusted
