heavily, and some of Mai’s clearest memories are of waking in total darkness to the sound of his
footsteps in the hallway, lying rigid in bed and trying to judge from the creak of the floorboards
whether he was coming to her room. On other occasions he forced her into the car late at night,
berating and hitting her while he drove, trapping her beside him in the dark with no way to get out.
As a girl she learned to study men’s movements, where the door was, how quickly she could run, what
corner of a room gave her the best chance of avoiding being cornered. Even now, when she describes
those years, what stands out is not only the fear but the certainty she developed that nowhere was
really safe and that she had been marked in some way that made her vulnerable.

She is now 23 and works as a ship captain, a role she fought hard to earn and one that fits her
self-image as disciplined and self-reliant. But life at sea and around docks regularly puts her in
the very situations that unsettle her most. Night watches are especially difficult. If she wakes in
her cabin and the room is dark, her body is immediately tense and ready, before she has fully
oriented herself; she listens for movement, scans the room, and feels a rush of fear and anger that
can take a long time to settle. Being alone with male crew members in enclosed spaces has a similar
effect. She finds herself mapping exits before she can focus on the conversation, gauging whether
she could get past them if she needed to. When she has to ride in a vehicle with other people after
docking, especially if a man is driving, she becomes rigid and hyperaware of every turn and stop,
with a powerful urge to get out.

Those moments do more than frighten her. They confirm what she has come to believe about the world
and about herself. When she is alone with a man and feels herself bracing, she reads that reaction
as evidence that the world is dangerous and that she is still the sort of person bad things happen
to. Waking in the dark and feeling immediate dread leaves her ashamed as well as afraid; to Mai, the
speed and force of her reaction mean there is something fundamentally wrong with her, something
defective that other people do not carry. She keeps much of this private. On the ship she maintains
authority competently, but inwardly she feels set apart from others, certain that if they saw how
much effort it takes her to feel basically safe, they would view her differently.

Over time, those beliefs have sharpened the power of the triggers. Because she has come to expect
danger in the world, darkness never feels neutral; a dark cabin is not simply a room without light
but the beginning of something threatening. Because she sees herself as different and somehow
damaged, being alone with men carries an added layer of humiliation and vigilance, as though she is
especially exposed. Even routine transport with coworkers becomes hard to dismiss as ordinary. She
studies who will be in the vehicle, where she will sit, and whether there is a door she can open
quickly, as if her old conclusions are waiting to be proven right.

The reverse is true as well: the more constantly on alert she feels, the more easily these
situations seize her attention. When her body is already keyed up, a male presence in a hallway, the
moment of waking into darkness, or the prospect of riding with others can pull her into full
readiness almost instantly. She notices exits before she notices people’s words. She cannot relax
into an environment until she has located how she would leave it. This has begun to shape her work.
She avoids lingering alone with male crew, delegates some nighttime tasks when she can do so without
comment, and dreads post-docking rides enough that she sometimes arranges separate transportation.
The cost has been increasing isolation. She remains single and tells herself she prefers it, but
privately she feels cut off, as if closeness would only expose how much fear she carries.

Mai sought help after a recent night watch when she startled awake in her dark cabin, heart
pounding, and could not shake the conviction that someone was about to come through the door. The
next day, trapped in a van with several crew members, including two men, she spent the entire ride
planning how to get out at the next stoplight. What frightened her most was not only the fear
itself, but the sense that her life was narrowing around it. She wants relief from living as though
danger is always one step away, and she is afraid that if nothing changes, she will never feel like
she belongs anywhere, even in the career she has built for herself.
