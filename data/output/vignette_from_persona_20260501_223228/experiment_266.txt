Hans Becker is a 76-year-old widowed man who came for treatment after months of insisting to himself
that he ought to be able to manage on his own. He works part-time as a cashier in a neighborhood
grocery store, a routine he took up after his wife died because he wanted structure and some contact
with people. He had spent part of his youth as a civilian in a war zone in Europe, and although he
had carried those memories quietly for decades, they returned with force in later life. What stays
with him are not long coherent stories but stark visual fragments: a street blown open, shattered
glass glittering in the air, the fixed look on a stranger’s face, the inside of a vehicle he had
been trying to reach as shelling began nearby. At times he can describe these scenes in a flat,
distant way, almost as if they happened to someone else. At other times an image seizes him so
completely that he feels he is there again in the danger, not remembering but reliving.

Over the years Hans had come to believe that the world is fundamentally dangerous and that whatever
trust he once had in other people, life, and God had worn away in that period. As these beliefs
hardened, the old memories seemed to return with even more force. When he thought, as he often did,
about why this had happened to him and what it had meant, he never arrived at comfort; instead he
found himself staring again at those frozen pictures, convinced they proved what he already feared
about the world. The memories in turn deepened the same conclusions. When a vivid image broke
through, he was left certain afterward that nowhere is really safe and that something essential in
him had been permanently damaged.

He speaks painfully about no longer being capable of the things that once defined him. Driving is
the clearest example. He used to take pride in handling himself, in going where he pleased without
relying on anyone. Now even riding in a car feels intolerable. The images tied to vehicles and
sudden attack are so immediate that he avoids driving altogether and refuses lifts unless there is
no other option. He has begun arranging his life around buses, short walks, and rides from a
neighbor only when absolutely necessary. That restriction has made him feel older and diminished,
and each new workaround seems to confirm his belief that he is not the man he used to be.

At the grocery store, these problems are woven into ordinary moments. Standing at his register, Hans
watches customers closely, scanning faces, hands, tone of voice, any sign that someone might turn
aggressive. If a man comes in tense or loud, Hans feels his body brace before he has consciously
thought anything. He keeps himself on guard all day and has trouble settling even at night, lying
awake because some part of him feels he must remain alert. When his guard is this high, the war
memories come more easily; a customer’s expression, the slam of a cart, or the glimpse of a car
through the front windows can pull up a visual flash so sharply that for a second he is not in the
store but back in danger. And the more he lives in this state of watchfulness, the more convinced he
becomes that his vigilance is justified, that people cannot be trusted and that the world will hurt
you if you stop looking.

In response, Hans has narrowed his life further. He avoids cars, but he also pushes away positive
feelings when they arise, because relief or pleasure feels unsafe, like letting his guard down. On
better days he distrusts the lightness almost immediately. He also spends long stretches turning the
trauma over in his mind, trying to understand why it happened and why it has followed him into old
age. None of this brings peace. Pushing away good moments leaves him more fixed on danger, and all
the searching for meaning seems only to circle him back to the same images and the same bleak
conclusions. The less room he allows himself for ordinary ease, the more jumpy he becomes; the more
he stays on edge, the more he feels the world has proven him right.

By the time he sought help, Hans was exhausted. Sleep had become fragmented by the felt need to stay
alert, work was harder because he could not stop monitoring customers, and his dependence on others
for transportation humiliated him. What finally brought him in was the fear that his life was
shrinking to the size of his precautions. He said he was afraid that if this continued, he would
spend whatever years he had left trapped between old pictures and constant watchfulness. What he
hopes for, cautiously, is not happiness exactly, but the possibility of feeling safe enough to rest
and to believe that he is still capable of living as himself.
