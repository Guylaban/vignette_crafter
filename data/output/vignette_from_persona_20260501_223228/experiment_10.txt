states of mind she associates with being unsafe. She keeps herself mentally occupied at all
times—podcasts in the truck, constant planning of the next repair, repetitive checking of
inventory—because if there is a quiet moment, the fragments come closer. She also tries hard not to
think about the abuse at all. Yet the effort to push it away seems to make the memory even less
integrated; what remains are isolated flashes and a stronger sense that the memory is alien to her.
By the end of the day, being underfed and mentally strained leaves her more jittery, less focused,
and more physically reactive to sudden movement.

Those coping habits have begun to shape what she can tolerate. She times jobs to avoid being around
clients for long, rushes through tasks when she feels watched, and sometimes chooses cramped solo
work over better-paying assignments with teams. The more she structures her day around staying busy,
not eating, and forcing thoughts down, the more easily shouting or anger catches her off guard, and
the more quickly she is launched back into that tense, vigilant state. Her husband has noticed that
she comes home wired and distant, eating very little and unable to settle even in their own house if
voices rise on television or a neighbor argues outside.

Mele sought help after snapping at a coworker who approached from the side while she was
concentrating on a pipe repair; she recoiled so sharply that she hit her head and then could not
focus for the rest of the job. What frightened her most was how immediate it felt, as if her body
had decided she was a child again before she knew what was happening. She is afraid that if this
keeps tightening, she will lose control at work or stop being able to do the job she is proud of. At
the same time, she wants to understand why the memories come in pieces and hopes she might learn how
to be in a room with other people’s anger without feeling that danger is already there.
