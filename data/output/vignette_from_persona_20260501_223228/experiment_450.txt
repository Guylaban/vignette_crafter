Amina is a 25-year-old surgical resident who came for treatment after months of telling herself that
what happened overseas should have stayed there. During her military deployment, she had been part
of a forward surgical team working close to active combat. The moment that returns most forcefully
came during a mass-casualty response when rotor wash hammered the canvas above the aid station and
the thudding chop of a helicopter seemed to sit inside her chest. She remembers dropping to the
ground beside a wounded service member, her gloves and uniform pressed into a rough, gritty surface
she can still feel on her palms and knees, and then a blast so loud it seemed to split the scene
open. What stands out is not a tidy sequence but a sensory assault: the aircraft overhead, the bang,
the feel of that texture under her hands, and a certainty that she was about to die. Even now, when
she tries to describe it in session, she stops and shakes her head. She says she cannot get it into
words properly; the memory arrives as terror and sensation before language.

In the months after returning to the United States, she noticed that the memory did not stay in the
past. A helicopter passing over the hospital helipad, a sudden bang from equipment being dropped in
an operating room, even brushing against a particular rough fabric or surface could bring the whole
scene back without warning. It was not like ordinary remembering. For Amina, those moments felt as
if she were back in the field hospital with the same panic and bodily alarm she had felt then, as
though the event were happening again rather than being recalled. Because the memory itself remained
largely sensory and hard to narrate, these sounds and textures seemed to reach straight into it; she
often felt ambushed by the full force of the experience before she had even registered what had set
it off.

Working as a surgeon has made these reactions impossible to keep at the edges of her life. Entering
the hospital each day, she now automatically identifies exits, notes where she could move if
something happened, and chooses seats in conference rooms with her back to the wall so she can see
everyone coming and going. In the operating room lounge, if a helicopter can be heard approaching
the helipad, she feels her body tighten and her eyes begin sweeping the space for danger before she
is even fully aware of doing it. Sudden bangs from metal trays or equipment make her flinch and
orient immediately toward the source. She tells herself these habits are necessary, the only way to
stay ahead of whatever might happen, and they have become especially fixed in places where she
cannot control the noise around her.

Over time, though, the constant preparation has made the world feel more perilous rather than less.
Because she is always scanning rooms, checking corners, and mentally rehearsing how she would get
out, she notices every aircraft sound and every sharp noise. On days when she is especially focused
on where the exits are and who is behind her, the chop of helicopter blades from the roof seems
louder, more distinct, impossible to ignore. Her attention catches on the very cues she dreads.
Sitting rigidly with her back against the wall in the cafeteria, she tracks movements and sounds
until a dropped pan or the scrape of a rough chair leg can jolt her into the edge of the old memory.
The strategies she relies on to feel safe end up keeping those triggers in the foreground of her
day.

This has gradually narrowed her life at work and outside it. She arrives early to unfamiliar rooms
so she can choose where to sit. She avoids relaxing in staff spaces unless she can position herself
to watch the door. Colleagues have noticed that she startles at bangs and that her eyes move
constantly around the room during meetings, and she has become more socially withdrawn rather than
explain herself. She is single and lives alone, and at home she still catches herself mapping exits
in restaurants or public places before she can settle. What frightens her most is not only the
memory itself but the sense that it can be summoned instantly by a sound, a texture, a split second
she cannot predict.

She sought help after freezing briefly in the hospital when a helicopter descended as a metal
instrument bin clattered nearby, leaving her shaken and ashamed before a case. She is afraid that if
this continues, she will lose her confidence in the one place she has always felt most competent.
More than anything, Amina wants to be able to hear a helicopter, feel ordinary surfaces, and remain
in the present instead of being thrown back into terror she still cannot fully put into words.
