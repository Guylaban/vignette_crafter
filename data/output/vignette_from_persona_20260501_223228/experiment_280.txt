Rana is a 21-year-old forester who came to treatment months after witnessing a fatal road accident
on a narrow service road at the edge of state woodland where she works. She had been driving between
survey points when she came upon two cars that had collided head-on. One driver was slumped over the
wheel, bleeding heavily; in the other car a young passenger was crying and trying to get out. Rana
remembers the red on the dashboard and on her hands after she tried to help, the sharp smell of
gasoline and hot metal in the summer air, and people shouting for someone to call 911. What has
stayed with her most is the conviction that she did not do enough. She had come to believe she
should have protected the people there and failed, and that in those confused minutes she acted
against the kind of person she thought she was.

In the weeks afterward, ordinary parts of work and daily life began to pull her back toward the
scene. Seeing blood, even from a minor cut in the field, made her stomach drop. Passing wrecked cars
on the highway or even seeing images of cars hit in accidents could stop her cold. If coworkers
started talking about crashes, she would feel herself tense before she could think. In those moments
the memory did not arrive as a clear story. Instead it came in fragments, along with the smell from
the accident, so vivid that for a second she felt she was breathing it again. At the same time, the
memory felt strangely unreal, as if it belonged to someone else rather than to her, and she would
push it away before it could come fully into focus. Those same triggers also brought back her
harshest conclusions about herself: that she had failed to protect others, that something in her had
been exposed as inadequate, and that the happy version of herself was gone.

Rana began arranging her life around not being caught off guard. At work, where she spends long
stretches driving logging roads and walking isolated tracts, she started scanning constantly for
signs of danger—vehicles coming too fast around a bend, sounds from the road, anything out of place
in the trees. If colleagues began swapping stories about accidents during lunch, she would leave or
shut the conversation down. At night she delayed sleep for hours, keeping videos on or scrolling on
her phone until she was exhausted enough to drop off. Part of this was fear of what would come when
things got quiet: whenever she let herself settle, the smell and scattered images pressed closer.
Part of it was also punishment. She was certain she did not deserve rest when she had failed people
who needed help.

These habits did not calm things for long. The more she pushed away reminders and cut off
conversations, the less the event felt like something that had happened and ended, and the more it
returned as disconnected flashes with that same intrusive smell. Staying up late left her worn down
and more vulnerable to being jolted by images of damaged cars the next day. Constantly checking her
surroundings kept her attention fixed on the possibility of another crash, so roadside details that
others barely noticed stood out to her immediately and set off the same cascade. In this way, the
very things she did to keep control seemed to make the memory more brittle and alien, and each new
jolt was taken as further proof that she was no longer the happy, capable person she used to be.

Over time, the effects became visible in her work. Forestry had once felt grounding to Rana; now
even routine field days could feel like exposure to danger. She took longer routes to avoid certain
roads and was distracted during site checks because she was watching for threats rather than
focusing on the land. She kept more to herself with coworkers, partly to avoid any mention of
accidents, partly because she felt ashamed of how much she had changed. When blood was mentioned or
a vehicle with front-end damage appeared at a gas station, she could feel the smell come back before
the rest of the scene, and with it the sense that she had failed at a basic human duty.

By the time she sought help, Rana was exhausted from sleeping only in the early morning hours and
from living in a state of alertness that never fully switched off. She did not come in saying she
wanted to “process trauma.” She said she was scared that if she let the memory come all the way
through, it would confirm what she already believed—that she had failed people and become someone
she could not respect. At the same time, a small part of her was hoping to feel like herself again,
or at least to learn how to be in the woods, on the road, and in her own mind without bracing for
the accident to happen all over again.
