but once the memory began to come back she became so flooded that she had to stop speaking and look
away. Often she pushed the memory away before it got to the worst part, as if finishing it would be
intolerable. Even in session, she seemed to brace against something just beyond reach.

after a long day and catches body odor in a crowded elevator, her chest tightens before she can

she becomes instantly alert, scanning the room and feeling unable to settle back into her code.
Sometimes an ordinary taste will do it—something metallic or sour in the back of her mouth when she
has skipped lunch and had too much coffee—and suddenly she is no longer just in the office. In those
moments the sensory cue pulls up pieces of the old scenes, and with them comes the same conviction
that she is not safe. She comes home keyed up, checks the deadbolt repeatedly, tests the windows,
and still cannot relax on the couch beside her husband.

finished recollections but as disordered, unbearable shards, and Priya is left with the feeling that

her arm—and because she cannot bring the memory to a clear end, her body acts as if the threat is
ongoing. She gets up to check the front door again, then the balcony lock, then returns to bed only

apartment, yet she cannot make herself believe it enough to rest.

unusual force. A trace of sweat on the subway or alcohol on someone passing her on the sidewalk can
set off a rush of half-memory. Because she meets these moments already braced, the recollections
feel even more overwhelming, and she cuts them off before they can unfold. The more she tries to
keep herself safe by staying vigilant and never letting down her guard, the more fragmented and
intrusive the memories seem to become.
