Marisol Ortega is a 26-year-old school counselor who grew up in a small apartment where, as a child,
she repeatedly witnessed her mother being assaulted by her stepfather. In one memory she returns to
often, she is standing in the hallway outside the kitchen, hearing a chair scrape hard across the
floor and her mother’s voice change from arguing to pleading. She remembers freezing near the wall,
trying to make herself invisible while still watching the doorway, convinced that if she had said
something sooner, called someone, or not hesitated, she might have stopped what happened. What stays
with her just as strongly is that neighbors heard and did nothing, and that adults who knew there
was trouble never protected them. Over time she had come to believe both that the people who were
supposed to keep children safe had failed her and that safety depended on her own constant
readiness, because people could not be trusted.

Those beliefs shaped the way the memories settled in her. When Marisol tries to describe the
violence directly, she often loses the thread, stalls, and says she “can’t get it out in order.” If
she pushes herself to keep going, she becomes flooded quickly and has to stop. The memory does not
feel like a finished story from childhood but something that can arrive whole and suddenly. A man’s
raised voice in a school hallway, the slam of a classroom door, or the scrape of metal chair legs on
tile can bring back the kitchen and hallway without warning. In those moments, what comes with the
image is not only fear but the old conclusion that adults did not step in and that she herself
failed to do the right thing.

Now, even in her own apartment, Marisol feels on guard. She checks where exits are when she enters a
room and sits with her back against a wall whenever she can, including in staff meetings at school,
where she chooses the chair in the corner that lets her see the door and everyone’s hands. If a
colleague brushes past her unexpectedly, she startles sharply and her body reacts before she can
orient herself. She is certain that danger comes from people, especially from shifts in tone,
posture, or movement that others seem to ignore. Because she mistrusts people so deeply, vigilance
feels necessary rather than excessive; to her, letting down her guard would mean repeating the
mistake of assuming someone else will keep things safe.

That sense of threat has narrowed her life over the past few years. Marisol avoids the neighborhood
where she grew up and will take a longer route across town rather than pass the block where the
apartment still stands. At work, when parents begin arguing in the counseling office or a father’s
voice grows forceful, she finds reasons to step out or call someone else in, while telling herself
she is being practical. At home she goes over and over the old scenes, ruminating on what could have
prevented the violence—if she had run to a neighbor sooner, if she had spoken differently, if some
adult had been pushed harder to act. The more she scans people for danger and arranges herself to
stay protected, the more alert she feels, and the more convinced she becomes that mistrust is simply
realism.

Her memories also drive these habits directly. Because reminders can pull the whole scene back
without warning and overwhelm her so fast, she tries to stay ahead of them by controlling where she
goes and who is around her. Avoiding that part of the city makes sense to her because she fears
being hit by the memory in the middle of the day. Watching people closely at school feels like
preparation against the next sudden jolt. Yet this constant readiness seems to keep the memory close
to the surface; the scrape of chairs in the cafeteria, a student flinching at a loud male voice, or
an unexpected touch in a crowded hallway can set her off again, and afterward she is left shaken,
more watchful, and more certain that the world is unsafe.

As these episodes repeat, her interpretations harden further. When she is startled or overwhelmed,
she takes it as proof that she has never really been safe and that others still cannot be relied
upon to notice harm until it is too late. She also reads her own distress as evidence that she
failed somehow at the time, that there was a right action she did not take. Recently, after freezing
during a tense meeting with separated parents at school and needing to leave abruptly when the
father stood and raised his voice, she felt humiliated and frightened by how instantly she was back
in the apartment hallway. That incident, along with the strain of feeling guarded even in her own
home, is what brought her to treatment. What frightens Marisol most is the idea that she will always
live as if violence is about to enter the room; what she hopes for, cautiously, is to be able to sit
through a conversation, come home, and not feel she has to keep watch every minute.
