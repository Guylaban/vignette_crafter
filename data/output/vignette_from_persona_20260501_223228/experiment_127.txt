Linh Tran is a 38-year-old welder who came to treatment months after an accident at work in which a
coworker was seriously injured. She works in a fabrication shop where most days are structured by
noise, heat, metal dust, and the close coordination of people moving heavy pieces through narrow
spaces. On the day of the accident, she was helping position steel for a weld while another worker
stood nearby guiding the placement. Something went wrong in the sequence, and her movement
contributed to the metal shifting and striking him. She remembers the sudden shout, the metallic
crash, and the immediate realization that her action had been part of what hurt him. What stays with
her most sharply is not a complete story but fragments: the look of the floor, the shock in his
voice, and a harsh, chemical taste in her mouth that comes back so vividly that for a moment she
feels she is there again. Around those fragments there are blanks, and those blanks torment her.
When she tries to remember, she is hit by a wave of guilt so strong that she feels she can barely
stand being inside the memory.

In the weeks after the accident, Linh found herself doing almost anything she could to keep the
event out of mind. If thoughts about the shop or her injured coworker began to come up, she would
cut them off, turn on the television, or throw herself into some task at home. She stopped answering
messages from coworkers and kept conversations brief because being around other people felt too
exposing; she feared they would be thinking the same thing she was thinking, that she was the one
who did this. The more she dwelled on what she had done wrong, replaying her mistake and asking
herself how she could have missed something so obvious, the more unbearable the memory became, so
she tried even harder not to think about it and not to be around anyone who might bring it up.

That effort did not bring relief. At work, specific moments set her off with little warning. The
taste in her mouth would suddenly return when the air filled with hot metal and burnt residue, and
her body reacted before she could steady herself: her heart pounded, her hands shook, and she felt a
rush of heat and nausea. Those reminders pulled up only pieces of the accident rather than a full
account, and what came with them was immediate guilt. She would stand at her station unable to focus
on a measurement or a line of weld because part of her mind was caught in those fragments while the
rest of her was trying desperately to push them away. Nights became difficult for the same reason.
As soon as the day quieted, bits of the scene pressed in, and she lay awake tense and alert,
dreading what would come into her mind if she let herself drift.

Over time, the lack of sleep and constant physical jolt of being reminded seemed to make the
memories even more intrusive and disorganized. When she was tired, her concentration was worse, and
at work even ordinary sensory details could break through her defenses and bring back that same
taste and those same broken images. She began to notice that after a bad night or after one of those
intense bodily reactions in the shop, the memory felt closer and more jagged, not less. She could
not hold it as a finished event in the past; it came as flashes with missing sections, and in each
return she felt freshly guilty. What frightened her most was that her own mind seemed to be turning
against her, refusing to let the event settle.

Her attempts to manage this gradually narrowed her life. She kept to herself on breaks, left quickly
after shifts, and avoided gatherings entirely. She told herself that staying away from people was
safer for everyone and easier than seeing pity or blame in someone’s face. She also treated thinking
itself as dangerous. Yet because she never let herself stay with the memory for long, it remained
broken and unresolved, surfacing in pieces that felt more potent each time. The more she pushed it
off, the more it seemed to return in exactly the form she dreaded: incomplete, sensory, and soaked
in guilt. Then she would spend hours silently going over what she had done wrong, circling the same
self-accusing questions without arriving anywhere.

By the time she sought help, Linh was still working, but only just managing. She had begun making
small errors because she was so tired and distracted, and that scared her in a job where precision
matters. She finally came in after a shift in which a familiar smell in the welding bay triggered
such a strong physical reaction that she had to step outside and could not regain her focus for the
rest of the day. She said she was terrified that she would never stop feeling responsible, that the
memory would keep ambushing her in pieces, and that if she did not get help she might either lose
her job or hurt someone again. More quietly, she said she hoped treatment might let her remember
what happened without feeling crushed by it every single time.
