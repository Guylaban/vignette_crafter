Priya Raman was 31, a middle-school teacher who had always thought of herself as steady under
pressure, the kind of person who could manage a noisy classroom, reassure a crying student, and keep
everyone moving. The event that undid that picture of herself happened on a rainy afternoon after
school. She was driving home from a parent meeting, tired and distracted, and at an intersection she
turned without seeing a cyclist coming through the slick crosswalk. She still described it in pieces
rather than as a whole: the flash of a yellow rain jacket, the sickening thud against the hood, the
shattered angle of the bicycle on the pavement. When she spoke about it, she said she could smell
wet asphalt and the sharp burnt odor from the brakes as if she were standing there again. The memory
did not feel filed away as something over; it arrived as if the collision were happening in the
present tense.

In the weeks afterward, what tormented Priya most was not only that another person had been
seriously injured, but her conviction that it had happened because of the way she had acted. She had
come to believe there was something fundamentally negligent in her, some flaw that had been exposed
in a single irreversible moment. From there the meaning of the crash spread outward. She was certain
she was no longer capable of doing the things that had defined her, especially being responsible for
other people. At school, where praise had once been easy to accept, even small positive comments
from colleagues or parents began to feel unbearable. If a principal thanked her for handling a
difficult class well, or a parent called her caring, the words jarred against what she believed
about herself, and that dissonance made her tense before she even understood why. Positive attention
had become dangerous terrain; even moments of warmth or pride made her wary, as though feeling good
meant forgetting what she had done.

Those reactions quickly took on a sensory force of their own. In the teacher’s lounge, if coworkers
casually discussed a traffic accident from the news, Priya would lose the room around her and be
back at the intersection, caught by the same visual snapshots: the yellow jacket, the body falling,
the bicycle twisted sideways. Sometimes a staff member’s cheerful praise after an observed
lesson—“You were amazing with those kids”—was enough to bring it on. The memory struck with the
smell of rain and hot brakes, immediate and bodily, not like recollection but reliving. She began to
dread exactly those moments when conversation turned upbeat or admiring, because they so often
flipped without warning into those frozen images and smells.

Her beliefs about herself also made those triggers more potent. Because she was convinced she had
caused the injury through her own actions, any conversation about accidents felt less like neutral
talk and more like an accusation waiting to happen. Because she saw herself as permanently changed
and set apart from other people, ordinary positive emotion became confusing rather than comforting;
laughter in the classroom, satisfaction after a successful lesson, even a pleasant dinner with her
husband could suddenly feel wrong, as if someone like her should not be allowed such feelings. In
those moments she scanned herself sharply, and the very effort to judge whether she was being
careless, undeserving, or falsely “normal” seemed to summon the crash back in pictures and smells.

Over months, the reverse happened too: once the memory had seized her in that vivid, present-tense
way, it left her more vulnerable to the next trigger. A single intrusive spell after hearing
students mention a car wreck would make her spend the rest of the day braced for praise, small talk,
or any hint of good feeling. She started interpreting compliments as openings through which the
crash could rush back. In faculty meetings she kept her head down to avoid being singled out. When
colleagues chatted about weekend plans and laughed, she stayed quiet, feeling oddly outside the
group, as though everyone else still belonged to ordinary life and she no longer did. That sense of
being an outsider hardened each time the memory broke through in the middle of school and she had to
compose herself before stepping back into her classroom.

By the time she sought help, Priya was still teaching, but the job felt like an exposure she could
barely sustain. The school setting confronted her daily with both praise and forced brightness, and
any mention of accidents by students or staff could send her back to those few unbearable images.
She came to treatment after breaking down in her car when a parent thanked her warmly at dismissal
and she immediately smelled wet pavement and heard the impact again. What frightened her most was
the possibility that this was who she was now: someone unfit to care for others, cut off from other
people, and one moment of praise or happiness away from reliving the worst thing she had ever done.
What she hoped, though she said it cautiously, was not to erase responsibility, but to be able to
remember the event as something that happened rather than something that was still happening.
