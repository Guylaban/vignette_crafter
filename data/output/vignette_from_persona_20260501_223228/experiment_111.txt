humiliating danger. The triggers felt less like reminders than like proof that the event was still
happening somewhere just outside the present.

Because those moments arrived so quickly and so bodily, Moana had come to believe that something in
them had fundamentally given way. When a manager pointed out an error in a spreadsheet and they felt

front of them, they did not think, this is a memory. They thought, I can’t rely on myself anymore.
Encounters with police were similarly loaded: even the sight of a uniform made them feel exposed and
disorganized, and afterward they would sit at their desk convinced that their reactions were

and they increasingly felt that nothing in their life carried the sense of purpose it once had.

The memory itself deepened those conclusions. What frightened Moana most was not only what had been

deterioration rather than a consequence of trauma. When the auditory fragments returned—certain
words, the pressure in the man’s voice—or when the taste of the event suddenly filled their mouth,
they were shaken by how real it felt. The vividness convinced them they had lost command over their
own mind. If a memory could seize them so completely, they reasoned, then they were no longer
someone who could trust their own reactions. From there it was a short step to the bleak conviction
that life had narrowed into endurance, without much meaning beyond getting through the day.

elevator or a news clip showing official authority was enough to make the day feel contaminated.
Their occupation, once a source of structure and pride, became another place where they felt

By the time Moana sought help, their world had constricted around these eruptions of memory and the

without every authority figure, every act of indecency, every correction turning into proof that
