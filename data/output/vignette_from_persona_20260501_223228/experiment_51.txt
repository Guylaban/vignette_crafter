Nadia is a 24-year-old single Arab American woman who works long hours as a tailor in a small
alterations shop, spending most of her day bent over hems and seams while customers come and go
behind her. Six months before seeking treatment, she was driving home after closing when another car
ran a red light and slammed into the driver’s side of her sedan. She remembers the impact as a
violent sideways blast of metal and glass, the cabin suddenly folding inward, the sound so loud it
seemed to erase thought. For a few seconds she was certain she would die there, trapped in the car
and unable to predict what would hit next. In the aftermath, what stayed with her most was not only
the crash itself but the sense that danger had appeared without warning, from an ordinary moment she
had not seen coming.

Since then, Nadia has come to believe that the world is fundamentally dangerous and that a person
has to stay especially careful because disaster can arrive at any second. She is more wary of other
people too, feeling that people are not always what they seem and that safety depends on not
relaxing around them. From that position, her habits have changed in ways that feel sensible to her.
She avoids driving altogether and refuses rides unless absolutely necessary, arranging her life
around walking, rideshares she can cancel at the last minute, and favors from relatives. In the
shop, she chooses the chair that lets her keep her back to the wall and her eyes on the front door.
She also rehearses disasters in her mind—what if a customer becomes aggressive, what if a car jumps
the curb, what if she gets trapped somewhere again—because she feels she must think through the
worst in order to be ready.

The memories themselves remain raw and immediate. When something reminds her of the collision, it
does not feel like recalling a past event; it feels as though she is back in the driver’s seat at
the instant of impact. She describes the scene replaying like a film she cannot stop, and each
replay brings the same terror she felt that night. Those moments push her further into protective
routines. If she hears tires screech outside the shop or catches sight of a dented fender on the
street, she begins scanning exits, checking who is near her, and telling herself not to get into any
vehicle unless there is no alternative. The more vividly the crash returns, the more convinced she
becomes that she must stay on guard.

That sense of current threat is now threaded through her days and nights. Nadia constantly scans her
surroundings for signs of danger, tracking footsteps, traffic noise, and sudden movements before she
can focus on her sewing. She has difficulty falling asleep because she feels she needs to remain
alert; lying in bed, she listens for sounds outside and imagines what she would do if something
happened. In restaurants or waiting rooms she cannot settle unless her back is against a wall and
she can see the whole room. Living this way keeps the collision close. Her body is already braced,
her attention fixed on possible danger, and in that keyed-up state the crash returns more easily and
more forcefully, as if her mind is always only a step away from the scene.

These habits that are meant to protect her have gradually tightened around her. By avoiding driving
and mentally preparing for catastrophe, she has less contact with ordinary experiences that might
feel safe, and she takes her continued vigilance as proof that her fears are justified. Each day
organized around watching, checking, and preparing confirms her belief that the world is dangerous
and that trusting others would be naïve. The constant scanning also leaves her jumpy and tired,
which makes her feel even less able to let her guard down. At work, customers have noticed that she
startles when the shop bell rings and that she positions herself to watch them closely; one coworker
told her she seems far away even while pinning a hem. Nadia feels ashamed of that, but she also
believes it would be reckless to behave otherwise.

Over the past several weeks, the cycle has become harder for her to contain. Her sleep has worsened,
the replaying memories are more frequent, and getting through a day at the shop now requires so much
vigilance that she goes home exhausted but still unable to rest. She finally sought help after
turning down a promising tailoring job because it would have required a commute by car. What
frightens her most is the thought that she may never feel safe again, that one ordinary moment can
always split open into catastrophe, and that if she stops watching for it, she will be caught
defenseless the next time it comes.
