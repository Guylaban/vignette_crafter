Nabil is a 75-year-old widowed sculptor who came to treatment months after a life-threatening
accident in his studio, an event he still returns to in his mind with the stunned disbelief of
someone who had trusted his hands and body for a lifetime. He had been moving a heavy stone form
into place, working alone as he often did, when the piece shifted unexpectedly. In trying to steady
it, he lost his footing and was pinned hard enough that, for a terrifying stretch of time, he could
not free himself and was certain he might die there on the studio floor. What remains most vivid to
him is the helplessness of feeling his body fail him at the exact moment he needed it most: the
sudden weakness in his legs, the pain, the sense that the strength and steadiness he had built a
life around could no longer be counted on. Since then, he has carried the conviction that something
fundamental was ruined. He says, in different ways, that his body is no longer trustworthy, that
what happened damaged him permanently, and that he has changed for the worse.

These beliefs have become the lens through which he now reads ordinary bodily experience. A small
ache after lifting groceries, stiffness when he first stands, a flutter in his chest when he climbs
stairs—Nabil treats each sensation as possible evidence that the accident left hidden injury behind.
Because he has come to believe he is physically vulnerable and permanently damaged, he checks
himself constantly. He presses at sore places, notices every pull in his back, takes inventory of
his breathing, and scans for signs that his body is about to fail him again. He also spends long
stretches thinking about why this happened to him at this stage of life, circling questions he
cannot answer: whether he was foolish to keep working with stone at his age, whether the accident
was some kind of punishment, whether it marked the end of the man he had been. The more certain he
feels that his body has been altered beyond repair, the more he tries to protect it by avoiding
anything that feels physically demanding.

That change is most visible in the studio, which had once been the center of his days and now feels
charged with danger. Nabil still goes there, but he moves through it differently. He no longer
attempts heavier pieces, and he avoids tasks that raise his heart rate or require straining,
bracing, or prolonged standing. If he feels even a flicker of pain while positioning clay or
carrying tools, he stops and studies his body until he can decide whether he is safe. He has come to
believe that physical stress might expose just how damaged he really is, so he works in shorter
bursts and abandons projects that would once have absorbed him for hours. He finds himself pausing
in the middle of shaping a form not because he has lost the artistic idea, but because he is
checking whether the tension in his shoulder means something serious. In this way, the belief that
his body is unreliable pushes him toward more monitoring and more avoidance, and the studio has
become less a place of mastery than a place where he watches for collapse.

Over the following months, these habits narrowed his life. He takes fewer walks, turns down
invitations that would require exertion, and structures his day around conserving himself. As a
widower, he had already grown used to long periods alone, but now the solitude has thickened around
his routines. He spends more time sitting with his thoughts, trying to make sense of why the
accident happened, and less time doing the physical work that once gave his days shape. Each bodily
sensation is taken seriously, rarely dismissed. If he wakes sore, it confirms his fear that he has
been permanently changed; once that thought takes hold, he becomes more vigilant about his body and
more determined to avoid strain for the rest of the day. The pattern feels sensible to him, even
necessary. What frightens him most is not pain itself but the possibility of discovering, too late,
that his body has become fragile without warning.

He sought help only after recognizing that he was no longer simply recovering from an accident but
reorganizing his entire life around the idea that his body could not be trusted. A recent afternoon
in the studio unsettled him: while reaching for a tool, he felt a brief pull in his side and
immediately stopped work, sat down, and spent the next hour checking himself and wondering whether
he had just worsened some old damage. Looking at an unfinished sculpture, he was struck by the
possibility that he might never work as he once had. By the time he came in, Nabil was not asking
for pain relief so much as for some way to live without constant surveillance of his own body. He is
most afraid that the accident revealed a permanent weakness and that any physical effort will
confirm it. What he hopes, though he says it cautiously, is to feel at home in his body again long
enough to return to his work without waiting for it to fail him.
