When he is this keyed up, the old film starts running again. A creak in his apartment or the sight
of an object that could be used as a weapon is enough to pull him back into the event, and afterward
he takes it as further proof that something in him has been permanently altered. Each time the
memory takes over so completely, he comes away convinced not just that the assignment was terrible,
but that he himself has been changed into someone damaged and unlikely ever to feel normal again.

What frightens Walter most is not only the memory itself but what he has come to believe it means.
He says that if he lets himself think about the disaster directly, he will not be able to handle it.
Because he had come to believe he was too altered and too fragile to bear the memory, the way he
managed his days began to feel like simple necessity: he kept himself distracted, spent impulsively
for brief relief, and arranged his body near walls and exits because trusting himself to cope
without those measures no longer seemed realistic to him. That conviction makes the fragments easier
to stir up, not easier to contain; the more certain he is that remembering will overwhelm him, the
more readily the old images and sensations break through, as if his mind keeps returning to the
scene to test the danger he has assigned to it. He has come to believe he will never feel normal
emotions again, and that nothing in his life feels meaningful anymore. Those convictions shape what
he does every day. And the more he relied on those measures to get through the day, the more they
seemed to prove their own premise to him: if he still needed to scan, position himself, and buy
relief in quick bursts, then he must indeed be damaged, unable to cope, and unlikely to get his life
back. He avoids letting his mind settle, buys things impulsively online and in stores for a brief
sense of comfort or distraction, and arranges himself physically against walls and exits as if
vigilance were the only thing holding him together. Over months, those habits have made reminders
harder to escape: every restaurant without the right seat, every office chair with its back exposed,
every image archive he tries to hurry past has become newly charged because his daily routines keep
marking these situations as places where something is wrong. Yet the relief is short-lived. The
spending leaves him ashamed and emptier; the constant positioning and scanning tell him the threat
must be real; the effort not to think about the event only seems to make the memory more intrusive
and unreal at the same time. He says it is like trying not to touch a bruise and ending up pressing
on it all day.

The loop has tightened over the last year. Because he freezes when startled and spends so much
energy monitoring his surroundings, he is less able to stay present during interviews and has pulled
back from some assignments that once gave his life structure. At home he sits in restaurants only if
he can face the door, and if he cannot, he leaves. In the office he dreads the image desk, yet when
a photo catches him off guard the memory floods back so vividly that the rest of the day is
organized around managing it. Each recurrence deepens his conviction that he is damaged beyond
repair, and that belief makes him clamp down harder, watch harder, avoid more. He finally sought
help after realizing that he no longer recognized his own life as meaningful and was spending nights
listening for intruders while old scenes replayed in his head. What he wants, cautiously, is not to
erase the event but to stop living as though it is still happening; what he fears is that if he
stops guarding against it, he will disappear into it completely.
