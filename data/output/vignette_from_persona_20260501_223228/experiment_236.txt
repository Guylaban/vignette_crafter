Thomas is a 62-year-old married potter who came to treatment after more than a year of living in a
life that had narrowed around the death of his wife’s sister, a woman who had been woven into the
daily fabric of his household for decades. She had died suddenly after what the family had expected
would be a routine hospital stay, and Thomas still spoke about the shock of the phone call in blunt,
disbelieving terms: one day they were discussing whether she would need help at home afterward, and
the next he was standing in his kitchen with his wife collapsed into a chair, realizing the future
they had all been assuming had vanished without warning. What stayed with him was not only the loss
itself but the way ordinary life had continued around it, as if the kiln still needed loading, mugs
still needed glazing, customers still needed answering, while something fundamental had been broken.

In the months that followed, Thomas did not describe dramatic outward collapse so much as a
determined constriction. He spent longer hours in his pottery studio behind the house, where the
repetition of wedging clay and trimming bowls gave him something to look at besides his wife’s grief
and his own. Yet even there, reminders of the loss were hard to avoid. His sister-in-law had often
stopped by the studio with coffee, teasing him about the shelves of nearly identical stoneware and
asking which pieces he was actually going to sell. Now a mug in the shape she used to favor, a half-
finished commission in her preferred blue glaze, even the sight of an extra chair by the worktable
could catch his attention. Thomas responded by quietly removing things. He boxed up photographs from
the kitchen, stopped using the glaze colors she had liked, and changed the radio station when songs
from family cookouts came on. He had also begun declining family gatherings where her absence would
be obvious, telling himself he was sparing everyone a difficult evening, though he acknowledged that
what he really could not bear was the jolt of seeing the place where she should have been.

Alongside this, Thomas had grown wary of anything that felt too good. He noticed that if he laughed
with a customer at an art fair, or felt briefly absorbed in shaping a vessel on the wheel, a hard
correction would rise in him almost immediately. Enjoyment felt disloyal, almost dangerous, as if
letting himself feel light for even a moment meant he was forgetting the dead or inviting some new
catastrophe. He would shut the feeling down by turning back to work, keeping conversation practical,
or finding some task that restored a familiar heaviness. His wife had remarked that even when
something pleasant happened — their son visiting, a strong weekend of sales, the first warm day of
spring — Thomas seemed to pull away from it. He did not argue with that. He said that positive
feelings now felt “like a trick,” something that made a person careless.

The same narrowing had extended into his sense of time. Before the death, Thomas and his wife had
talked loosely but happily about the next few years: maybe reducing custom orders, maybe taking a
driving trip through the Southwest to see ceramics studios, maybe finally renovating the old barn on
their property into a larger workspace. Since the loss, he had stopped discussing any of it. If his
wife brought up travel, retirement, or even booking a holiday visit months ahead, he would answer
vaguely or say they should wait. Making plans for the future felt unbearable to him, not because he
lacked ideas, but because the death had made him feel that looking ahead was naive. He had come to
believe that the future could disappear without warning, and he preferred not to stand emotionally
inside anything that might be taken away.

By the time he sought help, this way of living had begun to alter the household in concrete ways.
His wife experienced him as present in body but difficult to reach, especially when family wanted to
remember her sister openly. In the studio, Thomas kept producing functional pieces, but had stopped
developing new designs and turned down invitations to apply for larger craft shows that required
planning well in advance. He was not asking for help because he had suddenly become more expressive;
if anything, he remained restrained and careful in session. What brought him in was the recognition
that his world had become organized around not feeling, not remembering, and not looking forward. He
said he was tired of living as though any reminder, any good moment, any imagined future might undo
him. What frightened him most was that if he kept going this way, the loss would not only have taken
one beloved person from the family, but would go on taking the shape of the life he and his wife
still had left.
