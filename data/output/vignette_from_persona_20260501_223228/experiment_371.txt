Mateo is a 22-year-old HVAC technician who came to treatment months after an on-the-job accident in
which a coworker was seriously injured. He had been helping move a heavy unit into place in a
cramped mechanical room above a restaurant kitchen. He remembers the heat, the metallic smell, and
the stale odor of grease rising through the vents. In a tight moment, when everyone was
repositioning at once, he shifted his grip and called out too late. The unit slipped and pinned the
other man badly. Mateo still speaks about that instant as if it happened in a gap outside ordinary
time: he can describe the angle of the machine and the smell in the room with painful clarity, but
the memory itself feels strangely unreal, as though it belongs to someone else and not to him.

In the days that followed, what settled most heavily was not only the image of the accident but his
conviction that he had failed in a way he could never undo. He had come to believe he would never
forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened. He replayed whether he should have braced
sooner, shouted louder, noticed the shift earlier. Alongside that guilt was a new distrust of
himself at the most basic level. His hands, his timing, his reflexes no longer felt dependable; he
was certain his own body had proven unreliable. When he looked at himself now, he felt
unrecognizable, as if the easygoing young man he had been before the accident had been replaced by
someone weaker and dangerous.

Those beliefs began to shape how the memory lived in him. Because he was so convinced that his
failure said something permanent about who he was, the accident did not sit in the past. It burst
into his mind without permission while he was driving, eating, or trying to fall asleep. Sometimes
when he thought of that mechanical room he could smell it again immediately—the hot metal, oil,
grease, and trapped air—so vividly that he would look around as if he had somehow been pulled back
there. The memory’s intrusiveness seemed to confirm his worst ideas about himself: that he was no
longer in charge of his own mind or even fully himself.

From there, Mateo began trying to manage his days in ways that felt sensible to him. He avoided
dates connected to the accident and became watchful as those calendar days approached. He also
started steering away from anything he thought might be stressful, turning down jobs that seemed
complicated or unpredictable and lingering only in a few places that felt controlled and
familiar—his apartment, his mother’s kitchen, one corner of the workshop. He spent long stretches
going over the accident detail by detail, convinced that if he analyzed it enough he might finally
find the exact moment he should have prevented it. In practice, these habits deepened the same
beliefs that had given rise to them. The more he withdrew from pressure and confined himself to
“safe” places, the more fragile and changed he felt; the more he reviewed the scene, the more
certain he became that the person he was now was someone he did not recognize and could not trust.

At work, this has become especially clear. HVAC service often means entering quiet basements, empty
hallways, and sealed utility spaces before a system kicks on. Mateo has found that unusual silence
now makes his body tense immediately, as if danger is about to break through it. In those still
moments, his chest tightens, his hands sweat, and he feels a jolt run through him before he can
think. A silent service corridor or the stale smell from a vent can bring the accident back at full
force, and once the memory forces its way in, his sense of danger spikes further. He has started
hanging back on calls, letting others enter first, and avoiding the more stressful jobs entirely.
His supervisor has noticed that he is less willing to take assignments alone.

That narrowing of his world has fed the fear. Because he keeps himself to a limited set of places
and avoids strain wherever possible, more and more settings now feel unsafe by contrast. Silence is
no longer just silence; it feels like the second before something goes wrong. When his body reacts
strongly to a reminder, he takes that as proof that he really is no longer reliable. And when he is
on edge like that, the memory comes more easily again—the smell, the image, the sickening suspended
second before impact—tightening the sense that he cannot get out of what happened.

He finally sought help after realizing he was considering leaving HVAC altogether, not because he
wanted a different future but because he no longer believed he could trust himself around other
people’s safety. What frightened him most was the thought that this was now his permanent self: a
man who could not handle stress, could not trust his own body, and could never stop the accident
from happening in his mind. What he hopes, though he says it cautiously, is that he might find some
way to feel like himself again and to be in a workroom without waiting for disaster.
