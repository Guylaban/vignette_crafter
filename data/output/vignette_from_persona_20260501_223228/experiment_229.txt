Martin Keller is a 49-year-old fashion designer who came to treatment nearly a year after his wife
was struck by a car while crossing a street outside his studio. He had been walking a few steps
behind her at the end of a long workday, distracted by garment notes on his phone, when he looked up
to the sound of brakes and saw her thrown sideways onto the pavement. What remained with him was not
a complete story but a series of brutally sharp fragments: the angle of her body under the
streetlight, one of her shoes in the road, the sound he made when he reached her. Around those
moments there were blanks he could not fill in. He could not clearly reconstruct who called 911,
what the bystanders said, or how he got to the hospital. When the memory came back, it did so
unevenly and with punishing force, and Martin was seized by the conviction that he should have been
paying attention, should have stopped her, should have done something different.

In the first weeks after she survived the accident and began a long physical recovery, Martin found
that remembering any part of that night could undo him. If an image surfaced, he was suddenly
flooded with guilt and a level of emotion so intense he had to stop whatever he was doing. In client
meetings he would lose track of a conversation; in the studio, while reviewing fabrics under bright
track lighting that reminded him of the street scene, his chest would tighten and his hands would
shake. Because the memory was both fragmented and unbearable, he started working hard not to let it
form at all. He kept podcasts playing in the car, answered emails late into the night, and filled
every spare moment with sketching or scrolling supplier lists, anything to keep his mind occupied.
He also stopped making plans beyond the immediate week. Looking ahead to a vacation, a show, even
dinner next month felt intolerable, as though the future itself had become unsafe and presumptuous.

Those efforts did not settle him. Martin lived in a state of strain marked by fear, anger, guilt,
and shame that seemed to flare without warning. He slept badly, lying awake replaying flashes he did
not want and then jolting up if he did drift off. At work he began avoiding the window facing the
crosswalk outside the building, and if he heard tires screech in the street during a fitting, he
felt a hot rush through his body before he could think. The strength of those reactions made him
even more determined to push the whole event out of mind. He told himself that if he could stay busy
enough and refuse to think about it, he might keep from falling apart in front of his staff or his
wife.

Instead, the more threatened he felt, the less room there was in his mind for anything but
fragments. Exhausted by poor sleep and constantly braced for reminders, he found that the memory
returned in the same jagged form: a few crystal-clear seconds surrounded by emptiness, followed
immediately by crushing guilt. He would begin to recall the accident and then shut it down almost at
once, turning back to work, opening another tab on his laptop, asking an assistant for an
unnecessary revision. That habit of cutting the memory off left it unchanged, still sharp where it
was sharp and blank where it was blank, so that the next reminder hit with the same force. In recent
months this has narrowed his life considerably. His wife has noticed that he refuses to talk about
future plans for her continued rehabilitation or for their anniversary, and he gives vague answers
when she asks what he wants six months from now. In the studio he remains productive in a driven,
brittle way, but colleagues have commented that he never stops moving and seems unable to tolerate
quiet.

By the time he sought help, Martin was frightened by how trapped he felt between the memory and his
efforts to escape it. He could see that his constant distraction and refusal to think ahead were not
giving him relief for more than a few minutes, yet he was afraid that if he let himself remember
fully, he would be overwhelmed by guilt and emotion he could not control. What brought him in was a
recent moment at work when the squeal of brakes outside his showroom left him shaking so visibly
that a junior designer asked if he needed an ambulance. Martin said he wants to sleep, to stop
living as if disaster is about to happen again, and most of all to find some way to remember what
happened to his wife without feeling that the memory proves he failed her.
