For most of his career, Javier Morales had been the person other people trusted in a crisis: a
62-year-old surgeon who could stand for hours under harsh operating lights with steady hands and a
narrowed, practical focus. The accident that now divided his life into before and after happened on
an ordinary evening drive home. A car coming fast through an intersection struck the driver’s side
of his sedan hard enough to spin it. He remembers the violent jolt through his chest and shoulder,
the crunch of metal folding inward, and the abrasive feel of the deployed airbag and seatbelt
against his skin. In the seconds that followed, he saw through shattered glass the other driver
stumble out, dazed but upright, and later learned it was someone from his own hospital community, a
face he recognized. That detail lodged in him with unusual force: it was not only the wreckage, but
the sight of a known person emerging from it, making the event feel uncomfortably close to his
ordinary life rather than sealed off as random disaster.

In the weeks after, Javier found that the accident did not settle into the past the way other
difficult experiences had. The memory came with a sharpness that startled him. He would say that it
felt more real than normal memory, as if the impact were happening in the room rather than being
remembered. It forced its way in when he did not want it to, especially in brief unguarded moments
between cases or while sitting in his car before turning the ignition. Yet when the sequence began
to unfold, he often pushed it away before he could reach the end of it. He could feel himself
stopping the recall midstream, refusing the final seconds before the spin came to a halt. That half-
interrupted quality seemed to leave the image raw and unfinished, and because the memory remained so
vivid and intrusive, certain reminders took on a charge of their own: the sight of crumpled cars on
the roadside or in news footage, the rough synthetic scrape of particular fabrics against his
shoulder, and the presence of people connected in some way to the hospital and accident scene could
all make it feel as though the collision were breaking through again.

Javier began organizing his days around not setting this off. At work he remained competent, but he
changed in small, accumulating ways. If a television in the surgeons’ lounge showed traffic footage
with damaged vehicles, he looked away or left the room. He stopped wearing one of his older jackets
because the lining had a texture too close to the burn and drag he remembered from the airbag. When
he crossed paths with colleagues who knew the other driver or had spoken with him after the crash,
he kept the interaction brisk and impersonal, already bracing against the surge of imagery those
encounters could provoke. He was just as determined not to think about the accident directly. When
the memory intruded, he cut it off, turned to charts, emails, anything concrete. He also became
increasingly reluctant to do anything that made his body feel strained or keyed up. He stopped
taking long walks with his wife and gave up tennis, not because he had lost all interest in
movement, but because a pounding heart, tight chest, or sudden bodily exertion felt too close to the
alarm of the crash.

Over the following months, those habits narrowed his life. The less room he gave the memory, the
more abruptly it seemed to return, and the more he arranged himself to avoid the thoughts, the more
sensitive he became to reminders he once might have managed. He was especially struck by how quickly
his attention found wrecked cars now, whether on the highway shoulder during his commute or in
images passing across a screen in the hospital waiting area. Avoiding physical stress also made his
world smaller in practical ways; he delegated parts of his routine he once took pride in, sat out
family outings, and spent more evenings at home in a chair, telling himself he was tired. His wife
noticed that he no longer suggested anything pleasurable and often declined what she proposed. He
could see the disappointment in her, which troubled him, but pleasure itself had started to feel
risky, as if relaxing his guard might let the accident in.

What finally brought Javier to seek help was not a dramatic collapse but a moment he found
professionally humiliating. In the parking garage after a long case, he saw a dented car with its
side panel crushed in, and the image came over him with such immediacy that he had to grip the door
frame of his own car and wait. He could feel the memory arriving in full color and force, and again
he shoved it away before it completed itself. Driving home, he realized how much of his life was now
organized around not feeling, not remembering, not quickening physically. He is frightened that this
is becoming permanent, that even in the controlled world of the hospital he is no longer fully in
command of his own mind. At the same time, he comes to treatment hoping for something simple and, to
him, almost radical: to see a damaged car, to feel cloth brush his skin, to meet familiar people
from work, and not be pulled straight back into the collision.
