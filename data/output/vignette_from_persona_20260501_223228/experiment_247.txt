Marisol Reyes is a 24-year-old warehouse worker who came to treatment months after surviving a
bombing at a crowded bus terminal on her way home from an evening shift. She remembers the
compressed details more than any whole sequence: the crush of bodies in line, a sharp metallic crack
she first took for something dropped, and then the force of the blast throwing people sideways. What
stayed with her most was movement—arms jerking up, strangers lunging, people running without
direction. The scene is broken in her mind. There are stretches she cannot account for at all, and
when she tries to explain what happened, she stops in frustration, saying she knows it happened but
cannot find words that feel big enough or precise enough. At other moments the memory returns with a
surge of rage so sudden it feels almost physical, especially when she thinks about how ordinary the
night had seemed before everything changed.

In the weeks after, Marisol had come to believe that the world was simply dangerous now, not in an
abstract sense but everywhere and all the time. She was certain that safety depended on staying
alert because danger could break through without warning. Just as unsettling, her body no longer
felt reliable to her. She hated how it jumped before she could think, how it seemed to react on its
own, and this made her feel she had to monitor herself as closely as she monitored everyone else.
From there, being on guard ceased to feel like a choice and began to feel like the only responsible
way to move through the day. At the warehouse she now tracks every forklift turn, every coworker
stepping suddenly out from behind stacked pallets, every dropped box. She avoids eye contact on the
floor because meeting someone’s gaze leaves her feeling exposed and distractible, and she keeps
scanning aisles, exits, and doorways instead.

That constant sense of danger has sharpened into a physical threat response that governs much of her
life. Sudden movements by other people put her immediately on guard; even a coworker reaching
quickly for a scanner can make her flinch and tense. She is jumpy in general, and nights are
especially difficult. Since the bombing happened after dark, evening brings a particular feeling
that the world is less controllable, as if threats are harder to detect and more likely to arrive
fast. When she leaves late shifts, she checks parking areas and the street repeatedly, watching
hands, body positions, and who is moving toward her. Because she feels keyed up so often, she has
begun to rely even more on scanning her surroundings constantly, as though relaxing for a second
would be the moment she misses something vital.

Her fractured memory also feeds these habits. The gaps are intolerable to her; not knowing exactly
what happened makes her feel unprepared, as if the missing pieces contain some warning she failed to
notice. When images or sensations from that night break through and she cannot put them into words,
she sometimes deliberately tries to blank out, going mentally distant to escape both the
helplessness of not remembering and the anger that comes with remembering too much. During breaks at
work, if a noise in the loading bay or a rush of bodies at shift change stirs the scene at the
terminal, she can feel herself pulling away inside, staring past people rather than at them. Those
same broken, wordless fragments also keep her body on edge; a burst of motion around her can feel
dangerous before she even knows why, as if the memory is being carried in reflex rather than
thought.

Over time, the ways Marisol has tried to protect herself have deepened the beliefs she already
holds. Constant scanning makes every setting feel full of possible threat, because there is always
another movement to track or another person to watch. Avoiding eye contact leaves other people
seeming less readable and more unpredictable, which confirms her sense that the world is unsafe.
Deliberately blanking out gives brief relief, but afterward she feels even less able to trust her
own body and mind, disturbed by how easily she can disappear from herself. When her startle response
fires or she finds herself most vigilant at night, it seems to prove that her danger is ongoing
rather than past. And when she reacts this way, the event remains not fully settled in memory but
raw, fragmented, and rage-filled, with the unspeakable parts still looming.

What finally brought her in was a recent incident at work when a pallet shifted and a coworker
grabbed her arm quickly to steady her. Marisol jerked away so hard that she nearly fell, then went
blank for several seconds and could not answer when he asked if she was okay. She was humiliated
afterward and frightened by how automatic it felt. She is seeking help now because she is tired of
living as if every movement around her might be the start of another attack, and because what
frightens her most is the thought that she may never again be able to trust either the world outside
her or the body she has to live in.
