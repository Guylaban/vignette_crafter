Richard is a 70-year-old married salesman who came to treatment months after being assaulted in a
strip mall parking lot he had used for years between client visits. He had stopped after work to
pick up takeout and was walking back to his car when a man who was visibly drunk approached him,
first asking for money and then turning aggressive when Richard hesitated. What stayed with him was
not only the shove and the blows, but the smell on the man’s breath when he got close enough to
curse at him—stale alcohol, heavy and sour—and the sudden, humiliating sense of being overpowered in
a place that had once felt ordinary. Richard remembered seeing the man’s face at close range as he
tried to shield himself, and that image had remained fixed in a way that felt more vivid than the
rest of the evening.

In the weeks afterward, Richard found that the assault kept forcing its way back into his mind.
Unwanted thoughts would break into his day without warning, especially if he caught the smell of
alcohol on a customer, passed someone on the sidewalk who resembled the attacker, or thought he had
seen the same man again near that shopping center. Even when he knew he could not be certain it was
the offender, the jolt in his body was immediate. Those moments did not stay confined to the moment
itself; they changed how he moved through his days. Because the parking lot where it happened had
become intolerable to approach, he began reorganizing his sales routes to avoid that entire area. If
a client meeting would take him nearby, he found reasons to reschedule or asked a coworker to cover
it. When unwanted thoughts pushed in, he tried to clamp down on them and force his mind elsewhere,
and when that did not work he would go emotionally flat, telling himself there was no point feeling
anything at all if it only made him vulnerable again.

These same moments also cut directly into the way Richard had come to see himself. Each time he
smelled alcohol and was thrown back to the assault, or each time he caught sight of a man he feared
might be the offender, he was flooded not only with fear but with a harsh, familiar conclusion: that
the event happened because of how he handled himself. He had come to believe he should have walked
away faster, spoken differently, seen the danger sooner. In his mind, the fact that he had been
caught off guard said something shameful about him. He described feeling that there was something
wrong with him as a person, and that the man he had become since the assault was unrecognizable to
him—more guarded, smaller, less certain. The triggers did not just remind him of what happened; they
seemed to confirm his worst view of himself every time they appeared.

Over time, those beliefs made the intrusions more potent rather than less. Because Richard was so
preoccupied with the idea that he had failed in some essential way, his mind kept returning to the
attacker’s face and the moments just before the first shove, searching for the exact point at which
he had “caused” things to go wrong. Seeing someone who resembled the offender could start a long
spiral of mental replay. A whiff of alcohol in a restaurant during a sales lunch could pull him into
the same self-accusing loop, leaving him distracted and tense through the rest of the meeting. What
frightened him most was not only that the memories came back, but that they seemed to prove he was
no longer himself. The more defective he felt, the more easily his attention snagged on cues of
danger and on the assault itself.

This pattern had begun to constrict his life. His work had always depended on ease with strangers
and comfort moving from place to place, yet now he planned routes around avoidance rather than
efficiency, and his sales numbers had slipped. At home, his wife could feel the distance in him.
Richard did not want to talk about the assault because he was trying so hard not to think about it,
and he often sat through evenings dulled out and unreachable. He finally sought help after realizing
he had turned down another longstanding client simply because the office was near the parking lot
where he had been attacked. What brought him in was a mixture of shame and alarm: shame that he
believed he had let one incident remake him, and alarm that the smell of alcohol, the glimpse of a
familiar-looking man, or an unwanted thought could still strip away the life he had known. What he
hopes for now is to feel recognizable to himself again; what he fears is that this diminished
version of him is permanent.
