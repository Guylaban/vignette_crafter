Meena Patel is a 79-year-old South Asian American woman who came to treatment after decades of
insisting she had managed on her own. She is still married and, remarkably for her age, has remained
closely tied to aviation, flying in limited professional roles and spending much of her working life
in airports, hotels, and aircraft cabins. The trouble she traces back to childhood began in the home
where she grew up, where she repeatedly witnessed domestic violence. What stays with her most is not
a single orderly story but flashes: a slammed door, her mother’s frightened breathing, a man’s
raised voice, the sharp medicinal smell from a hospital visit after one assault, and, incongruously,
a song playing somewhere in the background that she still cannot hear without feeling her body
seize. Even now, when the memory comes, it does not feel like something long ago. It arrives as if
it is happening in the present, vivid and too real, in disconnected pieces that leave her straining
to make sense of them.

Over the years, certain situations came to carry the force of the danger itself. Sleeping alone in a
hotel room during work trips, or simply being in an unfamiliar place at night, could make Meena feel
exposed in a way she could not talk herself out of. The antiseptic smell of a jet bridge first-aid
kit or an airport medical office could bring back the hospital scenes with such immediacy that she
would stay up very late rather than risk lying in bed with those sensations. If a certain old song
came on in a terminal restaurant or taxi, she would become tense and sharp with people around her,
scanning even in her own home later that night and waking to check that she was safe. These episodes
also left her ashamed; she had come to believe that if a smell, a song, or a night alone could undo
her so quickly, it proved she was a lousy coper and could not rely on herself.

That sense of personal weakness hardened over time and began to organize how she responded. When
Meena told herself she could not deal with even the slightest upset, she reached for the few things
that seemed to blunt it: eating excessively late at night, keeping herself awake until exhaustion
overtook her, and privately imagining revenge against the people responsible for what happened in
her childhood. The revenge fantasies gave her a brief feeling of force instead of helplessness. More
often, though, they were followed by self-disgust and the conviction that her reactions showed
something deficient in her character. The same beliefs fed her vigilance. Because she no longer
trusted herself to cope, she felt she had to stay on guard, even at home, and she woke repeatedly in
the night to check doors, sounds, and shadows.

Her memories and her alertness now intensify one another. On nights when she wakes abruptly already
braced for danger, the fragments come back with unusual force; she is no longer an elderly pilot in
her own house but a girl in a violent home, caught in images that feel more real than ordinary
remembering. Afterward, she is certain this means she is getting weaker with age, less able to
master herself than other people. In that state, even small cues become charged. A faint
disinfectant smell in an airport corridor, a familiar melody over cabin speakers, or the prospect of
sleeping alone before an early flight can set off the whole sequence again because the memory is
already close to the surface.

Her ways of coping have also helped keep these cues potent. By staying up late, she meets the night
already overtired and more easily startled; by overeating, she feels uncomfortable and restless in
bed; by feeding fantasies of retaliation, she keeps the old violence emotionally active. Each of
these leaves her more likely to wake alert, more convinced she cannot handle distress, and more
susceptible to being thrown by the next song, smell, or solitary night away from home. She knows
this has shaped her marriage: her husband has long noticed that she is edgier at night and more
irritable after work trips, and she dreads his seeing how frightened she still becomes.

What finally brought Meena in was a recent overnight stay for work-related travel. Alone in a hotel,
she smelled antiseptic in the hallway and heard one of the songs from childhood downstairs in the
bar. She stayed awake until nearly dawn, ate continuously from the minibar, woke in brief snatches
to check the room lock, and spent the next day furious with herself. She sought help because she is
tired of living as if danger is always about to step through the door. What frightens her most is
not only the memories themselves, but the belief they have exposed something broken in her—that she
cannot rely on her own mind—and what she hopes for, quietly, is to sleep through the night without
bracing for the past.
