Daniel Weber, a 35-year-old special education teacher, traced the start of his difficulties to an
evening mugging eight months earlier. He had stayed late at school preparing individualized lesson
materials and left carrying a backpack full of student files. On the walk to his car, a man came up
behind him, shouted at him to hand everything over, and yanked him by the shoulder hard enough to
wrench his neck. Daniel remembered seeing, almost frame by frame, the man’s mouth open in a yell,
the angle of the parking lot lights, the pressure in his neck, and then the sudden arrival of police
lights after it was over. What stayed with him most was not only fear but a hot, humiliating rage
that he had been overpowered so quickly, mixed with guilt that he had been carrying school materials
and had, in his mind, “put himself in that position” by leaving alone so late.

In the weeks that followed, those details did not soften. Daniel described the memory as if he were
looking at still photographs laid out in front of him rather than remembering something finished and
past. Certain moments in daily life pulled those images up immediately. If a student raised his
voice sharply in the classroom, or if shouting echoed in the hallway, he was back in the parking lot
for a second, seeing the mugger’s face and feeling the surge of rage and guilt that came with it.
Neck pain was especially potent; if he turned too fast while helping a child and felt a twinge, the
image of being grabbed returned with startling clarity. The sight of police near school dismissal
could do the same. Even though he knew they were there for ordinary reasons, their presence called
up the aftermath of the robbery so vividly that he would momentarily feel as if the event were
happening again.

As Daniel tried to make sense of what had happened, he had come to believe it said something basic
and damning about him. Somebody else, he thought, would not have gotten into this situation. A more
capable man would have noticed danger earlier, reacted faster, fought back better. He spoke about
himself as inadequate in a flat, convinced way. From that position, being constantly on guard felt
necessary rather than excessive. He was certain he had to monitor everything because he was the kind
of person things happened to. Even in safe places he could not relax; the conviction that he was
vulnerable and insufficient kept his body braced, his attention fixed on possible threat, and his
eyes moving automatically to exits the moment he entered any room or building.

Those same harsh conclusions also colored the way he remembered the mugging. When the scene came
back to him, it did not come as a neutral replay. It arrived with self-accusation already attached:
he saw the images and immediately judged himself against an imagined other person who would have
handled it differently. The more inadequate he felt, the more guilt there was in the remembering,
and the more the memory burned with rage at the mugger and at himself.

By the time he sought treatment, the sense of current danger and the memories were feeding each
other tightly. Because he was so keyed up, any loud voice at school, any pull in his neck, any
glimpse of a police officer in the parking lot was noticed instantly and felt loaded. That constant
monitoring made the world seem full of reminders, and the reminders brought back the photographic
flashes of the attack. Once that happened, he would feel himself jolt into the same state
again—heart racing, body tight, unable to settle, scanning the classroom door and nearby exits as
though he might need to move his students to safety. He hated that he sometimes became less
available to the children when this happened; while a child was dysregulated and shouting, part of
Daniel was managing the lesson and part of him was no longer fully in the room.

Over months, this pattern deepened his view of himself. Each episode in which he froze internally or
stayed tense for hours afterward seemed to prove that he was inadequate and that other people would
cope better. That belief, in turn, kept him watchful and unable to let down, which meant he noticed
triggers even faster. He finally came for help after realizing he was organizing his entire workday
around how to avoid being caught off guard, yet still having flashback-like moments in class. What
frightened him most was the possibility that this was simply who he was now: a man who could not
protect himself, could not relax, and could not be fully present for the students who depended on
him. What he hoped, cautiously, was that he might learn how to walk into a room without first
looking for the exits and without feeling the parking lot come rushing back.
