Arjun Mehta is a 24-year-old American diplomat who was posted to a conflict zone overseas last year,
early in what had felt like a promising career. He had been proud of the assignment and had told
himself he could handle pressure. The event he returns to began at what was supposed to be a routine
evening gathering near the diplomatic compound, where alcohol had been served and the mood had
briefly seemed almost normal. Then there was a sudden explosion close enough to throw people to the
ground. In the confusion that followed, he remembers the sharp smell of spilled liquor mixed with
smoke and dust, the concussive bang repeating somewhere in the distance, and the helpless exposure
of being out in the open with strangers running in every direction. He says he knows exactly what
happened, in images and fragments so clear they feel preserved, but when he tries to describe it
aloud he loses words and goes silent, as if the experience is still too immediate to put into
language.

In the weeks afterward, Arjun did not feel that the danger had ended. Loud bangs in the city, even
harmless ones, could make him jerk and momentarily feel he was back at the blast site. Passing
someone who smelled of alcohol could bring with it not just anxiety but the odor from that night so
distinctly that he would stop and scan his surroundings before he knew where he was. What frightened
him most was how quickly ordinary moments turned: if he felt emotionally exposed in conversation,
especially when his girlfriend asked how he was doing, that sense of vulnerability itself became a
cue that something bad was about to happen. Those moments pulled up the memory in a vivid, sensory
way, and the memory arrived with such force that he became certain safety could not be trusted, no
matter what anyone around him said. Strangers approaching on the street or in government buildings
began to feel dangerous on sight.

As these reactions continued, Arjun started making meaning of them in ways that deepened his
distress. He had come to believe there was something about him that made the event happen, as though
his presence, his misjudgment, or his failure to anticipate danger had invited catastrophe. The more
vividly he remembered the blast and the smells and the scramble for cover, the more he concluded
that people, institutions, and life itself could not be relied upon; whatever faith he had in order
or protection had thinned into bitterness. At his lowest moments he spoke as if his future had
already narrowed, as if this one event had revealed that he would never feel normal or safe again.
Those beliefs made every trigger more potent. A bang was no longer only a noise, and the smell of
alcohol no longer only a reminder; each became proof, in his mind, that the world was still hostile
and that he himself was somehow marked for harm.

At work, this has become visible. As a diplomat he still has to move through secure buildings, meet
unfamiliar people, and attend receptions where alcohol is present, and he has begun dressing in
concealing layers even when they are out of place, wanting to draw as little attention as possible.
Before and after official events he pushes himself through punishing workouts, less for health than
to exhaust himself into numbness. On some nights he uses drugs to blunt the edge of what gets
stirred up. These strategies make sense to him in the moment: if he can stay covered, emptied out,
or chemically dulled, he might not feel so exposed. But they seem to tighten the problem. Entering a
crowded room after already bracing himself, he is more alert to strangers and less able to believe
reassurance; after using drugs, fragments of the night return in disorganized but vivid flashes,
with the smell sensations especially intrusive. He notices too that after he has been pushing
himself physically for days, he is more raw, and the next bang or whiff of alcohol catches him
faster.

Over months, the cycle has spread into his relationship. When his girlfriend tries to get close
emotionally, the vulnerability of being seen can itself set him on edge, and he pulls back,
convinced she cannot really keep him safe or understand what he cannot properly say. Feeling that
distance, he becomes more certain he has no future and that trust is gone for good, which leaves him
scanning more intensely for danger and reacting more sharply to sounds, smells, and unfamiliar
faces. Each new jolt seems to confirm the old memory, and each return of the memory strengthens his
conviction that the world is unsafe and he is permanently altered.

He is seeking help now because he is afraid he is becoming unrecognizable to himself and that he
will lose both his relationship and the career he worked for. He wants, cautiously, to believe he
can sit through a meeting or walk into a reception without bracing for disaster, but what he fears
most is that the next bang, the next smell of alcohol, or the next moment of emotional openness will
prove that the war never really ended for him.
