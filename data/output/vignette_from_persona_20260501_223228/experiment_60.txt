Marisol Alvarez is a 59-year-old physical therapist who came to treatment after years of telling
herself that what happened in her childhood was over and should not still have such force. She grew
up in a home where a caregiver could turn physically abusive without warning. What she remembers
most clearly is not a single coherent story but the feeling of being cornered in ordinary domestic
spaces, trapped by an adult’s movements and voice. Even now, when she tries to speak about it, the
memory does not unfold in a steady way. Small pieces break through suddenly—a hand closing a
doorway, the sound of footsteps coming toward her, the sense of having nowhere to stand safely—and
then vanish again. At other times she can describe the events in a flat, detached way, as if she
were talking about something that happened to another child. She has also come to feel that the
whole thing is strangely unreal, so extreme that part of her still cannot believe it truly happened
to her.

That disturbed quality of memory has shaped the way Marisol lives. When fragments of the past break
into her mind without warning, she becomes intent on shutting down anything that might stir her
further. She avoids situations she expects could become stressful, even when they are minor or
ordinary, because stress itself feels like the beginning of losing control. At home she cleans the
kitchen counters, rearranges drawers, and straightens already straightened objects until she can
feel the tightness inside her settle a little. On bad evenings she eats far past hunger, describing
it as a way to blunt herself and get through the next hour without more pieces of memory surfacing.

The same broken, unreal quality of recall has also left her moving through the present as though
danger can step out at any moment. In public, strangers approaching her in routine situations feel
menacing before she has time to reason herself out of it. At the outpatient rehabilitation clinic
where she works, she is most comfortable in treatment rooms where she can position herself with her
back against the wall and keep the doorway in view while speaking to patients or family members. If
someone comes up behind her in a hallway or enters her space unexpectedly, her whole body jolts with
the old certainty that she is not safe. Nights are not restful either. She wakes abruptly and checks
the apartment, scanning for signs that she is secure, as if vigilance might prevent something she
cannot fully name.

Over time, that constant sense of threat has narrowed her life further. She takes extra steps to
keep encounters predictable, choosing less busy grocery hours, avoiding social invitations she
imagines could become tense, and staying away from anything she suspects will leave her keyed up.
The cleaning has become more repetitive when she feels on edge, especially after a stranger has
stood too close to her at work or in a checkout line. Eating has become another way of dampening the
alarm when she gets home. These habits make sense to her in the moment because they give her
something to do with the fear, but they have also made her world smaller. At work she has become
known for preferring closed treatment spaces and for becoming visibly uncomfortable when schedules
change unexpectedly.

The threat also seems to pull the past closer. After a day in which she has been especially
watchful—keeping her back to the wall in the gym, tracking who is entering and leaving, feeling
activated by unfamiliar people—the memories are more likely to flicker in by night. She may wake
sharply alert and find a fragment already there, vivid for a second and then gone. The more unsafe
she feels in the present, the less the past stays in the past; it returns in disconnected pieces
that leave her unsettled and ashamed of how much power they still have.

Her attempts to manage this have not brought relief for long. The more she organizes, cleans,
avoids, and eats to push distress away, the less she allows herself to sit with any continuous sense
of what happened, and the memories remain disjointed, numb, and unbelievable. She has spent years
hoping that if she could just keep herself calm enough, the past would stay buried, yet the effort
to control it seems only to preserve it in fragments.

Marisol finally sought help after snapping at a longtime patient’s adult son who had approached her
from behind in the clinic. She was shaken not only by how frightened she felt, but by how quickly an
old image flashed through her and disappeared, leaving her detached for the rest of the afternoon.
She says she is tired of living as if ordinary people are dangerous and tired of arranging every
room around escape and visibility. What frightens her most is the idea that she will spend the rest
of her life braced against something invisible; what she hopes, cautiously, is to feel safe without
having to control every corner of her world.
