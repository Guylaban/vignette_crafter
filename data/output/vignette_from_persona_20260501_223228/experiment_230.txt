have carried an immediate sense of danger that feels to him less like remembering and more like
being thrown back into that corner.

stepped into that corridor. He had come to believe the assault happened because of the way he acted,
that he had made himself vulnerable by being polite, isolated, and inattentive. Alongside that self-
blame was a bitter conviction that what happened was deeply unfair and that nothing had been done
about it. He did not feel rage in any full, freeing way; instead he spoke of being flattened, as
though normal feeling had been switched off and might never come back. When he found himself
constantly scanning doorways, listening for footsteps, and jolting as if something terrible were
about to happen, those reactions seemed to prove his bleak conclusions. To Nabil, the fact that his
body kept acting as though he were under threat meant something fundamental had been damaged, and it
deepened his sense that he would never feel normal again.

His days gradually narrowed. At work, if an ambulance stopped outside and the siren echoed up
between the buildings, he would shut his laptop, wait in the restroom until the sound passed, or
leave by a back staircase to avoid the street entirely. If security staff or police officers were
visible in the lobby, or if he saw uniforms near the transit entrance, he would stand frozen until
he could find another route. He stopped going near the side corridor where the assault occurred,
even when that was the quickest way from the parking garage to his office. When meetings ran late
and he might be the only person left with another man in a conference room, he invented technical
reasons to reschedule or insisted on keeping the door open. These habits made sense to him because
he already felt he could be caught off guard anywhere; avoiding that block, authority figures, and
situations with men was the only way he could reduce the constant sense that danger was about to
break through.

Over time, those efforts to manage his fear began to organize his entire life. Nabil came to feel
safe only in a few tightly controlled places: his apartment, his locked car, and a small workstation
in the office when colleagues were nearby and the blinds were closed. Because he kept rerouting
around the area of the assault and timing his arrivals to avoid police, building security, or
emergency vehicle traffic, more and more ordinary parts of downtown took on the same charge. The
sight of an ambulance near any curb, the sound of sirens from several blocks away, even noticing a
uniformed person across the street could set off the same immediate alarm. His world had become
something to scan continuously, and the more he organized himself around escape and avoidance, the
more convincing it felt that danger was everywhere.

That shrinking of his life also fed the way he thought about what had happened. Missing team
lunches, declining after-hours work, and leaving abruptly when sirens sounded left him ashamed and
isolated, and in that isolation his self-blame hardened. He told himself that a stronger man would
have reported it differently, fought back differently, recovered differently. Each accommodation he
made to fear became, in his mind, evidence of permanent damage. He could see the unfairness of the
assault clearly, especially when he passed the hospital district and thought about how public and
unremarked the setting had been, but because nothing in his life had widened again, the unfairness
curdled into the certainty that nothing would ever be put right.

By the time he sought help, his work had started to suffer. He was asking to remain fully remote,
not because coding had become difficult, but because the office required too much vigilance:
listening for sirens, checking who was in the hallway, calculating how to avoid being alone with men
or crossing paths with uniforms downstairs. He came to treatment exhausted by how small his safe
world had become. What frightened him most was not only another assault, but the possibility that
this constricted, emotionally blunted version of himself was now permanent. What he hoped, though he
said it cautiously, was that he might stop living as if every siren were the start of it happening
again.
