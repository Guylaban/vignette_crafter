David is a 52-year-old school principal who had worked for years in the same public middle school, a
job he once described as busy but predictable, until he was assaulted late one afternoon on campus.
The building had mostly emptied out after dismissal. He had stayed behind for a parent meeting and
was walking from his office toward a side exit when a man confronted him in a dim stretch of hallway
near a maintenance alcove. David remembers the suddenness of it more than a clear sequence: the
flash of movement, the man stepping too close, the shock of being shoved back and pinned awkwardly
against the wall. Even now, when the memory comes up, his body seems to take over before his mind
does; his shoulders tense and draw inward, and he feels himself fixed again in that trapped
position, as if the assault is happening in his muscles rather than in the past.

In the days after, what unsettled him was how unevenly the event stayed with him. If someone asked
him to explain exactly what happened from beginning to end, he found himself struggling. He could
not easily call up the whole memory on purpose, and this left him feeling embarrassed and
frustrated, especially as an educator used to being organized and articulate. But the memory did not
stay away. Instead, it returned in a repetitive, involuntary way, “like a film,” as he put it,
running through the same fragments over and over: the hallway, the man’s proximity, the instant of
being cornered. Those replays did not feel like recollection in the ordinary sense. They seemed to
seize him, and with them came the physical impression of being forced back into the same posture he
had been in during the assault.

Over the following months, those looping, bodily memories began to shape the way David moved through
his days at school. Entering the auditorium for an assembly, walking into a classroom observation,
stepping into the district office for a meeting, he would immediately scan for exits before he took
in anything else. In his own building, where he was supposed to project steadiness, his eyes went
first to doorways, blind corners, heavy objects on desks, and anything that might serve as a weapon
if someone came at him again. A metal stapler, a pair of scissors in the front office, a fire
extinguisher mounted by a door: these caught his attention automatically. The repeated film-like
returns of the assault, along with the sensation of his body being put back into that pinned
position, made the threat feel current rather than over, and he had come to believe that staying
alert in this way was the only reason he might not be caught off guard a second time.

This was most obvious in the places that should have felt most familiar. During crowded parent
nights, David positioned himself where he could see the main doors. In staff meetings he took seats
with a clear path out. If a visitor stepped unexpectedly into the office doorway, he startled so
sharply that he felt humiliated afterward, sometimes covering it by pretending he had simply been
distracted. The startle itself would often be followed by a quick sweep of the room—who was near
him, what was in their hands, where he could move if he needed to. Because he could not reliably
summon the memory deliberately, each uninvited replay seemed to arrive with its own authority,
convincing him again that danger could erupt without warning. The more the assault returned in that
repetitive, bodily way, the more he monitored his surroundings as if vigilance were part of his job.

His wife has noticed that he now studies restaurants the same way, pausing at the entrance and
choosing seats based on escape routes rather than comfort. David does not describe this as fear in a
simple sense. It feels to him like necessity, like good judgment learned the hard way. At the same
time, he is exhausted by how little rest his mind allows him. What finally brought him to seek help
was a recent incident at school when a student dropped a metal binder near him in a hallway; he
jolted so violently that several students stared, and he spent the next hour replaying the assault
in fragments while trying to carry on with his day. He was shaken not only by the reaction but by
the realization that the school he leads now feels organized around possible danger. What frightens
him most is that this may be permanent—that he will keep living as if every room needs an escape
plan, and that the memory will go on finding him even when he cannot find it himself.
