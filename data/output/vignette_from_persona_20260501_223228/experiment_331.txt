Eleanor is a 73-year-old widow who has made her living for years as a painter, working alone in a
converted room at the back of her house. Two years ago, she was caught in a man-made disaster when
an intoxicated man forced his way into a small downtown gallery during an evening artists’ reception
where several of her paintings were hanging. He shoved past guests, grabbed at people, and
overturned bottles and glasses as he moved through the room. Eleanor remembers being knocked down
beside a display wall, pinned awkwardly on one hip with her arm trapped under her, unable at first
to get up while people screamed and cried around her. What stayed with her most vividly was the
smell of spilled alcohol on his breath and clothes, the feel of strangers’ hands pulling at her to
move, and the sound of sobbing in the room as everyone tried to understand whether more danger was
coming.

Since then, those same cues have become charged in ways that feel immediate rather than symbolic. If
someone reaches out and touches her unexpectedly, she stiffens and her body reacts before she can
think. If she catches the smell of alcohol on a customer, a neighbor, or even drifting from the
street outside her studio, she becomes sharply keyed up, scanning and listening as though someone
may be about to come through the door. The sound of crying, even on television or from another room,
affects her in much the same way. In those moments she grows irritable and short with people, and at
night she lies awake listening for sounds that might mean someone is entering her home.

The triggers do not stay in the present for long. The smell of alcohol or the pressure of a hand on
her arm can bring back the gallery with a brutal clarity, “like photographs,” as she describes it,
fixed images rather than a story: the tipped glasses, the floor at eye level, shoes moving past her
face. When these memories come, her body curls into the same cramped position she had been in that
night, as if she is back on the floor with her arm pinned. Crying is especially potent; hearing it
can pull up the scene so quickly that she feels she is dropped into it without warning.

Over time Eleanor has come to believe that the disaster broke something permanent in her. She says
her old assumptions about people and ordinary safety are gone, that the world no longer works in any
reliable way. She has also begun to fear that she will never feel normal emotion again, only
agitation, numbness, or guilt. That belief leaves her exquisitely sensitive to the very cues she
dreads. She notices tears in a face immediately, the brush of contact in a crowded place, the
faintest trace of alcohol, already bracing because she is certain these moments mean she is about to
lose control of herself again. The same convictions shape how she remembers the event: whenever the
images return, they are saturated with guilt that she survived, that she froze, that others in the
room were more frightened or more hurt than she was. Each recollection seems to confirm her sense
that her life has narrowed into something damaged and barely worth living.

The memories themselves deepen that conviction. Because they arrive as stark, frozen pictures with
the shame and bodily helplessness intact, they do not feel like something finished. After an
episode, Eleanor is left thinking not only that the world is unsafe, but that she herself has been
permanently altered by what happened. She finds herself saying that she is no longer the woman she
was, and in her bleakest moments she is not sure life is worth much now. Once she has been pulled
into those images, the ordinary world feels contaminated by reminders; touch becomes harder to
tolerate, crying harder to hear, and the smell of alcohol nearly unbearable.

Her days have gradually organized around trying not to set any of this off. She no longer watches
the news if there is any chance of coverage of public violence or disasters like the one at the
gallery. In her studio she keeps the radio off and works constantly, telling herself that if she can
keep her mind occupied mixing paint, stretching canvas, cleaning brushes, then the images will stay
down. When they press in anyway, she tries hard not to think about them, sometimes painting late
into the night out of sheer mental exhaustion. The cost has been visible. She avoids exhibition
openings and declines visitors to her workspace because she cannot tolerate accidental touch or the
possibility that someone may arrive smelling of wine. Customers have found her abrupt, and she knows
she has become harsher and more easily angered than she ever was before.

At night, after forcing the day into busyness, she often sleeps poorly, listening for sounds at the
doors and windows. The vigilance leaves her frayed, and being so on edge seems only to harden her
belief that she is beyond repair. What finally brought Eleanor to treatment was a recent incident
when a fellow artist embraced her after a local show and she jerked away, smelled wine on the
woman’s breath, and spent the rest of the evening shaken, angry, and flooded with images of the
gallery floor. She came in frightened that this is how the rest of her life will be—guarded,
sleepless, emotionally deadened—and hoping, though only faintly, that the memories might stop
feeling as if they are happening all over again.
