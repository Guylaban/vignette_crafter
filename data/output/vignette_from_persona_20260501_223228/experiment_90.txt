cars like the ones used around the place where he was taken, the atmosphere of command carried by

should feel good but suddenly make him uneasy. Even now, he says that if he catches sight of a
similar car on the street or in the hotel parking area, his body reacts before he can think.

him, Mateo finds a reason to leave the room or changes the subject quickly. He has become careful
around security staff and police officers who pass through the hotel lobby, keeping his eyes down
and taking longer routes to avoid being near uniforms. When he notices a car that resembles the ones
linked in his mind to the torture, or when related media content appears on a television in a guest
room, he feels cornered by the reminder and reaches for cigarettes more often, stepping outside on
breaks to smoke until the intensity drops. These habits make sense to him as ways to keep himself
from being overwhelmed, even though they increasingly dictate where he goes and what he can
tolerate.

The reminders do not only push him to avoid; they also make him feel under immediate threat. A
similar car cruising slowly near the hotel entrance can leave him tense for the rest of his shift,
scanning faces and bracing when strangers come toward him in the hallway. If a news clip or
dramatized scene related to torture or captivity flashes on a guest-room television, he becomes
keyed up that night and wakes repeatedly to check locks and windows, as if danger may already have
followed him home. What confuses him most is that even positive emotions can set off the same alarm.
On the rare evenings when he laughs with a friend or feels a moment of relief, the feeling can
suddenly turn suspicious, as if letting his guard down would be reckless; the swing itself leaves
him restless and watchful.

and when he cannot avoid them he smokes beforehand or immediately afterward to steady himself. He
wakes during the night to check whether he is safe, and the poor sleep leaves him more raw the next
day, more likely to flinch at touch and more likely to read an unfamiliar face as dangerous. In that
state, the world offers him more potential warnings, and he notices cars, uniforms, and other
reminders almost automatically.

Over time, the very strategies he relies on seem to keep these cues vivid. Because Mateo avoids
conversations when others mention events like his own, even indirect reminders remain unprocessed

part of the same loop: stepping outside for cigarettes places him in parking lots and on sidewalks

alarm. The more he organizes his day around not being caught off guard, the more easily he is caught
off guard.

and still felt unsafe that night, waking again and again to check the apartment. Divorced and
increasingly isolated, he is frightened that his life is becoming organized entirely around escape

without feeling that danger is already beside him.
