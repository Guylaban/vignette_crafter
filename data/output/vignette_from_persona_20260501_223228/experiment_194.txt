Anna Weber, a 35-year-old counselor, came to treatment months after a car accident in which another
person was seriously injured. She had been driving home after a long day, replaying difficult
conversations with clients, when she moved through an intersection and struck a cyclist she had not
seen in time. What remained with her was not a single continuous memory but jagged fragments: the
sudden shape in front of her headlights, the sound of impact, a bicycle wheel twisted at an
impossible angle, someone shouting for an ambulance. She could not put the sequence together into a
clear story, even privately. When the memory broke through, it brought a wave of grief so intense it
felt almost physical, as if she were back at the roadside knowing that someone else’s life had been
permanently altered.

From the beginning, Anna had come to believe that the injury happened because of the way she acted.
She was certain she should have protected the cyclist and failed. That conviction was not abstract;
it lived in the way she talked about herself, as someone who had done harm when she should have been
the person who prevented it. Over time, those thoughts darkened into a broader sense that nothing in
her life held meaning anymore. The more she judged herself as unforgivably responsible, the more on
edge she became. Nights were the worst: she lay awake replaying what she did and did not do, flooded
with guilt and horror, listening for every sound outside the house as if danger were still close.

Her work as a counselor made this especially hard to contain. She was used to meeting clients with
direct attention, but after the accident she began avoiding eye contact in session, looking down at
her notes or toward the clock because another person’s gaze made her feel exposed and accused. In
the medical office building where she worked, the distant sound of an ambulance outside or seeing a
patient arrive with a brace was enough to make her body tense before she had fully registered why.
She became superalert in those moments, scanning hallways, flinching at sirens, feeling a sick rush
of dread and shame. That state of watchfulness fed her conviction that she was dangerous or morally
ruined, and each fresh surge of fear seemed to confirm that the accident had revealed something
terrible about her.

The memory itself remained disorganized in part because Anna fought so hard against it. When images
of the intersection surfaced, she tried immediately to push them away and not think about the trauma
at all. She also began going out of her way to avoid hospitals, ambulances, and even routine
physical examinations, because any medical cue could bring back the bicycle, the shouting, the sense
of irreversible injury. In the short term that helped her get through the day, but it left the event
sealed off in disconnected pieces. Because she would not linger with the memory, she never arrived
at a fuller account of what happened; instead the fragments returned abruptly, each one carrying the
same raw grief.

Her sense of threat and her efforts to manage it gradually tightened around each other. The more
frightened, guilty, and keyed up she felt, the more she suppressed thoughts and steered away from
medical reminders and from the eyes of other people. Those habits narrowed her world. She started
taking longer routes to work to avoid passing the hospital entrance. At home, her husband noticed
that she would turn away during conversations, especially if he asked about the accident or about
how she was doing. Sleep grew more broken. In the small hours, hypervigilant and exhausted, the
fragmented images were harder to keep out, and because they arrived without context they felt
immediate and unbearable. Each recurrence left her more shaken the next day, more determined not to
think, and less able to make sense of what had happened.

Over the months, that pattern deepened her despair. Being unable to form a coherent story made the
accident feel unfinished and ever-present, which kept her body on guard; feeling constantly on guard
only strengthened her belief that she had failed in the most basic duty to protect another person.
By the time Anna sought help, she was still going to work but doing so mechanically, with a growing
fear that she could sit compassionately with clients while feeling that her own life no longer meant
anything. What finally brought her in was realizing she had begun avoiding a routine medical
appointment and then crying in her car when she heard an ambulance pass. She said she was afraid
that if she ever let herself remember the whole event, the guilt would destroy her, but she was also
beginning to hope that if she could finally face it, the memory might stop ruling every part of her
life.
