buildings, schools, and municipal properties where she is sent into boiler rooms, maintenance
corridors, and occupied homes to fix leaks or replace fixtures. She grew up in a household marked by
physical abuse from a caregiver, a person whose voice could turn sharp without warning and whose
footsteps, shouted use of Amina’s name, and sudden noises in the house signaled danger before the
blows came. What stayed with her was not only being hit, but the atmosphere of being singled out and
called over in that voice, already bracing before contact. Even now, hearing a woman call her name
in a certain clipped tone, catching a voice with a similar rough cadence, or hearing related
household sounds can stop her in place with the old sense that something is about to happen.

Over the years, those moments have come to feel like confirmations of what she has long believed
about herself. When a tenant calls “Amina” down a hallway and the sound lands wrong, or when a
supervisor’s voice echoes through a utility room in a way that resembles the caregiver’s, she does
not experience it as a neutral reminder. She immediately feels there is something wrong with her as
a person, as if her body is reacting because she is the same flawed child she once was. In those
moments she has come to believe the abuse happened because of the sort of person she is, and the
rush of shame can hollow out the rest of the day. She describes a drop inside herself afterward, a
sense that nothing in her life feels meaningful anymore, as though the trigger strips away her adult
life and leaves only the old conclusion that she was the one who invited mistreatment.

Those beliefs have also made her world more crowded with reminders. Because she is convinced at some
level that she is defective and marked, she listens for danger in voices and in the way her name is
spoken, scanning for that familiar contempt before she is even fully aware she is doing it. At work
she notices instantly when a building manager has a commanding tone or when someone calls to her
from another room, and what might be ordinary workplace noise becomes charged. Amina says it is as
if her mind is always proving to her that the past is still present; once she starts from the idea
that something is wrong with her, more voices seem menacing, more sounds seem loaded, and even
hearing related names in passing can catch in her chest.

To manage this, she has developed habits that make sense to her in the moment. She dwells for hours
on what other people have done to her, replaying the caregiver’s cruelty and extending that same
focus to current supervisors, landlords, or building staff she experiences as dismissive. She avoids
authority figures whenever she can, lingering in her van before entering a job site if she knows a
facilities director in uniform is waiting, and she asks coworkers to handle calls in places where
she expects a stern manager or security staff. She also steers away from anything that reminds her
of the perpetrator, skipping jobs in certain neighborhoods and leaving rooms when a resident’s voice
or manner resembles the caregiver’s.

But these strategies have tightened the same painful beliefs she is trying to escape. The more she
circles around what was done to her, the more fixed she becomes on the idea that her life has been
organized by other people’s harm and that there is nothing solid or meaningful underneath it.
Avoiding authority figures has gradually fed her sense that she is weak, damaged, and unable to
stand in ordinary situations other tradespeople handle without a second thought. Each time she turns
down a work order because the client’s voice unsettles her, or sends her apprentice to speak to a
building superintendent while she stays bent over her tools, she feels temporarily safer, yet
afterward more convinced that something is fundamentally wrong with her.

Those same habits have made the triggers more immediate and more frequent. Because she avoids
perpetrator cues so vigilantly, even small encounters have become potent: a voice over an intercom,
a resident using a related name, a sharp sound in an apartment can feel unbearable partly because
she has spent so much effort trying never to be near anything similar. Her avoidance of authority
means that in the plumbing environments where she cannot avoid them—city inspectors, school
administrators, uniformed maintenance chiefs—she arrives already tense and listening for danger. Her
rumination keeps the old voice vivid, so that current voices easily merge with it.

By the time Amina sought help, the effect on her work and home life was hard to ignore. She was
taking fewer assignments, arguing with her husband when he encouraged her to “let it go,” and
privately feeling that there was no point to anything beyond getting through the day. What
frightened her most was not only the memories of childhood, but the fear that she was right about
herself—that she was damaged in some essential way and would keep building her life around dodging
voices, names, and people who made her feel small. What she hopes for, though she says it
cautiously, is to be able to hear her own name called at work without feeling as if she has become
that trapped child again.
