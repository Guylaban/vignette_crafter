Jason is a 39-year-old construction worker who came to treatment after months of telling himself he
should be able to handle what he had seen. The event happened on a job site outside Denver, where he
had been working demolition on an older commercial building with a small crew he knew well. Late in
the afternoon, part of a concrete section gave way without warning. A younger coworker on the lower
level was trapped in the collapse before anyone could reach him. Jason was close enough to see the
instant of impact and the angle of the man’s body afterward, close enough to smell the pulverized
concrete and metal dust in the air, and close enough that when he shouted for help he could still
taste the grit in his mouth. What stayed with him afterward was not a coherent story so much as a
series of frozen images: the flash of the collapse, the gray cloud, the man’s boot caught at an
unnatural angle. Even when he tried not to think about it, the memory came back in pictures, with
the smell of dust and hot machinery and the dry, chalky taste returning so vividly that he would
stop what he was doing and swallow hard as if the debris were in his mouth again.

In the weeks after the accident, Jason found that what unsettled him most was not only that someone
had died in front of him, but that the whole event seemed to prove something awful about the world
and about himself. He had come to believe that the world did not work in any dependable way, that
disaster could tear through an ordinary workday without warning, and that when it did he was
powerless to stop it. He said more than once that if he had been faster, stronger, or better, maybe
something would have been different, and this settled into a broader conviction that he was
inadequate. Those conclusions seemed to make the memory hit even harder. The images did not feel
like a past event filed away; they felt like evidence, returning again and again to confirm that
nothing is safe and that he cannot protect anyone, including himself.

That way of understanding what happened shaped how he managed himself afterward. Jason became
determined never to feel anger, especially at work. He would clamp down on it when he felt angry at
the company, at the site supervisor, or at the randomness of what had happened, because anger seemed
dangerous and useless to him; in his mind, it only underscored how little control he really had. He
also started gambling in the evenings, sometimes driving to a casino after a shift, sometimes
betting online for hours in his apartment. He described it less as fun than as a way to get out of
his head or feel something sharp enough to drown everything else out. At other times he would
deliberately try to blank out altogether, staring at a wall or at the television until he felt far
away from his body. These habits made sense to him because he was certain he could not afford to
feel what was underneath: helplessness, humiliation, and the sense that the world had become
fundamentally unsafe.

The job site itself became a place where the cycle tightened. The smell of concrete dust, the sight
of a worker below scaffolding, even the taste of dry air in his throat could bring back the scene in
an instant. On those days the memory arrived first as an image, then with the smell and taste so
strongly that he felt pulled backward into the collapse. Once that happened, he was even more
convinced that the danger had never really passed and that he was still the same man who had stood
there unable to stop it. He would leave work and gamble longer, or sit in his truck trying to
disappear into a numb, detached state before driving home. The more the memory returned in those
sensory fragments, the more he treated his own mind as something to outrun, and the less confidence
he had that he could handle ordinary life.

His divorce had happened before the accident, and he lives alone now, which has left him with few
people who see how much his world has narrowed. Coworkers have noticed that he is quieter and harder
to read. He does his tasks, but he avoids conversation and keeps everything inside so tightly that
even minor frustrations seem to pass through him without expression. What finally brought him in was
a recent moment on site when a cloud of dust rose after a wall was cut and he suddenly smelled the
same dry, metallic air and tasted grit at the back of his throat. He had to step away and could not
make himself go back in for nearly an hour. He came to treatment frightened that he is losing his
grip and that these memories will keep proving he is weak and incapable, but also faintly hoping
that if he can understand why he keeps going blank and chasing distraction, he might not have to
live as if the collapse is still happening.
