Mateo Alvarez was 24 when he sought treatment, a quiet, careful QA engineer who had always taken
pride in catching problems before they caused damage. The trauma that now organized his days began
eight months earlier during what should have been an ordinary evening downtown. He had been meeting
friends near a busy entertainment district when a vehicle was deliberately driven into a crowd
outside a concert venue. Mateo had been only yards away. He remembered the sound first—the engine
revving too hard, people yelling, the impact and scraping metal—and then the confusion of bodies
moving in every direction. One of his friends fell, and for several seconds Mateo was frozen between
running toward him and trying to pull another stranger out of the path. Police cars, sirens, and
shouted commands arrived almost immediately, but what stayed with him was the sick certainty that in
those moments he had not done enough. He had come to believe that he should have protected people
and failed.

In the first weeks afterward, Mateo found that certain moments pulled him back into that night with
unnerving force. If coworkers discussed a car crash in the break room, or if a related story came up
in the news on a monitor at work, he felt his surroundings turn strangely flat and unreal, as if he
were no longer fully in his own body. The same thing happened when a podcast mentioned an accident
during his commute, or when a headline about another vehicle attack flashed across his phone. In
those moments his mind did not simply register a reminder; it leapt straight to what he had not
done. He would think, with a fresh stab of conviction, that he had failed the people beside him and
that decent people could be harmed at any time by anyone. A passing discussion of accidents could
leave him sitting at his desk staring at a bug report, feeling detached and silently telling himself
that nothing good could really be ahead now.

Those reactions gradually shaped how he managed his life. When crash discussions started at work,
Mateo would put in his earbuds, leave for coffee, or change the subject abruptly. As a QA engineer
he worked in an open-plan office where news clips sometimes played on communal screens and coworkers
casually traded stories about disasters and public safety failures; he began timing his breaks to
avoid those spaces and stopped joining team lunches if certain colleagues were there. He also quit
watching television and stopped reading general news because he had learned how quickly related
coverage could make him feel unreal and trapped in the memory. After any such trigger, he often
spent hours in counterfactual loops, replaying the scene and imagining how life would have been
different if he had crossed the street earlier, shouted sooner, pulled his friend faster, or never
gone downtown at all. Avoiding reminders made sense to him because he was sure that once he started
thinking about the attack, he would not be able to stop.

His beliefs hardened over the following months. Mateo was certain now that you could never know who
would harm you; the ordinariness of that evening before the attack had become, in his mind, proof
that danger arrived without warning. Police were especially difficult for him. It was not that he
blamed them in any simple way, but the sight of uniforms or patrol cars brought back the cordoned
streets, the flashing lights, and his own helplessness. When officers stood near the transit station
outside his office building, he crossed to another entrance or waited until they were gone. In the
same stretch of time he withdrew further into thoughts about the life that should have continued
uninterrupted—the version of himself who would still meet friends downtown, casually stream movies,
and scroll headlines without scanning for catastrophe. The more convinced he became that he had
failed others, that harm was unpredictable, and that nothing good could happen anymore, the more he
organized his days around not seeing, not hearing, and not being reminded.

By the time he came to treatment, the cost was becoming harder to contain. His manager had noticed
that he avoided common areas and missed informal meetings where project issues were often resolved
quickly. Friends had stopped inviting him out after he repeatedly declined if the plan involved
crowds, public screens, or busy streets with a police presence nearby. Mateo described feeling as if
his world had narrowed to work, home, and mental reruns of the night everything changed. What
finally brought him in was a recent incident at the office: a conversation about a highway pileup
played on a nearby screen while colleagues commented on it, and he suddenly felt detached, almost
unreal, before spending the rest of the afternoon consumed by the thought that he should have saved
someone that night and that this was all his life would be now. He said he wanted help because he
was afraid that if he kept living this way, he would lose whatever future was left, but what
frightened him most was the possibility that he deserved exactly that.
