than a year of living as though danger could break through an ordinary day without warning. The
assault happened at dusk, near the end of a long job. He had been locking up a small commercial site
when he heard a door bang behind him and turned too late. What he remembers most clearly are a few
sharp pictures: the door swinging open, a man stepping fast into his space, a flash of a raised arm,
the concrete coming up toward him. The rest is broken and missing. He cannot put the event into a
full sequence, only return to isolated moments that arrive with a physical jolt, and whenever they
do he is flooded with guilt, convinced he should have seen it coming or handled it differently.

In the weeks after, dusk became the hardest part of the day. As the light starts to change, Mika
feels his body tighten before he has consciously thought about why. The sound of a door opening
sharply at a worksite, or slamming in a store, can pull up one of those frozen images so abruptly
that for a moment he feels detached and unreal, as if he is no longer fully in the present. In those
moments he does not just remember the assault; he lands again in the belief that it says something
shameful about him. He has come to believe the attack happened because of the kind of man he
is—careless, weak, the sort of person who ends up in that situation when someone better would not
have. By evening, especially at the same time of day it happened, his thoughts darken quickly into
the sense that nothing ahead of him is likely to improve.

Those meanings have made him live as if vulnerability is visible on him. At work, if someone moves
suddenly in the edge of his vision, he jerks back as though bracing for another blow. If a coworker
touches his shoulder without warning, he reacts with instant alarm and then embarrassment. He avoids
unfamiliar buildings whenever he can, telling himself he needs to know exits and blind spots before
he can relax, but underneath is a conviction that he is the kind of person things happen to. The
broken, snapshot quality of the memory keeps that danger feeling alive; because he cannot fill in
what happened between the fragments, he treats what he cannot remember as further evidence that he
was overwhelmed and failed.

Mika has built his evenings around not having to think. When dusk approaches, or when a door bangs
and leaves him briefly unreal and shaky, he buries himself in his phone for hours, scrolling,
gaming, clicking through anything that can keep his mind occupied. On worse nights he drinks until
he feels dulled enough not to see the pictures so vividly. He has stopped watching films or shows
with assaults or tense confrontations because he knows how quickly they can set him off. The same
happens when the memory intrudes on its own: once the images start, and the guilt comes with them,
he reaches for a screen or alcohol to push them away.

These habits have narrowed his life and deepened the conclusions he draws about himself. He is less
present with his partner in the evenings, sitting beside her but absorbed in his phone or drinking
past conversation. He feels ashamed of how much time disappears this way, and that shame confirms
his hopelessness: in his mind, a man who has to manage himself like this is not someone with much of
a future. The drinking and constant distraction also leave him less grounded, so the next day he is
more keyed up, more likely to react to sudden movement or touch, and more dependent on scanning
whether a place feels safe enough to enter.

Over time, the strategies meant to keep the assault out of mind have made the triggers more central
in his life. He organizes routes and jobs to avoid unfamiliar places, but that means doors slamming
at the sites he does attend, and the approach of dusk on every workday, stand out even more. He
notices the time constantly. He has begun to dread the first hint of feeling detached, because he
takes that sensation itself as a sign that he is slipping back toward the assault and toward those
same accusing thoughts about what sort of person he is. When he finally sought help, it was after
snapping away from a coworker who touched him unexpectedly and then sitting in his truck after work,
unable to face going into another building. What frightened him most was the sense that his world
was shrinking around a handful of moments he could not fully remember, and what he hopes for is
simple: to get through the end of the day without feeling that the assault is still waiting for him.
