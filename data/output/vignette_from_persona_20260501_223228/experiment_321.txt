At 80, Meera Patel still worked three days a week in the dental office she had run for decades, a
small practice in a busy commercial strip where ambulance traffic and city noise were part of the
background. The event that divided her life into before and after happened two years earlier, when
she was caught in a bombing outside a downtown federal building after leaving a continuing education
seminar. She remembered the force of it less as a sequence than as a few fixed impressions: a blast
that threw people to the pavement, the sharp chemical smell of smoke and burning wiring, sirens
arriving almost immediately and growing louder from every direction, and the helplessness of being
pinned behind a concrete barrier while armed officers shouted orders and kept everyone in place.
What stayed with her was not only fear of dying, but the feeling of being trapped while strangers
with authority controlled where she could move, telling her to stay down and not to leave.

In the weeks that followed, those same cues began to seize her in ordinary life. If a fire truck
passed her office with its siren on, she felt her body lock and her attention narrow as if she were
back on the sidewalk waiting for another explosion. The smell of smoke from a restaurant kitchen, a
neighbor’s fireplace, even scorched wiring from a faulty outlet in the building could make her heart
race. Being directed sharply by anyone in a position of authority—airport security, a police
officer, even a brusque building manager—carried a similar charge. She had come to experience these
moments as warnings that danger could break open again without notice, and she responded by changing
how she moved through the world. In her operatory she began positioning herself so she always had a
clear line to the door. At restaurants she insisted on the table nearest the exit. If she had to go
downtown for a specialist appointment, she would first study where the doors were and sit closest to
one. She also stopped going anywhere near the federal building where the bombing happened, driving
several extra miles to avoid that part of the city altogether, because she was certain that if she
were there again she would not be able to keep herself safe.

These habits gradually hardened into a daily way of managing herself. Meera scanned constantly: the
waiting room window, unfamiliar cars idling too long, the tone of people’s voices, the smell in the
hallway, the location of every door. At work, when a patient’s family member raised their voice at
the front desk or an emergency vehicle passed outside, she would interrupt herself mid-charting and
look toward the entrance, already calculating how quickly she could leave. Staying near exits and
checking every environment felt necessary to her, but it also kept the possibility of danger in the
foreground. The more she searched for signs of trouble, the more quickly she noticed sirens, smoke,
or any interaction that carried even a hint of someone else having control over her. In that sense,
the precautions that were supposed to settle her made those cues feel more immediate and more
loaded.

That pattern had become especially visible in her dental practice. Dentistry already required close
attention and a confined room, and Meera now arranged the operatory stool and instrument cart so
nothing blocked her path to the door. She preferred not to schedule patients in the chair furthest
from the hallway because she felt too enclosed there. If she smelled something faintly burnt from
sterilization equipment or heard sirens swell on the street outside, she became tense and
distracted, sometimes pausing treatment to step into the corridor and look around. Her staff had
noticed that she often chose treatment rooms nearest the building exit and repeatedly asked whether
the back door was unlocked. What frightened her most was the possibility of being trapped again,
unable to decide for herself where to go.

Over months, avoiding the bombing site also expanded her sense that parts of the city were unsafe.
She turned down invitations if they required going downtown and stopped attending in-person
professional meetings held near government buildings. Because she spent so much effort monitoring
and positioning herself, she was increasingly aware of every siren and every trace of smoke, as if
her mind had been trained to catch them before anything else. Even being told at a courthouse
checkpoint to stand in line and wait during a minor administrative errand left her shaky for the
rest of the day; after that she avoided that entire block too.

Meera finally sought help after a patient had a medical scare during a routine appointment. When
paramedics were called and sirens approached the office, she found herself backing toward the exit
before she fully realized what she was doing, scanning the hallway instead of staying focused on the
room. She was deeply embarrassed afterward and troubled that her staff had stepped in around her.
She said she did not want to retire with people thinking she had become incapable, but she was
equally afraid that if she could not stop living by doors, escape routes, and constant vigilance,
her world would keep shrinking to whatever felt easiest to flee.
