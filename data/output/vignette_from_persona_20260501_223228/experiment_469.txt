prayer and discipline should be enough. Two years earlier, she had been accompanying parish
volunteers at a public religious festival when an explosion tore through the crowd. She remembers
the force of people surging, the scream of metal, and then the sight that has stayed with her most
vividly: blood on clothing, blood on the pavement, blood on her own hands as she tried to help
someone on the ground. In the confusion she felt pain shoot through her side where she had been
knocked against a barricade. What she can recount deliberately is oddly patchy; when she tries to
sit down and tell the story from beginning to end, she loses pieces of it and feels ashamed by how
inaccessible it becomes. But at other times the memory arrives without warning and with brutal
completeness, as if she is back inside the scene with all her senses engaged at once.

Since the bombing, certain moments have become especially potent. If she wakes from a nightmare
still half inside it, she spends several minutes disoriented and certain something terrible is about
to happen. If the old pain flares in her side when she bends or carries supplies in the convent, her
body seems to recognize danger before her mind catches up. The most immediate trigger is blood: a
cut finger in the kitchen, a stain while helping an elderly parishioner, even the bright red of a
sanitary pad in the laundry can stop her cold. In those moments the festival does not feel like the
past. The images force themselves into her mind and she feels horror, dread, and a sickening
certainty that nowhere is truly safe.

Her daily life as a nun has narrowed around that sense of threat. When the sisters go into the city
for ministry, María Elena studies every chapel hall, parish office, and community center until she
knows where the side doors are and how quickly she could leave. She cannot settle in a room until
she has identified an escape route. Crowded masses, feast days, and public prayer events now feel
intolerable; she has become convinced that danger hides exactly where people gather and let their
guard down. Over time she has come to feel safe only in a small number of places—her own room, the
convent chapel at off-hours, one quiet corner of the garden—and the rest of the world feels exposed,
unpredictable, and thin-skinned, as if catastrophe could break through at any moment. Because she is
so watchful for signs of danger, blood, bodily pain, and that dream-soaked state on waking seem to
leap out at her more quickly, confirming what she already fears.

The memories themselves have shaped what she does to get through the day. After an intrusive
reliving episode, she will spend hours turning over details of the attack, trying to make sense of
where she had stood, what she missed, whether she should have moved someone sooner, whether there
had been a warning she failed to notice. She knows this circling never brings resolution, but she
feels driven to keep examining the fragments. Just as often, she retreats from closeness with the
other sisters. They have noticed she no longer lingers after evening prayer or accepts comfort
easily. What frightens her most is how quickly a simple question—“Are you all right?”—can bring her
near tears or drag the scene back into full sensory life, so she keeps her inner world tightly
sealed.

That withdrawal and constriction have had consequences she hates. The more she limits herself to a
few “safe” places and avoids crowded ministries, the smaller and more precarious her life feels. She
has begun declining community outings and asking to be assigned to quieter duties inside the
convent, which leaves her feeling guilty and apart from the vocation she once lived with openness.
Emotional distance has also deepened her fear; being alone with her thoughts makes the negative
feelings larger, not smaller, and her certainty that she must stay guarded only hardens. After
nights broken by nightmares, she dreads morning because the residue of sleep makes the memories
easier to access and the day immediately feels dangerous.

María Elena finally sought help after freezing during a parish first-aid training when a volunteer
opened a kit and she saw blood on a gauze pad. She had to leave the room abruptly, then spent the

was to explain to her Mother Superior why a small stain had undone her. She says she is afraid that
if she cannot regain some sense of safety, her life will continue to contract until she can serve no
