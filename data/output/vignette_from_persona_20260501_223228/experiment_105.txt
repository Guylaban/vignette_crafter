Mariam is a 78-year-old widow who has supported herself for years through pottery, working alone in
a small studio behind her house. The assault happened there one winter evening after she had stayed
late glazing bowls for a local market. She heard a noise near the back door and assumed it was the
wind rattling the frame, but when she turned, a man was already inside. What has remained with her
is not a coherent story so much as a set of stark images: the dark shape in the doorway, the shine
of metal from a tool he carried, the flash of her own face in the studio mirror as he shoved past
her, wide-eyed and disbelieving. Since then, when the memory comes, it does not feel like something
past. It arrives without warning and as pictures, so vivid that she feels for a moment that she is
back in the studio with him entering again.

In the weeks after the assault, Mariam had come to believe that the world was simply dangerous and
that safety could vanish in an instant. What frightened her most was how strongly her body still
reacted even in daylight, in her own home, as if some part of her had not learned that the assault
was over. She became convinced that if she let herself think directly about what happened, she would
be overwhelmed and unable to manage it. From that belief, her habits began to narrow. She tried hard
to push the assault out of mind whenever it surfaced. She started checking the locks on her doors
and windows repeatedly before bed and again after she lay down. She also found herself looking into
mirrors again and again, especially the one by the studio entrance, as if confirming what was behind
her might prevent another attack.

Those same beliefs kept her body on alert. Because she now saw danger as everywhere, she listened
constantly for signs that someone might be entering the house. At night she would wake suddenly and
sit upright, certain she needed to check whether she was safe. Even when neighbors were nearby or
her doors were bolted, she could not settle into ease. She described it as her nerves refusing to
believe reassurance. That ongoing watchfulness fit with what she had come to think: that her body
was an unreliable enemy, primed for danger whether or not danger was present.

The memories themselves deepened this conviction. An image of the doorway or of her own frightened
face in the mirror would force its way into her mind while she was trimming clay or carrying
finished pots to cool. In those moments she felt the assault was happening right now, and the
intensity of that reliving seemed to prove to her that she was not truly safe and perhaps not truly
recovering. Each episode confirmed her fear that thinking about the assault was dangerous in itself.
If a memory could seize her so completely, she reasoned, then deliberately allowing it into her mind
might undo her altogether.

Because the memories came this way, Mariam became more determined to manage them. She would stop
herself mid-thought, turn on a light, check the back door, then check it again. In the studio she
began using reflective surfaces to scan the room behind her, looking over her shoulder and then back
into the mirror, trying to get ahead of the next image before it took hold. At home, when a memory
burst in at bedtime, she got up to inspect windows and locks rather than remain in bed with the
pictures. These rituals gave her a few moments of relief, but they also kept her attention fixed on
the possibility of another intrusion.

Over months, that pattern hardened. The more she checked and scanned, the more real the danger felt
to her. Repeatedly testing locks never led her to feel secure for long; instead it seemed to confirm
that constant vigilance was necessary. Pushing thoughts away made them feel more potent when they
returned. She had come to see her own reactions as evidence that the world remained unsafe and that
she herself could not handle remembering. Her pottery changed as well. She stopped working after
dusk, kept the studio door shut even in warm weather, and interrupted her work whenever a sound from
the alley made her freeze and listen. Orders fell behind, and she felt ashamed when customers asked
why pieces were not ready.

By the time she sought help, Mariam was exhausted from broken nights and from the strain of never
relaxing. She said she was tired of living as if someone were always about to come through the door,
and tired of being ambushed by pictures that made the past feel present. What finally brought her in
was the fear that her life had shrunk to locks, mirrors, and listening. What she wants most is to be
able to work in her studio again without feeling that every sound means danger, but she is deeply
afraid that if she stops guarding herself, the assault will happen all over again.
