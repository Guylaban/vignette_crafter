Thomas is a 65-year-old prosecutor who had spent most of his career believing that order, procedure,
and the rule of law could hold violence in check. The event that fractured that certainty happened
outside a courthouse after a late afternoon hearing. A man had been shouting on the steps, arguing
with officers in a slurry, hard-to-follow rush of words. Thomas remembered the sound first:
overlapping voices, radio noise, people talking at once until the speech became almost
incomprehensible chatter. He had turned toward the commotion just as one officer drove the man to
the ground with a force that Thomas still describes as shocking, even before he fully understood
what he was seeing. The man was not complying, but what stayed with Thomas was the sense that
something had crossed a line in front of him—authority used in a way that felt morally wrong,
uncontrolled, and humiliating. Other officers moved in, crowding the scene, and Thomas stood frozen
with the police uniforms filling his vision and the man's broken protests dissolving into noise.

In the weeks afterward, the situations that shook him most were not random. At work, if people in
the hallway outside his office were loud, bickering, or acting unruly, he felt his body tense before
he had fully registered why. In the courthouse elevators, when several conversations blended
together and he could not make out the words, he became intensely alert, listening for danger in
speech he could not organize. The sight of police officers in the building—men he had worked
alongside for years—now had an immediate effect on him. He found himself watching everyone around
him closely for signs of sudden aggression, studying hands, posture, distance, and tone. He had
become superalert in public spaces, especially in the courthouse lobby and parking garage, and at
night he lay awake unable to settle, his mind still braced as if something could happen without
warning.

That constant sense of threat gradually shaped how he tried to cope. When Thomas felt himself
locking onto other people in a restaurant or the courthouse cafeteria, scanning for who might turn
volatile, he would reach for something loud as soon as he got home—television turned up high, music
filling the room—because silence gave too much space for his vigilance. On bad evenings he asked his
wife, again and again, if she thought he was all right, if she thought he was overreacting, if she
believed he was safe. In public he rarely relaxed his watchfulness; instead he checked exits,
tracked movement, and monitored the room in a way that felt necessary to him, because he was certain
that failing to stay on guard would leave him exposed.

Over time, these habits began to keep the problem alive in ways he only partly recognized. Flooding
the room with loud sound did not calm him for long; it made him more sensitive to bursts of noise
and more unsettled by speech he could not immediately understand the next day in court. His repeated
reassurance seeking brought momentary relief, but it also left him returning to his wife with the
same urgent questions, unable to hold onto comfort once he was alone with his thoughts. Constantly
checking his surroundings meant that in every courtroom hallway, security line, and stairwell, he
was actively looking for threat, and he reliably found reasons to stay tense. The more he scanned,
the more danger seemed present; the more danger seemed present, the less imaginable it felt to stop
scanning.

The cycle became particularly visible at work. As a prosecutor, Thomas still had to move through
holding areas, conference rooms, and crowded corridors where defendants, officers, clerks, and
families all mixed together under pressure. If someone was argumentative with a bailiff, or if
several people spoke over one another in a way he could not sort out, his attention snapped toward
them immediately. He would study faces and hands, shift his position to keep a clear line to the
exit, and carry that keyed-up state home with him. Later, with the television blaring, he could
still feel his eyes searching the room. His wife had started telling him he was not really with her
in the evenings. He hated that observation because part of him knew it was true.

By the time he sought help, Thomas was exhausted by poor sleep and by the strain of having to appear
composed in a profession built on judgment and control. What finally brought him in was not one
dramatic incident but the accumulation: asking his wife for reassurance so often that both of them
fell silent, noticing himself track police officers in the courthouse as if they were potential
threats, realizing he no longer trusted his own sense of safety around disorder or authority. What
frightened him most was not only feeling on edge; it was the possibility that this was becoming his
permanent way of living, that every raised voice, every patch of incomprehensible chatter, every
uniformed presence would keep pulling him back into the same state of alarm. He said he wanted help
because he did not want the rest of his life to be organized around watching for the next blow.
