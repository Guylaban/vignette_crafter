Marisol is a 27-year-old Latina animator who came to treatment months after leaving a physically
abusive relationship with a boyfriend she had once imagined building a life with. When she describes
what happened, she does not linger on the chronology of each incident so much as on the moment her
sense of herself seemed to split. During the worst assault, in the apartment they shared, an
argument escalated and he shoved her hard enough that she hit the edge of a table and fell. She
remembers the shock less as pain than as disbelief that she had stayed in the room, kept arguing,
and then tried to calm him down. In the weeks after she left, what settled in most deeply was not
only fear of him but a corrosive conviction about what the violence meant about her. She had come to
believe that the experience had changed her permanently for the worse, that some part of her had
been damaged and could not be restored.

Since then, Marisol has understood the abuse through a harshly moral lens. She is certain that the
relationship turned violent because of the way she acted—because she challenged him, because she did
not leave sooner, because she kept trying to explain herself when she sensed his anger building.
Although she can say abstractly that no one deserves to be hit, she does not apply that logic to
herself. Instead she returns to tiny moments as if they are evidence in a case against her: the tone
she used, the text message she sent, the fact that she went back after earlier incidents. In
session, what pains her most is not just that he hurt her, but that she believes she participated in
becoming the kind of person to whom this could happen.

That belief is tied to an equally painful sense that she violated her own values during the
relationship. Marisol had always thought of herself as direct, self-respecting, and protective of
other people. Now she speaks with visible shame about lying to friends who asked why she had a
bruise, about minimizing what was happening to her younger sister, and about monitoring his mood so
carefully that she stopped noticing her own. She cannot reconcile those choices with the person she
thought she was. When she talks about staying, apologizing to him, or trying to soothe him after he
had already frightened her, she does not describe those moments as survival. She describes them as
proof that she betrayed herself.

These appraisals have come to dominate her daily life more than any single memory of the assaults.
At the animation studio where she works, she can still complete assignments and sit through long
hours refining motion and expression frame by frame, but she experiences herself differently now.
She notices hesitation in ordinary interactions and interprets it as evidence that she has become
weak. If a supervisor gives routine feedback, she absorbs it with disproportionate force, hearing
confirmation that she is diminished. Her work has become slower because she second-guesses decisions
she used to make intuitively, and she feels humiliated by needing reassurance from coworkers on
details she once handled independently. Being single now does not feel like freedom to her; it feels
like further proof that she is marked by what happened.

Marisol’s shame has narrowed her world. Friends have tried to reconnect, but she often declines
invitations because she cannot bear the thought of being seen by people who knew her before and
might, she imagines, detect what she believes was ruined. She does not describe herself as angry so
much as contaminated by the relationship, as though the abuse revealed something defective that had
been there all along. Even moments of competence—finishing a demanding sequence at work, paying her
own rent, making it through a week without contact from him—rarely soften that conclusion. They sit
beside a more entrenched conviction that she is no longer the woman she was and may never be again.

By the time she sought help, Marisol was exhausted by the constant self-accusation. What finally
brought her in was a late night at the studio when she caught her reflection in a dark monitor and
was struck by the thought that this damaged version of her was becoming permanent. She began to cry
at her desk, not only from remembering the abuse, but from the certainty that she had failed some
essential test of character and could not repair it. In coming to treatment, what she hopes for is
modest and urgent: not to erase what happened, but to find out whether it is possible that she is
not as ruined, culpable, and morally fallen as she now believes. What frightens her most is that
therapy might confirm the opposite—that the person she became in that relationship is the person she
truly is now.
