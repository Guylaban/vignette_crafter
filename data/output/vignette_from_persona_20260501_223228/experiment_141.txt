David is a 53-year-old married social worker who came to treatment after months of telling himself
he should be able to handle this on his own. The assault happened late one evening after a home
visit in an unfamiliar apartment complex on the edge of town. He had stayed longer than planned with
a client in crisis, and by the time he walked back through a narrow, poorly lit corridor toward the
parking area, the building felt close and airless. A man cornered him in the enclosed passageway and
sexually assaulted him before disappearing into the dark. What has remained with David is not only
the violation itself but the trapped feeling of that space, the helpless terror of not knowing if
anyone could hear him, and the sickening shame that rises the moment his mind goes back there. When
he tries to describe what happened, he often stops mid-sentence, jaw tight, saying he knows exactly
what happened but cannot seem to find words for it.

In the first weeks afterward, the memories came back with a force that felt almost identical to the
original experience. A glance at a cramped hallway, an elevator door closing, even being in a small
office with no easy exit could bring the same bodily terror surging through him. With the memory
came intense shame; he would drop his eyes and think, with painful certainty, that something about
the assault made him look weak or exposed. Because recalling it was so overwhelming and humiliating,
he began trying to force the thoughts away whenever they appeared. He told himself not to think
about it, pushed images out of his mind during the day, and when the memory still broke through he
found himself circling around angry fantasies of getting even with the man who assaulted him. Those
revenge ruminations felt easier to hold than the humiliation of what had happened.

The assault also changed the way David moved through the world. He had come to expect danger in
places he could not quickly read or control. For his job, he was often expected to travel to
unfamiliar neighborhoods, enter new buildings, and wait for clients who were running late. Now, if
an appointment took longer than expected to start, his mind jumped immediately to the sense that
something bad was unfolding out of sight. Walking from his office to his car, he repeatedly checked
behind him to see whether someone was following. He became wary of unfamiliar places altogether,
especially if they involved stairwells, apartment corridors, or small waiting rooms. These reactions
were tied closely to the memory of being trapped and overpowered; each moment of uncertainty seemed
to call that terror back, and with it the same wordless, shaming recall.

Over time, the way he responded to that sense of threat narrowed his life further. He started
refusing certain home visits and asking coworkers to swap cases that would take him into buildings
with enclosed hallways or unknown layouts. He delayed entering elevators, took longer routes to
avoid tight spaces, and sometimes canceled visits altogether if he could not picture the setting in
advance. At work he kept scanning, looking over his shoulder in parking lots and hallways, and the
more he did this the more convinced he became that vigilance was necessary. Avoiding unfamiliar
places and enclosed spaces gave him brief relief, but it also left him less able to test whether he
was actually safe; his world felt less predictable, not more. Even his habit of trying to suppress
the memories seemed to sharpen his alertness, because any small delay, any footstep behind him,
could suddenly break through his efforts not to think.

The threat he felt did not stay confined to the outside world. When he caught himself checking
behind him or assuming the worst because someone was late, those reactions made the assault feel
newly present, as if his mind were proving to him that the danger had never really ended. In those
moments the memory returned not as something in the past but as a rush of terror and shame in the
present, and again he would either clamp down on the thoughts or slip into bitter, repetitive
fantasies about confronting his assailant. His wife has noticed that he is more distant and harder
to reach; he says little about the assault itself, partly because he cannot find language for it and
partly because speaking seems to expose him to the shame all over again.

What finally brought David in was a recent incident before a scheduled client visit in a building he
had never entered before. Standing outside the narrow entryway, already checking over his shoulder,
he found himself unable to go in. He sat in his car afterward, shaken and humiliated, thinking that
his work and marriage were beginning to bend around something he still could not say aloud. He is
seeking help now because he is afraid that if this continues, he will keep shrinking his world to
avoid that terror. What he hopes, though he says it cautiously, is that he might eventually remember
what happened without feeling trapped by it again.
