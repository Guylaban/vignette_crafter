David is a 61-year-old bus driver who came to treatment after months of worsening nights, telling
his wife he felt more on guard after dark than at any other time of day. Years earlier, he had been
detained and tortured. When he first described it, the account came in jagged pieces rather than a
smooth narrative: a concrete room, the smell of damp fabric, footsteps outside a door, a burst of
pain, someone else crying out nearby. Some moments stood out with unbearable sharpness, while long
stretches felt missing altogether. What stayed most alive in him was not only what was done to him,
but his conviction that he had failed other people who were there. He had come to believe he should
have stopped it, should have protected them, and that the fact he had not done so proved something
basic and shameful about him—that he was weak.

Those beliefs shaped the way he remembered the trauma. When he tried to think about what happened,
he did not arrive at a fuller story or any sense of perspective; instead he was pulled toward the
same accusing conclusion. The fragments he could access most clearly were the ones that seemed to
confirm his self-condemnation: hearing others, being unable to intervene, feeling overpowered. The
blank spaces troubled him almost as much as the vivid images. He would say that if whole sections
were missing, perhaps there had been some moment when he could have done more and had failed to. In
that way, the gaps in memory became proof to him rather than uncertainty, feeding his sense that he
had not protected others and would never forgive himself.

Remembering also brought back the original terror with startling force. A sound, a smell, even a
certain quality of footsteps could seem to collapse time, and his body reacted before he had fully
oriented himself. At home at night, if the house settled or he heard movement outside, he would feel
a surge of alarm and listen intently for signs that someone was trying to enter. Because the
original experience remained so broken—certain moments crystal clear, others blank—he never felt he
had a settled past event behind him. Instead it was as if danger could still emerge from the missing
pieces. The memory itself kept him scanning, especially in darkness, straining to catch the first
sign of intrusion.

His work as a bus driver gave these reactions a daily setting. On late shifts, when passengers
boarded heavily and the bus filled with the sound of boots on the steps or voices from the rear, he
could feel his chest tighten and his hands go slick on the wheel. He knew he was on a city route in
daylight, yet his body responded as though something was about to happen. By the time he got home,
he was already wound tight, and once night fell he moved through the house checking locks and
pausing at small noises. He listened for sounds that might mean someone was entering, and his wife
had noticed that he rarely settled fully into bed. The hypervigilance was not experienced by him as
excessive; it felt necessary, because he was certain that a man who had once failed to protect
others could not afford to miss danger again.

That same vigilance deepened his shame. Each night of listening at doors and windows seemed to David
like evidence that he had been permanently changed by what happened, and not in a way he could
respect. He saw his fear as weakness. What frightened him almost as much as the noises themselves
was what his reaction to them seemed to say about his character. He believed other men would have
recovered, or would have fought harder then, or would not still be living this way now. The more
alert and physically reactive he became, the more he judged himself, and those judgments made the
nights feel even less safe.

Over time the loop tightened. The fragments of memory reignited terror; the terror kept him
monitoring for threat; the monitoring was taken as proof that he was weak and had failed; and that
harsh self-appraisal drew him back again to the worst, sharpest parts of the trauma. He did not
describe broad symptoms beyond this so much as a narrowing of life around nighttime and vigilance.
His wife urged him to get help after he admitted that evenings had become something he dreaded hours
in advance, and that even in his own home he listened like a man waiting for a door to break open.
What finally brought him in was not only exhaustion, but the fear that he would die still believing
the same thing he told himself in session: that when it mattered most, he had failed others, and
that the terror had never really ended.
