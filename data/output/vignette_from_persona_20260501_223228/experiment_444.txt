become dangerous while the rest of the family carried on as if nothing had happened. Even now, when
she speaks about that period, she returns to the fact that the people responsible were never held to
account. She had come to believe that this was how the world worked: harm could be done to a child
and simply disappear into silence.

and she had not. In her work as a photographer, she could function well enough as long as she stayed
in control of the interaction, but she remained acutely watchful. During portrait sessions she
tracked changes in tone, posture, and expression with exhausting intensity, scanning clients and
assistants for signs of irritation or unpredictability. She was certain she had to stay on guard all
the time. That same conviction shaped how she managed herself: she tried hard to control her
emotions, kept her face still, and when she felt herself becoming exposed she would cover her face
with her hands, a gesture that felt both protective and reflexive. She also found herself circling
the old question of why this had happened to her, turning it over privately for hours without
arriving at relief.

Those efforts did not bring clarity. The more Meera tried to contain herself and make sense of the
abuse, the more unstable the memories felt when they came. If she attempted to deliberately remember
specific incidents, her mind seemed to go blank around the middle of them; she could approach the
edge of a scene but not hold onto it in an orderly way. Yet when something pierced that distance,
the memories arrived with the same terror she had felt as a child, immediate and bodily, and with an
intense rush of shame that made her want to hide her face. In session she described a familiar
pattern: she would begin trying to think through what had happened and why, clamp down on tears and
panic, and then suddenly feel overtaken by fragments that were unbearable rather than

sharply to call to a grandson across the room. Meera knew, rationally, that nothing was happening to
her, but the movement hit her like a blow. In an instant she felt the old terror in her chest and
heat in her face, and shame followed so quickly that she half turned away and covered her face with
her hands under the pretense of adjusting her camera strap. Afterward she spent the evening asking
herself again why this had happened to her and why no one had ever answered for it. The more
convinced she became that the world was still a place where people could harm and get away with it,
and that she had no real place of safety with others, the more tightly she tried to police every
sign of feeling. At work she shortened sessions, avoided collaborative shoots, and kept
conversations clipped so no one would notice when her expression changed.

or children because she could not bear the unpredictability of intimate dynamics in the room. She
kept acquaintances at a distance and rarely let anyone photograph her. What frightened her most was

cautiously, to understand why the memories still have this force, but she is afraid that looking
directly at them will bring back the same terror and shame she has spent a lifetime trying to hide.
