Sione Tui is a 63-year-old community organizer who has spent most of his adult life walking into
tense public spaces and trying to steady them. Last year, on his way home from an evening
neighborhood meeting, he was caught in a life-threatening traffic accident when a pickup truck ran a
red light and struck the driver’s side of his car hard enough to spin it into a barrier. What
remains with him is jagged rather than complete. He can see, with unnerving precision, the wash of
headlights across the windshield and the sudden sight of a uniformed officer leaning down near the
shattered window, but whole stretches are blank. He says he knows something terrible happened in
between, yet when he tries to describe it, words fail him; he stops, shakes his head, and says only
that parts are “gone” no matter how hard he reaches for them.

In the weeks after the crash, Sione became preoccupied with how he had behaved in those final
seconds before impact. He had come to believe the accident happened because of the way he acted,
replaying his decision to keep driving through the intersection and wondering if a more alert, more
disciplined man would have avoided it. The worst part for him was not only the fear he had felt
then, but the sense now that his own body had betrayed him and kept betraying him afterward. His
chest tightens, his muscles brace, and his heart surges in ordinary moments, and he reads these
reactions as proof that his body is now an enemy, stuck in danger even when he knows he is in a
meeting room or at home. Over time he has also begun to describe himself as feeling dead inside, as
if the crash hollowed something out that has not come back.

Those beliefs shape the way he tries to remember. When he sits with the event, he does not approach
it with curiosity so much as accusation, searching the blank places for evidence of what he did
wrong. The more he tells himself that the accident happened because of his own actions, and that his
body’s continuing alarms mean something in him is permanently damaged, the more the memory seems to
fracture under him. A few images stand out with painful clarity, while the missing portions feel
unreachable; he becomes frustrated, falls silent, and says again that he cannot find words for what
happened. The gaps themselves have taken on meaning for him, as if not being able to tell the story
properly confirms that something is fundamentally wrong in him.

At the same time, the shape of the memory determines what now feels dangerous. Because one of the
clearest surviving images is of a uniform at his car window, police and military uniforms have
become especially potent. He notices them immediately on the street, at city buildings, even at
public events he helps organize, and in those moments the crash feels less like a past event than
something half-returning. The same is true of anger. He cannot fully recall all that was said around
the scene, but he retains a vivid sense of sharp voices and command in the aftermath, and now even
calm expressions of anger from other people can set his body off. Unwanted thoughts arrive with
these reminders, not as a full story but as abrupt flashes and pressure to remember what is missing.

His work has made this especially hard. As a community organizer, he spends his days in church
basements, school auditoriums, and municipal meetings where conflict is common and uniformed
security or police officers are often present. When a resident speaks angrily at a planning meeting,
even in a measured tone, Sione feels his body jolt before he can think. In those moments he becomes
certain that his reactions mean he is still broken and unsafe in his own skin. Seeing how strongly
he responds also deepens his self-blame: if he were stronger, he thinks, a voice or a uniform would
not have this kind of power over him. What frightens him most is that these encounters seem to prove
the crash is still inside him.

The cycle has tightened over months. A trigger at work or on the street brings intrusive thoughts
and a bodily surge, and Sione reads that surge as evidence that his body cannot be trusted and that
he somehow caused lasting damage by how he acted that night. Those conclusions send him back toward
the memory, but only in a punishing way, and the result is more blankness, more difficulty putting
the event into words, and more fixation on the few sharply lit fragments that make uniforms and
anger impossible to ignore. He has become less present with colleagues and more detached with
family, not because he does not care, but because feeling “dead inside” seems safer than being
caught by another rush of alarm.

He sought help after freezing during a neighborhood mediation when two men began arguing and a
uniformed officer stepped into the doorway. Sione lost track of the discussion, felt the old jolt
flood his body, and afterward sat in his car unable to explain to a coworker what had happened. He
came in frightened that he is becoming a man ruled by fragments, and hoping, quietly, that if he can
finally find words for the crash, the world might stop feeling so ready to happen to him again.
