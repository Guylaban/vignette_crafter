Nia is a 35-year-old African American police officer who had been proud of the steadiness she
brought to patrol work before she was sexually assaulted by a fellow officer after a late shift. She
does not tell the story in a smooth sequence. What comes first is the fluorescent light in the
locker room hallway, the smell of sweat and disinfectant, his hand pinning her wrist, and the sharp
pain in her hips and shoulders. Other parts disappear when she reaches for them. She knows there are
stretches she cannot account for no matter how hard she tries, and those blank spaces trouble her
almost as much as what she does remember. When the memories break through, they do not feel distant.
Her body hurts in the same places again, and along with the pain comes a surge of rage so strong
that she has to grip the edge of a table or leave the room.

In the weeks after the assault, Nia had come to believe that what happened said something
unforgivable about her. She compared herself harshly to an imagined stronger, smarter woman who
would have seen danger sooner, fought harder, or never ended up alone with him in the first place.
The broken, incomplete quality of the memory seemed to confirm this view in her mind: if she could
not even hold onto the whole event, maybe she had failed in some essential way. She also felt
altered in a way she could not reverse. She described herself as permanently changed for the worse,
less sure of her instincts, less solid inside. At the same time, she began to feel cut off from the
sense of community that had once anchored her, both in the department and in the wider Black
community and family networks that had mattered to her; she felt as if she no longer stood where she
used to stand with other people.

Those beliefs began to shape how she moved through her days. Because she was certain that
disagreement could escalate into something she could no longer control, she started avoiding
conflict wherever she could, even when the situation plainly called for her usual directness. At
work, she would let other officers take the lead in tense exchanges and would go quiet in briefings
rather than challenge anything. She also began steering away from anything likely to stir strong
feeling. She skipped family gatherings if she thought people might ask why she seemed different,
kept conversations with her husband on the surface, and shut down topics that touched on intimacy,
anger, or fear. Privately she spent long stretches thinking about how her life would have been if
that night had not happened—how she might still feel at home in her body, in her marriage, in
uniform. That line of thought did not comfort her, but she returned to it repeatedly.

The memories themselves drove more of this narrowing. On shift, a trip down a station corridor or
the slap of a locker door could suddenly pull her back to the hallway outside the locker room. When
that happened, pain shot through her body and the rage rose so fast that she feared what might show
on her face or voice. To keep from feeling that, she avoided parts of the building when she could,
swapped tasks, and found reasons not to be alone in certain areas. She also avoided arguments on and
off the job because anger felt too close to the fury that came with remembering. What frightened her
most was not only the memory, but the sense that if she let herself feel anything fully, she would
lose control.

Over months, the loop tightened. Each time she recoiled from a charged conversation or abandoned a
situation before emotion rose, she felt further from the person she had been. Each time a memory
arrived with pain and rage, the missing pieces and bodily force of it seemed to prove that the
assault had damaged her permanently. The more damaged she felt, the more she withdrew from her
husband, from colleagues she once trusted, and from the cultural and communal ties that had once
made her feel known. She could still perform the visible parts of police work, but it had become a
thinner version of herself doing it.

Nia came to treatment after her husband told her he felt he was living beside her rather than with
her, and after she left a briefing abruptly when a flash of memory hit so hard she thought she might
be sick. She is seeking help now because she is tired of arranging her life around what she cannot
bear to feel. At the same time, she is afraid that talking about the assault will make the pain and
rage come back in full force, and that if the memory ever becomes complete, it will only confirm
what she already believes—that she has been changed for good.
