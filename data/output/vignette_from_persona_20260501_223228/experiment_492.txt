Tane is an 18-year-old American young man of Pacific Islander background who came to treatment after
years of feeling as if danger could arrive without warning. He grew up in a home where, as a child,
he repeatedly witnessed his mother being terrorized by a man who had been drinking. What stayed with

a door being shoved, the sight of an adult male body blocking the way out, and the feeling of being
very small and unable to predict what would happen next. Even when he tries to describe it now, Tane
says he can only get pieces. Large parts feel missing, and the parts he does recall come as
disconnected fragments that do not fit into a full story. When those fragments surface, they do not
feel distant; he is hit by the same terror he felt then.

Over time, certain moments in ordinary life began to carry the force of those childhood scenes. The
smell of alcohol is especially potent, and being alone with men can make his body tense before he
has had time to think. Even a bicycle can catch him off guard; seeing one or hearing it approach can
pull up a jolt of fear tied to half-remembered childhood moments outside the house. These triggers
do not just remind him in a mild way. They seem to pull memory toward him in fragments, and because
the memory arrives in pieces, the trigger itself feels harder to make sense of and more dangerous
the next time it appears.

He now works as a project manager, and the daily demands of coordinating people have become one of
the places where his symptoms are most visible. If he has to meet a male vendor alone, or pass a
coworker in the hall who smells faintly of alcohol from the night before, he becomes sharply alert.
He watches men’s faces, posture, and movements for any sign that they might turn threatening.
Ordinary office noises make him jump. Before settling at his desk or leaving a meeting room, he
finds himself checking doors and locks repeatedly, as if failing to do so would leave him exposed.
The bicycle rack outside the building has become another point of strain; even noticing the
clustered bikes when he arrives can make him scan the area and feel as though something bad is about
to happen.

Because these reactions feel so immediate, Tane has developed ways of getting through the day that
bring short-term relief but narrow his life. After encounters that set him off, he smokes outside
the office to calm down. He has also been drinking and using medication or drugs to blunt the
aftereffects when the fear lingers into the evening. He avoids people more than he used to,
especially situations where he might be alone with a man, and he has started structuring work so
that conversations happen by message or with others present. His girlfriend has noticed that he
withdraws after certain days and becomes difficult to reach.

The same pattern tightens once a memory has been stirred. When a smell or situation brings back one
of those fragments, the terror returns so strongly that he reaches for whatever will shut it down
fastest: a cigarette, alcohol, medication, distance from other people. Yet these efforts leave him
more cut off and more vigilant. The more he avoids and numbs, the more uncertain the past feels, and
the less complete his memory becomes, so that the next trigger lands as something sudden and
unexplained. He has grown increasingly preoccupied with making sure doors and windows are locked,
startling at ordinary sounds at home, and studying other people closely in public. That constant
watchfulness means he notices every possible cue—men nearby, alcohol on someone’s breath, a bicycle
passing—and each cue is given immediate importance.

In recent months this has begun to affect his work and relationship in ways he can no longer
dismiss. He spends too much energy monitoring rooms instead of focusing on projects, and he
sometimes rearranges meetings for reasons he cannot easily explain to colleagues. At home, he checks
locks repeatedly before bed and remains keyed up if a sound catches him off guard. What finally
brought him in was a recent day at work when the smell of alcohol on a client, the sight of a
bicycle outside, and being briefly alone with another man in a conference room set off a rush of
terror he could not contain. He left to smoke, later drank to settle himself, and was shaken by how
quickly he had felt like a frightened child again. Tane is seeking help because he is afraid this
fear is running his life, and because he wants, more than anything, to be able to remember what
happened without feeling trapped inside it.
