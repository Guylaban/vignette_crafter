outdoor music event. She had gone with friends at the start of summer, expecting noise, lights, and
the careless pleasure of being young in a packed crowd. When people began surging toward one of the

metal barrier. Someone’s arm was jammed across her chest, another body pressed into her back, and
for several moments she could not move her own arms. What stayed with her most was not only the fear
that she might be trampled, but the sensory force of it: sweat, stale alcohol, the sharp smell of
metal and overheated bodies, the pressure of being held down while people shouted and pushed. When
she tries to describe it now, Claire often stops mid-sentence and says she knows exactly what
happened but cannot find words that feel big enough for it.

In the weeks afterward, the event returned in a way that did not feel like remembering. If she was
overtired after rehearsal, or caught the sensation of someone close behind her on the sidewalk, or
felt even an accidental grip in partnering practice, the whole scene seemed to come back as if it
were happening again. She did not just picture it. She could smell the mix of sweat and metal, feel
the crush of bodies, and lose the sense that she was safely in the present. Those moments made
ordinary parts of her life feel dangerous. At the studio, if another dancer moved unexpectedly near
her, she flinched violently; at night she woke suddenly and scanned her room to check that she was
safe. Because she was so alert to movement and so easily jolted, she began noticing signs of danger
everywhere, especially when she was tired or walking alone, and she would become convinced someone
was following too closely.

Claire responded by shrinking her world. She started turning down invitations, avoiding going out
unless she absolutely had to, and leaving for rehearsal only at the last possible minute. On the
subway she covered part of her face with her hands, as if making herself smaller might protect her
from being seen or hemmed in. She gave up pleasant things that had once anchored her, including
nights out after performances and, gradually, even parts of dance that used to feel joyful. These
choices made sense to her: she had come to believe that if she let herself think too much about the
crush, she would not be able to handle it, and avoiding people, noise, and closeness seemed like the
only way to keep herself together. The more the sensory reliving broke through, the more urgently
she pulled back from anything that might stir it.

Her sense of what the event meant also hardened over time. Claire was certain what happened to her
was deeply unfair, and she remained angry that such a dangerous situation had unfolded with no real
accountability. Each time the memory hit with full force, it seemed to confirm something had been
permanently altered. She would be left with the thought that other people had simply gone back to
normal while she no longer belonged anywhere—not in a carefree crowd, not fully with her friends,
not even in the dance world that had always been home. That belief fed her retreat. If she already
felt apart from everyone, it seemed easier to stop going out, to hide her face, to stop reaching for
enjoyment she no longer expected to feel.

The retreat, however, tightened things rather than easing them. Because Claire spent so much time
avoiding busy places and dropping the social and pleasurable parts of her routine, she became more

behind her without warning, her whole body reacted before she could think. She began to dread lifts
and close partnering because the slightest sensation of being held or blocked could set off another
vivid reliving of the barrier and the crush. Avoiding the city and making herself small in public
also left her less able to test whether she was actually safe, so being out in the world felt
increasingly strange and threatening. A tired walk home after class could become a scan for
footsteps behind her; once she noticed that possibility, every passerby felt suspect.

By the time Claire sought help, her life had narrowed around rehearsal, home, and broken sleep. She
was still dancing, but with increasing effort and less pleasure, frightened by how quickly
exhaustion, movement nearby, or a hand at her waist could pull her back into the disaster. What
finally brought her in was the fear that if this continued she would lose dance altogether, along

plainly about the event might make it overwhelm her completely. What she hopes for, tentatively, is
not to erase what happened, but to stop living as if the crush is waiting for her everywhere.
