himself he should be able to handle this on his own. Before he returned to the United States, he had
spent time as a relief worker in a war zone, helping move food and medical supplies through

warning. What stayed with him most was not one single image but the press of bodies around him when
distributions began, the way grateful faces could turn panicked in an instant, and the crack of
gunshots that cut through moments that were supposed to feel hopeful. He remembered one afternoon
especially clearly: children had been laughing near a supply line, adults were reaching toward him,
and he had felt a brief rush of relief that this delivery might go smoothly. Then shots rang out
nearby, people surged toward him, and that small feeling of safety vanished so fast that he now
mistrusted it.

Back home, Javier resumed running his business, trying to move through long workdays as if he had
simply returned from a difficult assignment. Instead, ordinary moments began to carry the shape of
the war zone. If a customer or employee stepped too close while speaking to him in the office, his
body tightened before he could think. Loud popping sounds from the street, a car backfiring, even
something that resembled gunfire in the distance could make his attention snap outward. What
unsettled him in a more confusing way were moments that should have felt good. A relaxed dinner with
his wife, laughter with a friend, or even a brief sense of pride when the business had a strong day
could abruptly feel dangerous to him, as though feeling good meant he was about to be blindsided
again. Because of that, he had begun to meet even pleasant moments with suspicion.

As these reactions took hold, Javier developed ways of managing them that made sense to him in the
moment. He started carrying a weapon, telling himself he was being practical, that a man who had
seen how quickly things turned could not afford to be unprepared. At work he positioned himself
carefully and paid attention to who came near him. When a trigger hit, especially a loud sound or
someone entering his space too fast, he would later go over and over why the event had happened to
him at all—why he had been there, why he had survived, what he should have understood sooner. He
could spend hours mentally circling the same questions without getting any closer to an answer. At
other times, when the unease built and he sensed one of those falsely “good” moments beginning, he
found himself picking arguments with employees, with vendors, and eventually with his wife. Creating
conflict gave him somewhere to put the tension. Anger felt more usable than vulnerability.

Over the following months, those habits began feeding the very sensitivity he was trying to control.

leaning in to ask a question could feel like a threat simply because he was already braced to defend
himself. The arguments he provoked also left him more keyed up, so that afterward he noticed
closeness, noise, and even warmth from other people as if they were warnings. His rumination worked
similarly. The more he revisited the event, trying to force meaning from it, the more alive its
atmosphere seemed in the present. Gunshot-like sounds felt less like reminders and more like
immediate alarms. Even positive emotions became harder to tolerate, because he had trained himself
to search every calm moment for what might shatter it.

This cycle began to alter his life in concrete ways. Employees had started commenting on how quickly

home, his wife no longer knew when a pleasant evening might turn into an argument Javier himself
seemed unable to explain. He hated that part most: he could see that he was spoiling the very
moments he wanted back, yet when things felt light or close or safe, he was certain something bad
was about to follow. By the time he sought help, he was exhausted from staying armed, from scanning
other people’s distance from him, and from replaying the same questions in his head. He came in
frightened by how much of his life had narrowed around these reactions, and quietly hopeful that he
might learn how to be near people, hear ordinary sounds, and feel a good moment without immediately
preparing for disaster.
