Since surviving a sudden, life-threatening illness eighteen months ago, Kwame Mensah, a 68-year-old
widowed policy analyst, has lived as if the emergency never truly ended. He had been at his desk in
a government office building, reviewing a budget brief, when a crushing wave of pain and dizziness
came over him so fast that he could not make sense of what his body was doing. He remembered
gripping the edge of the conference table, hearing chairs scrape back, and seeing a coworker’s mouth
moving before the room blurred. In the ambulance and later in the hospital, what stayed with him was
the terror of being trapped inside a body that seemed to turn against him without warning. Even
after he was told he had survived, that moment never settled into something finished. Parts of it
returned with a peculiar numbness, as if he were describing a stranger on a stretcher, but at other
times the memory came with such force that he felt he was back under fluorescent hospital lights,
overwhelmed and unable to keep thinking about it.

In the weeks after discharge, Kwame had come to believe that the illness had exposed something
irreversible about him. What frightened him most was not only that he might die, but that his own
body could no longer be counted on. He was certain now that his body was unreliable, an enemy
capable of sounding an alarm at any moment, and he found himself watching it closely for signs of
another collapse. That belief kept him tense through the day and night. He noticed every flutter in
his chest, every spell of lightheadedness, every shift in his breathing, and even at home in his
apartment he felt on guard, as if safety could be revoked without notice. He used to think of home
as the one place where he could exhale after work; now he sat upright in the living room listening
to his body, unable to settle.

His work as a policy analyst became one of the main places where this threat state showed itself.
The ordinary setting of conference rooms, computer screens, and long stretches of concentrated
reading had also been the setting of his medical crisis, and sitting through staff meetings could
abruptly pull him back into the original moment. A colleague leaning across the table, the stale
office air, or the pressure of trying to focus on a dense document could make the memory feel
present rather than past. When that happened, his body tightened immediately, his attention
narrowed, and he struggled to follow what was being discussed. Sometimes he would stare at the page
and realize he had absorbed none of it because he was braced for danger again. The memory itself
drove the sense of current threat; once it broke through, he was no longer a man in a meeting but
someone in immediate peril.

Over time, the process also ran in the other direction. Because he stayed so alert to his body and
surroundings, even minor internal sensations could cue the memory before he fully knew what was
happening. A rush of tension in his chest while working at his desk, or the simple awareness that he
was alone at home in the evening, could make the hospital scene surge back in fragments. He might
first notice that he was suddenly rigid and scanning for danger, and only then find himself flooded
with the image of the ambulance doors or the helpless certainty that something catastrophic was
happening inside him again. On other occasions he could begin recalling the event in a flat,
detached way, only for the emotion to slam into him so strongly that he had to stop and leave the
thought unfinished.

As these episodes repeated, Kwame drew darker conclusions about what they meant. Each time his body
leapt into alarm in a safe room, he took it as proof that something fundamental had been damaged. He
had come to believe he would never feel normal emotions again, because his responses no longer
seemed to match ordinary life; either he felt shut down and distant from what had happened, or he
was overwhelmed by it. The constant tension and inability to concentrate seemed to confirm his view
that his body was still reacting as if danger were ongoing and that he had no real control over it.
That interpretation made him more watchful still, especially at home, and more convinced that
another medical disaster could arrive without warning.

By the time he sought help, the strain had narrowed his life considerably. He still went to work,
but tasks that once suited his careful, analytic mind now took much longer, and he had begun quietly
declining some meetings because he feared freezing or losing focus in front of colleagues. Evenings
in his apartment were the hardest; without distractions, he felt his body on alert and waited for it
to betray him again. What finally brought him in was the sense that he was no longer living after
the illness but remaining inside it. He said he wanted, more than anything, to feel that the event
was over, but he was afraid that if he could not trust his own body, he would never again be able to
feel safe, or fully himself.
