Sokha is a 60-year-old Cambodian American man who has worked for years as a hotel manager, a role he
once valued because he knew how to keep a busy building calm and orderly. The event that still
organizes his life began during a maintenance emergency at the hotel, when a cleaning chemical was
accidentally mixed in a poorly ventilated service area. He remembers the sharp, acrid taste that hit
the back of his throat almost immediately, the metallic-bleach flavor that made him gag and
convinced him something was entering his body that he could not stop. In the confusion, staff were
coughing, a guest was shouting, and Sokha could not tell who had been exposed or how serious it was.
What stayed with him most was the sensation that his own body had betrayed him in the very moment he
needed it; he has come away certain that he cannot trust his body to warn him properly or protect
him, and with a broader feeling that bad things can happen to him without any way to prevent them.

In the weeks after, those beliefs hardened rather than fading. If he felt a dryness in his throat, a
change in his breathing, or a strange taste while drinking coffee, he took it as proof that his body
was unreliable and that danger could already be underway before he understood it. Because he had
come to believe he could not stop bad things from happening, he began checking himself repeatedly
for signs of injury or exposure, pressing at his throat, noticing his breathing, scanning for any
sensation that might mean the chemicals had done lasting damage. The same beliefs spread outward
into how he moved through the hotel. He watched hallways, housekeeping carts, maintenance closets,
and the expressions on staff faces for signs that something was wrong. He also pulled away from
reminders, cutting off conversations when employees mentioned the spill or later safety changes,
because being reminded of it felt less like remembering and more like being returned to a moment in
which he was helpless again.

The memories themselves have remained unusually immediate. He does not simply think about the
exposure; at unpredictable moments the memory forces itself into his mind, and when it does he can
taste that same chemical bitterness as if it is on his tongue again. Standing near the laundry room
at work, catching the smell of industrial cleaners, or hearing a guest complain that a room smells
“too strong” can make him feel, for a few seconds, as though the event is happening right now. Those
moments have reinforced his conviction that his body cannot be trusted and that he is not really at
home anywhere anymore. He says the hotel used to feel like his place, but now every back corridor
and supply room can turn unfamiliar in an instant, and even at home he feels set apart, as if he no
longer belongs in ordinary life with people who are not braced for something toxic to happen.

Because the memories arrive so forcefully, he has become more vigilant and more avoidant over time.
When an intrusive image or the chemical taste comes back during a shift, he starts checking the air
vents, looking for leaks, watching staff movements, and monitoring his chest and throat. He may
reroute himself away from the service areas for the rest of the day or abruptly end a discussion if
someone starts revisiting the incident. These habits give him brief relief, but they also keep his
attention fixed on signs of threat. The more he scans his body and surroundings, the more easily
normal sensations or small delays become loaded with danger; if housekeeping is late answering a
radio call, he is quick to assume something bad has happened. Once that fear spikes, his body goes
tense and ready, and in that state the memory becomes even easier to trigger. A routine pause
outside a chemical storage room can bring back the taste, the panic, and the sense of being trapped
in the original exposure.

Over months, this has tightened into a daily pattern. His body feels constantly on alert, and false
alarms are frequent; a harmless smell, a cough from a staff member, or a late response over the
hotel radio can make him react as if there is an immediate emergency. That alarm then confirms, in
his mind, that he was right not to trust his body or the world around him. He becomes more certain
that he cannot prevent bad things, more convinced that he does not belong in spaces other people
move through casually, and more dependent on checking, scanning, and shutting down reminders. At
work this has changed how he manages. Staff have noticed that he hovers near maintenance issues,
repeatedly asks whether anyone feels dizzy or unwell, and avoids longer debriefs about safety
procedures if they come too close to the day of the spill. At home, his partner sees him go quiet
after certain smells or after delays that other people would dismiss, and then watch his own
breathing for long stretches.

By the time he sought help, Sokha was exhausted by how quickly an ordinary workday could become a
scene of danger in his mind and body. What finally pushed him into treatment was realizing that a
minor cleaning complaint from a guest had left him shaking, tasting the chemicals again, and unable
to finish the conversation without stepping out to check himself in the mirror. He is frightened
that this will keep shrinking his life and his work until there is nowhere he feels safe or at ease.
At the same time, he is hoping that treatment might help him trust his body enough to stop living as
if another exposure is always about to happen.
