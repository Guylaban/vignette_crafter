decades, in a rhythm once organized by prayer, communal meals, and visits to the parish school. The

small infirmary room. Margaret had been the one to hear the fall. She still remembers the sound of
the body hitting the floor and the sight of her friend lying at an angle that immediately felt
wrong. Some parts of those minutes are fixed in almost photographic detail—the hem of the sister’s
habit twisted beneath her, the smell of floor polish, the cold brightness from the window at the end
of the hall—while other moments drop away entirely. She cannot say clearly who called 911 or exactly
how long she knelt there, only that she has flashes of pressing at her friend’s shoulder and
thinking, with rising panic, that she should have checked on her earlier.

happened because of the way she acted: if she had not delayed, if she had noticed some sign sooner,
if she had insisted on more supervision, perhaps her friend would still be alive. What torments her
most is the conviction that she failed in a duty that mattered deeply to her, that she should have
protected another sister and did not. From there a broader doubt has taken hold. She no longer
trusts her own judgment in the simple, ordinary ways she once did; she is certain that when
something important depends on her, she will miss what matters. Those beliefs seem to fasten onto
the memory itself. The most searing images return with absolute clarity, as if her mind is
presenting evidence against her, while the blank stretches feel to her like proof that she cannot
even rely on herself to know what happened.

In the months that followed, those memories began arriving without warning. Walking past a polished

sudden thud during chores could bring back the whole scene instantly, so vividly that for a moment
she felt she was back on the floor beside her friend. Because the memory could overtake her so
abruptly, she began avoiding the corridor where the collapse occurred, taking a longer route through
the building even when it made her late for communal prayer. She also started pulling away from
grief reminders more broadly: she leaves quickly if conversation turns to the sister who died,

other sisters have noticed how often she volunteers for tasks in distant rooms and declines anything
near the infirmary.

“safe enough,” as if failing to do so might allow another catastrophe. These rituals make sense to
her because she feels so unable to rely on herself; if her own judgment failed once, she reasons,
she must guard against failure in every possible way. Yet the more she depends on these private acts
and on steering away from reminders, the more fragile she feels. Each avoided hallway seems to
confirm that she cannot bear what happened, and each ritual quietly reinforces the idea that danger
will return unless she manages it perfectly.

That pattern has narrowed her world and sharpened the memory rather than softening it. By staying
away from the site and from reminders of the sister she lost, she has never had the ordinary
repetition that might make the memory feel like something in the past; instead it remains preserved
in jagged pieces, either intensely clear or abruptly blank. When it breaks through, it does so with
the same force, and Margaret reads that force as further evidence of failure: if she were stronger,
if she had done the right thing then and were handling it rightly now, she believes she would not be
so vulnerable to it. In turn she doubles down on avoidance and on the mental prayers, trying to
prevent another loss and to prevent the memory from taking hold, only to find herself more watchful,
more restricted, and less trusting of herself.

Her husband, from whom she has long had a warm but somewhat geographically divided marriage because
