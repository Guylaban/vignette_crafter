Martin Weber, a 55-year-old counselor who had spent most of his adult life helping other people
steady themselves in crisis, came to treatment after months of feeling that his own mind and body
had become unreliable. The event he returned to, reluctantly and only in fragments, was a fire set
intentionally in the counseling center where he worked. Late one evening, after a tense
confrontation elsewhere in the building, he had smelled smoke before he understood what was
happening. By the time he opened his office door, there was a harsh burnt odor in the hallway,
alarms blaring, and somewhere down the corridor the crash of objects being hurled as people tried to
force open a jammed exit and clear a path. He remembered the confusion less as a sequence than as
sensations: the scorched smell, the impact sounds, the certainty that danger had already entered the
building before anyone could get ahead of it.

In the weeks that followed, those sensory details began intruding into ordinary life. At work, if a
patient knocked a water bottle off a table or a file was tossed into a bin, Martin felt his whole
attention snap toward the sound of an object being thrown. If he passed a break room where toast had
burned or caught even a faint smoke smell outside the building, he would feel the emotional force of
that night hit him all at once, often without any picture in his mind. Sometimes, when he tried to
explain this, he said it was as though the feeling arrived first—raw fear, urgency, a sense that
something terrible was underway—and only later, if at all, did any recognizable memory follow. At
other moments, simply thinking about the fire brought back the smell itself so vividly that he found
himself checking the room, convinced for a second that something nearby was burning.

He had come to believe that his body was now working against him. What frightened him most was not
only the memory of the fire, but the way his chest tightened, his stomach dropped, and his attention
narrowed whenever he encountered those cues, even in settings that were objectively safe. To Martin,
these reactions meant his system still “thought” he was in danger; he said bitterly that his body
kept reacting as if the event were present tense. From there it made sense to him that he had to
stay especially careful because you never knew what might happen next. The more he watched himself
for signs of losing control, the stranger the memory became. He sometimes described it as if it
belonged to someone else, as though he were reporting on a case rather than recalling his own
experience, yet the emotional intensity remained immediate and unmuted.

Because he was certain that deliberately thinking about the fire would overwhelm him, Martin began
organizing his days around not letting his mind go there. He cut short conversations with friends
who asked how he was doing and gradually stopped seeing them at all, partly because he did not want
to be asked direct questions and partly because social gatherings felt full of unpredictable noises
and smells. In session rooms at the counseling center, he positioned himself near the door and kept
a private watch on his thoughts, scanning for any sign that he might be “going crazy” if the memory
surfaced. That constant monitoring did not calm him. It made him more aware of the moments when the
burnt smell seemed to come back from nowhere or when a wave of fear surged up without an image,
which only confirmed his sense that the event was lodged somewhere beyond his control. The more he
tried not to think about it, the more the memory returned in these disjointed sensory and emotional
forms.

Over months, the loop tightened. A smell of smoke in the parking lot could pull up the memory’s odor
with startling force, and once that happened he became even more convinced that ordinary cues were
dangerous and that his body could not be trusted. A clatter in the office—someone angrily throwing a
stack of folders into a cabinet—could leave him preoccupied for the rest of the day, avoiding
colleagues and redoubling his efforts to keep the event out of mind. His work began to suffer in the
quiet ways that embarrassed him most: he was less present with clients, more distracted by scanning
his own internal state, and increasingly isolated in a profession built on human contact.

What finally brought Martin in was a moment of humiliation after he smelled something burnt in the
staff kitchen and had to leave abruptly before meeting with a client. He was shaken less by the
interruption than by what it seemed to prove to him—that even now, a smell or a sound could reach
inside him and pull him back into an experience that did not feel fully his and yet still
overwhelmed him. He said he wanted help because he was tired of living as if catastrophe were always
about to break through the next ordinary moment. What he feared most was that if he ever let himself
face the event directly, he would not be able to handle what came up. What he hoped, quietly, was to
stop feeling ambushed by his own mind.
