Kwame is an 18-year-old African American police officer who came to treatment exhausted by the sense
that he is living braced for something terrible. When he describes where this began, he goes back to
childhood nights in a small apartment where he would hear his mother’s boyfriend moving through the
rooms before the violence started. What stayed with him are not long, coherent memories but a set of
frozen pictures: the boyfriend standing in the hallway, his mother cornered near the kitchen, a hand
grabbing, the charged stillness just before shouting and blows. He remembers his own body wedged
behind a doorway, muscles locked, trying not to make a sound. Even now, when those moments come
back, his body pulls into that same tight, crouched position before he has fully realized what he is
remembering, and the memory is accompanied by a crushing grief for his mother and for the boy he was
then, unable to stop what was happening.

Over time, Kwame had come to believe that those reactions said something deeply wrong about him. He
was certain he should have been able to do more, even as a child, and now he takes his own startle,
tension, and mental replaying as evidence that he cannot rely on himself. He worries that the force
of these reactions means he is going crazy. Because he feels helpless in the face of danger, he
approaches the world as if bad things are always about to break through if he lets his guard down.
He avoids unfamiliar places because not already knowing the layout feels intolerable; he says he
needs to be able to assess exits, objects, and who is present. Silence is especially hard for him.
If a room goes unusually quiet, his whole body reads it as the kind of hush that used to come before
violence, and he immediately starts scanning for anything that could be used as a weapon.

Those scans are not abstract. At work, when he enters a home on a call or even steps into a break
room he has not used before, his eyes go first to knives, heavy tools, glass objects, anything that
could become dangerous. If someone moves in a way he reads as suspicious, or if a person comes up
behind him and touches his shoulder unexpectedly, he feels a jolt of raw discomfort that is out of
proportion to the moment but completely convincing to him. Those moments pull the old pictures
forward. An unexpected touch can bring back the image of a hand grabbing in the hallway; a tense,
watchful person can instantly call up the snapshot of his mother’s boyfriend before an assault. The
triggers feel potent partly because he has come to expect threat in them, and the more he watches
for suspicious behavior or the wrong kind of silence, the more readily ordinary moments seem to
match the old scenes.

Once the memories are stirred, they intensify everything else. He feels himself physically pulled
back into the posture of hiding and waiting, and that bodily reenactment makes the present
environment seem dangerous, not just reminiscent. After a memory surge, he becomes even more
vigilant, more convinced that silence signals imminent harm, more fixated on locating possible
weapons around him. The memories also deepen his beliefs about himself. If a few sensory details can
overtake him that completely, he takes it as proof that he is not in control of his own mind and
cannot depend on himself under pressure. What frightens him most is not only the memory itself but
the idea that having it means he is losing his sanity.

That interpretation, in turn, makes the next trigger more likely. Because he is already judging his
reactions as intolerable and incomprehensible, even mild discomfort in public feels dangerous. He
watches himself so closely that suspicious behavior in others, unfamiliar settings, and unprepared
touch all become loaded before anything has actually happened. Then the environment starts to narrow
around him. He keeps to familiar routes, arrives early so he can study a room, and stays tense in
quiet moments on shift because the absence of noise feels like the prelude to an attack. The more he
lives that way, the more his mind stays organized around those childhood images, and the more easily
they return.

By the time he sought help, the strain was affecting both work and the little life he keeps outside
it. He is single and increasingly withdrawn, choosing predictability over seeing people or going
anywhere new. What finally brought him in was the fear that one day an unexpected touch or a
suspicious movement on the job would flood him so completely with those old pictures that he would
stop trusting his own judgment altogether. More than anything, Kwame wants to know whether he can
learn to feel safe without having to stay on guard every second, and whether these reactions mean he
is damaged beyond repair.
