of an abusive relationship that had unfolded largely behind the doors of the apartment he had once
shared with his former partner. The worst assaults happened at night after his partner had been
drinking. Mateo could still picture one episode with painful clarity: the smell of alcohol on his
partner’s breath filling the small hallway, the apartment door slamming hard enough to shake the

other man’s weight. What stayed with him was not only the fear but the humiliation and grief of
realizing, even in the moment, that someone he had loved was hurting him. Since leaving, he had
refused to go near that building. He told himself there was no reason to pass that block, but the
thought of the entrance, the stairwell, even the sound of a heavy door in a nearby corridor was
enough to make his body lock up and his face go blank as he fought to keep any feeling from showing.

his days moving between parish offices, community rooms, and clients’ homes, and he noticed how
often doors opened suddenly behind him in those spaces. A volunteer pushing into an office, a client
entering a meeting room too quickly, or the sharp slam of a church side door could make him jolt as
if he were about to be grabbed. If someone brushed past him in a narrow hallway, he reacted to the
movement before he could think, his shoulders tensing and his breath stopping. The smell of alcohol
on a congregant after an evening event could do the same. Those moments did more than unsettle him;
they brought back the apartment with startling immediacy, and in his mind he could hear the same
slammed door and the same drunken voice from the assaults as though the scene were happening again.

Because he had come to expect danger in these flashes, Mateo increasingly organized his life around
preventing them. At night he moved through his own apartment checking the locks on the front door
and windows over and over, unable to settle until he had reassured himself again. Night felt
especially unsafe to him, the hour when he was most certain something could happen without warning.

response that had overtaken him during the abuse, and afterward he would clamp down hard on any sign
of fear or sadness. He worked to keep his voice even, his expression controlled, and his body still,

his partner’s voice as if they were echoing in the room around him. What hit him most forcefully on

grief for the version of himself who had believed he could calm things down if he kept control. That

only to leave the memories sealed off and vivid, ready to be reawakened by the next slammed door or
the next trace of alcohol on someone’s breath.
