At 48, Laura had spent years doing social ministry work in church basements, hospital waiting rooms,
and cramped apartment kitchens, usually as the steady person in other people’s crises. The accident
that changed her happened on an ordinary workday when she was driving between visits. Another car
came into her lane and struck the side of her vehicle hard enough to spin her across the road. She
remembers the violent jolt, the helpless skid, and the sickening instant of seeing twisted cars at
impossible angles before everything stopped. She was injured but survived. What stayed with her most
was not only the fear that she might die, but the experience of being completely unable to stop what
was happening. In the months afterward she had come to believe that what happened was deeply unfair
and that no one had really answered for it, and that conviction sat beside a more private
conclusion: if catastrophe could arrive like that once, she could not count on herself, her body, or
the world to protect her.

Those meanings sharpened the things that now caught her. Seeing damaged cars on the shoulder of the
highway, even in passing, pulled up the same image of vehicles smashed by impact. Feeling physically
unwell—a wave of dizziness, a tight chest, simple fatigue—could suddenly feel ominous to her, as if
her body were again proving itself unreliable. Situations in which she felt pushed or forced into
doing something carried a particular charge. Since the crash, being told to hurry, to get in a car,
or to go along with someone else’s plan stirred the same trapped feeling she associated with the
seconds when she had no control over the spinning vehicle. Because she was convinced bad things
could happen without warning and that she would be unable to stop them, those moments landed not as
inconveniences but as signs of danger.

Her sense that she could no longer trust safety began to shape the way she moved through the world.
In the community centers and shelters where she worked, Laura now scanned for exits as soon as she
entered a room. She noticed doorways, stairwells, and paths out before she took in who was present.
Ordinary sounds made her jump—a dropped binder in an office, a slammed church door, a tray
clattering in a cafeteria—and even when colleagues reassured her that everything was fine, she found
she could not believe them. The same beliefs that had settled after the accident—that she was
helpless, that her body might fail her, that the world was unfair and unsafe—fed this vigilance. If
she could not trust her own body or anyone else’s reassurance, then staying on guard felt like the
only sensible thing to do.

That vigilance steadily narrowed her life. The most obvious change was that she avoided driving
whenever she could and refused rides unless there was no other option. She started rearranging
client visits to keep them within walking distance or asked coworkers to cover appointments across
town. When she did have to travel, she spent the ride rigid and watchful, tracking traffic and exits
rather than the conversation. She also stopped allowing herself to relax. At home she kept herself
busy with chores, paperwork, or television because letting her guard down felt dangerous, as though
calm itself would leave her exposed. She began avoiding physical strain too; if climbing stairs
quickly, carrying boxes of donated goods, or even feeling flushed and winded made her body feel
stressed, she backed away. Underneath was the conviction that her body could not be relied on and
that once events were in motion she would be helpless again.

But the strategies that were meant to protect her seemed to make the world smaller and more charged.
Because she drove less and avoided being a passenger, the sight of wrecked cars when she did
encounter them hit with even more force, vivid and immediate. Because she continually monitored her
body and steered away from physical stress, ordinary sensations of illness or fatigue stood out more
and more, quickly becoming cues for danger. Because she would not let herself relax, her baseline
tension stayed high, making her more jumpy at sounds and quicker to search rooms for exits. Her
caution at work had begun to affect her ministry. She sometimes delayed home visits that required a
long drive, and in meetings she chose seats near doors and struggled to stay present when a noise
startled her. She felt ashamed of that, especially because her role had always depended on conveying
steadiness to people in distress.

By the time she sought help, Laura was exhausted by the effort of trying to stay ahead of danger.
She could see that the more she organized her days around avoiding cars, bodily stress, and any real
relaxation, the less safe she actually felt. She came in after realizing she had turned down an
important visit with a congregant because the route required highway driving, then spent the evening
shaken by a news clip showing a traffic collision. What frightened her most was the sense that her
life was being run by a constant expectation of disaster and by the belief that she would be
