school, where much of the day was spent escorting students through noisy hallways, waiting at pickup
lines, and watching for small changes in children’s behavior. The stalking began in a way that was
hard to pin down at first. Over several weeks, there were repeated moments outside her apartment and
near school when she had the distinct sense of someone behind her: footsteps that seemed to keep
pace when she turned onto a side street, the prickling feeling of being watched from a parked car,
the impression that whoever was following knew her routines. What made it frightening was not one
single dramatic incident but the accumulation of these encounters, each leaving her more certain
that she was being tracked and observed in ordinary places she could not avoid.

When Moana describes that period now, the memory does not come as one coherent story. Some pieces
are sharply preserved: the sound of footsteps closing in behind her after she left school at dusk,
the way she did not want to look back, the sensation of holding her keys too tightly in her hand.
Other stretches are absent altogether. She says there are blanks where she knows something happened
but cannot retrieve it, and the whole sequence feels like disconnected pieces rather than a
continuous account. The moments when she felt most intensely on guard are the moments that remain
clearest; the rest seem to fall away, which leaves her with an incomplete picture that still feels
unresolved.

In the months that followed, the same kinds of cues began to pull those fragments to the surface. If
she was walking from the staff parking lot and heard footsteps behind her, or if she noticed someone
lingering in her peripheral vision near the school entrance, she would instantly tense and begin
trying to work out what she had missed back then and what she should have done differently. She
would replay routes, timing, and small decisions—whether she should have left school earlier,
crossed the street sooner, told someone immediately—as if enough mental review might finally produce
the missing parts and keep it from ever happening again. She also stopped allowing herself to relax;
even sitting on the couch with her partner in the evening felt reckless, because letting down her
guard seemed to invite danger. Instead she checked windows, looked over her shoulder on walks, and
scanned parking lots and hallways before moving through them.

Those habits gradually took on a life of their own. At school, where she needed to appear calm for
students who were easily dysregulated, Moana found herself repeatedly looking toward the classroom
door when she heard movement in the corridor. During dismissal she monitored the sidewalk, the

jolt before she had even identified what had startled her. The more she checked her surroundings and
kept herself keyed up, the more watchful she became overall; being “superalert” started to feel less
like a temporary response and more like the only safe way to move through the day. She had come to
believe that if she ever stopped scanning, she would miss the sign that mattered.

That constant state of alertness also shaped how the stalking remained in her mind. Because she was
always looking for danger in the present, new moments of suspicion seemed to merge with the old ones
rather than settle into the past. Hearing footsteps behind her on the way to her car did not just
frighten her in the moment; it brought back a few vivid shards of the original experience while
leaving the rest as blank as ever. She became increasingly preoccupied with those blanks. If the
memory was incomplete, she reasoned, then she might still be overlooking something crucial, and that
thought sent her back into more replaying, more route-checking, more refusal to relax.

Over time, this made the triggers broader and more frequent. Because she was so busy monitoring who

lingering after pickup, footsteps in the corridor, someone turning suddenly in a grocery store
aisle. What once had been specific reminders now seemed to be scattered through her whole day. Her
partner noticed that even on quiet evenings Moana would pause mid-conversation to listen for sounds
outside or get up to check the lock again. She was exhausted, but rest itself felt dangerous to her,
as though calmness were exactly what would leave her exposed.

By the time she sought help, what frightened Moana most was not only the memory of being stalked but
the sense that her life had reorganized around anticipating it. She was still teaching, still
showing up for her students, but she was doing so while braced, watchful, and mentally rehearsing
how she might prevent the next threat. She wanted therapy because she was tired of living as if
danger were always just behind her, and because she feared that if she could not make sense of the
missing pieces, she would never be able to stop looking over her shoulder.
