Samira Haddad is an 80-year-old widowed social worker who still thinks of herself as someone who
should be able to stay steady in the presence of suffering. The event that fractured that sense of
herself began years ago when her adult son became critically ill and was hospitalized. She remembers
the fluorescent light on his skin, the steady drip from the IV, the sharp visibility of needles and
tubing against the hospital sheets, and the antiseptic smell that seemed to cling to her clothes
even after she went home. Day after day she sat by his bed while machines marked time she could not
control. What stayed with her most was not a single picture so much as the bodily force of it: the
wave of dread and grief that would overtake her while she watched people move around him and do
things she could not stop. She had come to believe, in those long hospital days, that she was
helpless in the face of catastrophe and that she had failed in ways that violated the kind of mother
and person she believed herself to be.

Afterward, the memories did not remain in the past. They forced their way into Samira’s mind without
warning, often not as a clear scene but as the full emotional intensity of those days descending on
her all at once. A discussion of someone losing a loved one at work could bring it on immediately;
so could the antiseptic smell in a clinic hallway or the sight of medical equipment, even something
as ordinary as a needle laid out for a blood draw. In those moments she was not simply reminded of
what happened. She was flooded by overwhelming grief, and with it the old certainty that bad things
can happen without warning and that she cannot stop them. The triggers seemed to prove to her how
permanently changed she was, as if the world had narrowed into places and conversations where loss
might break through again.

Because she interpreted these reactions as signs that something in her had been damaged, Samira
began organizing her life around not setting them off. She pushed thoughts of the hospitalization
out of her mind whenever they appeared, cut down on going out, and avoided hospitals, ambulances,
and even routine physical examinations. In her work as a social worker, this became increasingly
difficult. Cases involving bereavement or family illness left her bracing herself before meetings,
scanning for words about death or loss, already tense at the possibility of being pulled back into
that emotional state. The more she anticipated being overwhelmed, the more potent those cues became;
even the idea of entering a medical building or catching a disinfectant smell in an elevator could
make her feel as though she were approaching the original disaster again.

Her efforts to keep the memories away gave her brief moments of relief, but over weeks and months
they tightened the problem around her. When a memory surged up with no clear image, only raw grief,
she would stay home the next day, cancel an appointment, or refuse a checkup, telling herself she
needed to avoid anything that might stir it further. Yet the shrinking of her world seemed to
confirm her bleakest conclusions. If she could not tolerate a doctor’s office or a client’s story of
losing someone, then perhaps she really had changed for the worse. Avoiding medical cues also left
the original experience feeling unfinished and unprocessed, so the next encounter with a needle, an
IV pole, or antiseptic smell struck with the same force. Each episode of uncontrolled remembering
deepened her sense of helplessness, and that helplessness made the next intrusion feel even more
convincing.

What frightened Samira most was not only the grief itself but what it said about her. She was
ashamed of moments during her son’s illness when she had felt numb, wanted to step out of the room,
or silently wished for the ordeal to stop; in her mind these responses meant she had acted against
her values at the very time she should have been strongest. When memories intruded now, that shame
came with them, and when she caught herself avoiding clients’ loss narratives or postponing her own
medical care, it reinforced her fear that she was no longer the capable, compassionate woman she had
been.

She finally sought help after recognizing how much of her life was being governed by this pattern.
She had recently left a work conversation when a family member described fearing a loved one’s
death, then sat in her car shaken by grief she could not place into pictures, only feeling. She
realized she was no longer simply mourning; she was living as if the hospital could reopen around
any corner. At 80, she says she wants some part of her life back. What she fears is that if she
stops pushing the memories away, she will be swallowed by them; what she hopes is that the sight of
a needle, the smell of antiseptic, or the mention of loss will no longer make her feel condemned to
relive the same helplessness.
