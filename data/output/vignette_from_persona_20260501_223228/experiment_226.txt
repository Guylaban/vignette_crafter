Leila is a 21-year-old Middle Eastern American woman who came to treatment several months after
leaving military service, speaking about the deployment in a flat, careful way that suggested how
much effort it took to stay composed. She described one night in particular that still stood apart
from the rest of her time overseas: a sudden attack after dark, when the base had dropped into that
tense half-silence she used to think of as the most dangerous hour. She remembered the jolt of
movement, the confusion of running, the sense that her body had been pushed past what it could
safely hold. What stayed with her was not a single visual image so much as the bodily memory of
strain and exposure in the dark, the feeling that once night came and she had to move fast, she was
no longer in control of what could happen.

Since returning home, Leila has built her life to keep that feeling from being stirred up. She now
works as a database administrator, a job she chose in part because it lets her sit in a predictable,
climate-controlled office and spend most of the day behind a screen. She avoids situations that put
her body under stress. She does not exercise beyond what feels absolutely necessary, declines
invitations that involve hiking or even long walks, and notices herself becoming uneasy if she has
to rush for a train or carry something heavy up the stairs. If her heart rate rises or her muscles
start to burn, she pulls back quickly, as though her body is approaching a threshold she cannot
afford to cross again. To her, physical strain does not feel healthy or invigorating; it feels like
the beginning of danger.

Her evenings have become increasingly restricted. Leila leaves work before sunset whenever she can
and feels a steady tightening in herself if a database update or server issue threatens to keep her
in the office late. When colleagues suggest getting dinner after work, she usually finds an excuse.
Walking through the parking garage at dusk, seeing the light flatten and the shadows lengthen, is
enough to make her grip her keys and hurry, her attention narrowing to getting inside the car and
home with the doors locked. She has stopped going out at night almost entirely. Friends have begun
to comment that she disappears after dark, but to Leila this feels less like a choice than a rule
she has had to live by: nighttime is when things happen, when visibility drops, when control
disappears.

Inside her apartment, she relies on order in a way that has gradually taken over more of her day.
She cleans and reorganizes repeatedly, especially after work and before bed. She wipes kitchen
counters that are already clean, lines up items on her desk until the edges match exactly, and
restacks bathroom supplies if one label is turned the wrong way. The rituals calm her briefly. She
describes a sense that if the room is exact, if everything is in its place, she can settle her body
for a moment and feel less exposed. On weekends she can spend hours moving between small
tasks—laundry folded and refolded, shelves sorted, surfaces cleaned again—not because she believes
the apartment is dirty, but because the repetition gives her a fragile sense of control.

These habits have narrowed her life in concrete ways. At work, Leila is dependable and meticulous,
but she volunteers for remote troubleshooting rather than any task that might require staying late
in the server room or crossing campus after dark. She keeps protein bars at her desk so she can skip
walking to crowded food spots, and she has quietly arranged her routines to avoid any unnecessary
exertion. Her sister has complained that visiting Leila no longer feels relaxed, because a dropped
jacket or dish left in the sink can pull Leila away mid-conversation to straighten and scrub. Leila
knows this makes her seem rigid, and she is embarrassed by how much relief she gets from these
routines, but she does not know how else to keep herself steady.

By the time she sought help, the structure she had built around herself was starting to feel like a
trap. A recent overnight systems failure required her team to rotate evening coverage, and the
prospect of being responsible after dark left her panicked and ashamed. She feared that if she could
not avoid night, and if her body were forced into stress again, she would lose whatever control she
had managed to construct. What she wants most is simple enough to say and hard for her to imagine:
to stay in her job, to see people without arranging her whole life around sunset, and to be able to
come home without feeling she must clean and organize for hours just to make it through the night.
