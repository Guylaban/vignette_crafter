Nabil Haddad was 52 when he came for treatment, a Lebanese American actor who had spent most of his
adult life working on sets, in rehearsal rooms, and at public events where being watched was part of
the job. Two years earlier, he had been with his wife at a crowded outdoor street festival when a
vehicle struck pedestrians at the barricade line. What remained with him was not a smooth story but
a series of still images: his wife turning at the sound of children crying somewhere behind them,
the metallic crash, a stroller tipped on its side, the frozen look on her face, the bright emergency
lights, and later a photograph-like glimpse of her hand on the pavement. He could not make the
sequence fully cohere. The memory came in jagged pieces, and each return to it brought a crushing
thought that he had failed her, that if he had pulled her back sooner or noticed danger faster she
might have lived.

In the months after her death, ordinary sounds became charged. Children crying in a grocery store,
sirens or screeching brakes outside his apartment, even news footage of cars plowing through crowds
or segments about public safety failures on television could jolt him back into those disconnected
snapshots. When that happened, he was overtaken not only by fear and horror but by a renewed
conviction that the people meant to protect the public had failed catastrophically and that those
responsible had gone on without real consequences. He spoke bitterly about city officials, event
organizers, and the security presence that day. He had come to believe that no one had truly been
held accountable, and every fresh reminder in the media reopened both the images and the grievance.

Those reminders also altered the way he moved through his days. Because a crying child or the sound
of brakes could set off a rush of fear and make his mind splinter into vivid visual fragments, Nabil
began arranging his life around not being caught off guard. He stopped going to shopping centers and
avoided premieres, theater lobbies, and public festivals where crowds pressed close and escape felt
uncertain. At home he kept doors open and the television on in the background because being alone in
a quiet room felt dangerous in a way he could not explain to others. If castmates suggested a
restaurant after rehearsal, he went along with whatever they wanted rather than risk disagreement or
separation from the group; compliance had started to feel like a form of protection.

His sense of threat no longer depended on what anyone told him. Friends, his agent, and even his
adult daughter would reassure him that he was safe now, but he found he could not take that in. On
set he lost the thread of dialogue during scenes if a child actor cried off camera or if a prop
vehicle made a sudden sound. He would become flooded with fear, then angry, then ashamed of how
quickly his concentration vanished. In those moments he was certain safety was fragile and
unreliable, and that certainty sharpened his attention to the very cues he dreaded. He scanned for
noises, for exits, for signs of crowding, which only made the sounds and media stories stand out
more vividly.

The more threatened he felt, the more rigid his habits became. He accepted invitations he did not
want, lingered in green rooms rather than dressing alone, and let others decide where to sit, when
to leave, and how to travel, because any assertion of his own preference felt like risking exposure.
Those strategies narrowed his life and, in a painful way, kept his wife’s death mentally close.
Staying attached to other people for safety meant hearing more ambient television in waiting areas,
more public conversation about current attacks, more unpredictable noise. Avoiding crowds required
constant calculation about where crowds might be, and that constant watchfulness made children’s
crying and traffic sounds leap out at him. After these episodes, the same visual shards returned,
along with the punishing idea that he had not done enough and still was not doing enough.

Over time he began to judge himself through the lens of those precautions. The fact that he could
not be by himself in his own apartment without spiraling, or enter a busy event without studying
every exit, seemed to him further proof that the world was not safe and that he had been naive
before. He also took his own people-pleasing as evidence of moral failure: if he now went along with
whatever others wanted just to feel protected, maybe he had always been too passive, the kind of
husband who would miss the moment when action was needed. That belief deepened the guilt already
attached to the memory and left him even more keyed up.

What finally brought Nabil in was a recent audition for a streaming series. In the waiting area, a
child in the hall began to cry while a monitor played muted news footage of a vehicle incident. He
lost focus, saw his wife’s hand on the pavement as sharply as if it were in front of him, and had to
leave before reading. He told the clinician that he was afraid he was becoming impossible to work
with and impossible to live as, a man organized entirely around not being alone and not being
surprised. More than anything, he feared that if he let go of his vigilance, he would be failing his
wife again; but he also said, quietly, that he wanted some way to remember her without being dragged
back into those frozen images every time the world made a familiar sound.
