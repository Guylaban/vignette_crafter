Since the exposure, Kwame, a 62-year-old bomb disposal technician, has described his world as
permanently edged with danger. The incident happened at work during what had first seemed like a
controlled response to a suspicious device in an industrial setting. When the container was opened,
it released a toxic chemical plume before the team could fully contain it. He remembers the sharp,
sterile-burning smell in the air, the confusion of shouted instructions, and the abrupt sense that
his own body had become unsafe terrain. In the aftermath he was taken through medical evaluation,
surrounded by antiseptic odors and emergency activity that have remained fused in his mind with the
moment he realized he might not be able to protect himself. What stays vivid for him is not only the
exposure itself, but the feeling that danger was invisible, already in the air, and could reach him
before he had time to react.

In the weeks afterward, the workplace itself became charged for him. Entering the facility, hearing
sudden loud noises, or catching even a faint hospital or antiseptic smell could pull him into the
same tense, immediate alarm. He found that these moments did not just upset him; they changed what
he did. He began avoiding medical settings whenever possible, putting off examinations and steering
away from hospitals and ambulances because the sights and smells felt too close to the exposure. He
also started arranging his days so that he was rarely by himself in a room or building, and when he
could not avoid those situations he felt markedly less steady. Most of all, he worked excessively.
Staying busy on the job made sense to him because it kept him from sitting alone with the memories
and bodily tension that seemed to gather the minute the environment reminded him of what had
happened.

Those same reminders also kept his body and attention in a state of readiness. At work, a loud
metallic bang or an unexpected sound in the bay would make him jerk and immediately search the area,
and he noticed that once he was keyed up he could not stop scanning for signs of danger. In any
building, including restaurants or his church, he was uncomfortable until he had identified the
exits and decided how he would get out if something went wrong. The antiseptic smell of clinics was
especially potent; even passing a medical office was enough to make him more vigilant, more aware of
who was near him, and more likely to react sharply if someone touched him without warning. His wife
has learned not to come up behind him because he startles so intensely that he feels embarrassed and
angry afterward.

Over time, this constant watchfulness seemed to make the reminders spread. Because Kwame was already
scanning his surroundings and mapping escape routes before he felt at ease, ordinary parts of the
workplace stood out to him as possible signs of threat. A routine noise he might once have ignored
now caught his full attention and felt loaded. When he was tense, even a cleaning product with a
sterile scent could register as if it belonged to an emergency room, and he would feel himself
transported back into the atmosphere of the exposure. The more alert he stayed, the easier it became
for the workplace, loud sounds, and medical smells to set him off again.

His ways of coping have tightened that pattern rather than easing it. By avoiding hospitals and
examinations, he gets short-term relief, but he remains convinced those places are unbearable and
dangerous to enter. By refusing to be alone, he never has the chance to discover that solitude
itself is not the threat he feels it to be; instead he stays on edge whenever separation seems
possible. Overworking has narrowed his life further. He takes extra shifts and lingers at work
rather than going home early, telling himself it is better to stay occupied than to be left with his
thoughts. Yet the fatigue leaves him even more jumpy, more likely to react to a touch on the
shoulder, and more dependent on scanning and exit planning to feel barely in control. His wife has
begun to complain that even when he is physically home, he is not really with her, just watching the
room.

By the time he sought help, Kwame was exhausted by how small and effortful his life had become. He
could still function in his highly specialized job, but only by staying in motion and never letting
his guard down. What finally brought him in was the recognition that he was organizing every setting
around escape, every day around avoiding reminders, and still feeling as if the danger could break
through at any moment. He says he wants to stop living “like the leak is still happening.” What
frightens him most is the possibility that if he stops monitoring and pushing himself, he will be
caught off guard again. What he hopes, cautiously, is that he might be able to be in a room by
himself, hear an abrupt noise, or walk past a hospital without feeling that his body has already
been overtaken.
