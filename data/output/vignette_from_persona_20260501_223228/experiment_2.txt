Luis Herrera is a 67-year-old widowed prosecutor who came to treatment after months of telling
himself that, at his age and in his profession, he should be able to manage on his own. The assault
happened late one evening after a work function tied to a case. Someone he knew professionally, a
man whose face and voice had once registered as ordinary and safe, offered to walk him to his car
and instead forced himself on Luis in a parked vehicle. What stayed with him afterward was not only
the humiliation and helplessness but the physical immediacy of it: the feel of cold air against
exposed skin when the car door opened, the pressure of another body too close, and the awful
dissonance of recognizing the person harming him. The next morning, he could still recount tiny
details with prosecutorial precision—the smell in the car, the angle of the dashboard light, the
temperature on his skin—and the clarity frightened him.

In the weeks that followed, those details did not settle into the past. If a colleague’s brother
appeared at the courthouse or a familiar face from the legal community approached him unexpectedly,
the memory forced its way in with such vividness that Luis felt pulled back into the car, as if the
assault were happening again rather than being over. Being near children could do it too, especially
in public corridors or outside family court, where the ordinary sight of a parent holding a child’s
hand suddenly filled him with shame and disgust at himself. A draft of cold air across his forearms
in an over-air-conditioned conference room could stop him mid-thought. In those moments he did not
simply remember; he felt contaminated all over again, and he was seized by the conviction that
something in his body had been permanently damaged.

Those meanings hardened quickly. Luis had come to believe that the world was fundamentally
dangerous, not in an abstract legal sense but in the intimate, bodily way of knowing that harm can
come from someone known to you, in a place you thought you could leave safely. He found himself
thinking that his body had been altered and made vulnerable, and when the memory arrived unbidden he
read its force as proof that the damage was enduring. The clearer and more present the recollections
felt, the more certain he became that the assault had left him dirty in some lasting way. That
belief, in turn, made encounters with related people—brothers, cousins, sons of colleagues—feel
charged before anything had happened, as though resemblance itself carried danger.

By the time he sought help, these convictions were organizing his days. Entering the courthouse, he
scanned immediately for exits. In meeting rooms he noticed letter openers, heavy staplers, and
anything else that might be used as a weapon before he registered who was speaking. He avoided
unfamiliar restaurants, new office buildings, and any setting where he could not quickly map the
doors and assess how trapped he might be. He told himself this was prudence, but the effect was
narrowing: he began declining professional dinners, asking staff to relocate meetings, and limiting
where he would go after work. Because he was constantly monitoring for danger, he was also
constantly picking up the very cues that set him off—a blast of cold air from a vent, a man whose
features reminded him of the assailant, children gathered in a lobby—and each cue reopened the
assault in his mind.

At work, the cycle had become especially punishing. Luis would enter a deposition room already
checking exits and noting possible weapons, and that state of alert made every known face
potentially threatening. If a witness arrived with a child, or a cooling system kicked on and
brushed cold against his skin, the memory surged back in full detail. Once it came, he lost the
thread of questioning and sat rigid, trying not to show that he was no longer fully in the room.
Afterward he would think, again, that this was evidence of how deeply he had been ruined—still
dirty, still vulnerable, still unsafe anywhere. Those thoughts sharpened his vigilance the next day,
and his vigilance made the next trigger easier to find.

He has told the therapist, with visible embarrassment, that what finally brought him in was not only
the persistence of the memories but the fear that they were changing the kind of man he was. He has
built a career on entering difficult rooms and staying clear-headed. Now he plans every route by
exits, avoids places he cannot evaluate, and dreads ordinary contact with people connected to people
he knows. What frightens him most is the sense that the assault is still present inside him and that
his body will never again feel like his own. What he hopes for, though he says it cautiously, is to
be able to walk into a room without first preparing for an attack and to have the memory feel, at
last, like something that happened rather than something that is still happening.
