Faris Haddad is a 40-year-old paramedic who came to treatment nearly a year after the call that
changed how he understood himself. He had been working a weekend shift when dispatch sent his unit
to a highway collision involving a family sedan and a delivery truck. When he reached the scene, he
recognized the passenger before he fully registered the wreckage: his younger sister’s husband had
been driving, and his sister Layla was in the back seat beside their injured son. There was blood
across Layla’s face and neck, glass in her hair, and the chemical smell of fuel mixed with the sharp
antiseptic odor from the trauma supplies he tore open with shaking hands. He remembers flashes with
terrible clarity—the wet shine of an open wound, Layla looking at him and saying his name, his
nephew crying—and other parts are missing altogether. He cannot piece together exactly when he moved
from the truck to the car, or the sequence of what he said. What does return comes back with the
same terror he felt there, not as a story but as something happening again in full sensory detail.

In the weeks afterward, Faris had come to believe that this memory proved something devastating
about him. He was certain he had failed in the one situation in which being a paramedic and a
brother should have meant he could protect his family. The blank spots in his memory tormented him;
because he could not account for everything he had done, he took the gaps as evidence that he could
not rely on himself when it mattered most. Each reliving left him feeling permanently altered, as if
the competent version of himself had been replaced by someone weaker and less dependable. Those
conclusions sharpened what caught his attention. On shift, visible injuries on patients no longer
looked like routine parts of the job but like accusations, and even passing the ambulance bay sink
when it smelled of antiseptic could make his body brace before he had consciously placed why.

Those reminders quickly began pulling him back into the crash. A scalp laceration on a patient, a
child mentioning his mother, the hospital-clean smell in an emergency department could make the
scene with Layla surge up as fully real, with the same sensory force and panic. At work he would
lose track of report details midway through listening, because his concentration narrowed around the
feeling that something awful was about to happen again. He startled violently if a colleague touched
his shoulder from behind, and in break rooms or waiting areas he automatically chose the chair with
the clearest view of the door and the most protected position against the wall. As he became more
keyed up, he noticed more reminders everywhere: every wound looked too much like that night, every
victim’s face risked turning into his sister’s, every medicinal smell seemed to announce another
disaster.

Faris responded by trying not to think about the crash at all. He would clamp down on images, push
away Layla’s voice, and tell himself to stay focused on tasks. On days off he found excuses to pick
up errands or projects around the house simply to keep his mind occupied, and when he could not
avoid work altogether he overworked within it—restocking excessively, volunteering for extra
administrative tasks, burying himself in checklists. Yet the harder he fought the memory, the more
abruptly it returned. Suppressing it seemed to make the missing pieces feel even more ominous, and
his frantic busyness left him more brittle, more easily startled, and more likely to scan for the
next reminder. He began dreading shifts because he knew they exposed him to injured bodies and
victim stories, but staying away from work also fed his fear that he was no longer the kind of man
who could do his job.

At home, the pattern tightened further. When his wife touched him unexpectedly while he was
distracted, he jerked away in alarm and then felt ashamed. That reaction confirmed, in his mind,
that he had changed for the worse. The shame pushed him to withdraw into chores or extra shifts
rather than talk, and the distance this created in his marriage became one more sign that he was
failing people he loved. His constant need to sit facing entrances in restaurants or family
gatherings embarrassed him, but he felt he had to stay prepared. Living that way kept him on edge,
and being on edge made the crash easier to relive and the triggers easier to spot.

He finally sought help after freezing during a pediatric trauma call when the antiseptic smell and
the sight of facial injuries brought Layla’s bloodied face back so completely that for a moment he
was no longer sure where he was. He finished the call, but afterward he sat in the ambulance unable
to concentrate, convinced he could not trust himself and terrified that next time someone would
suffer because of him. What he wants most is to be able to go to work, come home to his wife, and
remember that night without being dropped back into it; what he fears most is that the man who
failed to protect his family is now the only version of him left.
