Mateo Alvarez is a 28-year-old prison guard who came to treatment months after the sudden death of
his younger brother, who was killed in a car crash late at night while driving home alone. Mateo had
been the person in the family everyone called steady and protective, and in the days after the death
he became fixated on the fact that his brother had called earlier that evening and Mateo had not
picked up because he was on shift. He returns to that detail again and again. In his mind, if he had
answered, if he had kept him talking, if he had insisted he wait until morning, his brother might
still be alive. What has settled in most deeply is not only grief but a harsh conviction that he
failed at the one thing he was supposed to do: protect the people he loves.

Since the death, reminders of loss have become difficult to tolerate in ways that feel immediate and
bodily. At work, the prison break room television often runs local news, and when a segment mentions
a fatal crash or a family speaking about someone they have lost, Mateo feels the room go strangely
far away. He describes a detached, unreal sensation, as if he is still standing there but not fully
inside himself. In those moments he pulls his hands over his face, partly to block out the screen
and partly to hide what is happening to him from other officers. He has also begun steering away
from almost anything that reminds him of his brother or of bereavement more broadly, changing the
channel, walking out when coworkers start talking about somebody’s funeral, refusing family
conversations that turn toward memories. When he cannot get away cleanly, he sometimes picks an
argument instead—snapping over minor things, escalating with his wife or a coworker—because anger
feels more manageable than that sliding, unreal feeling.

These moments do more than upset him; they confirm the conclusions he has drawn about himself.
Hearing stories about other people losing loved ones or feeling that sudden detachment can bring
back, with full force, the belief that he is a man who did not protect his brother and that whatever
used to be hopeful or light in him is gone for good. He says he used to be a happy person and now
feels miserable almost all the time. The future, when he imagines it at all, looks flat and empty.
That conviction follows him into ordinary settings. During intake he described standing in the
prison corridor after hearing an inmate mention his dead mother during a phone call, and immediately
thinking, not just of his brother, but of himself as someone marked by failure and with nothing
ahead except more of the same.

Over time, those beliefs have begun shaping what catches his attention. Because he has come to
expect pain and see himself through this lens of failed protection and permanent misery, talk of
loss jumps out at him everywhere. The news in the staff room, a passing conversation about a death
in another officer’s family, even the first hint of feeling unreal in his own body all register as
signs that he is not okay and may never be okay. He listens for them without meaning to. The world
has narrowed around themes of death and absence.

His ways of coping have made that narrowing worse. Avoiding grief reminders has left him
increasingly cut off from his wife and extended family; he leaves the room when they want to talk
about his brother, and afterward he takes his withdrawal as proof that happiness and closeness are
over for him. Covering his face at work spares him a few moments of exposure, but it also reinforces
his sense that these reactions are dangerous and shameful, something that must be hidden. The
arguments he starts to distract himself bring brief relief, yet afterward he feels guilty,
exhausted, and more convinced that he has become someone miserable to live with. Each of these
habits seems to deepen the same bleak appraisal: that he failed, that he has changed permanently,
that there is no real future to recover into.

They also leave him more easily thrown the next time something brushes against loss. Because he
avoids reminders, even a short news clip can hit him with greater force. Because conflict has become
one of the few ways he knows to escape the ache, tension at home and on the job keeps him keyed
toward the next upsetting exchange. Because he hides his face and waits for the detached feeling to
pass, the feeling itself has become a trigger he dreads and notices immediately.

Mateo sought help after his wife told him she no longer recognized him and after a recent shift in
which a television report about a fatal accident left him frozen and unreal in the break room, hands
over his face, before he picked a fight with a coworker who asked if he was all right. What
frightens him most is that this is all that remains of him now: the brother who failed, the husband
who brings home anger, the man with no future. What he hopes, cautiously, is not to forget his
brother, but to be able to hear about loss without disappearing inside it.
