When Arjun’s eight-year-old son was admitted to the pediatric ICU with a rapidly worsening
infection, the days that followed narrowed into fluorescent light, antiseptic air, and the
mechanical rhythm of monitors. Arjun spent nights in a vinyl chair beside the bed, half-awake and
listening for any change in the boy’s breathing. He still remembers being told to step aside for
procedures, watching masked staff move quickly around his son while he stood uselessly at the edge
of the room. What lodged most deeply was not a single dramatic moment but the repeated sense that
catastrophe could arrive at any second and that he had to stay alert enough to catch it first. By
the time his son recovered and came home, Arjun had the outward appearance of relief, but internally
he had not left the hospital.

In the months afterward, certain moments pulled him back with unnerving force. The sharp smell of
antiseptic in a clinic hallway, a routine medical examination of his own, even checking into a hotel
for a work conference and realizing he would have to sleep alone all carried the same jolt. As an
animator, he often worked long hours in a dim studio and occasionally traveled for production
meetings; the first night away from home became especially difficult because being alone in an
unfamiliar room felt too close to those nights beside his son’s hospital bed, except now there was
no way to keep watch. When these situations arose, Arjun’s mind would immediately lurch into frantic
effort. He would run through worst-case scenarios in detail, telling himself he needed to be
prepared for the next emergency. He repeatedly asked his wife, doctors, or even coworkers whether he
seemed all right, whether his son was really out of danger, whether missing some sign had become
possible again. He also found himself circling angrily around thoughts of how to get even with the
people he felt had failed his family during the hospitalization, replaying delays and perceived
dismissals and imagining confrontations that might finally balance what had happened.

These habits did not settle him for long. The more Arjun rehearsed disaster, the more easily
ordinary cues took on the force of warning. A mild disinfectant smell in an urgent care waiting room
became almost unbearable because he had already spent the morning mentally staging scenes of
collapse and medical crisis. Before his own checkups, he would lie awake imagining bad news, and by
the time he entered the exam room his body was keyed up enough that the paper on the examination
table, the blood pressure cuff, and the clinician’s brisk manner all felt charged. Nights alone
became harder in the same way: in hotel rooms on business trips, he would scan for signs that
something might go wrong at home, call or text repeatedly for reassurance, and mentally rehearse who
to contact if his son became ill again. Those efforts left him more vigilant, not less, so the room
itself began to feel unsafe simply because he was alone with his thoughts.

At home this has gradually changed family life. His wife has become used to late-night questions he
already knows she cannot definitively answer—whether their son looked pale at dinner, whether a
cough sounded different, whether they should book another appointment just to be sure. Arjun
experiences these questions as necessary, almost responsible, yet he can see the fatigue in her
face. At work he remains highly skilled, but on days when he has to attend a medical appointment or
after he catches a hospital-like smell in the building, he loses hours to spiraling preparation and
checking. He has also nursed a bitter internal ledger about the hospital stay, returning again and
again to fantasies of exposing or punishing the staff he believes did not grasp how close his son
came to dying. He recognizes that these thoughts do not lead anywhere, but he cannot seem to stop
using them, as if staying angry might keep him from ever being that helpless again.

He came to treatment after turning down another out-of-town studio trip because the prospect of
sleeping alone in a hotel felt intolerable. What frightened him most was not only the surge of panic
in those moments, but the growing sense that his life was organizing itself around preventing
another catastrophe he could neither predict nor control. He wants to be able to walk into a clinic,
travel for work, or spend a night away without his mind immediately building disaster scenes. More
than that, he hopes to stop living as though his son is still in that hospital bed, while fearing
that if he lets his guard down for even a moment, something terrible will happen again.
