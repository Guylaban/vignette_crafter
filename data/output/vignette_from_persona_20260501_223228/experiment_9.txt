systems that could be monitored, corrected, and backed up. His illness shattered that sense of

tubing approach, and feeling that each breath might be the one that failed. The terror of that time
seemed bound to the sensory details of it. Even now, anything that echoed those moments—tightness in
his chest, the sensation of not getting a full breath, a blood draw tray, a hospital corridor—could
pull him back into the same fear.

was carrying around strong, difficult emotions that came on with startling force: fear first, but
also anger at his body and a quiet shame about how panicked he had been. He said his body no longer
felt neutral to him; it felt primed, tight, watchful, as if danger could break through at any
moment. When something reminded him of the hospitalization, his reaction was immediate and physical.

he had fully registered what had set it off. It was out of this constant sense of threat that his
habits began to change. He became determined to manage anything that might stir up bodily sensations
or place him back in contact with medical settings.

Daniel started by avoiding exercise. Before the illness he had occasionally used the treadmill in
his apartment gym, but he gave it up completely because any increase in breathing felt too close to
the start of the medical crisis. He also began eating in a rigid, controlling way, restricting what
and when he ate because the discipline itself made him feel less vulnerable, more able to keep his
body from doing something unpredictable. Most noticeably, he avoided medical cues wherever he could.
He postponed routine checkups, looked away from clinic advertisements, and took a longer route to
his office building if an ambulance was parked near the hospital entrance across the street. These
strategies made sense to him in the moment, because they seemed to lower the chance that he would
feel that old terror again, but they also kept his world narrowed around the possibility of danger.
The less he tested his body and the less contact he had with ordinary medical situations, the more
tense and reactive he became.

That pattern had tightened over time. Because he avoided exercise, ordinary exertion left him
deconditioned, and climbing the stairs at work could bring on mild breathlessness that felt
immediately ominous. Because he restricted eating so strictly, physical sensations in his body
became something he monitored closely and mistrusted. Because he steered clear of examinations and
hospitals, the sight of medical equipment remained charged and unfamiliar rather than ordinary. In
his daily work life this was increasingly hard to hide. When a coworker mentioned a recent
outpatient procedure, Daniel felt his shoulders lock and had to leave the break room. During a

physical reaction that he told his manager he was ill and went home. Even seeing a drip stand on a

handle one. Each time he avoided exertion, he had less confidence in his breathing. Each time he
controlled his eating more tightly, he felt more preoccupied with his body and more easily
frightened by it. Daniel had come to believe that if he let his guard down—even with something as
simple as jogging, a checkup, or sitting through a blood draw—he might be overwhelmed again, trapped
in the same horror and helplessness he felt in the hospital.

careful. What finally brought him in was a recent episode at work: after hurrying across the parking
lot and arriving short of breath, he felt a surge of panic so intense that he had to lock himself in
a restroom stall until the shaking passed. He was frightened by how quickly his body could still
turn on him, and by how small his life had become around avoiding that fear. More than anything,
Daniel wanted to stop living as if the next breath might be a threat.
