Amina is a 21-year-old African American woman who came to treatment in the first year after
returning from combat deployment. Before enlistment, and even during training, she had thought of
herself as steady under pressure, the person other people trusted with difficult conversations; that
identity had carried naturally into her current work as a counselor. The event she returns to most
often happened during a night operation. The area had dropped into near-total darkness except for
intermittent aircraft light and the heavy chopping sound of helicopters overhead. In the confusion,
there had been a brief exchange about getting word to someone’s family if the team did not make it
back, and that detail fixed itself in her mind alongside the darkness and the aircraft noise. Since
then, those elements have not felt like neutral reminders. They land as if they are pulling her
straight back toward the moment when she believes something in her failed.

In the weeks after returning home, Amina found that comments about family could unsettle her
abruptly. Even routine conversations at work, when a client mentioned a mother calling too often or
a coworker joked about family drama, could make her chest tighten and her attention narrow. If she
left the office after sunset and had to cross a dim parking lot, or if a helicopter passed overhead,
she felt the same jolt. In those moments she did not only feel frightened; she also became harshly
certain about what the reminders meant. She had come to believe there was something about her that
made the event happen, that someone more competent would not have ended up in that situation, and
that the version of herself she respected—the capable, reliable one—was gone. The trigger was not
just a cue; it seemed to prove, again and again, her own defectiveness.

She started organizing her life around preventing that collapse. Because family talk had become so
charged, she withdrew from people she had socialized with before deployment, especially those likely
to ask about home or to compare who she was now with who she had been. At work as a counselor, she
became skilled at redirecting conversations away from anything that sounded too close to family loss
or reunion, and she volunteered for tasks that let her stay behind a closed office door rather than
chat in shared spaces. She stopped trying to build new friendships and shut down early signs of
romantic interest, convinced she no longer had the capacity to be the person others would expect.
She also stopped watching films and television that involved war, military scenes, or even rescue
aircraft, because the risk of hearing rotor blades in a living room felt intolerable.

These efforts brought short-term relief, but over months they narrowed her world and deepened the
meaning she made of what had happened. The less she saw old friends, the less chance she had to
experience herself as more than the person from that night. Avoiding new relationships left her
increasingly alone with the thought that no one who really knew her now would see her as capable.
Steering away from media that touched anything similar kept the whole subject feeling dangerous and
unfinished. She took the fact that she needed so many precautions as evidence that she truly was no
longer able to do the things that once defined her. Each retreat seemed to confirm her private
comparison: somebody else would have handled this better, recovered faster, and gone back to living.

Her avoidance also made the triggers spread and sharpen. Because she kept refusing dim restaurants,
evening gatherings, and post-work invitations, ordinary darkness remained unfamiliar and menacing.
Because she changed the channel or left the room whenever aircraft sounds came on, even a distant
helicopter over the city could stop her cold. Because she cut herself off from social contacts,
comments about family at work struck with even greater force; she had no buffer, no ordinary
conversation in which “family” could mean anything other than the charged fragment she carries from
deployment. In session she described feeling ambushed by small things that other people do not even
notice, then hating herself for being ambushed at all.

By the time she sought help, the strain was showing in her job and daily life. She was still
functioning enough to keep working as a counselor, but she had begun arranging her schedule to avoid
late hours, declining collegial lunches, and leaving break rooms when conversation turned personal.
She is single, and what troubles her is not only that she feels lonely but that she has started to
see loneliness as safer and more fitting for who she is now. She came to treatment after realizing
she was building her entire life around not hearing, not seeing, not being asked. What frightens her
most is the possibility that this withdrawn, diminished version of herself is permanent; what she
hopes, though she says it cautiously, is that she might learn how to be in the world again without
every dark space, helicopter, or family remark turning into proof that she is to blame and no longer
capable.
