Arjun Mehta was 37 when he came for treatment, a married Indian American psychologist who was
embarrassed by how much effort it now took to get through an ordinary day. The event he returned to
in fragments had happened eight months earlier, when he and his wife were leaving an evening
community program at a cultural center in the city. It was already dark. As they moved toward the
parking area, gunfire erupted nearby and people scattered in confused waves, ducking behind cars and
concrete planters. Arjun could remember the metallic crack of the shots far more clearly than
anything visual. What stayed with him was the instant collapse of order: bodies pushing, his wife
pulled out of his reach for several seconds, the sickening knowledge that he could not control where
to run, who was safe, or whether another shot was coming. By the time they got home, he had begun to
feel that something essential had shifted. He was not only shaken by what had happened; he had come
to believe that the world itself had revealed its true nature as violently unsafe, and that when
danger came, he was powerless to stop bad things from happening.

In the first weeks after the attack, nighttime became especially difficult. Waking in the dark, even
in his own bedroom, brought back the same bodily jolt he had felt in the parking lot, as if the loss
of control were happening again before he had fully opened his eyes. Sharp sounds outside—an engine
backfiring, construction noise, anything resembling a gunshot—could stop him in place. In those
moments he found his mind snapping to the same conclusions: this is what the world is really like,
danger can break in anywhere, and he cannot protect himself or the people he loves. He was
disturbed, too, by what he saw in himself. He had spent years helping patients think clearly under
stress, and now he found himself reacting before he could reason. The person he had become felt
unrecognizable to him.

Those reactions began shaping his days. At the hospital clinic where he worked, he started turning
down cases he expected to be emotionally intense, telling colleagues he needed to protect his
bandwidth. He left evening meetings early so he would not have to walk to the parking garage after
dark. If there was a choice between a manageable inconvenience and anything that felt potentially
stressful, he avoided the stressor. When a loud sound from the street cut through a session, he
noticed his attention drifting away from the patient into an inward, unreal space where he did not
have to be present for the tension in his body. At home he began pouring a drink most nights before
bed, and on some nights added leftover medication from an old prescription, hoping to blunt the edgy
anticipation that came with darkness. The more convinced he became that the world was dangerous and
that he was no longer the steady, capable man he used to be, the more these habits made sense to him
as necessary ways of getting through.

But over the following months those ways of coping deepened the very beliefs that frightened him.
Each avoided errand, each refusal to stay out after sunset, seemed to confirm that ordinary life
really was too dangerous to manage. When he drifted off mentally during tense moments, he felt
ashamed afterward, reading it as proof that he could no longer face reality directly. Drinking and
medicating gave temporary relief, yet the next morning he often woke groggy in the dark before dawn,
disoriented and immediately on edge. That state made him feel even less in control, strengthening
his conviction that bad things could happen to him at any moment and that he would be helpless
again.

The cycle tightened around specific triggers. Because he increasingly organized his life around
avoiding stress, his world became narrower and more brittle; small disruptions hit him harder. A
late-night phone alert, fireworks in the distance, or waking to a dark room after his wife had
already gone downstairs could set off an outsized surge of alarm. He noticed that after evenings of
drinking, ordinary nighttime sounds seemed more menacing, and his startled response to anything like
gunfire was quicker, not less. Even at work, in an office where he once felt grounded, he found
himself scanning for exits when a door slammed in the hallway. He would retreat into detached
daydreaming after these moments, or cancel the rest of his schedule if he could, because the trigger
had already convinced him that the day was no longer safe.

His marriage had begun to feel the effects. His wife had stopped asking him to join friends for
dinners or concerts after dark because he almost always found a reason not to go. He hated the
shrinking look of concern on her face when he checked locks repeatedly before bed, yet he also felt
certain that if he relaxed, he would be failing to prevent the next catastrophe. What finally
brought him in was a recent incident at the clinic: a car backfired outside, and he lost the thread
of a patient’s disclosure completely, staring past her and feeling himself disappear into that
private, far-off place. He was ashamed that his patients now encountered the version of him he
barely recognized. Sitting in treatment, he said he was afraid this was permanent—that he had
learned something terrible and true about the world and about himself—but he was also hoping,
cautiously, that he might not always have to live as though the gunshots were about to start again.
