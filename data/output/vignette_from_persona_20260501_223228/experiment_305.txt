Laila Haddad is a 43-year-old pharmacist who came to treatment after months of telling herself she
should be able to manage on her own. She grew up in a home where her primary caregiver was
physically abusive, most often after drinking. Some of her clearest childhood images are of being
cornered in a kitchen while alcohol was on his breath, hearing his voice harden into commands, and
learning that the safest way to survive was to obey instantly and stop showing feeling. What stayed
with her was not one discrete incident so much as a recurring sense that danger could arrive in
ordinary domestic moments, especially when an adult was giving orders and she could smell alcohol.

In her adult life those old conditions are woven into the setting of her work. Laila spends long
shifts behind the pharmacy counter, where she is constantly approached by supervisors, physicians,
and impatient customers telling her what needs to be done and how quickly. If a manager gives a
brisk instruction, or if a customer leans in smelling of liquor, she feels her body react before she
has time to think. She becomes certain something is wrong even when nothing objectively is
happening; her attention narrows, her concentration slips, and later that night she lies awake
feeling she has to stay alert. The same moments can pull up memories that feel more real than
ordinary remembering, as if she is back in the childhood scene rather than at work. At times the
emotion surges so forcefully that she has to mentally cut herself off; at other times she can
picture what happened with a chilling numbness, as though it occurred to someone else.

Those reactions have come to mean something devastating to her. When she is shaken by a supervisor’s
tone or by the smell of alcohol on a customer, she does not read it as a passing reminder; she takes
it as proof that she still cannot stop bad things from happening to her. She has come to believe
that nothing in her life feels meaningful anymore, and that she has failed in her most important
role in life. She speaks about this vaguely but painfully, with the sense that she has let down the
people who depend on her simply by being so inwardly ruled by fear. Once she is in that state, even
neutral aspects of work begin to stand out as potential warnings. Authority itself has become
charged for her, and the pharmacy—full of instructions, hierarchies, and close contact with the
public—starts to feel like a place where she is always bracing for the next blow.

Over time she has organized her life around not setting these reactions off. At work she avoids
supervisors when she can, keeping interactions brief, asking colleagues to relay messages, and
dreading anyone in a position of authority. Outside work she turns down dinners, family outings, and
occasions that might make her feel light or hopeful, almost superstitiously avoiding positive
moments. She says that letting herself feel good seems dangerous, as if hope only leaves her
exposed. On the worst evenings, when a vivid memory has broken through and she feels overwhelmed
beyond words, she hurts herself physically to interrupt the images and force her mind onto something
immediate. These strategies make sense to her in the moment: if orders, authority, and alcohol smell
can pull her straight back into terror, then shrinking her world seems safer.

Yet the habits that are meant to protect her have tightened the problem. Avoiding supervisors has
made the workplace feel more hostile and unpredictable, so that authority cues catch her even
faster. Refusing positive experiences has deepened her conviction that life has lost meaning and
that she has already failed. Hurting herself may cut off a memory for a moment, but it leaves her
frightened by how far things have gone, more alert at night, and more convinced that she is not in
control. The more she scans for danger, the more readily she notices commanding voices, uniforms,
and alcohol on someone’s breath. The more often she escapes or shuts down, the more the memories
return either with unbearable vividness or in detached, emotionless fragments that still seem to
confirm that this is not really over.

By the time she sought help, the impact was visible in her work and marriage. She was making more
effortful checks at the pharmacy because she could not hold her concentration, and she was going
home depleted, withdrawn, and ashamed. What finally frightened her was how small her life had become
and how automatic her reactions felt. She wants relief from the sense that danger is everywhere, but
what she fears most is that she is permanently trapped in the role she learned as a child—helpless,
watchful, and unable to protect what matters.
