At 44, Linh Nguyen came to treatment reluctantly, at her wife’s urging, describing herself as
“stupidly jumpy” since a toxic chemical exposure at work the year before. Linh is a tattoo artist in
a busy custom studio, a job she has always loved for its precision and for the way it lets her
control pace, touch, and atmosphere. The exposure happened on an unusually hectic afternoon when a
disinfecting solution and another cleaning agent were mixed in a back prep area. She remembers the
sharp, sterile smell turning suddenly overwhelming, catching in her throat and nose with a force
that felt wrong immediately. Within minutes her eyes were burning, her chest tightened, and
coworkers were shouting over one another, telling her to sit down, to stay still, to let someone
else decide what to do. She was dizzy enough to need emergency care, but what stayed with her most
vividly was not only the physical panic of not being able to trust the air she was breathing. It was
the feeling of being handled, watched, and directed while she struggled to understand what was
happening inside her own body.

Since then, certain moments have carried the same charge as the exposure itself. The smell of
antiseptic is the clearest one. When a client comes in freshly cleaned with a medical-looking skin
prep, or when the studio uses stronger disinfectant at the end of the day, Linh feels her body tense
before she can think it through. She also notices it when passing a clinic corridor in the medical
building next door, where the air sometimes carries that cold hospital smell into the shared
hallway. She becomes quiet and vigilant, scanning the room as if something dangerous might already
be in the air again.

What unsettles her nearly as much is the feeling of being watched. Before the incident, being
observed while she worked was ordinary; clients often looked in the mirror, apprentices hovered, and
walk-ins peered over portfolios while she tattooed. Now she finds herself acutely aware of eyes on
her. If a client studies her too closely while she sets up her station, or if the shop manager
stands behind her shoulder, she feels exposed in a way that is hard for her to explain. She has said
that being looked at can feel “like that day all over again,” when staff in the emergency department
monitored her face, breathing, and movements while she sat there unable to regain any sense of
command over herself.

The other trigger is more interpersonal but just as immediate. Linh reacts strongly when someone
tells her what she must do, especially in a pressured or authoritative tone. Her wife has noticed
that even small directives—“sit down for a minute,” “you need to call them back now,” “let me handle
this”—can make Linh bristle or abruptly shut down. In the studio, if a client becomes controlling
about the process or insists she proceed in a way that makes her feel boxed in, she has to fight the
urge to leave the room. What rises in her in those moments is not simple irritation. It is the same
trapped feeling she remembers from the exposure and its aftermath, when other people took over while
she was frightened and physically overwhelmed.

Over the months, these reactions have narrowed her world in practical ways. Linh still works, but
she has changed the conditions under which she can tolerate it. She avoids being in the shop during
major cleaning, asks coworkers to handle certain prep tasks if the smell is too strong, and prefers
clients she knows well because they are less likely to stare or micromanage. She has become more
particular about how appointments are scheduled and who is in the room, which has caused some
tension with colleagues in a workplace that used to feel easy and communal. At home, she and her
wife argue more often about ordinary decisions because Linh hears urgency or control in comments
that were not meant that way. She knows this has made her seem rigid and touchy, and that
recognition carries embarrassment as much as frustration.

By the time she sought help, Linh was exhausted by how quickly her body could be pulled back into
those moments. She was still functioning, still producing careful, admired work, but doing so with a
sense that parts of her daily life had become organized around avoiding particular smells,
particular kinds of attention, and any situation where someone else might take charge of her. What
frightened her most was how easily an ordinary workday could become contaminated by those
sensations. What she hopes for now is not simply to be less reactive, but to feel that her own body
and choices belong to her again.
