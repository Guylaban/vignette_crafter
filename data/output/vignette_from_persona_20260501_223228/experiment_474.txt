Arjun Patel was 36 when he came to treatment, a married homicide detective who had built a career
around staying composed in scenes that unsettled other people. In the first sessions, though, it
became clear that what he was living with did not begin on the job. He grew up with a caregiver
whose violence was unpredictable but had its own sensory signature. He remembered lying rigid in bed
as a boy, noticing the cold draft across his bare arms when a bedroom door opened, and then the
sharp sound of it hitting the wall or slamming shut. At other times it was the feel of sudden
temperature on his skin—a blast of cold air from a hallway, the chill of sheets in a room where he
was alone—that told him someone was coming and that he would have no control over what happened
next. Those moments had never settled into the past. They still arrived in his body as if danger
were present.

What made this especially painful for Arjun was the meaning he attached to those reactions now. When
a door banged in the precinct or a witness room door opened behind him, he felt himself jolt and go
tense before he could stop it. If he had to stay overnight for work in an unfamiliar motel during a
case, or if his wife was visiting family and he had to sleep alone, he became acutely aware of the
temperature of the sheets and the air against his skin, and he would lie there listening for every
sound in the hallway. He had come to believe that these moments exposed something shameful about
him. A man his age, a detective no less, should not be thrown by a door opening or by being alone at
night; when it happened, he told himself that his reactions proved he was a lousy coper, that
something was wrong with him as a person, that whatever steadiness he once had was gone. Over time
he also began to scan for these cues more readily, almost bracing for the cold touch of air or the
sound of a door, as if his own conviction that he was fundamentally defective made those sensations
stand out everywhere.

Months and years of living this way had shaped his routines. Because he was certain that being so
affected meant he was weak and altered in some basic way, he organized his life around not having to
face how easily he could be set off. He avoided people more than he used to, keeping conversations
at work clipped and functional and turning down dinners with colleagues after shifts. On days off he
often avoided going out altogether, preferring the controlled space of home to restaurants, stores,
or crowded public places where doors might swing open behind him and where he could not regulate who
came near him. Nights were the most entrenched pattern. If his wife had an overnight trip or fell
asleep earlier than he did, he delayed sleep for hours, staying up with the television on or
scrolling through case files until exhaustion overpowered him. In his mind this made sense: if he
did not have to lie awake alone in the dark, he would not have to confront the fact that he still
reacted like that frightened child.

Those strategies, however, left him feeling worse about himself. The more he withdrew from other
people, the more he felt unlike the version of himself he used to recognize—engaged, competent, able
to move through the world without elaborate workarounds. Staying up late made him irritable and
foggy on the job, and he was ashamed of how much energy went into simply getting through a night. He
noticed himself thinking, after another evening spent avoiding going out with his wife or another
sleepless night on the couch, that this was further proof he could not cope normally. He spoke in
session about feeling “broken” and said that sometimes he no longer knew who he was outside of
managing these reactions.

The habits that were meant to protect him also seemed to keep the triggers sharp. Because he so
often avoided other people and avoided going out, ordinary sounds and situations never had a chance
to become ordinary again; a door slamming in the station could still hit him with full force.
Delaying sleep left him alone with the very conditions he dreaded most: the chill of the room on his
skin, the unfamiliar stillness of a guest room on work travel, the awareness of being by himself
after midnight. His wife had started noticing that he would pace the apartment rather than come to
bed if she was away, and that he was increasingly reluctant to accept assignments that might require
overnight stays. For a detective who prided himself on reliability, that occupational narrowing was
hard to hide and harder to forgive in himself.

What finally brought Arjun in was not a single crisis but the sense that his world was getting
smaller while his shame was getting larger. After an overnight case assignment, he spent nearly the
entire night awake in a hotel room, flinching each time he heard a door open in the corridor and
feeling the cold hotel sheets against his legs. The next morning he caught himself thinking, with
unusual certainty, that there was something fundamentally wrong with him and that he was
disappearing into these patterns. He sought help because he was afraid that if this continued he
would lose his marriage, his effectiveness at work, and whatever part of himself still felt solid.
What he hoped for, though he said it cautiously, was to sleep in a room alone without feeling hunted
by a sound, a draft, or what those reactions seemed to say about who he was.
