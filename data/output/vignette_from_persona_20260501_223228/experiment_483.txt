Nour is an 18-year-old Arab American woman who recently began working in a small architecture office
while taking introductory design courses, and she came to treatment after months of feeling as if
her body had never fully left the night she watched a man get badly hurt. Late one evening, she was
leaving the studio after staying late over drawings when she heard shouting from the street below.
From the sidewalk she saw an argument escalate suddenly; one man lunged, there was a sickening
impact, and another collapsed onto the pavement. She remembers the sound first as much as the
image—the sharp crack, the yelling, the stunned stillness around it—and the sight of blood spreading
under the streetlights while people froze before anyone seemed able to help. She was not the one
physically attacked, but she stood close enough to see the injured man’s face and to feel, with
immediate certainty, that terrible things could happen in an instant and without warning.

In the days afterward, what stayed with Nour was not just distress but a persistent sense of danger.
She moved through her routines as if violence might break out again at any moment. At work, when she
remained in the office into the evening reviewing plans or adjusting digital renderings, she found
herself repeatedly looking up at sounds from the hallway, the elevator, or the street outside. If
voices rose even slightly near the building entrance, her attention snapped toward them before she
could stop it. She described being superalert all the time, scanning other people’s expressions and
listening for changes in tone, as though vigilance were the only thing standing between order and
catastrophe.

Her nights became particularly difficult. When she lay down, the quiet did not feel restful; it felt
exposed. Nour would keep listening for footsteps outside her apartment, for engines idling too long,
for any abrupt sound that might signal that something was wrong. She had difficulty falling asleep
because she felt she needed to stay alert, and she often remained awake far later than she intended,
staring at the ceiling and trying to gauge whether the building was truly settled. Even when
exhausted, she resisted letting go into sleep, as if lowering her guard would be reckless. By
morning she was drained, but the fatigue did not lessen her watchfulness.

Alongside this, strong negative feelings had become a near-constant backdrop. Fear was the most
obvious, but not the only one. She felt horror when the scene came back to her in fragments, anger
that strangers could turn on each other so quickly, and guilt that she had stood rooted for those
first moments before emergency help arrived, even though she knows rationally that many people
freeze. At times she also felt ashamed of how much the event had changed her. She was young and
ambitious, proud of being taken seriously in a professional setting, and she hated that a slammed
car door or raised voice could throw her into a state of silent alarm. She had come to feel that the
world was less predictable than she had believed, and that she herself was no longer someone who
could relax the way other people seemed able to.

Over the months, this constant state of guard narrowed her life. In the architecture office she
preferred to keep her desk positioned so she could monitor the entrance, and she disliked being the
last person there. If a client meeting ran late, she became tense well before anyone else noticed
the hour. Coworkers knew her as quiet and composed, but privately she was tracking every sound in
the environment and counting how long it would take to leave the building if she needed to. At home,
bedtime became something she dreaded rather than welcomed, because night was when she felt most
responsible for staying watchful. The lack of sleep made everything feel sharper and harder to bear,
yet she still felt unable to stop scanning.

What finally brought Nour in was not a new traumatic event but the realization that her life had
become organized around danger. After nearly nodding off at her desk following another night of
staying awake to listen, she frightened herself with how depleted she had become. She told the
therapist that she was tired of feeling on guard in every room and tired of carrying so much fear,
anger, and guilt alone. What frightens her most is the possibility that this permanent alertness is
now who she is, that she will never again feel safe enough to sleep without listening for something
terrible about to happen. What she hopes, though she says it cautiously, is that she might learn how
to live in the world without feeling she has to watch it every second.
