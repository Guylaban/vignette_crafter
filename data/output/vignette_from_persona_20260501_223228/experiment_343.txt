Ama came to treatment at 38 after more than a year of living as though the worst moment of her life
were always about to happen again. She is a married Ghanaian American woman who works on a
commercial fishing boat out of a small harbor, leaving before dawn and spending long hours around
engines, weather reports, and the blunt practical talk of crews who are used to risk. The trauma
centered on her younger brother, who died in a car crash on a day she still replays with punishing
precision. He had called her before driving home, tired after a long shift, and she had told him to
go on because he sounded eager to get back. By evening there were flashing lights, a folded
guardrail, and later a photo in the local news of his crushed car at an angle she cannot forget.
Since then, what has lodged in her mind is not only that he died, but that she had a chance to stop
him and did not.

In the months that followed, Ama came to believe she had failed in the most basic duty a sister
could have had. She was certain the event happened because of the way she acted, because she did not
insist he rest, because she did not go get him, because she let the conversation end. The thought
that she should have protected him and failed returns with a harsh finality; she says she will never
forgive herself for not doing more. Those conclusions do not sit in her mind as abstract guilt. They
bring surges of fear, shame, anger, and horror so strong that her chest tightens and her hands
tremble when his death is brought to mind, and she becomes short-tempered with people around her,
snapping at her husband or crew mates over minor mistakes because she feels constantly on edge and
already braced for catastrophe.

Her triggers are painfully specific. On the anniversary of his death she starts tracking the date
weeks in advance, dreading the morning as if she were approaching a cliff edge. If the men on the
boat start talking about roadway accidents, or if a wrecked car appears on a phone screen, on
television in a marina office, or beside the highway when she is being driven to the docks, she
feels her body react before she can think: a rush of heat, pounding heart, a sick drop in her
stomach. What frightens her most is how quickly she is transported back to the image of twisted
metal and to the phone call she wishes she had handled differently. After those moments she cannot
let the subject go. She goes over and over how the crash could have been prevented—if she had said
one different sentence, if she had taken his keys before on other occasions, if she had somehow
understood what was coming.

That dwelling has become one of the ways she tries to manage the danger she feels. Ama tells herself
that if she keeps reviewing the chain of events, she might finally identify the exact point where
she failed, as though perfect understanding could keep anyone else from dying. On nights when the
fear and guilt are strongest, she stays up until very late because sleep feels like letting her
guard down; in the quiet she circles the same questions and searches old messages. At other times
she buys gear, clothes, or household items she does not need, describing it as trying to get a few
minutes in which her head is occupied by something else. Yet these efforts do not settle her. The
later she stays awake, the more raw and irritable she is the next day, and the more easily any
mention of accidents sets off another wave of physical alarm. The impulsive spending leaves her
ashamed and defensive when her husband notices money missing from savings, which only confirms for
her that she is reckless and incapable of protecting the people around her.

Her work has become one of the places where the cycle is most obvious. At the docks, crews trade
stories constantly, including news about collisions on the highway to the harbor, and Ama finds
herself going silent or leaving the bait shed abruptly when those conversations start. Afterward she
often lies awake into the early morning, replaying her brother’s death and scrolling online, where
images of crashed cars appear and trap her attention even though they make her feel sick. The next
day she is exhausted, jumpy, and more likely to lash out at deckhands, then spends money after work
to soothe herself. Each of those moments seems to feed the old conviction that she is failing again.

She finally sought help after the most recent anniversary, when she erupted at her husband for
mentioning a traffic fatality on the news and later sat awake until dawn buying things on her phone
while thinking, with absolute certainty, that she ruins everything she touches. What she fears most
is that this means she is the kind of person who cannot keep others safe. What she hopes, more
quietly, is that she might one day hear talk of an accident or see a wrecked car without feeling her
whole body seize and without immediately returning to the belief that her brother died because of
her.
