Howard is a 71-year-old married man who has worked for years as a security guard, the kind of
employee who once took quiet pride in being steady, watchful, and useful. The event he circles
around in treatment happened during a late shift at a downtown office building when a man entered
the lobby carrying a bag and shouting before detonating an explosive device. Howard remembers the
blast with terrible clarity: the flash against the glass doors, the pressure in his chest, the smell
of smoke and scorched wiring, the sight of people dropping to the floor and screaming. Certain
fragments remain almost painfully sharp, as if preserved outside time. At other moments, when he
tries to recount the sequence from the man’s entrance to the arrival of first responders, he loses
the thread entirely. He says he can see pieces “like photographs,” yet cannot make them into a full
story, and sometimes he describes the scene in a flat, detached voice, as if it happened to someone
else.

In the weeks afterward, what unsettled Howard most was not only what he had witnessed but what he
had come to believe it said about him. He was certain that somebody else, someone more competent or
quicker, would not have gotten caught in that situation. As a man who had spent his life thinking
his job was to protect people, he felt he had failed in his most important role. That belief spread
beyond the event itself. Small frustrations at home or at work began to feel like proof that he
could no longer handle anything; he had come to believe he could not deal with even the slightest
upset without coming apart inside.

Those conclusions shaped the way he began to live. Back at work, Howard started positioning himself
so that he always sat with his back to the wall, scanning the whole lobby and every doorway. In
restaurants with his wife, he insisted on the same seat and grew tense if he could not see the room.
He watched people constantly for signs of danger—the set of a jaw, a bulky coat, a bag held too
tightly—and his attention fastened especially on anyone who seemed agitated or out of place. Beneath
that vigilance was a grim line of thinking that occupied him for hours: fantasies of how he might
get even with the attacker, as though imagining revenge could undo the humiliation and failure he
felt. To Howard, these habits made sense; if he was as vulnerable and incapable as he feared, then
constant readiness seemed like the only way to prevent another disaster.

Over time those strategies did not settle him. Sitting rigidly in corners and monitoring everyone
around him seemed to keep the bombing close, as if his mind never got permission to place it in the
past. Details of the attack remained unnaturally vivid—the bag near the entrance, the burst of
noise, the faces in the lobby—because he was repeatedly searching the present for their equivalents.
His revenge-filled rumination pulled him back into the event as well, rehearsing the danger and its
aftermath rather than letting it become a completed memory. Yet the more he stayed locked in these
mental and physical precautions, the less coherent his account became. He could summon exact sensory
fragments but not a clear beginning, middle, and end, and at times he spoke of the bombing with an
eerie numbness that frightened his wife, who said it was like listening to him describe a news clip
instead of the worst day of his life.

Those same habits also deepened the beliefs that had first driven them. Howard’s constant scanning
left him exhausted and on edge, which he took as evidence that he truly could not handle normal
stress anymore. When a customer at work approached too quickly and Howard felt his heart pound, he
read that reaction not as fear but as proof of weakness. His insistence on controlling where he sat
caused arguments with his wife and led him to avoid going out unless he could manage the setting,
which only reinforced his sense that he was no longer the dependable husband and protector he
believed he should be. Even his hours spent imagining revenge hardened the feeling that he had been
reduced by the attack into someone defined by helplessness and unfinished business.

By the time he sought help, Howard was still going to work, but the job had narrowed into a daily
exercise in surveillance. Every person entering the building could become a possible threat. He
could not relax in public unless he had a wall behind him, and even then he was not at ease. What
finally brought him in was his wife telling him that he was physically present but never really with
her anymore, always watching the door, always somewhere back in that lobby. Howard says he wants to
be able to sit in a room without mapping every exit and every stranger. What frightens him most is
the conviction that the bombing revealed something permanent—that he failed when it mattered, and
that he will never again be the man who could keep anyone safe.
