stopped it, would have read the situation sooner, would have fought harder. In the months afterward
she began to think there was something fundamentally wrong with her, and that she was no longer
capable in the way she had once defined herself—competent, protective, able to move confidently
among vulnerable people and difficult situations.

certain parishioners who remind her of that social world—can set off a rapid shift in her body. At
work, if she sees one of them approaching in a hallway, she is instantly superalert, scanning where
the nearest door is, feeling her muscles tighten before she has fully registered why. Feeling
uncomfortable in a room now matters to her as if it were a warning she must never dismiss again.

letting her guard down is exactly how she became vulnerable before. That interpretation makes
ordinary moments of ease feel suspect, and her body answers with tension, watchfulness, and repeated
checking of doors and locks at home.

its own: the room, the door, his face, the sickening sequence of realizing what was happening. When
she passes a familiar man in the church office or notices herself beginning to relax after a good
meeting, the memory can force its way in, vivid and unwanted, as if the event is occurring again
rather than being remembered. Those intrusions leave her body keyed up for hours. She begins
checking entrances, noticing every sound, sitting rigidly upright, certain she must stay ready. The
more sharply the memory replays, the more she feels she is living in a world where danger is
immediate.

Over time she has built routines that make sense to her in the moment but have narrowed her life.
She now makes sure to stay near an exit wherever she goes, including in the counseling rooms and
community spaces where she works. She chooses seats with a clear view of the door, keeps meetings
short if the layout makes her uneasy, and sometimes applies heavy makeup before work because it
helps her feel covered and less exposed. In the evenings, especially after a day of intrusive
memories or tense encounters, she eats excessively to settle herself. These habits are tied not only
to fear but to shame: if she is defective, if she no longer has the strength that once defined her,
then she feels she must compensate by controlling the environment, her appearance, and her body in
whatever ways she can.

Yet these strategies have begun to feed the very state she is trying to escape. Staying close to
exits means she is always orienting to escape routes, which keeps her watchful and prevents any real
sense of safety. The heavy makeup has become part of how she prepares for contact with people
connected to church life, almost a signal to herself that she is heading into danger. Overeating
leaves her physically uncomfortable and self-critical afterward, which can sharpen her sense that
something is wrong with her and make the next day’s encounters feel more threatening. Even at home,
repeated checking of doors and windows escalates once she starts; each check briefly relieves her,
but also convinces her there must be something nearby to guard against.

Those habits also make triggers easier to find. Because she is constantly monitoring who is in the
room, whether she feels even slightly uncomfortable, and whether she is becoming too relaxed, she
notices the smallest cues and treats them as significant. A friendly conversation with a known
parishioner, a rare moment of contentment after helping a family, the ordinary awkwardness of being
in a cramped office—any of these can become charged. Then the memory film starts again, her body
goes on alert, and she doubles down on staying near the door, locking up, putting on more armor in
the form of makeup, or eating to push the distress down. At work this has cost her. She has pulled
back from some face-to-face ministry tasks and avoids being alone in certain rooms, which leaves her
feeling even less like the capable woman she used to be.

Mereana finally sought help after noticing that she was structuring nearly every day around escape,
surveillance, and recovery from intrusions. What frightened her most was not only the persistence of
the memories, but the possibility that she was becoming permanently diminished—that she would never
again feel at ease with people she once served so naturally, and that even happiness might remain
unsafe. What she hopes for, cautiously, is to stop living as if the next familiar face or unguarded
moment will trap her all over again.
