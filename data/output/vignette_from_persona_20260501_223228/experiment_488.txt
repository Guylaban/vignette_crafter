home where she was working as a housekeeper. She described the attack in fragments, the way she has
carried it since: some moments are fixed in unbearable detail, while others disappear into blank
spaces that leave her feeling as if the worst part may still be hidden somewhere just out of reach.
She remembers the man blocking the doorway and the shock of realizing she could not leave. She
remembers his voice turning loud and aggressive when she resisted, the force of being grabbed and
overpowered, and the sharp pain in her body at the points where he held and hurt her. Other parts
are missing entirely. What troubles her is not only what she recalls, but the incompleteness of it;
she feels she has only pieces of the assault, and those pieces return with such force that when they
come back she feels pain again in the same places in her body, as if the event is happening against
her skin in the present.

Those broken, bodily memories have made certain moments in daily life feel charged and dangerous. At
work, when a homeowner gives instructions in a sharp or commanding tone, Nadia does not experience
it as ordinary direction but as the beginning of being controlled again. If a voice rises anywhere
in the house, even from another room, she feels her body tense before she has fully registered why.
Seeing frail older clients, or signs that someone in the home is vulnerable, can unexpectedly pull
up images of harmed people and with them the sense that she is back in contact with what happened to
her. The triggers are potent because they seem to arrive attached to the clearest shards of the
assault: the shouted voice, the trapped feeling of being forced, the immediate physical pain.

Once those reminders are stirred, Nadia becomes intensely alert to danger. In clients’ homes she
catches herself scanning rooms, windows, and hallways rather than focusing on the cleaning in front
of her. She listens closely for footsteps or doors opening, and at home she does the same, straining
for any sound that might mean someone is entering the apartment. Her attention is so pulled toward
possible threat that concentration has become difficult; she loses track of tasks, forgets where she
left supplies, and has to repeat simple chores because part of her mind is always monitoring the
environment. She says that when someone shouts or when she feels pressed to do something she does
not want to do, her first thought is not annoyance but danger: she has come to believe that being
cornered can happen again with almost no warning.

That state of readiness has, in turn, widened what counts as a trigger. Because Nadia is constantly
listening for signs that someone may be coming in, ordinary noises now set her off: a neighbor at
the front door, a client’s family member returning unexpectedly, a raised voice from the street. Her
own vigilance makes the world feel full of alarms. In the houses where she works, if she notices
herself scanning for exits or tracking where people are standing, she becomes even more sensitive to
anyone directing her movements, and the sense of lost autonomy returns quickly. She has started
avoiding being alone in enclosed parts of clients’ homes and prefers tasks near an open doorway, not
because she feels safer in any broad sense, but because she is certain she needs to be able to see
and react immediately.

The result over time has been a constricted life organized around anticipating another violation. At
home, her husband has noticed that she startles when he enters a room unexpectedly and that she
sometimes stops mid-conversation because she is listening for sounds elsewhere in the apartment. At
work, the concentration problems and watchfulness have slowed her down, and she worries clients will
think she is careless or disobedient when in fact she is trying to keep herself from being trapped.
What finally brought her to seek help was the sense that the assault was no longer confined to the
past. The missing parts of the memory frighten her almost as much as the clear ones, and she is
afraid that every shouted instruction, every reminder of vulnerability, every sound at the door will
keep pulling her back into the same terror. More than anything, Nadia hopes she can stop living as
if danger is already inside the room.
