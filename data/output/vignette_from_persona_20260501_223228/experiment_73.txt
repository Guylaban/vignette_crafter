Claire Martin is a 39-year-old city council member who came to treatment after months of worsening
insomnia and a growing sense that her world had narrowed to a few places where she could still
breathe normally. She is married and outwardly composed in public, known for sitting through
contentious council meetings with a steady face, but in the consulting room she described living as
if danger could step out from almost anyone at any time. What finally pushed her to seek help was
not a single new event, but the recognition that even in ordinary settings she was scanning
constantly—watching hands, jaw tension, shifts in tone, the way one person moved toward another—and
that she was no longer certain she could keep doing her job this way.

The experiences that organize her distress trace back to childhood, when she repeatedly witnessed
violence between adults at home. Claire does not tell that history as a complete story. Instead, it
comes in jagged pieces: one instant is unnervingly sharp, the next entirely absent. She can describe
with striking precision the angle of a kitchen chair tipped over on linoleum and the sound of a body
being shoved against a wall, yet there are stretches around those moments that disappear when she
reaches for them. Some parts, she said, are “just gone,” no matter how hard she tries to recover
them. What is clearest is not always visual. At times when the memory intrudes, she feels her own
body lock into the same position she held as a child—shoulders pulled in, back rigid, breath
shallow, as if she is again trying to become small and still enough not to be noticed.

Those fractured memories feed directly into how unsafe the present feels to her. Because the past
comes back in flashes and bodily states rather than as something finished and over, Claire has come
to feel that danger is difficult to predict and could emerge anywhere behind an ordinary
interaction. She watches other people closely for signs they might turn harmful: a spouse cutting
another off mid-sentence at a restaurant, a staff member’s voice hardening in the hallway outside
her office, a man at a council meeting standing too abruptly from his seat. In those moments, what
comes over her is not simply recollection but the sense that her body already knows what is about to
happen. The result is that only a very small number of places feel safe—her bedroom with the door
shut, her office when no one else is there, occasionally her parked car before she has to go into a
building. Everywhere else requires vigilance.

That vigilance, in turn, seems to keep the old experience close to the surface. At work, where
public disagreement is routine, Claire finds herself studying the room instead of hearing the full
discussion, tracking who is flushed, who is clenching a fist, who is leaning too close across the
dais. Later, when she lies down at night, keyed up from a day of monitoring everyone around her, the
clear fragments return with force while the missing parts remain maddeningly blank. She may feel her
spine press rigidly into the mattress as if bracing against a wall, her arms held tight to her sides
before she even realizes why. Sleep is hard to fall into and hard to stay in. She wakes alert,
listening. The more closely she has watched for threat during the day, the more vividly her body
seems to re-enter those childhood moments after dark.

Over the years this has altered how she lives with her husband and how she manages the ordinary
demands of public life. She prefers to meet people in controlled settings and feels markedly more at
ease if she knows exactly where she can sit and how quickly she could leave. At city hall she
sometimes lingers in her office rather than stepping into crowded informal conversations, telling
herself she works better alone there, though privately she knows the room feels containable in a way
the corridor does not. Her husband has noticed that she relaxes only in a few tightly bounded spaces
and that evenings are often spent with her attention divided, one part of her following him and
every nearby sound. Claire is troubled by how small that “safe zone” has become. She fears that if
she keeps living this way, she will lose the ability to be fully present in the career she values
and in her marriage. She has come for help because she wants, more than anything, to stop feeling as
though her body is still trapped inside moments she cannot fully remember, and because she is
frightened that if she does not, nowhere outside those few protected places will ever feel safe
again.
