Thomas Weber, a 68-year-old state politician, traced the start of his difficulties to a highway
collision eight months earlier, when he was being driven home after a late fundraising dinner. A
truck crossed into their lane and struck the side of the car hard enough to spin it into the median.
What stayed with him were not a smooth sequence of events but isolated images: the headlights
suddenly filling the window, the crunch of metal folding inward, a sharp smell of spilled liquor
from a broken bottle in the other vehicle, and the white-hot pain along his ribs and hip where the
door caved in against him. He survived with fractures and bruising, but when he tries now to
describe the crash he loses the thread, stopping in the middle of a sentence as if the memory breaks
apart in his hands.

In the weeks after, the world around his work began to feel charged with reminders. As a politician
he spends much of his day being driven to meetings, campaign events, and legislative sessions, and
even passing a wreck on the roadside or seeing television footage of cars struck in an accident
could make his stomach drop. The smell of alcohol at receptions was especially potent, bringing back
the crash scene with such force that he would feel pain again in the same parts of his body. Those
moments did not just remind him; they pushed him into hours of dwelling on what he had done wrong.
He would replay the evening and tell himself he should never have stayed so late, should have chosen
another route, should have prevented it somehow. Increasingly he found reasons to avoid driving or
even riding as a passenger, cancelling appearances when possible or insisting on remote
participation.

Thomas had come to believe the accident said something shameful about him. He spoke as if being
struck on the road fit “the sort of person” he was, someone fundamentally flawed and exposed. In
crowded public settings he felt people could see what had happened to him just by looking at him, as
though his fear and injury were written on his face. Because he saw himself that way, each jolt of
discomfort in a car or waft of alcohol seemed to confirm that he was still marked and vulnerable.
The more he viewed himself as damaged and perpetually needing to be on guard, the more ordinary
travel, roadside scenes, and even the uneasy quiet inside a car seemed loaded with danger.

That sense of danger soon organized his nights and days. He often woke abruptly and checked the
room, the locks, the hallway, needing reassurance that he was safe. Unusual silence frightened him;
if the house was too still, or the official car moved along without conversation, he felt something
terrible was about to happen. At work he struggled to concentrate during briefings, especially on
days with multiple car journeys. In that heightened state, his mind seemed primed to catch anything
resembling the crash, and once caught, the memory flashed back as those same frozen pictures. The
alertness also narrowed his world: scanning for threat made him more likely to notice disabled
vehicles on the roadside or tense up the instant he smelled wine at a donor event.

The fragmented memory fed his self-judgment. Because he could only access snapshots and could not
tell the story clearly from beginning to end, Thomas took this as evidence that he had failed in
some essential way. When the pain returned with the memory, he experienced it as proof that the
accident still had power over him and that he had not handled it as a stronger man would have. In
response he ruminated even more, circling through self-blaming questions late into the night and on
long afternoons in his office, trying to think his way to an explanation. Yet the more he did this,
the more vivid the images became, and the more quickly he reacted to passing crash scenes, the smell
of alcohol, or simply the discomfort of sitting in a moving vehicle.

Over months this hardened into a constricted routine. Staff noticed he was refusing in-person
appearances that required travel and losing focus in meetings. His wife began accompanying him at
night because he was repeatedly waking to check whether the house was secure. Thomas understood that
his efforts to stay safe were taking over, but he was equally certain that letting his guard down
would invite another catastrophe. He finally sought help after freezing during a campaign ride when
the motorcade passed a recent accident and he felt the same pain in his side, saw the headlights in
a single blinding image, and spent the rest of the day blaming himself for being unable to function.
What frightened him most was not only the memory of the crash, but the fear that he had become a man
permanently exposed, watchful, and unable to trust even a quiet road.
