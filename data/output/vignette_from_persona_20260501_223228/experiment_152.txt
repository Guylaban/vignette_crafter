Margaret Fischer is a 79-year-old widow who worked for years as a tailor, and even now still does
alterations from a small workroom off her apartment. She traces the beginning of her current
difficulties to her military service as a young woman, when she was assigned to a field hospital
during a period of combat. The memory she circles most often is not a single clean scene but a
jumble of impressions from one mass-casualty night: uniforms cut away, hands slick with blood,
shouted orders, metal trays crashing, a young soldier’s body twisting as staff tried to hold him
down. What happened that night has never settled into a story she can tell from beginning to end.
When she tries, the sequence slips apart. She can describe patches—the red on her hands, the smell
in the room, the force of her anger at being unable to stop what was happening—but she says the
event itself “comes back without words,” as if her body knows before her mind does.

In the decades since, particular moments in ordinary life have remained charged. Blood is the
strongest cue. Even a cut finger in her tailoring shop, a client pricked by a pin, or a dark red
stain on fabric can stop her cold. Certain conversation topics also catch her off guard, especially
when people speak casually about war injuries, hospitals, or “the good old days” of service. At
other times the trigger is internal, with unwanted thoughts arriving uninvited while she is bent
over a hem or sitting alone in the evening. In those moments she becomes convinced all over again
that something essential in her has been permanently destroyed. A glimpse of blood or a stray
conversation does not feel to Margaret like a reminder of the past; it confirms her belief that she
is no longer the happy woman she once was, that she is dead inside, and that nothing good is waiting
for her.

Over time those beliefs have narrowed what she notices in the world. She has come to expect that
anything touching the old trauma will break through, and because she sees herself as someone for
whom happiness is over, her attention is quickly captured by conversation about injury, by red
stains, by the first sign of an intrusive thought. She listens for danger in subjects other people
forget a moment later. What frightens her most is not only the reminder itself but what it seems to
prove: that she cannot leave it behind and never will.

When Margaret is pulled back to that night, the memory does not arrive in orderly form. It comes as
disjointed sensory fragments and a surge of rage so strong that she clenches her jaw and has to put
down her scissors. Because she cannot put the experience into words or in the right order, she is
left with the feeling of still being caught inside it. That fragmented, bodily quality makes the old
scenes easier to trigger again; a drop of blood at work or an unwelcome thought in the kitchen is
enough to set off the same raw state. Each time this happens, it deepens her conviction that she is
miserable now by nature, emptied out, with no future that can genuinely improve.

Her way of coping has been to shrink her life. She avoids friends, especially the few who still
invite her to church suppers, birthdays, or coffee, because she cannot trust what topics might come
up and because the effort of pretending to be pleased feels false. More strikingly, she avoids
situations that might make her feel happy or hopeful at all. If a neighbor suggests an outing, if a
granddaughter brings exciting family news, Margaret pulls back. She pushes away warm feelings almost
as soon as they appear, as though hope itself were unsafe. In her mind, letting herself feel good
would only expose her to a harder fall, and the old memory—wordless, angry, out of sequence—has
taught her that any opening can be invaded. The result is that her days have become smaller and
flatter, and that self-protection has fed the very conclusions she dreads: she looks at her quiet
apartment, her reduced clientele, the friends she has not called back, and sees proof that she
really is cut off from happiness and that nothing good lies ahead.

She sought help only recently, after a customer nicked her hand during a fitting and Margaret found
herself trembling, furious, and unable to finish the alteration. She went home and ignored two phone
calls from friends she had been avoiding for months. Sitting alone that night, she was struck by how
completely joy had disappeared from her life. What brought her in was not only distress but fear
that she will spend whatever years remain shut away from other people, ambushed by reminders she
cannot name and unable to feel anything except misery or anger. What she hopes, cautiously, is not
to become someone new, but to find some way to live without feeling that every reminder proves her
life is already over.
