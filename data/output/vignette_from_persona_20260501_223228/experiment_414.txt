Amina is a 25-year-old nurse who came to treatment months after leaving a war zone where, as a
civilian, she had spent prolonged periods trapped among the injured and the dead. Before coming to
the United States she had helped in makeshift treatment areas when shelling hit her neighborhood,
and the scene that returns most often is not a single detached image but a whole sensory moment that
takes her over. She can still see herself kneeling on a dirty floor, trying to compress a wound
while people shouted around her and another blast sounded nearby. What breaks through most painfully
is the look on the face of a young woman she could not save. When the memory comes, Amina does not
experience it as something finished and past; she feels, in her words, as if she is “right back
there,” with the same rush of terror and helplessness in her body. The recall quickly becomes
unbearable, flooding her with grief so intense that she has to shut it down.

In the weeks and months afterward, those returns of memory began to shape how she thought about
herself. Each time she was pulled back into that scene and felt again how overwhelmed and unable to
do enough she had been, she came away more certain that what happened had exposed something shameful
about her. She had come to believe that she was inadequate, that a capable person would have done
more, endured more, saved more. The grief attached itself to a deeper judgment: not only had she
failed in an impossible situation, but the event had revealed that there was something fundamentally
wrong with her. She spoke about this with a bowed posture and obvious reluctance, saying she felt
permanently humiliated by what she had lived through, as if surviving while others died had marked
her in a way other people would see if they looked closely enough.

Those beliefs began to govern how she managed her days. Because she felt defective and exposed,
Amina avoided talking about the war with her boyfriend, with coworkers, and even with relatives who
knew where she had been. When people asked careful questions, she gave clipped answers or changed
the subject, already bracing for the shame she expected to feel if she put the memories into words.
She also tried not to think about it when she was alone. If her mind edged toward the treatment
room, the blood, or the face of the woman who died, she would push herself into a kind of private
mental distance, drifting off into fantasies and imagined scenes that had nothing to do with her
life. She described it less as comfort than as disappearing for a while, a way to avoid contact with
the version of herself she now despised.

At work as a nurse, this pattern had become especially visible. A trauma page overhead, the metallic
smell of blood, or the sight of a young female patient crying on a gurney could bring the war back
with such force that for a few seconds she felt transported, her chest tightening as though the
bombardment were happening again. The grief and overwhelm came so fast that she would have to turn
away internally. She found reasons to leave the room, kept conversations strictly procedural, and
afterward stared at the medication cart while her mind floated elsewhere, trying not to think at
all. The more vividly the memory arrived, the more urgently she retreated from it into silence,
blankness, and fantasy.

Over time, those strategies seemed to make the memories more jagged rather than less. Because Amina
cut them off the moment they surfaced, they kept returning in the same raw form, with no sense of
distance or completion. A brief reminder during a shift could set off the whole scene again, and
each return landed with the same emotional force, as if nothing had been processed or laid to rest.
In that way her efforts not to think, not to speak, and to mentally drift away left the memories
poised to break back in on her, vivid and overwhelming. And when they did, they renewed the same
conclusions about herself: that she was weak, deficient, and indelibly shamed by what had happened.

This had begun to affect her relationship as well as her work. Her boyfriend told her she seemed
absent even when she was sitting beside him, and Amina knew he meant the moments when she vanished
into her own head rather than risk talking about where she had gone emotionally. She finally sought
help after a shift in which a patient’s cries abruptly pulled her back into the war, and she had to
step off the unit and lock herself in a bathroom, shaking and grieving as if the loss had just
occurred. What frightened her most was not only the memories themselves, but the conviction that
they proved who she really was. What she hopes for, cautiously, is some way to remember without
being overtaken and some chance of no longer feeling that the worst thing that happened to her is
the truest thing about her.
