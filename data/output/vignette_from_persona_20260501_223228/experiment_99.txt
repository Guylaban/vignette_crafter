Mele is a 71-year-old Tongan American police officer who has spent decades in patrol and community
response, and she speaks about the night of the trauma with the blunt precision of someone used to
writing reports, though the account still catches in her throat. Her husband had been driving home
after meeting friends for dinner when another car crossed the median and struck his vehicle. Mele
was not in the car, but she arrived at the scene before she could stop herself, having heard the
dispatch and recognized enough details to know it might be him. What remains with her is not a full
story in sequence but a set of brutally clear fragments: the crushed side of the car, the angle of
the headlights against wet pavement, the smell around the wreck that she immediately registered as
alcohol, and the sounds she heard at the roadside—metal settling, voices calling out, the strained
noise of someone trying to breathe. Even now, when the memory comes, it comes as sharp pictures
rather than thoughts, and with the pictures she sometimes hears those same sounds in her head. She
says the memories are so clear and detailed that she can see the scene “like it’s right in front of
me again.”

In the weeks afterward, Mele found that these sensory fragments seemed to prepare the ground for
what would set her off in daily life. Because the memory is carried in images and sounds, certain
moments hit her with unusual force. Driving past cars that have been hit in accidents can stop her
cold; she does not just notice the damage but instantly sees her husband’s vehicle superimposed on
it. The smell of alcohol, whether on a person she encounters at work or lingering outside a
restaurant, can bring back the roadside scene so vividly that she feels as if she is standing there
again. Even hearing other officers or citizens talk about crashes can pull up the same visual
flashes and the remembered sounds. As a police officer, she encounters all of this in exactly the
places she cannot easily avoid: at roadside calls, in squad-room conversations after collisions, and
in contact with intoxicated drivers. The memory seems to give those ordinary job moments a direct
line back to that night.

What unsettles Mele most is where her mind goes next. When she sees a wrecked car or catches the
smell of alcohol, she does not only remember; she immediately begins to judge herself. She has come
to believe that the event happened because of the way she acted, turning over small decisions from
that evening and treating them as if they should have changed the outcome. In those moments she
becomes certain she should have protected her husband and failed. A discussion about a traffic
accident at work can leave her silent and rigid, not because she has nothing to say as an
experienced officer, but because inside she is flooded with the conviction that she missed
something, should have intervened earlier, should have prevented it. The trigger does not stay
external for long; it lands as proof, in her mind, that she was not able to keep someone she loved
safe.

Over months, this pattern has hardened. Passing damaged vehicles on patrol, catching alcohol on
someone’s breath during a stop, or overhearing talk about collisions can pull her back into the
scene and into the same conclusions: that she failed, that her actions mattered in the worst
possible way, that she cannot stop bad things from happening. That sense of helplessness has been
especially painful because it cuts against the identity she built over a lifetime in policing. She
has always been the person others relied on in emergencies. Now she finds herself dreading parts of
the workday that once felt routine. She still goes in, but certain calls stay with her long after

home, her husband’s survival has not brought her peace; instead she watches him with an uneasy sense
that safety can vanish without warning and that she cannot trust herself to prevent it.

when it mattered, she did not protect him, and that she may never feel capable of protecting anyone
