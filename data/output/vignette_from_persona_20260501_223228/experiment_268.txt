Nadia Haddad was 36 when she was hospitalized with a rapidly worsening illness that left her
convinced, more than once, that she might die. She had been working long shifts on a construction
site, used to trusting her body and priding herself on being able to keep up with men who
underestimated her, when she developed symptoms that escalated frighteningly fast. In the emergency
department she remembers monitors sounding, staff moving quickly around her, and the unmistakable
look on her husband’s face when he was told to wait outside while they stabilized her. What has
stayed with her most is the sense that her body had become dangerous and unpredictable without
warning. She had gone in as someone strong and capable; she came out feeling that catastrophe could
break through at any moment and that there was nothing she could do to stop it.

In the weeks after discharge, Nadia did return to work, but she no longer experienced her body as
reliable. On site, the clang of dropped metal or the sudden revving of machinery made her jolt hard
enough that coworkers noticed. At night she struggled to fall asleep and woke repeatedly, already
tense, as if braced for an alarm no one else could hear. Much of the day her muscles stayed tight
and ready, and she watched herself react to ordinary sensations with a kind of dread. These
reactions frightened her in their own right. Lying awake with her heart racing, or catching herself
jumping at a noise, she had come to believe that something in her mind was giving way. If she could
not calm down, if her body stayed this wound up all the time, maybe she was going crazy. The
persistence of it also fed a darker conviction: if danger could erupt inside her once and leave her
this changed, then she had no real future to count on and no way to stop bad things from happening
to her.

Those beliefs began to shape how she managed her days. When she felt trapped by the thought that she
was losing her mind, or by the certainty that nothing good lay ahead, Nadia did not try to sit with
the fear. She stayed late at work, volunteered for extra tasks, and threw herself into physically
demanding jobs to keep her mind occupied. The construction site became more than employment; it was
a place where there was always something to carry, measure, or fix, and as long as she was moving
she did not have to think as much. At home, when the dread pushed through anyway, she ate past
hunger because the heaviness and routine of eating blunted her enough to get through the evening.
She also described going emotionally flat with her husband, not because she felt nothing mattered,
but because numbing herself seemed safer than letting the panic and despair fully in.

Her sense of constant alarm also drove these habits. After a day of jumping at noises and feeling
her body stay keyed up, overworking felt like the only way to outrun the agitation, and overeating
became a quick way to dull the edge of it at night. Yet the relief never lasted. The more she lived
this way, the more she read it as evidence that something was fundamentally wrong with her. Seeing
herself unable to rest, unable to stop filling every hour, unable to face her feelings without
eating or shutting down, Nadia became even more certain that she was going crazy. When she went
emotionally numb with her husband and could not explain what was happening inside her, she took that
distance as further proof that the illness had ruined her life and left her without a future she
could trust.

Over the following months, the pattern tightened. Poor sleep left her more jumpy the next day; the
jumpiness confirmed her fear that she was no longer in control; and that fear pushed her harder into
work and further into numbing routines that kept her from feeling anything directly. Her husband
began urging her to slow down, but she heard this less as concern than as confirmation that he could
see her changing. She ate in secret sometimes, ashamed of how desperate she felt for distraction,
then lay awake tense and discouraged, telling herself that if she were stronger she would be able to
stop this. A small part of her still noticed how relentless it all had become.

Nadia finally sought help after startling violently at work when a steel beam was dropped nearby and
having to hide in a portable office until she could steady herself. What shook her most was not only
the reaction itself, but the thought that followed immediately: this is never going to end, and I am
becoming someone I do not recognize. She came to treatment frightened that she is losing her mind
and that another disaster could strike at any time, but also hoping, cautiously, that if someone
could help her understand why she has been living in a state of constant alarm, she might still have
a future that feels like her own.
