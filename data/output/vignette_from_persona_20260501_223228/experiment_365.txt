Martin Keller is a 56-year-old professional dancer who came to treatment months after a traffic
accident in which, by his account, he accidentally caused a serious injury to another driver. He had
been driving home after an evening performance, exhausted and distracted, when he misjudged a light
and turned into the path of an oncoming car. He remembers the violent angle of impact more than
anything else: one car striking another broadside, the crumpling metal, the sudden stillness
afterward. He got out and saw the other driver trapped and badly hurt. What has stayed with him is
not only the image of the damaged cars but the conviction that in those seconds he did not do
enough, quickly enough. Although emergency crews arrived, Martin has become fixed on the idea that
he should have prevented the crash altogether and, once it happened, should have somehow protected
the other person better.

In the weeks afterward, reminders began to take on a punishing force. If a song came on that had
been playing in the theater that night or in the car during his drive home, he felt jerked back into
the scene and almost immediately found himself thinking, with familiar harshness, that he had failed
in the most important role of his life. Seeing crashed cars on the road or in media coverage had the
same effect: the visual impact did not remain just an image but turned quickly into self-accusation,
a replay of what he believes he failed to stop. Crowded spaces became especially difficult. In busy
theater lobbies and packed rehearsal studios, the sensory overload left him feeling cornered, and
from there his mind moved to the same conclusion—that he was the kind of man who had not protected
others when it mattered.

Over time, those beliefs began to shape what he did. Martin had come to believe he did not deserve
ease or distance from the event, and he also feared that if he allowed himself to stop reviewing it
he would be letting himself off the hook. He dwells repeatedly on how he used to be before the
accident, comparing that version of himself—a dependable husband, a disciplined performer, someone
he thought others could rely on—to the person he now sees as having failed. Yet he also tries hard
to push the trauma out of mind, cutting off thoughts when they start and steering conversations away
if his wife asks how he is doing. He avoids going near the intersection where the crash occurred,
taking longer routes across the city to rehearsals and performances. The avoidance makes sense to
him because that location feels like proof of what he did, a place that confirms his belief that he
did not do enough.

These efforts have not brought relief. The more Martin suppresses thoughts and organizes his days
around avoiding the intersection, the less settled he feels. At night he lies awake, not because he
thinks another accident is about to happen in his bedroom, but because he cannot let his guard down;
he feels he needs to stay alert, as if vigilance might prevent some further failure. Even in
objectively safe situations he cannot relax. During rehearsals he loses focus easily, missing counts
or drifting off when choreography is being explained, a change that has embarrassed him in the
studio and made him worry about his standing in the company. His wife has noticed that he startles
when music starts unexpectedly at home and that he sits tensely through evenings that used to help
him unwind.

That constant internal watchfulness also seems to prime the world around him. When he is already
keyed up from a sleepless night or from straining to stay composed in rehearsal, a crowded subway
platform or a song over the studio speakers hits with greater force. In those moments his attention
narrows quickly onto anything that resembles the accident—noise, pressure, impact, the idea of
collision—and the trigger lands as if it were happening now. His own state of tension appears to
make these reminders easier to detect and harder to shake.

At the same time, the meanings he has attached to the accident seem to widen the field of what can
set him off. Because he is certain he failed to protect someone and cannot forgive himself for not
doing more, songs, crowds, and images of damaged cars no longer feel like ordinary parts of city
life; they have become loaded with accusation. By the time he sought help, he was structuring much
of his life around not thinking, not passing that intersection, not being caught off guard in
public. What finally brought him in was a rehearsal in which a familiar song came on, his
concentration vanished, and he had to step out of a crowded room shaking and ashamed. He told the
clinician that he is frightened this is who he is now—a man who failed when it counted and cannot
recover—but he is also hoping, cautiously, that he might find some way to live without feeling
perpetually on trial.
