Linh Tran is a 74-year-old software engineer who came to treatment after months of worsening sleep,
saying she felt as if her body had never really left the hospital where her daughter nearly died.
Although the medical crisis happened years ago, she still speaks about it in the present tense at
times, as if the danger might resume the moment she stops paying attention. Her daughter had
developed a sudden life-threatening illness and was rushed into intensive care. Linh remembers long
stretches of waiting beside machines and doors that did not open quickly enough, the sharp
antiseptic smell on her clothes and hands, and the terrible moments when the usual hospital noise
dropped away. What lodged in her most deeply was not only the fear, but the silence before staff
came in, the kind of silence that felt unnatural, suspended, as though it announced catastrophe. She
had driven back and forth to the hospital for days, and even now the sound of passing cars can pull
her straight back to those urgent drives when she believed she might be arriving too late.

In the months after her daughter recovered, Linh found that certain moments reached her before she
had time to think. If a room fell suddenly quiet, or she caught the smell of disinfectant in a
clinic, office hallway, or even on someone’s freshly cleaned hands, she was hit by the same terror
she had felt while waiting for news. At times there was no picture in her mind at all, only a wave
of dread and bodily certainty that something awful was already happening. Car sounds could do the
same thing, especially when she was working from home and heard tires passing outside in the
evening; she would feel her chest tighten as if she were once again on the road to the hospital.
These moments did not stay as mere reminders. In the same instant, she would think, with renewed
conviction, that the world is fundamentally dangerous and that disaster can arrive without warning.
The triggers seemed to prove to her that safety is an illusion.

As these episodes continued, Linh became increasingly sure that she had failed in some essential way
during her daughter’s illness. When the memories came back with full force, shame accompanied them.
She would replay decisions she made, conversations she had with doctors, the timing of when she left
or returned, and she had come to believe that the event happened because of the way she acted, or
because she had not acted quickly or correctly enough. The terror of remembering and the shame of
remembering fused into a broader conclusion: bad things happen, and when they do, she cannot stop
them. That sense of helplessness hardened over time into a worldview in which danger is always near
and she is never truly capable of preventing it.

That belief has shaped her days and especially her nights. Linh says she has difficulty falling
asleep because some part of her feels she needs to stay alert. If she lets herself settle into the
quiet of the house, the silence feels loaded, like the charged stillness outside an ICU room, and
she becomes superalert, listening for sounds in the street or elsewhere in the home. Because she
sees the world as dangerous and herself as unable to stop what is coming, staying watchful feels
necessary rather than excessive. Her vigilance has spread into ordinary settings. At work, even
though she remains highly competent, she notices car sounds outside the office or sudden lulls in
open-plan noise and immediately scans for what might be wrong. She has begun keeping background
audio on while coding because complete silence feels intolerable.

The more on guard Linh stays, the more easily she detects the very cues that undo her. A brief hush
in a meeting, the antiseptic scent in a medical building near her workplace, or traffic outside her
window catches her attention instantly because she is already listening and searching. That
sharpened watchfulness makes the triggers feel omnipresent, and once noticed, they bring back the
terror again—sometimes vividly, sometimes as raw emotion without a clear scene attached. After
nights of lying awake in a state of alert anticipation, the memories are even closer to the surface
the next day, and she finds herself once more pulled into shame and self-blame about what happened
to her daughter.

Linh finally sought help when her husband told her that the house no longer felt restful because she
could not tolerate quiet and rarely slept through the night. She was distressed by how small her
world was becoming around sounds, smells, and vigilance she could not switch off. What frightens her
most is the sense that if she ever stops monitoring, another catastrophe will strike and she will
again be powerless to prevent it. What she hopes, cautiously, is not to erase what happened to her
daughter, but to live in a world that no longer feels moments away from disaster.
