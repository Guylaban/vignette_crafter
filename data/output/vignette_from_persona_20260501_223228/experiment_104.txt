Mele had run a neighborhood liquor and convenience shop for almost twenty years, the kind of place
where regulars lingered to talk story and pick up a six-pack on the way home. Two years ago, her
husband died suddenly after collapsing in the back room of the store at closing time. She still
cannot give a clean, orderly account of that night. What comes instead are fragments that arrive
with the force of a replay: the sharp sour smell of spilled beer when a bottle broke as he fell, the
scrape of a chair, his body on the floor, her own voice calling his name and not hearing an answer.
When she tries to tell it straight through, the sequence slips; she loses track of what happened
first and what she did next. Yet the scene itself comes back unbidden, as if a film starts without
her consent.

Since then, the things that pull the memory forward are painfully specific. If customers in the shop
talk about someone losing a spouse or a parent, Mele feels the room narrow around her and the images
force themselves in before she can block them. The alcohol aisle is especially difficult. The smell
of liquor on a customer’s breath or from an opened bottle can suddenly bring back the back room with
such immediacy that for a moment she is no longer standing behind the register but back beside her
husband on the floor. Funerals and cemeteries have become nearly unbearable. She has skipped family
memorials because grief rituals do not feel symbolic to her; they feel like a trapdoor into that
night. These encounters do more than stir sadness. They make her react as if danger is present now,
her body jerking into alarm, unable to settle even when she knows she is in her own store or at
home.

Over time, Mele has come to believe the death ruined the life she had and the person she was. She
says she used to know herself as capable, warm, and steady with people, but now she does not
recognize the woman who startles at conversations, scans customers’ faces, and braces when someone
walks in smelling of alcohol. Because she has begun to see her future as closed off and her life as
destroyed, reminders of loss seem to stand out everywhere. A customer mentioning a funeral, a
condolence card display, a neighbor asking after a bereaved cousin—these moments catch her
immediately, as if her mind is always tuned to evidence that love ends in sudden disappearance. In
those same moments she becomes certain she will not be able to bear what comes into her head, and
the memory indeed surges in, choppy and involuntary, confirming for her that there is no getting
free of it.

The memory itself deepens that despair. Each time the film-like replay starts, Mele is left thinking
that this is all her life is now: a loop she cannot stop, a story she cannot make sense of, a self
organized around one terrible night. Not being able to hold a coherent narrative of her husband’s
death feels, to her, like proof that something essential in her has been broken. The more the memory
breaks through without warning, the more hopeless she feels about ever having a future beyond
surviving the next intrusion. And because those replays have become attached to the smell of alcohol
and to grief-related events, the range of what counts as a trigger keeps feeling broader to her, not
narrower.

Her sense that her life is over also feeds the way she lives in constant alarm. Mele is often
reacting as though something catastrophic is about to happen even when nothing is wrong. In the
shop, she can be standing in the safety of a familiar afternoon, hearing ordinary refrigerator hum
and neighborhood chatter, and still feel unable to relax. If a customer raises a voice from the back
of the store, her heart jumps before she has even understood the words. That persistent state of
readiness leaves her more convinced that the trauma has permanently changed her, and every fresh
jolt of fear becomes further evidence, in her mind, that she is not the person she was.

The alarm has changed her days in concrete ways. She keeps to the front counter and avoids the back
room whenever possible, asking an employee to stock shelves there. She watches the door closely,
dreading the stagger or smell that might set her off, and when she does have a flashback she
sometimes has to grip the counter until she can tell herself where she is. Customers have noticed
she no longer chats. A few have gently asked whether she is all right, which only makes her think
how visible and damaged she has become. As the false alarms keep happening, she has grown even more
wary of funerals, cemeteries, and conversations about bereavement, anticipating that they will knock
her into that same frightened state.

What finally brought Mele to treatment was a recent funeral for an older cousin she could not avoid.
The scent of alcohol on mourners’ clothes after the reception and the talk of who had lost whom sent
her into a full reliving of her husband’s collapse. She left convinced she was disappearing into
this forever. Now she is seeking help because she is frightened that her world will keep shrinking
until even her own shop is unmanageable, and because some part of her still hopes that if she can
finally make sense of that night, she might recover a future and a self she can recognize.
