Daniel Weiss is a 37-year-old financial analyst who came to treatment more than a year after being
held captive during a business trip overseas. He describes the captivity in fragments that are at
once sharply detailed and hard to place in ordinary time. He remembers being kept in a small room
where long stretches of dead silence would suddenly be broken by other people’s sounds elsewhere in
the building, especially a child crying somewhere beyond the door. He can still bring back the smell
of the room with disturbing immediacy—stale sweat, damp fabric, and something metallic—and when the
memory comes it does not feel like recollection so much as re-entry. He says those moments are
“clearer than yesterday,” more vivid and more real than most of the rest of his life.

What remains most painful to Daniel is not only what was done to him, but what he believes the
ordeal revealed about him. He had come to believe that in captivity he was reduced to something less
than a person, handled and controlled like an object. He is ashamed of ways he complied in order to
get through it, and he speaks about those moments as if they violated something essential in him, as
though survival itself came at the cost of becoming someone he does not respect. The fact that no
one has been held accountable has fixed this meaning even more firmly for him; in his mind, the
absence of consequences confirms that what happened can simply be done to a person and left there.

Since returning home, Daniel has lived as if danger has followed him into ordinary spaces. He is
acutely watchful even in his apartment, checking locks repeatedly and scanning hallways and windows
before he can settle. At night he delays sleep because giving in to sleep feels like abandoning his
post. He tells himself he has to stay alert, and the longer he lies there listening, the more any
change in sound becomes charged. Sudden complete silence in the apartment building makes him tense
rather than calm, and a child crying from a neighboring unit can have the same effect, his body
reacting before he has fully registered why.

Those sounds do more than startle him. When he hears children crying in the street below his office
or in the lobby of his building, he is pulled back toward what he cannot forgive in himself. The
crying does not just signal possible danger; it brings a rush of disgust and shame, with the old
thought that in captivity he became the kind of man who would do anything to endure. Physical
affection has become similarly loaded. When someone reaches to hug him, he stiffens immediately, and
with that recoil comes the sickening sense of being handled, reduced again to a body for someone
else’s use. His girlfriend ended their relationship after months of him turning away from touch he
could not explain.

His vigilance also seems to keep the captivity close. On evenings when he patrols his apartment and
tries to listen for every sound, the memories arrive with more force. In those keyed-up states he
does not just think about the room; he can smell it, and the images sharpen until they feel more
present than the room he is actually standing in. Those intensely real memories harden his beliefs
about himself. When he can smell the air from captivity and see it with such clarity, it feels to
him like proof that what happened defined him permanently, that being dehumanized was not a moment
but the truth of what he is now.

At work this has narrowed his life. As a financial analyst in an open-plan office, he times his
lunch to avoid the cafeteria because families sometimes pass through the ground floor of the
building and the sound of an upset child can leave him rattled for the rest of the afternoon. If the
office suddenly goes quiet after a burst of conversation, he feels his shoulders rise and his
attention leave the spreadsheet in front of him. He begins scanning faces, exits, windows, as if the
silence itself means something is about to happen. Once he is in that state, he notices every sound
that could become a warning, and he organizes his day around preventing those moments.

Over time he has also started to treat his own reactions as evidence that he is damaged in a deeper
way. Being so constantly on guard at home makes him feel less like a man living his life and more
like someone still under someone else’s control. The fact that he cannot accept a hug, cannot
tolerate silence, cannot hear a child cry without bracing, becomes part of the same bleak
conclusion: that captivity stripped away something human in him and left him permanently altered. In
quieter moments he recognizes how circular it has become, that the more he watches for threat, the
more these cues take on power, and the more power they have, the more convinced he feels of the
meanings he dreads.

He finally sought help after a meeting at work was interrupted by a colleague bringing in her
toddler, who began to cry. Daniel felt the room drop away and had to leave abruptly, later sitting
in his locked car, shaken and ashamed. He says he is frightened that his world is getting smaller
and that he will end up unable to work or be close to anyone. What he hopes for, cautiously, is not
to forget, but to be able to hear silence, or a child crying, or feel someone’s arms around him
without being taken back to that room and to what he has come to believe it says about him.
