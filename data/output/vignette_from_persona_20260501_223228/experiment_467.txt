Nadia Haddad is a 77-year-old woman who came to treatment after years of insisting she could manage
on her own. She had grown up in the Middle East and was living in a city that became a war zone
during her adulthood. What she returns to most often is one afternoon when shelling began without
warning near the apartment building where she was staying with relatives. She remembers the
concussive blast, the dust in the stairwell, and the crush of bodies trying to get out. She had
stood frozen for a moment before forcing herself down the stairs, keeping her eyes on the exit
because it seemed like the only thing that might save her. Even now, what makes the memory so potent
is not only the danger but the sense that death could arrive at any second, from nowhere, and that
survival depended on never being trapped away from a way out.

In the months after leaving the war zone and resettling in the United States, Nadia did not feel she
had truly left it. A slammed door, a low-flying helicopter, or the sudden press of people in a
hallway could bring the scene back without warning. When that happened, she did not feel she was
remembering something old; she felt as if she were back in that stairwell again, with the same surge
of terror in her chest and the same certainty that she had to get out immediately. She had come to
believe that if she let herself think directly about what happened, she would not be able to handle
it. That conviction made the memories feel even more dangerous to her, and when they broke through
anyway they seemed to arrive with full force, as if her mind had no distance from them at all.

Nadia speaks about herself now with a quiet harshness. She says she is a weak person, not like the
woman she used to be before the war, and she often dwells on that earlier version of herself: more
social, more engaged, more alive. In the present she describes feeling dead inside. Those beliefs
shape the way she moves through her days. Because she is certain she will come apart if she gets
caught in distress, she sits near exits wherever she goes, scanning a room as soon as she enters it.
She avoids television programs, films, and news stories about bombings, refugees, or war, not out of
simple dislike but because she is convinced that once those images start, she will lose control of
herself. Even in session, she edges toward safer topics, as if turning her mind toward the event
would prove her weakness.

Her work as a tutor has narrowed around these habits. Nadia still values the structure of helping
children with reading, but she arranges chairs so she is closest to the classroom door and feels
agitated if a parent blocks her path while speaking to her. If another tutor mentions world news in
the break room, she leaves quickly. These small acts of protection make sense to her in the moment;
staying near an exit and shutting out reminders are how she tries to keep the terror from starting.
Yet over time the strategy seems to have taught her that the memories are indeed unmanageable. The
more she organizes her life around not being trapped and not being reminded, the more sudden and
overpowering the recollections feel when something slips past her guard. A sound in the corridor or
a fleeting image on a student’s tablet is enough to bring back the event in a rush, and she is again
overwhelmed by the same terror.

That pattern has deepened the way she judges herself. After hurrying out of a staff meeting because
the room felt too closed in, Nadia spent the evening thinking about the capable woman she had once
been and telling herself that only a weak person would need to sit by a door. She becomes more
emotionally shut down after these moments, speaking in a flat voice and saying there is nothing left
in her. The protections that are meant to keep her steady end up confirming her fear that she cannot
cope, and that belief, in turn, makes her more determined to avoid reminders and to guard every
exit.

She finally sought help after abruptly leaving a tutoring session when construction noise outside
sounded like incoming fire. In front of one of her students, she went pale, stood up, and moved
straight to the door before she understood what she was doing. She was ashamed afterward, less
because of the fear itself than because it seemed to prove what she has long believed about herself.
What frightens Nadia most is that at her age this is simply who she is now: hollow, fragile, always
one sound away from being back there. What she hopes for, though she says it cautiously, is to be
able to sit in a room without planning her escape and to remember what happened without feeling as
if it is happening all over again.
