Marisol Herrera is a 61-year-old bus driver who came to treatment after months of feeling as if a
single moment on the road had swallowed the rest of her life. Last year, while driving her usual
city route, she watched a sedan run a red light and get struck broadside by a truck just ahead of
her bus. She remembers the impact with unbearable clarity: twisted metal, the sudden burst of red, a
body thrown forward in a way that did not look survivable. She had passengers behind her and a large
vehicle she could not abandon in the middle of traffic, and by the time she stopped and radioed for
help, other people were already rushing toward the wreck. Since then, what has fixed itself in her
mind is not only what she saw, but what she did not do. She has come to believe that she should have
protected others and failed, that she did not do enough to stop what happened, and that she will
never forgive herself for that inaction.

In the weeks after the crash, the road stopped feeling ordinary. Blood on a television drama, even a
smear of red at a butcher counter, could pull her instantly into the conviction that she was back at
the intersection. Seeing damaged cars on the highway or even a vehicle pulled over after a fender-
bender made her chest tighten with the thought that she was once again arriving too late. What
frightened her most was the feeling of lack of control: traffic bunching unexpectedly, a car edging
into her lane, the helpless knowledge that events can turn catastrophic before anyone can intervene.
In those moments, the old judgments returned hard and fast—she was the kind of person who failed
when it mattered, a person who could not keep others safe—and the trigger and the self-accusation
seemed to strengthen each other each time they met.

Her memories do not come back as distant recollections. Marisol says that when they hit, they feel
completely real across all her senses at once: the sound of the collision, the sight of blood, the
sick bodily jolt of knowing something irreversible has happened. At other times there is no picture
at all, only a wave of dread and shame so intense that she feels she is inside the event again, as
if it is happening now rather than long ago. Those episodes seem to confirm her harshest beliefs. If
the memory is still this alive, she thinks, then perhaps she has no right to move on; perhaps a
decent person would suffer if she truly failed someone. And when she is already caught in the
thought that her life may not be worth living anymore, it takes very little—a glimpse of a crushed
car, a stain she first reads as blood—for the memory to surge forward.

Over time she began trying to manage this in ways that made immediate sense to her. Before starting
her route, she repeats silent phrases in a fixed order, a private mental ritual meant to keep
another disaster from happening. If she loses count, she starts over. She avoids being touched, even
by coworkers brushing past her in the depot or by her husband reaching for her arm in the kitchen,
because the sudden physical contact can bring a full-body shock that drops her back into the crash
before she can think. She also refuses funerals, memorial services, and even family conversations
about people who have died; anything that reminds her of loss feels too close to that intersection
and too close to the people she believes she failed. Yet these strategies have narrowed her world.
On her bus route she scans constantly for wrecked vehicles and abrupt movements in traffic, and the
more she relies on rituals and distance, the more fragile her sense of safety becomes. A missed
ritual, an unexpected touch, or a roadside accident can set off another spell of reliving, which
leaves her feeling even more responsible, more defective, more convinced that she must stay
vigilant.

At home, her marriage has grown strained in small, painful ways. Her husband has stopped trying to
comfort her physically because she recoils, and Marisol reads his hurt as further proof that she is
no longer the woman she was. She has begun dreading work she once took pride in, because every shift
confronts her with cars, intersections, and the possibility of catastrophe. What finally brought her
to seek help was a recent morning when she saw a minor accident from the driver’s seat, felt the
whole scene become present again, and had the sudden thought that maybe her life was not worth much
if all she did was fail people and wait for the next terrible thing. She is asking for treatment
because she is frightened by how real the crash still feels and by how much of her life is now
organized around preventing, warding off, and reliving that one moment. More than anything, she
wants to believe that the event is over, but she is terrified that letting go of her vigilance would
mean failing someone again.
