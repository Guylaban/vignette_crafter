She responded by shrinking her world in ways that made immediate sense to her. If a coworker started
talking about crime, hospitals, or anything that might touch the assault, she would abruptly leave
or change the subject to avoid being reminded by others. At work she kept her distance from floor
managers and tried not to make eye contact with security guards near the entrance, timing her breaks
to avoid passing them if she could. At home, when tension built to an unbearable level, she
sometimes turned quickly toward sexual fantasies or sexual activity, not out of desire so much as
from a desperate need to discharge the agitation in her body and replace the assault with something
she could control. Each of these strategies was tied to the same conviction: that thinking or
feeling too much about the event would overwhelm her.

But the things she did to get away from the memory seemed only to keep it raw. Because she cut off
conversations, avoided reminders, and refused to look directly at what had happened, the assault
remained in jagged fragments, returning without context and with full sensory force. After nights
when she tried to manage her distress through sexual activity, she often felt hollow and more
inadequate afterward, as if she had confirmed something damaged about herself. Avoiding supervisors
and uniforms made those encounters rarer, but also more potent; a glimpse of a security officer at
the call center doors could set off immediate scanning, and the ambulance bay behind the building
became a route she would go far out of her way not to pass. Never staying in those moments long
enough to learn that nothing was about to happen, she was left with the feeling that her precautions
were the only thing holding danger back, which kept the sense of threat as high as ever. In this
way, the very habits she relied on to get through the day kept teaching her that danger was still
immediate and that only constant vigilance and escape were keeping her safe. Even happy families
began to register less as ordinary scenes than as cues for what she believed she had lost, which
made those images sting on sight.

Over months, the loop tightened. The more she scanned for danger, the more movement and emergency
sounds caught her attention. The more she avoided, the more easily reminders broke through when she
could not control them. Her husband noticed she always chose the seat facing the exit and startled
when he came up beside her in the kitchen. At work her concentration slipped because she was
tracking the room instead of the calls, and she had begun considering quitting after a panic-filled
afternoon when an ambulance siren outside the building brought back the taste in her mouth so
vividly she had to lock herself in the restroom. Seeking help now, Lila says what frightens her most
is that if she ever stops guarding herself, it will happen again—or that if she allows the memory to
come fully, she will fall apart completely. What she hopes, more quietly, is to feel present with
her husband and move through an ordinary workday without bracing for danger at every turn.
