Amina is a 72-year-old widowed woman who has spent most of her adult life working outdoors and now
still works as a forester, covering long stretches of public timberland in a state park system in
the American South. She came to treatment reluctantly, saying she had managed on her own for years,
but lately it had become harder to do her job and harder to get through the nights. The trauma she
returned to was from her youth, when she was a civilian in a war zone in East Africa. She described
nights when shelling would begin without warning, the dark turning from stillness into confusion in
a second. She remembered lying awake listening for distant blasts, trying to decide whether the next
sound was far enough away to ignore or close enough to run, and watching adults study other people’s
faces and movements for signs of danger. What remained with her most vividly was not a single image
but that atmosphere of waiting: darkness, sudden explosions, and the sense that safety could vanish
if you misread the intentions of the people around you.

In the years after she immigrated to the United States, Amina built a disciplined life. She raised
children, was later widowed, and settled into forestry work that suited her preference for being
self-reliant and alert to her surroundings. Yet the habits that once felt practical never fully left
her. On her routes she notices people before they notice her. If a car is parked where she does not
expect one, or if someone lingering near a trailhead shifts their posture, keeps looking around, or
seems too interested in who is coming and going, her body tightens before she has fully taken in
why. She scans tree lines, access roads, and parking lots, telling herself it is just good field
sense, but she knows the intensity goes beyond the job.

Over time, she developed ways of getting through the day and especially the night that seemed
sensible to her because they promised a little control. At home she keeps the television or radio on
loudly, particularly in the evening, so there is no silence for her to listen into. If she wakes in
the dark, she turns on sound immediately rather than lie still with the room quiet. She checks
locks, windows, and the yard repeatedly, and when she is out working she studies people carefully,
tracking who is approaching, who is lingering, who feels “off.” What she has begun to notice, and
what finally worried her, is that these efforts have not calmed her world so much as sharpened it
into one where danger feels easier to find. The more she scans for threat in strangers and keeps
herself surrounded by loud sound, the faster she reacts to suspicious behavior, to a bang from
equipment, or to waking in darkness. She described it as having trained herself never to let her
guard drop; now even ordinary moments arrive already charged.

This has become especially clear at work. Forestry means early starts, shaded roads, and long days
around trucks, chains, maintenance equipment, and occasional controlled burns. When a machine
backfires or a load drops with a crack, she feels the jolt instantly, and afterward she is more
watchful of everyone nearby, trying to read whether someone is acting strangely or whether something
worse is about to happen. If she has already spent the morning scanning every turnout and every
person she passes, those sounds seem to hit harder. On some evenings, after a tense day in the
field, she comes home and turns the television up as soon as she enters the house. Later, if she
wakes before dawn and finds the room dark, she listens for a second, heart pounding, then reaches
for noise and starts checking the house again. She knows this routine by heart, and she is tired of
living inside it.

Her family has told her she seems distant when she visits, more preoccupied with who is outside and
what noises are coming from the street than with the conversation in front of her. Amina herself is
most troubled by how narrow her life has become despite her determination. She still goes to work
and prides herself on that, but she avoids some inspections that would keep her out near dusk, and
she feels ashamed of how much effort it takes to walk calmly past unfamiliar men at trail access
points without assuming the worst. She sought help after waking one night to a thunder-like boom,
flooding the house with sound from the television, and then spending the next day unable to settle
even in familiar parts of the forest. What frightens her most is the thought that at her age she has
not outlived the war at all, that she has been carrying its darkness and its watchfulness into every
room and every trail. What she hopes, quietly, is to be able to wake in the night without feeling
that she must prepare for danger before she can breathe.
