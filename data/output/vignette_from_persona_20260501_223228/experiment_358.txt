Robert Weiss is a 67-year-old school principal who came to treatment nearly a year after a life-
threatening car accident that occurred on his drive home from campus. He had been stopped at an
intersection after a long school day when he heard shouting from somewhere outside, looked up, and
in the next instant another vehicle slammed into the side of his car. What stayed with him was not a
clear story he could tell from beginning to end, but shards: the violent impact, the metallic crush,
the blast of sound, the sense that time had broken apart. Even now he says he cannot really put what
happened into words. Instead, the accident returns as a film that plays over and over, and when it
comes back he does not feel as though he is remembering something in the past; he feels as if it is
happening again.

In the months afterward, Robert had come to believe that the crash revealed something fundamental
and terrible. The world no longer felt basically safe to him but dangerous in a way he had not
understood before. He judged himself harshly for having been there at all, thinking that someone
else would not have gotten into that situation, and he worried that he had been permanently changed
for the worse. Those beliefs seemed to prime him for danger everywhere. Shouting in the hallways at
school, a loud noise from a locker bank or the cafeteria, or even seeing cars crumpled after an
accident on the local news could seize his attention immediately, as if his mind were already
searching for signs that another catastrophe was close. When those cues caught him, the crash
replayed with a vivid, sensory force that made him feel trapped back inside the car.

His role as a principal made these encounters hard to avoid. A normal day on campus meant raised
voices between students, the sudden clatter of dropped equipment, or the jolt of being called to
supervise a chaotic moment. He found himself bracing before entering common areas because he was
certain that if things became loud or unruly, something bad might happen. That same sense of threat
spread beyond obvious reminders of the collision. If his wife was late getting home or a teacher
took longer than expected to answer an urgent message, he quickly assumed there had been an accident
or some other disaster. He could not relax even in his own house, and nights became long stretches
of listening for sounds, unable to settle into sleep. The more on edge he felt, the more easily a
shout, a bang, or roadside crash imagery hooked him and pulled the accident back into the present.

The memories themselves drove much of what happened next. Because they arrived so abruptly and
intensely, Robert began avoiding work whenever he could manage it, taking days off, delaying his
arrival, and finding reasons not to be in the busiest parts of the school. He told himself he was
just trying to stay steady. He also forced himself into a kind of rigid optimism, trying to think
only positive thoughts whenever the accident intruded, as though refusing darker thoughts might keep
him in control. When that failed and the tension kept building, he ate excessively in the evenings,
describing it as one of the few things that could dull him enough to get through the night. Yet each
of these efforts left him feeling weaker and less like the capable administrator he had always been,
which fed his conviction that he had changed for the worse.

That conviction tightened the whole pattern. Because he saw himself as damaged and the world as
unsafe, his constant scanning and inability to relax felt justified to him rather than excessive.
But living in that state made him more sleepless, more certain that delays signaled disaster, and
more vulnerable to the sensory replay of the crash. The more he stayed away from school, shut out
difficult thoughts with forced positivity, and ate to manage distress, the less confidence he had in
himself. He began to compare himself bitterly to the version of himself who used to handle
emergencies calmly, and to other principals who, in his mind, would not be unraveling this way.
Avoiding the school environment also made ordinary campus noise feel less familiar, so that when he
did return, shouting and sudden loud sounds struck him as even sharper and more threatening.

By the time he sought help, the problem had become impossible to dismiss. A confrontation between
two students in a corridor had escalated into shouting, and Robert froze as if the collision were
happening again; later that evening, seeing footage of a highway accident on television set off the
same reliving response. He barely slept that night and called in sick the next morning, ashamed and
frightened by how small his world had become. What finally brought him in was the fear that he was
no longer able to do the job that had defined him for decades and that this broken, hypervigilant
version of himself might be permanent. What he hopes for, cautiously, is not simply to feel better,
but to believe he can walk into his school, hear ordinary noise, and not be transported back to the
moment he thought he was going to die.
