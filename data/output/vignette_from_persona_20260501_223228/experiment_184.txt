Sokha is a 69-year-old Cambodian American man who has spent most of his adult life working as a
plumber, a job that still takes him into basements, utility rooms, and strangers’ homes where he
often has to assess a space quickly before stepping fully inside. During intake he described a
period of captivity many years ago, speaking in fragments that repeatedly broke apart. He said he
had been confined by men, watched closely, and hurt when he could not move as he was told. What
remains most vivid is not a complete story but pieces that return with force: the presence of male
guards standing too near, the sick rising feeling in his stomach, and pain and stiffness in his neck
from how he was held and from constantly bracing. When he tries to tell it in order, he loses the
thread, falls silent, and says he knows exactly what happened but cannot find words that stay long
enough to get it out.

Over time those fragments have kept coming back as if the event were still unspooling in front of
him. Sokha says the memory plays “like a film,” without his wanting it to, and particular sensations
pull it closer. A wave of nausea or a pull in his neck can suddenly feel less like an ordinary
bodily feeling than a doorway back into that time. Being alone with men is especially potent. On
plumbing calls, if a male customer leads him down a narrow hallway or into a cramped basement, he
may feel the old scene rushing up before he can think. The trigger and the memory arrive almost
together, and once the images start he finds himself flooded by details he cannot organize into
language.

Because of that, he has developed rigid ways of moving through the world that make immediate sense
to him. Before entering any building, he studies exits and mentally keeps an escape route. If he is
repairing pipes in a house, he prefers to position himself near a doorway and notices whether
another man is standing between him and the exit. Around dates tied in his mind to the captivity, he
changes routines and avoids family gatherings or community events without fully explaining why. He
also tries to detach from the memories when they surface, pushing himself emotionally away from them
as if distance alone might keep them from taking over. These habits are strongest precisely when he
feels nauseous, notices neck pain, or anticipates being alone with men; the triggers set him
searching for a way out, turning away, or numbing himself before anything can gather force.

Yet these same strategies seem to keep the memories in the same unfinished, intrusive form. When
Sokha pushes them away, the recollections do not settle into a coherent account; instead they return
later as the same repeating film, vivid but disconnected, and he remains unable to explain clearly
what happened from beginning to end. Avoiding anniversaries preserves the sense that those dates are
too dangerous to touch. Constant escape planning keeps his attention fixed on the possibility of
being trapped again, so bodily sensations and situations involving men stay sharply linked to the
old captivity. In this way, the more he organizes his life around getting away from reminders, the
more readily those reminders seem to find him.

The memories also leave him living as if threat is close by even in places he knows are safe. He
described being on guard in his own home, noticing where doors are and disliking being surprised by
anyone’s movement in the next room. Sudden movements by other people put him immediately on alert,
especially if a man steps toward him quickly or shifts position without warning. When something
reminds him of the captivity, his body reacts before he can reassure himself: he tenses, feels sick,
and becomes acutely watchful. His wife has noticed that he startles and scans the room even during
ordinary evenings at home. For Sokha, this vigilance feels necessary; the old memory is so
immediate, and so hard to put into words, that part of him is certain he must stay prepared.

What finally brought him to seek help was the way this pattern had begun to constrict both his work
and his marriage. He recently turned down several plumbing jobs that would have required him to work
alone in houses with male occupants, and his wife told him she felt he was never fully at ease even
sitting beside her in their living room. He was shaken by how quickly a customer’s abrupt movement
in a utility closet had sent him into a familiar spiral of nausea, neck tension, scanning for exits,
and the replay of scenes he still could not narrate. He came to treatment worried that his world is
getting smaller and that he will end his life still captive to something he cannot fully say. What
he hopes, cautiously, is to be able to remember without being pulled back into it, and to walk into
a room without first having to plan how to escape.
