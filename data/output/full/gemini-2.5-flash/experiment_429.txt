Koa, a 71-year-old man of Oceania descent, presented with a quiet demeanor, his hands, still stained
faintly with ochre and cerulean from his work as a painter, often clasped tightly in his lap. His
current distress, he explained, stemmed from an act of terrorism he witnessed fifteen months prior
while sketching in a bustling city market. He had been capturing the vibrant chaos when a sudden,
deafening explosion ripped through the space, throwing him to the ground. For what felt like an
eternity, he lay amidst the debris, unable to move, his body pressed against the cold, gritty
pavement, the air thick with smoke and screams. This profound sense of helplessness, of being
utterly vulnerable and at the mercy of forces beyond his control, had irrevocably altered his
perception of himself and the world around him.

Since then, Koa’s life had narrowed considerably. What frightened him most was the pervasive belief
that the world is a dangerous place, an interpretation that felt undeniably true after the market
attack. This conviction, that danger lurked in every public space, led him to increasingly avoid
going out. He rarely left his apartment now, except for essential errands, and even then, he was
hyper-vigilant, scanning every face, every shadow. He had also come to believe, with a growing sense
of despair, that he couldn't rely on himself. The memory of his paralysis during the attack, the
utter inability to protect himself or others, convinced him of his own incompetence and fragility.
Consequently, this deep-seated belief that he couldn't rely on himself made him try hard to control
his emotions, fearing that any outward display of distress would confirm his perceived weakness. He
would clench his jaw, take shallow breaths, and force a stoic expression, hoping to contain the
rising panic.

These efforts, however, offered little true relief. Koa found that when memories came to mind of
what happened, he would feel his body was in the same position as when the frightening event
occurred, a visceral bodily reenactment that made the past terrifyingly present. These vivid
memories of the frightening event, clear and detailed as if they had just happened, forced their way
into his mind when he did not want them to, often triggered by a distant siren or a sudden loud
noise. These intrusive recollections, particularly their uncontrolled access, reinforced his belief
that he couldn't rely on himself to even manage his own thoughts, let alone navigate a hostile
world. He would then redouble his efforts at emotion control, attempting to push the images and
sensations away, but this suppression often made the memories even more insistent and vivid later
on.

His reliance on others, too, had shifted. Koa had always been a solitary man, finding solace in his
art, but now, his conviction that he couldn't rely on other people had deepened. He felt a profound
sense of isolation, believing that no one could truly understand or help him, a sentiment that was
ironically strengthened by his maladaptive strategies. His constant reassurance seeking, where he
kept asking his few remaining friends and family to tell him he was okay, often left him feeling
more exposed and less secure when their patience eventually wore thin. The very act of seeking
constant validation, he felt, was further proof that he couldn't rely on himself, and if he couldn't
rely on himself, who else was there? His painting, once a source of joy and expression, now felt
flat and uninspired, his studio, once a sanctuary, now a cage. He had stopped exhibiting his work,
and the creative spark seemed to have dimmed, a consequence that further solidified his sense of
personal failure and the world's pervasive danger. It was this creeping stagnation, the slow erosion
of his identity as an artist, that finally prompted him to seek help, though he remained profoundly
skeptical that anyone could truly make the world safe again, or mend his fractured sense of self.
