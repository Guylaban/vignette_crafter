Arthur, a 65-year-old prosecutor, sought therapy after years of struggling with a pervasive sense of
unease that had begun to significantly compromise his professional and personal life. His
difficulties stemmed from a violent incident years prior, when he, a respected figure in the legal
community, had been caught in the crossfire of a chaotic protest. He had been observing a
demonstration, a familiar face to many of the officers present, when the situation escalated
unexpectedly. Despite his attempts to identify himself and de-escalate, he was singled out and
subjected to a brutal beating by a group of officers, some of whom he recognized from his own
courtroom. The injustice of it, the profound moral violation by those sworn to uphold the law, was a
shock that reverberated through his entire being. He remembered the cacophony of the crowd, the
shouts and crackle of police radios blending into an incomprehensible chatter that still haunted
him, alongside the terrifying presence of the very authority he represented.

Now, years later, Arthur found himself increasingly on edge. The most innocuous events could become
potent triggers, plunging him into a state of intense apprehension. If he saw people misbehaving in
public, like jaywalkers or someone cutting in line, a familiar knot of anger and fear would tighten
in his stomach, recalling the arbitrary violence he had endured. Similarly, the sight of police
officers, whether in the courthouse or on the street, would instantly make him feel exposed and
vulnerable. Even the mundane auditory experience of overhearing garbled conversations, or the
indistinct chatter from a colleague's office across the hall, could ignite a sudden spike of
anxiety, mimicking the confusing chaos that preceded his assault. These triggers invariably led to a
profound sense of threat. He would become superalert and watchful, his hypervigilance making him
constantly scan his environment for any signs that he might be in danger, his gaze darting from face
to face in the courtroom. He found himself watching other people closely, scrutinizing their
expressions and movements for any hint that they might intend him harm, a constant internal warning
system that left him exhausted. Unsurprisingly, this heightened state of arousal also manifested as
severe sleep problems; he would lie awake for hours, unable to fall or stay asleep, replaying
fragmented scenes from the past.

In an attempt to manage this relentless sense of threat, Arthur had developed a repertoire of
maladaptive strategies. When the hypervigilance became overwhelming, particularly at home, he would
retreat to his study and put on loud music or the TV, using the sensory distraction to drown out the
internal noise and the perceived external dangers. He also found himself constantly asking his wife,
Eleanor, for reassurance that he was okay, that everything was safe, needing her words to
momentarily quell the rising tide of his fear. Moreover, his habit of constantly scanning his
environment for signs of threat, a desperate attempt to feel safe, paradoxically made him even more
attuned to potential dangers, inadvertently increasing the frequency with which he encountered his
triggers. This constant vigilance meant he was always on the lookout for moral violations or the
presence of authority figures, feeding the very cycle he sought to escape.

These maladaptive strategies, rather than alleviating his distress, often served to intensify his
sense of threat. His reliance on sensory distraction, while offering momentary respite, prevented
him from truly processing his underlying fears, keeping his nervous system in a perpetual state of
alert. Similarly, his constant scanning for danger, a maladaptive strategy itself, kept him

feeling that he needed to watch others for signs of harm. The consequences had been subtle at first,

scanning, coupled with his difficulty tolerating the routine presence of law enforcement, had begun
to affect his performance. Eleanor, though patient, had expressed concern about his incessant need

trapped, exhausted by the constant vigilance, and acutely aware that the very methods he used to
protect himself were now isolating him and slowly dismantling the life he had built. He desperately
wanted to regain a sense of safety and peace, but he was terrified of what might happen if he
lowered his guard.
