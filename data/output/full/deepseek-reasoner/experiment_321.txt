Priya was seventy-five when the bombing happened. She had been volunteering at a community health
fair held in the basement of the local mosque, offering free dental screenings to families in the
neighborhood. Mid-morning, a man entered the main prayer hall upstairs and detonated an explosive
device. Priya heard the blast from below—a sound that was less a boom than a sudden, physical
pressure in her chest—and then the sirens began. Within seconds, smoke started seeping under the
door of the basement room, thin and gray, carrying the acrid smell of burning carpet and dust. For
the next forty minutes, she and several dozen others were trapped inside that room while the attack
was ongoing. The man had not yet been subdued; no one knew if more bombers were present. Prira felt
entirely powerless: there was nothing she could do for the patients, nothing she could do for
herself, no way to leave, no information coming in. The smoke thickened, the sirens wailed outside,
and the minutes stretched into a kind of suspended terror that she would later describe as “being
inside a held breath.” Two people were killed upstairs; Priya and those in the basement were
eventually evacuated unharmed, but something in her had already begun to fracture.

In the weeks and months that followed, Priya found that her life had quietly rearranged itself
around the threat of that moment. She would not drive anywhere near the mosque, taking a route three
miles out of her way to avoid the street where the event had occurred. But the triggers followed her
farther than she could avoid. The first time she heard a siren after the bombing, she was in her
dental office cleaning instruments; the sound sent her hand darting to the counter edge as if to
steady herself, and she caught herself scanning the operatory door, the windows, the hallway—any
egress she could reach. She began, without conscious decision, to sit with her back to the wall in
every room and to choose chairs closest to an exit, whether at home, at a restaurant, or in the
waiting room of her own practice. When she heard a siren, her eyes would sweep the immediate
environment for signs of threat and for an escape route, and she would feel a sharp, urgent need to
be near a door. The connection between the trigger and the strategy became automatic: a siren meant
danger, and danger meant she had to check and locate an exit.

But the strategies themselves also kept the threat alive. Because she was constantly scanning her
surroundings for exits and suspicious people, she was also more likely to notice distant sirens that
she might otherwise have ignored, or to catch a whiff of smoke from a passing barbecue and feel her
pulse spike before she even identified the source. Her vigilance made the world feel saturated with
potential evidence of danger. When she was in the operatory with a patient, she would find herself
glancing toward the hallway, checking for the exit sign, and that very act would remind her of the
basement room and the trapped feeling, which in turn made her more alert to any strange sound or
smell. She had come to believe that if she let her guard down even for a moment, something
catastrophic would happen, and that it was her responsibility to remain watchful—a conviction that
only reinforced the scanning and the exit-seeking, which then fed back into her sensitivity to
triggers.

The power imbalance trigger was subtler but equally disabling. Prira now felt a visceral discomfort
in any situation where someone else held authority over her—a dental board inspector, a physician
during a routine checkup, even a teller at the bank when she needed to sign a form. The sensation of
being subject to another person’s control re-created the helplessness of that trapped hour. She
began to avoid such situations: she postponed her annual physical for two years, she declined a
recommendation to attend a continuing-education course because it would mean sitting in a classroom
with a lecturer at the front, and she ultimately switched to a bank that offered drive-through
service so she would not have to enter and interact with staff.

The avoidance had taken a toll. Her practice continued, but she no longer enjoyed the work; she
found herself rushing through procedures, staying near the door, and feeling relieved when patients
left. Social invitations were declined or left unanswered. She had not been inside a mosque since
the bombing, and she no longer spent time with friends who might suggest a large gathering. What
finally brought her to seek help was an incident on the freeway: a car backfired, she heard the
noise and smelled a faint oil smell from an overheated engine, and she became so disoriented that
she nearly swerved into the median. She pulled over and sat shaking for twenty minutes. That was the
moment she understood that she could no longer manage this alone. Priya now hopes that therapy might
loosen the grip of those sirens and smells, but she is also terrified that revisiting the memory
will only make it stronger.
