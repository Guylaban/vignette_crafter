Marco was forty years old, a trauma surgeon who had spent nearly two decades working in a busy Level
1 center, but nothing had prepared him for the night the wildfire swept through his neighborhood. He
had been in surgery when his wife called, her voice thin with smoke. By the time he reached their
street, the fire had already consumed three homes, including his own. He remembered the roar of the
flames—a deafening, crackling thunder that seemed to come from everywhere at once—and the acrid,
sweet smell of burning wood and plastic. He ran through the front door thinking his wife was still
inside, only to find the living room already collapsed. A firefighter pulled him back just as the
roof caved in. His wife had escaped through a back window and was safe, but the image of that
burning house, the heat, the noise, the smell—those fragments lodged themselves somewhere deep and
began to dictate his life.

Over the following weeks, Marco found that any loud noise could send him straight back. When a
gurney clattered in the hallway, his heart would pound and his vision would narrow, and suddenly he
was back in that burning house, the memory surfacing without warning, incomplete and jumbled—he
could feel the heat but not the sequence of his own steps. At first, he tried to ignore it. After a
particularly bad shift, he would pour himself a whiskey, letting the alcohol blur the edges. He also
started using sexual fantasies as a deliberate distraction, retreating into them during quiet
moments at home to push the memory away. But neither strategy truly worked; the memory kept breaking
through, always with that same overwhelming grief for the life they had lost—their photographs, the
heirloom furniture, the sense of safety.

The smell of smoke became his worst enemy. In the operating room, the cautery device sent up thin
plumes with a distinctly burnt odor, and Marco would freeze mid-surgery, his knuckles white on the
scalpel. That smell triggered the memory instantly, and the memory, in turn, made him hypervigilant:
he would glance around the OR to check for smoke, his ears straining for any sizzling sound that
might signal a real fire. He had come to believe that he could not trust his environment—that a loud
noise at night, a whiff of char on his clothes, could herald another collapse. This constant
scanning exhausted him, and at home he withdrew further, using distraction and alcohol to numb the
dread. But the drinking seemed to make the memory even more fragmented. He would wake the next
morning with only the grief intact, the sequence of events more scrambled, as if the alcohol had
erased the narrative and left only the raw emotion.

His wife grew worried. Marco had always been a careful surgeon, but now he was avoiding emergency
calls that involved burns or any case in which he might smell smoke. He had also stopped jogging
through the park because someone had been barbecuing near the trail. At work, he found himself
relying on sexual fantasies as a way to escape during brief breaks, but this left him feeling
disconnected and guilty, and when he returned to the OR, the memory would intrude again, more
insistent. The cycle was reinforcing itself: a trigger set off the memory, the memory made him
hypervigilant for triggers, and his maladaptive strategies—drinking, distraction, sexual coping—only
deepened the fragmentation and grief, leaving him feeling helpless.

What finally brought Marco to seek help was a moment during a routine cholecystectomy. A loud alarm
from a malfunctioning monitor sent him into such a dissociated state that his attending had to take
over. He stood trembling, the burnt smell from the cautery filling his nostrils, and for several
seconds he did not know where he was. Afterward, he drove home and sat in the driveway, unable to
enter the house because the scent of a neighbor’s fireplace drifted across the street. That night,
he told his wife he could not keep living like this—afraid of sounds, of smells, of his own
memories, and terrified that one day his distraction would cost a patient their life. He scheduled
an appointment the next morning, hoping that someone could help him piece together the fragments and
find a way to live with the grief without losing himself.
