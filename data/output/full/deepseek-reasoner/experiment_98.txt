Darnell is a 58-year-old married hairdresser who owns a small salon in a mid-sized Midwestern city.
Sixteen months ago, as he was closing up for the night, a man smelling of cheap whiskey stepped from
the alley and pressed a knife to his ribs, demanding his wallet and phone. Darnell handed everything
over, arms shaking, and the man fled. For weeks afterward Darnell could not shake the images: the
glint of the blade, the cracked pavement, the sour odor that filled his nostrils. The memory would
replay like a film each evening as he tried to sleep, and when his wife, Marlene, asked what was
wrong, he found he knew exactly what had happened but could not arrange the words to describe it—his
mouth would go dry and he would shake his head.

Early on, Darnell tried to push the whole event out of his mind. He would tell himself, *Just don’t
think about it,* and would force his attention to the television or his clippers. But the
suppression only made the images return more vividly, often triggered by the faintest whiff of
alcohol—a customer’s cologne, a spilled drink at the barbershop next door. Each time the smell hit
him, the mugging scene played again in full color. Soon he began to notice that anyone who acted
vaguely suspicious—a man lingering too long at the bus stop, a customer shifting his weight
oddly—would set off the same replay, and Darnell would feel his heart race and his hands tremble
while cutting hair.

He started avoiding people. He stopped going to the grocery store after dark, turned down
invitations from friends, and began closing his salon an hour early to minimize contact with late-
afternoon foot traffic. The avoidance felt necessary, but it also left him alone with his thoughts,
and he would spend those empty hours dwelling on what he had done wrong: *Why didn’t I lock the door
sooner? Why did I stay late? I should have seen him coming.* This self-blame rumination made him
hypervigilant in the salon; any slight from a client—a frown, a correction about a haircut—felt like
a confirmation that he was failing, that people were judging him, that danger was near. The
criticism became a trigger in itself, as potent as alcohol smell. When Marlene gently pointed out
that he had been short-tempered lately, Darnell snapped at her and then spent the rest of the night
replaying the argument as another version of the mugging—a situation where he had not been in
control, where he had been caught off guard.

The cycle tightened. Every perceived threat (a customer who seemed “off,” a man walking too quickly
past the salon window) triggered a vivid memory fragment, which in turn drove him to avoid that
person or leave the floor. And every act of avoidance left him more isolated, more convinced that he
was to blame for his own fear, and therefore more vigilant for the next threat. His salon business
suffered: regular clients noticed his distracted demeanor and shorter hours; one longtime customer
complained that Darnell seemed “jumpy” and stopped booking appointments. Marlene felt shut out and
grew frustrated. What frightened Darnell most was the feeling that the world had become a place
where danger lurked in ordinary gestures—a turned head, a raised voice, a whiff of cheap beer—and
that he could not trust his own judgement to distinguish real threat from memory.

He finally came to therapy after Marlene threatened to leave if he did not get help. Sitting in the
office, he struggled to describe the mugging, the words again failing him. He said he was tired of
being scared of his own customers, of flinching when his wife touched his shoulder from behind. What
he hoped for, he admitted, was simply to feel normal again—to cut hair without watching the door, to
smell a cologne and not see a knife.
