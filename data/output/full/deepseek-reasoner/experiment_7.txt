miles per hour. The impact crushed his pelvis, tore ligaments in his lower back, and left him
trapped in the wreckage for forty-five minutes while the fuel line dripped. Surgery and months of
rehabilitation followed, but Minh healed physically. What did not heal was what happened inside his
mind.

Now, at work, a patient’s crutch clattering to the linoleum floor would send electricity through his
spine—his heart slamming, his eyes darting to corners, searching for the source of danger as though
a car were about to break through the wall. That split-second focus on threat was exhausting, yet he
could not stop himself; the loud noise had already activated the hypervigilance that now governed
his days. His concentration fractured: a colleague’s laugh could become a distant scream, and a
spilled water bottle might as well be shattering glass. At night, he woke with dream residue—the
screech of metal still ringing—and lay rigid, convinced that his body was fundamentally unreliable,
that the healed fractures were a mask over permanent damage. Those intrusive thoughts and nightmare
fragments also fed his sense that the world was full of hidden dangers: you could never know who
would harm you, or where the next lethal blow would come from.

That conviction—that his body could not be trusted and that others were unpredictable
threats—spawned a set of rituals. Minh began mentally rehearsing worst-case scenarios before every
interaction: picturing a patient collapsing on the treatment table, a fire alarm going off mid-
session, a stranger walking in with hostile intent. He also cleaned and organised his treatment room
repeatedly—aligning the weights, stacking the Therabands, polishing the counter—because the order
made him feel, fleetingly, in control. At home, he found himself checking the sharp edges of kitchen
knives, noticing the heavy lamp that could be used as a weapon, though he never told his wife why.
These strategies, however, backfired: scanning the environment for signs of threat only sharpened
his vigilance, so that a shadow moving in the hallway would trigger another startle response,
another wave of fear that something terrible was about to happen. The more he checked, the more
dangers he saw—a loop that tightened around his every conscious moment.

His wife began to notice. He would come home from work hollow-eyed, and when she asked about his day
he said little, because explaining that he had spent hours replaying the worst possible outcomes for
a routine ankle sprain seemed absurd. She saw him lock the front door three times before bed, then
check the windows. She asked what he was afraid of, and Minh could only say, “Anyone could come in.
I don’t know.” The fear of harm had graduated from a thought to a constant state; it made him avoid
crowds, avoid driving past the intersection where the accident happened, avoid letting his wife
drive alone because he could not stop imagining the wreckage. At work, he became known as the
meticulous therapist, but his colleagues did not know that the compulsion to tidy was a fragile dam
against panic.

What brought him to therapy, finally, was a morning when a patient’s grandson slammed a door in the

how to be normal anymore.”
