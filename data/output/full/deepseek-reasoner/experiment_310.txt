David is thirty-six, a retail worker at a large home-goods store in a suburban strip mall, and he
has been married for seven years to a woman named Sarah. Three years ago, he was working security at
a sold-out outdoor concert when a portable generator near the stage caught fire. The flames spread
fast, and the crowd—already packed tight—surged toward the exits in a single, panicked mass. What
David remembers most vividly are not the flames themselves but the way people behaved: grown men
shoving women aside, a teenager climbing over a fallen couple, someone screaming that it was a
terrorist attack when it was only a faulty wire. He was pinned against a metal barrier, the heat
pressing his face, and he felt the grit of ash and burnt debris settle into the collar of his
uniform—a coarse, greasy texture that clung to his skin and that he could not wash off for days
afterward, no matter how many times he scrubbed. He got out, but the images stayed: a woman’s hand
reaching, a child’s shoe left behind, each moment a still frame in his mind, sharper and more real
than anything that has happened since.

At first he told himself it was not that bad—he survived, after all—and he tried hard not to think
about it, filling his days with double shifts and late nights until he was too tired to dream. But
the memories kept breaking through, and when they did, they brought a shame so thick it felt
physical: he should have done more, should have stopped the stampede, should have been braver. Shame
made the snapshots even harder to shake. He found that if he pressed his thumbnail into the web of
his other hand, or dug the edge of a key into his forearm, the sharp pain would cut the memory
short. Scratching became a habit, a private way to silence the thought, though the shame always came
back later, heavier.

The crowds at the store have become unbearable. When a customer cuts in line or yells at a cashier,
David feels a hot tightness in his chest and immediately tells himself the event was nowhere near
that bad—magical thinking that lasts only a moment before he catches himself. The proximity of a
busy aisle, with people pressing close to reach a discount sign, sends a vivid snapshot into his
mind: the surge, the heat, the hand. Because those memories feel more real than the fluorescent
lights and tile floor around him, the slightest cue—the coarse weave of a fire blanket on a shelf,
the rough texture of a scratchy towel—triggers another picture, and another. Each time that happens,
he tries to suppress the thought, but suppression only makes the next snapshot arrive sooner,
sharper, and with more shame. He has realized that his effort to ignore the trauma actually makes
him scan the aisles for potential reminders, so that now a mere glimpse of the blanket aisle sets
off a cascade: the texture, the memory, the surge of shame, then the need to hurt himself to stop
it.

Sarah has noticed the scratches on his arms and the way he flinches in crowds, but David cannot
explain it. What finally brought him to a therapist was a scene at work last Tuesday: a customer
knocked over a stack of bed linens, and as David bent to pick them up his fingertips brushed the
rough cotton. He froze, saw the ash, felt the heat, and in front of a dozen people he scratched his
forearm raw. His manager sent him home. That night, sitting in the dark living room, David told
Sarah it was getting worse. He is terrified that if he stops trying to tell himself it was not that
bad, the snapshots will flood him completely and he will never leave his house again. Yet he is
equally afraid that the strategies he has built are the only thing keeping him from falling apart,
and that without them he will have no way to hold the shame at bay.
