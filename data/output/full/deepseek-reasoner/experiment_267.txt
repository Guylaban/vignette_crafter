Arjun was twenty years old and a writer, but he hadn’t written a word in months. The words had dried
up the night his older brother Ravi died—a car accident that shouldn’t have happened, that couldn’t
have happened, because Ravi was a careful driver, because the world was supposed to make sense.
Arjun had been at his desk, editing a short story, when the phone rang. He remembered the exact
sentence he’d been working on, something about a character walking through a park in autumn, and how
that sentence now felt like a cruel joke. That was four months ago. Since then, he had come to
believe that the world was a dangerous place, not just sometimes but always, and that happiness—the
kind he used to take for granted—was gone for good.

The shattered beliefs weren’t abstract. They showed up every time he tried to fall asleep. He would
lie in bed, unable to relax even knowing the door was locked and his apartment was safe, because
safety no longer felt real. Ordinary sounds—a car door outside, a neighbor’s footsteps—made him
jump, his heart hammering before his mind could catch up. He knew it was irrational to fear a
closing car door, but his body didn’t care. What was rational, anyway, when a loved one could vanish
without warning? That line of thinking had become automatic: the world is dangerous, so I cannot
relax. It also drove him to ask his friends to reassure him that he was okay, that he wasn’t falling
apart, even though he knew he was. He kept cycling through the same question: “Do I seem okay to
you?” And they would say yes, but he never believed them, because he could feel the misery pressing
down like a weight.

Arjun had stopped making plans for the future. A writing deadline came up and he pushed it back,
telling himself he just needed a few days. Then he pushed it back again. He avoided anything that
reminded him of Ravi—the coffee shop they used to go to, the playlist they had built together, even
certain words that carried his brother’s voice. This avoidance, he believed, was the only way to
keep from collapsing entirely. But each time he turned down a friend’s invitation or skipped a
meeting, the world felt more dangerous and he felt more broken, as if his own cowardice proved that
he could no longer handle life. The more he avoided, the more convinced he became that he had lost
himself.

The avoidance also fed his hypervigilance. Because he spent so much time alone in his apartment,
trying to control his environment, the smallest unexpected noise became a catastrophe. He would
startle, and the startle itself would confirm that he was unsafe, that relaxation was a luxury he
could not afford. He tried to rationalize it—it was just the radiator, it was just a car
backfiring—but the feeling of danger never abated, and his sleep grew worse. He lay awake night
after night, replaying the phone call, watching the words of his story dissolve, wondering if he
would ever feel normal again.

What finally brought Arjun to a therapist was the recognition that he had stopped writing, which was
the only thing that had ever made him feel like himself. He was terrified that if he returned to the
page, he would find nothing there—no sentences, no stories, just the echo of Ravi’s absence. But he
was equally terrified of staying stuck. He wanted to believe that recovery was possible, but his
shattered beliefs made even that hope feel naive. He sat in the waiting room with his hands on his
knees, thinking about the paragraph he had abandoned, and wondered if he could ever finish it.
