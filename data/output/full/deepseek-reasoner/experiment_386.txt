Kwame is a 62-year-old stock broker, divorced for nearly a decade, who has been a steady presence on
the trading floor at a mid‑size firm in Atlanta. Two years ago, his thirty‑five‑year‑old son,
Malcolm, died suddenly when a driver ran a red light and struck his car. Kwame received the call at
his desk—he remembers the phone ringing, the unfamiliar tightness in his assistant’s voice, and then
fragments: the drive to the hospital, a nurse’s hand on his arm, the glare of fluorescent lights.
The rest is missing. He cannot deliberately summon the sequence of events, only shards that hit him
without warning—the smell of antiseptic, the hum of a ventilator—and then vanish.

Because the memory arrives only in jagged pieces, Kwame has spent the intervening months going over
and over why this happened to him. He replays the phone call, the drive, the hospital hallway,
trying to force the gaps together, searching for a meaning that would make the loss bearable. Yet
the more he ruminates, the more the fragments seem to slide apart; when he tries to deliberately
recall his son’s face in the last hours, the image slips away, replaced by the ringing of that
phone. He has come to believe that if he could just hold the full memory, he would finally
understand why Malcolm was taken—but the harder he tries, the more elusive the narrative becomes.

The sudden pieces themselves terrify him. A flashback—the sound of a phone, the reflection of a
fluorescent strip—can land in the middle of a trading session, and suddenly Kwame feels the same
horror and helplessness he felt in that hospital. The negative emotions follow: guilt that he was at
work instead of answering his son’s last call, shame that he sometimes feels anger at Malcolm for
dying, and a raw, unnameable grief that seems to have no end. These emotions have themselves begun
to warp the memory; when he is most ashamed or afraid, the fragments become even more disorganised,
and deliberate recollection feels almost impossible.

Kwame has tried to cope by suppressing any thought of the trauma. He forces the images away, keeps
his mind on the blinking numbers of his trading screens, and avoids every reminder of his son—the
framed photo he turned face‑down, the family restaurant he no longer drives past. But the
suppression only intensifies the flashbacks; a sudden, unbidden fragment often breaks through just
when he is most determined not to think about it. And the avoidance has a cost: rumination fills the
quiet moments, a circular, exhausting loop of “why me” that seems to strengthen the very pieces he
is trying to escape.

The threat he feels from his own mind—the guilt, the fear of another flashback—has driven him into a
behaviour that surprises even himself: taking outsized, reckless risks at work. He has started to
make bets on volatile stocks far larger than his firm’s guidelines allow, as if courting danger
gives him a momentary grip on something real. The risks are a kind of pressure relief, but they also
generate more fear and shame, which in turn make the memory fragments more intrusive and his
deliberate access to the trauma even poorer. On the trading floor, the sound of an incoming call or
a colleague’s sudden movement can trigger a flashback, and Kwame has come to dread the ordinary
rhythms of his day because they so often become the doorway to the hospital.

He finally sought help three weeks ago, after a risky trade that nearly bankrupted a client’s
account—a consequence that shook him into recognising that his strategies were no longer working.
What frightens him most now is the sense that his memory of Malcolm is dissolving into noise, that
he will lose the real boy behind the fragments. He hopes therapy can help him piece together what
happened without the constant loop of guilt and rumination, so that he can remember his son as he
was, not as a trigger.
