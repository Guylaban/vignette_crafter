sat in the parking lot — a melancholic ballad she had hummed while adjusting his crown, and which he
must have recorded. The memory of that song, drifting through the closed blinds of her operator
room, still made her stomach drop. She called the police, who took a report but could not arrest him
until he violated a restraining order. By then the damage was done: Lien had learned that safety was
something other people promised but could not deliver.

Months passed, and she returned to work. But the world had grown porous. When a patient’s phone rang
with a familiar melody — the same minor-key progression, the same lazy guitar — she felt the threat
before she could name it. Her chest would tighten, her gaze would sweep the waiting room for exits,
and she would excuse herself to the sterilization alcove, where she pressed her back against the
wall until the feeling passed. Even when her husband assured her the stalker was in another state,
that the police had confirmed it, Lien found the words meaningless. “I know you’re safe,” he would
say, and she would nod, but inside she knew safety was not a thing you could know, only a thing you
could fail to lose. She began to choose her seat carefully everywhere she went: always a table with
a wall behind her, never a booth in the center of the room. At the office she rearranged her own
treatment station so that her dental chair faced the door and her back rested against a solid
partition. It was the only way she could concentrate on a root canal without her neck prickling.

exposure, she later learned to call it, but at the time it simply felt like a door swinging open.

ambush. The sight of a police cruiser in her neighborhood no longer brought relief; it brought a
flash of that afternoon when an officer had told her, with kind eyes, that they could not watch her
house every night. Police presence now meant the threat was real, not that it was handled. She would
drive an extra block to avoid passing the precinct.

The cycle tightened. A song on the office radio meant she could not focus; she would ask the
hygienist to change the station, and then feel ashamed. A news article about stalking meant she
would lie awake, replaying the old voicemails in her mind. Each trigger fed the same conviction: the
world was not safe, and her own vigilance was the only barrier between her and catastrophe. She
stopped attending her weekly mahjong group because the host’s house did not have a single seat that
faced both the door and a window. The consequences were concrete: friendships frayed, and her
husband quietly stopped suggesting date nights because she would scan the restaurant for exits
rather than look at him.

What finally brought Lien to seek help was not a crisis but a quiet exhaustion. She had just
finished a crown preparation, and a pop song from the common playlist had made her hands shake so

between reasonable caution and a life ruled by fear. She wanted to believe that safety could be
learned, not merely defended. She was not sure it was possible, but she was willing to sit down with
a stranger and try.
