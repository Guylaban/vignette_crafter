Aisha, a 35-year-old police officer, was sexually assaulted two years ago during a routine traffic
stop. The suspect, after a brief struggle, overpowered her, held a gun to her head, and raped her.
There are parts of that night she cannot retrieve no matter how hard she tries—memory gaps that
leave her feeling as though her own mind has betrayed her. When the memory does surface, it arrives
with a searing physical pain in her lower abdomen and back, the same places where the assault
occurred, and an overwhelming rage that makes her want to break something.

After the assault, Aisha returned to duty but soon found herself steering clear of any situation
that might stir strong emotions. She began to avoid domestic disturbance calls and any interaction
with citizens that could escalate into disagreement or confrontation. At the station, she would find
reasons not to join conversations about difficult arrests, and at home she sidestepped arguments
with her husband, letting small resentments build rather than risk conflict. The rage that erupted
when she remembered the assault felt dangerous, and she came to believe that if she let herself feel
anything intensely, she would lose control entirely. This conviction was reinforced by her growing
belief that she had permanently changed for the worse—that the woman she had been before the assault
was gone, replaced by someone brittle and unsafe. Another officer, she told herself, would never
have let the situation unfold that way. The self-blame festered, and she began to imagine how life
would have been different if the event had never occurred: she would still trust her instincts,
still feel connected to her partner, still belong in her uniform. Instead, she felt she had lost her
connection to her community and culture. As a Black woman in law enforcement, she had once taken
pride in bridging two worlds; now she felt alienated from both, convinced that neither the citizens
she served nor her own cultural roots could ever understand what had been done to her.

The memories themselves—the gaps, the pain, the rage—deepened these appraisals. Each time she was
ambushed by a flashback, the physical pain seemed proof that she was permanently damaged, and the
gaps in her recall made her feel she had lost an integral part of her own history, which fed her
sense of being cut off from her former self and her community. The rage, in turn, confirmed for her
that she was not the calm, capable officer she had once been, and that no one else would have let
the assault define them this way. This cycle tightened over months: a memory would surface, her
appraisals of permanent damage and lost belonging would sharpen, and she would pull away from
emotional situations and conflict even further. She stopped responding to critical incidents
altogether, volunteering for desk duty, and at home she withdrew from her husband, avoiding both
intimacy and any discussion of her feelings. The counterfactual thinking became a nightly
ritual—what if she had called for backup?—and each imagined alternative made the present feel more
unbearable.

What finally brought Aisha to seek help was a training exercise that involved a simulated traffic
stop. The role-play was too close: the actor’s posture, the sudden movement, the sound of the car
door opening. She froze, then ran, and spent the rest of the day in a locked bathroom, trembling and
fighting the urge to scream. Her supervisor insisted she take leave and see someone. Aisha is
terrified that talking about it will unleash the rage she has worked so hard to contain, and she is
not sure she can ever feel whole again. But beneath that fear is a sliver of hope that maybe, if she
can find a way to reconnect with herself and the people she once trusted, she might not have to
carry this alone.
