moving. She stayed until paramedics arrived. He survived, but with a traumatic brain injury and
months of rehabilitation. That night, and for weeks afterward, Margaret told herself it was an
accident. But the accident kept living in her body.

In the months that followed, she found she could not relax, even when she was in her own living room
with the door locked. She would sit down to watch television and feel her shoulders locked, her jaw
clenched, her breathing shallow. Her concentration became unreliable: during a fitting, she would
lose track of the stylist’s instructions, staring at the fabric without hearing the words. Her body
felt constantly tense, as if she were waiting for something else to go wrong — for another crash,
another mistake. She described it as “being on guard in a room where nothing is happening.” The
tension never fully left her, and it colored everything.

What frightened her most was the way that constant state of alertness seemed to feed a relentless
inner voice. Whenever she felt her heart race or her muscles tighten, the thoughts would follow like
a reflex: *Somebody else would not have gotten into this situation. A better driver would have seen
the light.* The humiliation felt permanent — she was certain that anyone who knew about the accident
saw her as careless, broken, a person who ruined someone else’s life. And then the guilt: *I will
never forgive myself for not doing more to stop what happened.* She had been tired, she had glanced
at the dashboard clock, she had not braked or honked. She replayed that moment of inaction again and
again, and each time her body tensed, the shame grew sharper.

the accident.* She could not hold the pose; she had to step away. The session was cut short, and she
