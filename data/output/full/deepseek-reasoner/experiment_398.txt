wife’s urging after years of silent suffering. He was born in a small village in Vietnam and lived
through the war as a civilian. One afternoon in 1968, during what he later learned was a bombing run
by American planes, he was caught outside his home. The explosions threw him into a ditch, and he
lay there for hours, choking on dust and smoke, unable to draw a full breath while the ground
shuddered with each impact. He remembers fragments—the smell of burning thatch, the screaming of a
neighbor’s water buffalo—but much of that day remains a jumble of disconnected sensations. The
memory feels alien to him, as if it happened to someone else, and the story itself seems too extreme
to be real; he has sometimes wondered if he imagined it all.

After the war, Minh moved to the United States, became a welder, and raised a family. But the old
sensations never fully left him. Certain car sounds—the rumble of a heavy engine or a backfire—would
jerk him back to that ditch. When that happened, his memory would surge, not as a complete narrative
but as visceral fragments: the pressure of the dirt, the terror of not being able to breathe. He
learned to redirect his thoughts quickly, forcing himself to think of something neutral—his
workbench, the blue sky—to shut the intrusion down. He also began to avoid talking about the war
altogether. What was the point? The people responsible had never faced any consequences, and he had
come to believe that the event happened partly because he had not run fast enough or hidden better.
That sense of injustice and self-blame made the memory even harder to discuss, so he stayed silent.

Over the decades, Minh found himself avoiding open spaces, especially fields or parking lots that
felt exposed; he would walk along walls or stay close to buildings. The breathlessness—any feeling
of being short of air, even from climbing stairs—would immediately trigger a flash of that
afternoon, and he would become hypervigilant, scanning his surroundings for threats. He had
difficulty concentrating at work because his mind was constantly on alert, and at night he struggled
to fall asleep, needing to stay vigilant even in his own bedroom. The hypervigilance itself made him
more sensitive to car sounds—every distant engine became a potential danger signal, which in turn
triggered more intrusive memories.

the next struggle for breath.

afraid of what talking might do—that it will make the memory more real, or that he will be overcome
by shame. But he also hopes, quietly, that perhaps there is a way to make the memory feel like his
