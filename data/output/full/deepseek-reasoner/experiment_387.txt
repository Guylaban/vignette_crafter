Chidera was thirty-three, an African American woman who had worked as a park ranger for nearly a
decade, when her younger brother Chidi died in a rockslide on the trail she had been leading. She
had watched from thirty yards away as the granite face gave way; she could still see, in
photographic clarity, the angle of his arm as he reached for a handhold, the dust cloud blooming
around him. The image came back in fragments — not the whole scene, but a piece: the sound, then the
stillness, then the feeling of her own legs refusing to move toward him. She grieved, but the grief
itself felt like a separate thing, something that arrived with the memory and then receded, leaving
her hollow.

In the months after, Chidera came to believe she had permanently damaged something inside herself.
She told her husband that she felt dead — not sad, but numb, as though the part of her that had been
capable and confident had simply stopped working. Because she no longer saw herself as the ranger
who could handle backcountry emergencies, she quietly dropped responsibilities: she requested desk
duty, stopped volunteering for search-and-rescue training, and avoided the trails where she might
encounter loose rock or sudden weather shifts. It seemed logical to her that if she was no longer
capable, she should not pretend otherwise.

This sense of being permanently changed also made the memory of Chidi’s death more insistent. She
often thought, *I am not the person I was before, so that moment is all I have left of who I used to
be* — and the memory would surface, unbidden, with photographic clarity and then dissolve, leaving
her clutching the edge of her desk. The fragments seemed to come without warning: a tourist’s high-
pitched laugh could trigger the sound of the rockslide; the smell of wet granite on a rainy morning
could bring back the image of his hand. Each time, the grief overwhelmed her again.

To cope, Chidera kept her mind occupied every waking hour. She listened to podcasts during her
commute, ran through species identification checklists while she ate, and replayed trail-maintenance
procedures in her head at night. She avoided relaxing completely — sitting still on her porch, she
told her husband, felt dangerous, as if letting her guard down would invite the memory or another
catastrophe. Her body stayed tense, her shoulders tight, her sleep fractured by dreams in which she
saw Chidi’s face and then heard the slide, waking with her heart pounding. The constant alertness
demanded that she never let herself unwind, and so she did not. But the mental distraction itself
seemed to feed the fragments — as soon as she stopped actively occupying her mind, the images rushed
back more vividly, as if they had been waiting. She noticed that the more she tried to push the
memory away, the more it came back in sharp, disconnected pieces.

At work, this created a cycle that tightened over the weeks. When a sudden piece of the memory
arrived during a routine patrol — a flash of the dust cloud while she was checking a trailhead — she
would immediately cut the patrol short, return to the station, and immerse herself in data entry.
She gave up not only the responsibilities that felt dangerous but also the ones that simply required
presence and stillness. Her supervisor expressed concern; Chidera said she was fine. At home, she
lay awake next to her husband, her mind racing through checklists, refusing the quiet that might
bring the images back.

What finally brought her to therapy was a morning in early spring. She had taken a brief assignment
to monitor a low-risk trail, but the sight of loose scree near the path triggered a photographic
fragment — Chidi’s reaching hand — and then a flood of grief that left her on her knees, unable to
move. A hiker found her. Her husband told her she could not go on this way. Sitting in the clinic,
Chidera said she wanted to stop feeling like a person who had been broken and left behind, but she
was terrified that remembering fully would destroy her entirely. She hoped the therapist could help
her find the trail again without losing herself in the rockfall.
