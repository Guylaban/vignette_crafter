Claire, a 48-year-old social ministry worker, had always been someone who moved through the world
with steady purpose—until the morning a driver ran a red light and slammed into her sedan at forty
miles an hour. She was trapped in the twisted frame for nearly twenty minutes, the smell of gasoline
thick, her chest pinned against the steering column. What stayed with her most was the raw physical
shock: the instant her body became a cage, the nausea that rose as she waited for rescue, the cold
certainty that she would die while paramedors tried to free her. She survived with a fractured
pelvis and deep bruising, but the damage to her sense of safety was complete.

In the weeks afterward, Claire noticed changes she could not explain. When her supervisor asked her
to cover a colleague’s home visit across town—a routine request—she felt a surge of panic so intense
she invented an excuse. Being told she *had* to do something (Loss of Autonomy) now felt like the
moment of being trapped again, and the feeling of being physically unwell (Feeling Ill) from even
mild exertion triggered vivid flashes of the wreck. She started to scan every room for exits the
moment she entered (Scanning Exits), checking door locations the way she used to check traffic.
Ordinary sounds—a car horn, a dropped tray in the church kitchen—made her jump (Startle to Sounds),
and no reassurance from colleagues could quiet the thought that she was never truly safe (Cannot
Trust Safety). The injustice of the accident haunted her (Injustice): the other driver had no
insurance, had not even stopped, and nothing had been done. She began to believe she could not stop
bad things from happening (Helplessness) and that her own body was now an unreliable vessel (Body
Unreliable) that could betray her at any moment.

To manage this constant vigilance, Claire developed strategies that gradually narrowed her life. She
refused to drive anywhere (Avoid Driving), which meant walking miles to work or relying on public
transport—but buses put her near traffic, and she would see cars swerve or brake, and the crash
imagery (Cars hit by accident) would flood back. Because her body felt untrustworthy, she avoided
any physical stress (Avoid Physical Stress), skipping her usual walks and even carrying groceries in
multiple trips. She also came to see relaxation as dangerous (Avoid Relaxation): sitting still with
nothing to do felt like letting her guard down, so she filled every evening with tasks. Her scanning
for exits (Threat) directly reinforced her avoidance of new places; she would only enter buildings
where she already knew the layout. In turn, her elaborate evasions (Maladaptive Strategies) kept her
constantly exposed to triggers: the bus ride that brought her past the intersection, the way a
sudden headache (Feeling Ill) sent her into a spiral of hypervigilance, the dread that she would be
forced into another vulnerable position. When physical exertion was avoided, her fitness declined
and she felt even more fragile—a weakness that amplified her startle response and deepened her
conviction that safety was an illusion.

Months passed, and the cycle tightened. Claire’s sense of injustice (Negative Appraisals) made her
interpret every small event—a loud noise, a slight fever—as proof that danger was everywhere
(Triggers), and that helplessness drove her to even stricter avoidance (Maladaptive Strategies). She
stopped attending church socials because the crowded room had no clear exit. Her supervisor noticed
her refusing assignments that required travel, and her caseload was reduced. What finally pushed her
to seek help was a morning when a colleague offered her a ride to a community event: Claire declined
with such trembling urgency that she could no longer pretend this was ordinary caution. She came to
the clinic not because she believed she could ever feel safe again, but because she was desperate to
stop her life from shrinking any further. The thing she hoped for most, she admitted quietly, was to
once again trust that her own body would not be the thing that betrayed her first.
