never got up from that floor.

Now he worked the night shift at a regional warehouse, stacking boxes in a long, dim aisle where the

smell could hit him without any warning—an innocent whiff of diesel near the loading bay would
suddenly drop him straight back into the dirt-floor room, his chest locked, his hands trembling. The
memory was always the same: hyper-detailed, the grain of the dirt under his cheek, the soldier’s
boot heel grinding into his calf, the rag pressed so hard that his nose ached for hours afterward.
When the memory came, it overwhelmed him. He would have to stop whatever he was doing, set down the
box, and press his back against a pallet rack until the feeling passed. His supervisor had noticed
and started calling him “the freezer,” because he would stand so still for so long.

The warehouse also meant occasional confrontations. Another worker, a younger man, had a habit of
crowding into Minh’s space during the shift change, barking orders about where pallets should go.
Minh had learned to avoid that man entirely, walking the long way around the freezer aisle, because
any raised voice made his stomach drop and his mind race back to the back room, to the shouts that
had preceded the knee in his spine. Each memory sharpened his vigilance. After an episode, he would
spend the rest of his shift scanning the aisles for anyone approaching too quickly, bracing for the
next ambush. The cycle had become automatic: a trigger brought the memory, the memory made him
hyper-vigilant, hyper-vigilance made him more likely to catch the next trigger.

his neighbor’s kitchen—greasy and sharp—sometimes reminded him of the diesel, and he would find
himself pressing his own hand over his mouth before he knew what he was doing. He had stopped
cooking anything with oil. He had stopped inviting anyone over. The world had shrunk to the path
between his apartment and the warehouse, and even that felt mined.

What finally brought Minh to the clinic was a new warehouse manager who had rearranged the storage
zones. The cleaning aisle was now next to Minh’s station. He could not stop the smell. It came every
shift, and the memories came every time, and he was having to lock himself in the break room for
twenty minutes just to stop shaking. His supervisor told him if he took one more unscheduled break
he would be let go. Minh was afraid of losing his job, but he was more afraid of the moment he
walked back into that aisle. He wanted someone to explain why a seventy-six-year-old man could still
be held down by a smell.
