with her husband Carlos—could become the site of irreversible harm. The argument had started over
something trivial, a misplaced work document, but it escalated with a speed that still felt unreal.
In a moment of exasperation, she reached for a heavy ceramic vase that had been a wedding gift,
swung it in a motion she had not intended, and struck Carlos on the temple. The sound was dull, not
sharp, but the sight of blood pooling on the tile floor was immediate. He collapsed. Paramedics
arrived, surgery followed, but the brain swelling was too severe. Carlos died eleven days later.
María was not charged; the district attorney called it a tragic accident. But that label meant
nothing to her at night, when she would wake with her heart pounding and no image in her mind—only a
wave of raw grief that she could not anchor to a specific picture. There were gaps in her memory:
whole stretches of that evening were missing, like a film that had been cut. She knew she had picked
up the vase, but she could not remember the second before impact. The feeling, however, came without
warning, unaccompanied by any clear recollection, as though her emotional brain had stored the
horror separately from the narrative.

Months passed. María returned to her job as a cybersecurity analyst at a regional bank, a position
that demanded steady attention and calm under pressure. The office was a landscape of triggers she
had not anticipated. A coworker kept a small paperweight on his desk that was shaped like a throwing
star—a souvenir from a martial arts competition. The first time she noticed it, her chest tightened
and her vision narrowed; she was suddenly back in the kitchen, her hand closing around the vase,
Carlos’s face turning. That visual trigger—any object that could be used as a weapon—launched a
cascade of fragmented memory: the weight of the ceramic, the sickening sound, the grief that
followed. She began to scan her environment constantly, though she tried not to show it. A metal
stapler, a decorative miniature bat, even a heavy coffee mug made her stomach drop. The memory
itself, when it surfaced, only sharpened her sensitivity. After a particularly vivid recall—one of
those moments when the grief hit without a clear image—she found herself flinching at the sight of a
colleague’s keychain that had a small Swiss Army knife attached. The memory had left her raw, her
defenses lowered, and the next trigger felt even more overwhelming.

vulnerability sent her heart racing; the raised voice, the abrupt gestures—they were not the same as
that evening, but her body did not seem to know the difference. The argument itself was a trigger,
and it immediately pulled up the memory of the shouting, the anger, the split second of action. And
then the memory looped back: the more she recalled the fight with Carlos, the more she expected
every conflict to end in violence. She avoided the break room where the paperweight sat, took longer
routes to her cubicle, and stopped attending team meetings that she predicted might become heated.

knew she was slipping, but the cycle felt locked: a weapon-like object caught her eye, memory
crashed in, and her heightened vigilance then made her see threats everywhere—a shadow, a sharp
angle, a coworker’s sudden movement. She had no words for what she was experiencing, only a growing
certainty that she could not trust herself with any object that could be used to hurt someone.

What finally drove her to seek help was a moment in the grocery store. She turned down an aisle and
saw a display of thick glass bottles. The light caught one of them, and for a second she was holding
the vase again. She dropped her basket and walked out, trembling. That night she wept for the first
time in weeks, the grief arriving without any clear image of Carlos’s face—just a deep, wordless
agony. She realized she could no longer pretend this was something she could manage alone. She
wanted to stop being afraid of her own hands, of the objects around her, of the memory that would
not stay in the past. She hoped therapy could help her find a way to remember without being undone
by it.
