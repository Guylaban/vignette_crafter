Manaia was a fifty-three-year-old airline pilot who had spent twenty-five years in the cockpit,
navigating everything from tropical storms to cramped airways. He was married to Elena, a high
school teacher, and they lived in a quiet coastal suburb. The traumatic event had happened three
years ago during a layover in a South Pacific island nation where civil unrest had flared without
warning. Armed men stormed his hotel at dawn, accused him of being a foreign spy, and dragged him to
a warehouse. For three days they tortured him: cigarette burns on his arms, repeated waterboarding,
and a mock execution where a pistol was pressed to his temple and the trigger clicked on an empty
chamber. He was released after the airline paid a ransom, but the experience had hollowed him out.

In the weeks that followed, Manaia tried to return to normal. He flew his usual routes, but the
cockpit—once a sanctuary of precision—became a stage for unwelcome intrusions. The photographic
quality of the memories startled him most: during quiet cruise altitude, a clear image of his
captor’s face would appear in his mind, as sharp as a printed photograph, and he would have to shake
his head to make it vanish. Worse were the smells. Sometimes, without warning, the sour odor of
stale sweat and diesel fuel from that warehouse would saturate the recycled cabin air, and he would
feel such overwhelming emotion that he had to grip the yoke and close his eyes for a moment.

His triggers emerged gradually and then took hold. Crowded places—like the boarding gate during a
delayed flight—became unbearable. The press of bodies, the noise, the lack of escape: it all made
him feel that something terrible was about to happen. He found himself watching every passenger who
was loud or defiant; if someone argued with a flight attendant or shoved a bag into an overhead bin,
Manaia would feel his pulse spike, and a flood of memory would come—usually the moment when his
torturers began shouting. The sound of crying, especially a child crying in the terminal, would yank
him straight back to his own sobs during the waterboarding, and he would have to suppress a shudder.

In response, Manaia’s threat-monitoring behaviors intensified. At home he checked the front door and
sliding glass windows three times before bed, sometimes returning to the door after he had already
lain down. In the cockpit, he studied his co-pilot’s hands and facial expressions for any sign of
aggression, noting subtle shifts in posture that might indicate hostility. During the long, silent
stretches of night flights over the Pacific, the absence of chatter felt ominous, and he would find
himself scanning the darkness outside the window for nonexistent threats. He had come to believe
that danger could spring from anywhere, and that only constant vigilance kept him safe.

These habits, however, made his triggers worse. The more he watched others for threats, the more he
noticed small misbehaviors—a passenger who stood too abruptly, a gate agent who raised her voice—and
each one triggered a fresh memory or a new wave of fear. The memory of the torture, in turn, made
him feel endangered in his own skin. After a vivid olfactory flashback during a pre-flight walk-
around, he would spend the next hour feeling that the danger was imminent, scanning the tarmac for
shadowy figures. That hypervigilance then led him to avoid any situation where crying or crowds
might occur. He started taking circuitous routes through airports, hiding in crew lounges until the
last call, and asking for seat assignments in the farthest corners of the cabin.

The cycle tightened. A crowded terminal triggered a photographic memory of the warehouse door
closing. That memory flooded him with fear, which made him scan the crowd for potential attackers.
In doing so, he inevitably spotted someone misbehaving—a man shouting into his phone, a woman crying
at the gate—and the trigger became stronger. His marriage suffered: Elena noticed he was distant,
irritable, and would flinch when she touched his shoulder from behind. He stopped going to family
gatherings because the noise and chaos felt unsafe. What brought him to a therapist was a near-miss
in the cockpit: during a memory surge, he misread an altitude call, and the co-pilot corrected him
sharply. Manaia knew he could not keep flying like this. He was terrified of making a fatal mistake,
but he was equally terrified of facing the memories directly. He hoped the therapist could extract
the images from his mind without having to talk about every detail, though a part of him already
knew that wasn’t possible.
