Karim, a forty‑one‑year‑old Middle Eastern man who works the evening shift at a fast‑food restaurant
in a busy strip mall, had been stable enough before the robbery. One Thursday night, just after the
dinner rush, two men came in with a shotgun and a knife. Karim was wiping the counter near the fry
station when one of them yelled at the cashier, then struck her across the face with the stock of
the shotgun. The sound—a flat, wet crack—seemed to stop time. Karim froze. He watched the cashier
crumple, blood spilling from her nose onto the tile, and he did nothing. He did not scream, did not
run, did not try to shield her. That moment, and the helplessness that filled him, began to rewrite
the world he lived in.

In the months that followed, his body started to react before his mind could catch up. When his
manager corrected him—even gently, even about something minor like a cold order of fries—Karim’s
chest tightened, his palms grew damp, and he felt a surge of dread that was far out of proportion to
the comment. The criticism, he came to believe, meant he had done something dangerously wrong, that
he was about to be blamed publicly, just as he had silently blamed himself for not acting during the
robbery. That same wave of alarm washed over him every time the clock neared 8:15 PM, the
approximate moment the men had entered—the exact time of day that now felt poisoned.

Loud noises were the worst. The slam of the walk‑in cooler door, the sudden clatter of a dropped
metal tray, the hiss of the fryer when a basket was lowered—each of these made him jump, his head
snapping around, his eyes scanning the dining area for a threat he was certain was there. He had
become hypervigilant to sound, listening for the wrong note in the ambient noise, always parsing
what his ears caught for signs of impending danger. What frightened Karim most was not the noise
itself but what it seemed to promise—that something bad was about to happen, that he would be caught
helpless again.

Because of this, he had come to believe that safety existed in only a very few places: his
apartment, with the door locked and the blinds drawn, and the driver’s seat of his old sedan, parked
in the same spot in the lot every night. Outside those two zones, the world felt unpredictable and
menacing. When a customer’s order took longer than expected—a delay of just a few minutes—Karim’s
mind immediately jumped to catastrophe: the manager must be angry, someone was going to shout, the
police would be called. That knot of anticipation in his stomach made it almost impossible to
concentrate on the next order. Even at work, where he spent eight hours a night, he never felt truly
safe; the kitchen was a place of constant sharp sounds and sudden demands, and the only way he could
function was to keep his back to a wall and his hands moving mechanically, trying not to think about
what could happen.

The pattern tightened over weeks. A piece of criticism from the manager triggered a spike of fear
that drove him to retreat even further into his safe routes. He stopped taking breaks in the break
room because someone might speak to him there. He stopped acknowledging coworkers who called his
name, afraid of being corrected or singled out. One evening, after a loud bang from the dishwasher
made him drop a tray of burgers, his manager told him to “get it together.” That remark—so
ordinary—sent him into a full‑blown panic in the back hallway, where he stood breathing shallowly,
convinced he was about to be fired, or worse, that the men from the robbery had returned in a
different form. He had become certain that his job, the only thing that kept him afloat, was fragile
and could be taken away at any moment.

What finally brought Karim to the clinic was a morning a week ago, when he woke already exhausted
from a night of vigilance and realized he could not keep doing this. He had missed his mother’s
phone call for the third time because he could not tolerate the sound of the ringtone. He had
stopped going to the grocery store after 7 PM. He was shrinking his life to fit inside his fear, and
he still could not outrun the triggers. He wanted help, but he was terrified that talking about the
robbery would bring it back into the room, and he was not sure he could survive that again.
