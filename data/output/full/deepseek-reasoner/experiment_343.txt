Amina, a thirty-eight-year-old fisherman who works the waters off the Gulf Coast, still remembers
the phone call that changed everything. She was out on her boat, hauling nets with her crew, when
her sister-in-law radioed that her husband, Malik, had been rushed to the hospital after a head-on
collision on the coastal highway. Another driver had crossed the median. Malik survived but with
multiple fractures and a long recovery. From the moment Amina heard the news, she could not shake
the thought that she should have been able to prevent it — that if she had been with him that
morning, she could have somehow stopped the crash. She had not been there; she had been at sea,
doing her job. Yet in the months that followed, the guilt only deepened. *I will never forgive
myself for not doing more*, she would think, replaying the same “if only” scenarios: if only she had
asked him to stay home, if only she had taken the car herself. This **prevention rumination** became
a nightly ritual. She would lie awake until two or three in the morning, turning over details of the
accident she had never even witnessed, and then **delay sleep** further by scrolling through home-
shopping apps, making small purchases she did not need — a new cooler, a set of tackle boxes,
expensive rain gear. The **compulsive spending** was a way to drown out the voice that told her, *I
should have protected him, and I failed.*

at her post on the boat, her heart pounding and her palms slick with sweat — **strong physical
reactions** that embarrassed her in front of her deckhands. The date itself became a trigger, but so
did any **discussion about car accidents**. If someone at the marina mentioned a fender bender or a
news report of a fatal crash, Amina would feel a surge of **irritability**, snapping at them to
change the subject or walking away without explanation. She came to believe that her own **self-
blame** was the reason she could not let the accident go — that the event had happened *because of
the way I acted*, because she had chosen to work that day instead of staying home. That belief fed
the **negative emotions** — guilt, shame, and a low-level anger — that simmered beneath every
interaction. When she felt that anger rising, she would often turn to her phone and buy something
else, a quick dopamine hit that promised a moment’s reprieve.

Because she spent so many late nights scrolling and buying, Amina was chronically exhausted. Her
fatigue made her even more reactive to reminders of the crash. A passing car with a dented bumper
could stop her in her tracks, triggering a flash of the image she had built in her mind — **cars hit
by accident** — even though she had never seen Malik’s actual wreck. These **intrusive images**
would then push her back into rumination, searching for ways she could have been there to intervene.

staying awake; the strategies deepened her shame and her sense of failure; and that shame made her
hypervigilant to any possible reminder of the accident.

Amina’s work environment became a minefield. At the docks, other fishermen often swapped stories of

**avoiding the marina coffee shop** entirely, but that meant missing safety briefings and losing
touch with her regular buyers — a concrete occupational consequence that hurt her income. At home,
her relationship with Malik, now mostly recovered, grew strained. He wanted to talk about the past;
she could not. She would stay up late to avoid being in bed with him, and then spend money online

strategies were only tightening the loop — that the more she dwelled on prevention, the more she
spent, the more sleep she lost, the more vivid the triggers became. She wanted to stop, but she did

would always feel like the one who failed. But by the time she sat in the intake room, she was also
hoping, for the first time, that she could learn to live with the memory without it controlling her
completely.
