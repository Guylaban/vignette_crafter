take leave. What emerged was not a single event but a prolonged and embedded trauma. When he was

neighborhood that had, over the preceding year, fallen under the control of a local cartel. One
evening, without warning, sustained automatic gunfire erupted three blocks away, followed by
shouting, the sounds of running, and then a heavy silence that lasted hours. Mateo and his
grandmother lay on the floor of a back bedroom, and through a gap in the curtain he saw a neighbor,
a man he had known since childhood, lying motionless in the street. They remained in that room until
dawn, and for the rest of the summer Mateo did not leave the house.

After returning to the United States, Mateo found that his body had not returned with him. He
remained constantly tense, his shoulders locked and his jaw clenched, and he noticed that his heart
would pound at sounds that other people did not even register — a car door slamming, a chair
scraping the floor, footsteps approaching too quickly. In class, and later in his first job as an
associate product manager, he sat with his back to the wall and monitored the room without intending
to. What frightened him most was the way unexpected movement close to him would cause him to flinch
so violently that others noticed, which embarrassed him and made him feel broken.

As the months passed, these physical states began to drive a set of behaviors that Mateo did not at
first recognize as connected. When the tension in his body became unbearable — which happened daily,
especially after a meeting in which a colleague had walked suddenly into his peripheral vision — he
would go to the bathroom and press his fingernails into his forearms until the physical sting
momentarily overrode the alarm in his chest. He had come to believe that if he did not release the
pressure somehow, something catastrophic would happen: his body would simply break. At the same
time, he found himself asking his roommate repeatedly whether he seemed okay, whether his face
looked normal, whether his breathing was fast. He would check his own pulse throughout the day and
examine his arms for scratches or bruises he might not have noticed, certain that any mark could be
a sign of an injury he had failed to detect while on guard.

The problem, as Mateo described it with growing frustration, was that these strategies only seemed
to make things worse. The more he checked his body, the more he noticed small sensations — a twitch,
a throb, a flutter — and interpreted them as threats he needed to monitor. The more he asked his
roommate for reassurance, the more he worried that his need to ask proved something was wrong. And
the self-harm, though it brought a fleeting release, left him ashamed and more vigilant afterward,
scanning his own skin for evidence of what he had done. By the time he sought help, his days had
become a closed loop: his body stayed on alert, which drove him to strategies that fed the very
vigilance he was trying to escape.

He could no longer sit through a full day at the office without needing to leave the floor at least
twice, and his manager had noticed that he flinched during a team standup when a coworker reached
past him for a marker. Mateo was referred to the employee assistance program, and from there to
trauma-focused therapy. What he hoped for, he said, was simply to stop checking — to stop feeling
like something was about to happen, to stop needing to hurt himself to feel still. But he was also
afraid that if he stopped monitoring, he would miss the thing that was coming.
