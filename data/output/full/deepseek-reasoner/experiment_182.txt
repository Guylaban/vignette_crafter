Kiet is a 42-year-old Hmong farmer who lives with his wife and their surviving son on a small plot
in California’s Central Valley. Eighteen months ago, his younger son, Bao, then five, was diagnosed
with acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Bao spent four months in the pediatric oncology unit, and Kiet
was with him every night—sleeping on a vinyl chair, listening to the beeping monitors, watching the
IV line snake into Bao’s small arm. The moment that lodged itself deepest was the night of the first
sepsis crisis: Bao’s skin went grey, his eyes rolled back, and for two hours Kiet held him while the
doctors worked. Bao survived, but Kiet did not.

In the months after the discharge, Kiet began to notice that certain things would send him straight
back to that hospital room. At first it was the sight of a child’s thin arm with a bandage or a
bruise—he would freeze, his heart hammering, the smell of antiseptic flooding his nose. Then he
realized that any darkness, especially waking up at two in the morning to an unlit bedroom, would
bring the memory surging back with such force that he could feel Bao’s weight against his chest
again. And there was a song—a lullaby his wife used to hum, *Sleep, My Baby*—that played once on the
radio in his truck and left him shaking in the parking lot of the feed store, the tears so sudden he
had to pull over. He learned to avoid the radio altogether.

The memories themselves were not like normal remembering. They came as fragments—the silver gleam of
a suture needle, the pale curve of Bao’s knee—and they did not feel past. When a memory arrived,
Kiet was no longer in the present; he was back in that chair, the terror fresh. The fragments
emerged without warning, triggered by a shadow, a sound, a stray patch of darkness outside the
window. And the more the memories came, the more Kiet’s brain seemed to scan for the things that
could set them off. He started noticing the dark more often—checking the corners of the barn as the
sun went down, his stomach tightening before he even knew why.

Kiet’s strategies for coping grew slowly, like a habit he did not choose. He began avoiding people:
his wife’s questions, the neighbour who wanted to talk about his own son’s soccer game, the farmhand
who hummed something tunelessly—all of it felt unbearable. He would work alone in the farthest
field, or find an excuse to leave if company came. But the hardest part was sleep. Going to bed
meant lying in the dark, and the dark meant the memory. So Kiet started staying up until one or two
in the morning, watching late-night TV with the volume low, or walking the farmyard until his legs
ached. He avoided lying down altogether until he was so exhausted that he would collapse on the
couch. But even then, the dark would find him. The avoidance looped back on itself: because he
stayed awake, he was more likely to be in the dark at odd hours, his senses sharpened, waiting for a
fragment to hit. And because the memories were so vivid, he was more determined the next night not
to go to bed. His wife slept alone now, and he could feel the distance growing between them.

What brought Kiet to a therapist was not the avoidance itself but a morning when his older son asked
him why he never looked at Bao anymore. Kiet realized he had been avoiding his surviving son’s body,

that hospital arm. He was exhausted, hollowed out, and frightened by what his mind was doing. He was
hoping, without quite believing it, that talking could somehow unhook the dark from the memory, that
there was a way to sleep again without waking up in a room that was not the present.
