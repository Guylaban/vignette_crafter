Mai is a sixty-year-old Vietnamese American woman who works as a call center agent, answering
customer inquiries from a small cubicle in a busy office. Two years ago, her only daughter, a
thirty-year-old teacher eight months pregnant, was killed instantly when a distracted driver ran a
red light. Mai’s husband found her that evening on the kitchen floor, clutching the ultrasound photo
of their unborn grandchild. Since that day, Mai has lived inside a loop she cannot name, let alone
break.

The memory of the phone call from the hospital arrives in sudden, vivid fragments—the sound of the
officer’s voice, the way the kitchen light flickered—but always stops short of the moment her
husband opened the door. She pushes the memory away before it can finish, yet the pieces that do
surface, like the ultrasound photo or the baby’s room they had started painting, bring a rush of
hot, suffocating fear and a flashback so real she can smell the hospital antiseptic. This surge of
threat then makes her even more determined to block the memory, creating a cycle where every
incomplete recall fuels more dread, and every wave of fear drives the memory further into fragments.

Because of this pattern, everyday stimuli become unbearable triggers. The cry of a baby in a grocery
store instantly pulls Mai into a dissociative state, the sound merging with the imagined wail of the
grandchild she never held. She now avoids funerals, cemeteries, and any grief-related gatherings,
convinced that one more image of a grave will break her entirely. Her partial, jagged memory has
made her hypersensitive: a glimpse of a pregnant woman on a bus, a lullaby from a passing car—each
one is a landmine. The threat she carries inside her has also sharpened her vigilance; she
constantly scans her environment for anything that could remind her of the loss, and this scanning
itself makes triggers more frequent and more intense.

To manage, Mai has adopted a series of avoidance strategies that have gradually consumed her life.
She refuses to talk about the accident with anyone, not even her husband, because the words feel
like reopening a wound. She has stopped seeing friends she used to socialize with before the event,
canceling dinner invitations and ignoring calls until they stopped coming. She avoids all reminders
of her daughter—the clothes, the photos, the half-painted nursery—by keeping that door in their home
permanently closed. These strategies, however, have only worsened her memory: by never speaking of
the event, by never allowing herself to fully recall it, the memory remains fragmented, popping up
without warning and disappearing before she can make sense of it. And the more she avoids, the more
any unanticipated reminder—a colleague mentioning their daughter’s graduation, a customer with a
crying baby in the background of a call—feels like an ambush, making her heart race and her throat
close.

At work, Mai has grown brittle. She takes extended bathroom breaks when a caller’s infant fusses in
the background, and she has asked to be moved away from a coworker who keeps a framed photo of her
grandchildren on her desk. Her sleep is broken: most nights she lies awake, the incomplete fragments
of that phone call replaying on a loop, until sheer exhaustion pulls her under. The strong, churning
guilt—should she have been with her daughter that day?—twists into anger at the driver, at herself,
at the world that kept spinning.

What finally brought Mai to seek help was a moment last week when a baby on her call cried, and Mai
found herself standing in the break room, gasping, having no memory of walking there. Her husband,
who had been watching her deteriorate, gently suggested therapy. Mai is terrified of what a full
memory might do to her, but she is equally afraid of living the rest of her life in this half-
existence, where only fragments remain and every piece cuts. She hopes, quietly, that maybe a
therapist can help her stop pushing it all away—without drowning in the grief she has been running
from for two years.
