Nadia is a 69-year-old Middle Eastern woman who works the late shift at a fast-food restaurant, a
job she took after retiring from years as a school secretary. She married young, raised three
children, and always thought of herself as steady and capable. That changed two years ago, when a
man began following her home from work. At first it was just a car that lingered too long at the
parking lot exit, then notes left on her windshield, then late-night phone calls where he said her
name and described what she was wearing. The police were called but could not identify him, and
after six months the stalking stopped without explanation. But what happened left Nadia permanently
altered.

Now she wakes several times each night, heart pounding, straining to hear footsteps or the creak of
a door. She has to get up and check every lock — front door, back door, windows — before she can
fall back into a shallow sleep. At work, she catches herself studying customers' faces and body
language, scanning for anyone who watches her too long or stands too close. She has come to believe
that people cannot be trusted, that anyone might be a threat, and this certainty drives her to keep
her distance from coworkers and strangers alike. When a customer smiles at her, she feels a twist in
her stomach: *What do they want?* She avoids thinking about the stalking directly because she is
convinced that if she lets herself dwell on it, she will fall apart — this belief makes her even
more alert to every possible danger, as though vigilance is the only thing holding her together.

The memory of the stalking arrives in strange, fragmented ways. Sometimes, when she walks past a
certain fast-food chain, she smells stale cigarette smoke and the faint odor of motor oil — smells
that were present on the night she found a torn photograph of herself taped to her car door. That
olfactory flashback hits her like a punch, and she immediately starts scanning for escape routes. At
other times, when she tries to recall the details, she feels nothing — she can describe the events
but feels completely numb, as if she is narrating someone else's life. That emotional flatness
frightens her even more than the fear did, because it makes the whole experience feel unreal, like a
story that could not have happened to *her*. The more the memory feels unreal, the more she
questions who she is now: she used to be a person who trusted her own mind, but now she does not
even recognize herself. That sense of identity loss, in turn, makes the memories feel even more
foreign and disconnected, as if they belong to a stranger.

The constant checking — doors, faces, sounds — feeds her belief that she is no longer the person she
was. She used to leave her back door unlocked while gardening; now she triple-checks it before bed.
She used to greet customers warmly; now she watches their hands. Each act of checking confirms that
danger is real, that she cannot let her guard down, and that her old self is gone. She has stopped
visiting her sister’s house because she cannot stand the unlocked bathroom window. Her husband tries
to reassure her, but she has grown quiet, mistrustful even of his patience. Last month, she nearly
quit her job after a customer’s jacket smelled like motor oil; she hid in the break room for twenty
minutes.

What finally brought Nadia to a clinic was a morning when she caught herself circling the house
three times, checking the same door locks in a loop, and realized she had no memory of eating
breakfast or talking to her husband. She was exhausted, ashamed, and afraid that she was losing her
mind. She hopes therapy can help her sleep without checking. But underneath that hope is a deeper
terror: that if she stops being vigilant, the man will come back — or that the numbness will swallow
her completely, and she will never feel like herself again.
