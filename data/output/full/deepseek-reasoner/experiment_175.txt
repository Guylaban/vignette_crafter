Raj Singh is a 61‑year‑old former paramedic who lives with his wife in a quiet Chicago suburb. Two
years ago, he responded to a chemical leak at a plastics factory. Following the building’s safety
officer into a dark storage room, Raj was hit by a sweet, alcoholic smell that turned out to be
concentrated isopropyl alcohol fumes. Within seconds he felt his throat close, his vision blur, and
a terror he had never known in thirty years of emergency work—a certainty that he was going to die
there, alone in the dark. His crew pulled him out, but the damage was done: a chemical pneumonitis
that kept him in the hospital for a week and left him with a chronic, rattling cough.

Since then, Raj’s life has been governed by the memory of those few seconds. A dimly lit hallway in
his own home or the faint odor of hand sanitizer in a grocery store can instantly bring back the
full sensory assault—the pressure in his lungs, the smell of alcohol, the blind panic—without any
warning. When the memory returns, he feels so overwhelmed that he has to stop whatever he is doing,
sometimes sinking to the floor until the terror subsides. Because the event itself remains so raw,
Raj has come to see danger everywhere: the ambulance bay where he used to work, his dark garage at
night, even a neighbor’s barbecue where someone is drinking beer. The triggers multiply, and each
one reconnects him to that moment.

To manage the onslaught, Raj developed a set of habits that have now become their own problem. He
constantly replays the incident in his mind, working through every detail of what went wrong—why the
safety officer hadn’t checked the air, why he himself hadn’t insisted on a respirator. This
rumination about prevention gives him a fleeting sense of control, but it also keeps the memory
fresh and vivid, so that the next trigger hits even harder. He also avoids being alone. He keeps
himself constantly surrounded by people—his wife, his adult children, old colleagues he calls on the
phone—so that he never has a quiet moment to be caught by the memory. Yet the constant socializing
means he never truly processes the trauma, and the memories ambush him in the middle of
conversations, forcing him to excuse himself in a sweat. Most crucially, Raj avoids all medical
cues: hospitals, ambulances, even routine physical examinations. He refused his own follow‑up
pulmonary appointments, and when his wife urged him to see a doctor for chest pain, he said no
because the waiting room smelled like antiseptic. This avoidance has reinforced his fear: every time
he steers clear of a clinic, he proves to himself that those places are dangerous, and he becomes
even more vigilant for the next trigger. In his daily life, he now scans every room for dark corners
and every person for the smell of alcohol, and each scan itself can pull the memory back.

The cycle tightened over months. Raj’s avoidance of his workplace meant he couldn’t return to the
job he loved—he is now on long‑term disability, and the financial strain has added to his wife’s
frustration. She has grown tired of his constant need for company and his refusal to seek medical
help, and she told him last month that she could not keep living this way. What finally brought Raj
to a therapist was the recognition that his own strategies were making things worse: the more he
avoided, the more triggers he noticed; the more he ruminated, the more frequently the memory
returned. He is afraid that if he ever lets himself be truly alone, he will lose control and the
terror will never stop. But he also hopes, dimly, that there might be a way to live without this
constant watching.
