Thong Nguyen was sixty-eight years old when he finally sat in a therapist’s office, five years after
his wife Lan was killed in the carjacking. They had just parked in their own driveway when a young
man appeared at Lan’s window. Thong saw the gun, heard the single shot, and then watched his wife
slump forward. In the weeks and months that followed, the memory never came as a whole story; it
arrived in shards—the glint of the barrel, the sound of the bullet, the weight of her body as he
tried to pull her upright. When these fragments surfaced, he often heard the gunshot ringing in his
head, and a grief so vast it would press him into his chair until his daughter called his name. He
had come to believe that if he had switched seats with her, she would still be alive.

Those memory fragments did not stay in the past. They made him feel that danger was still right
there, even in his own living room. He began checking the front door lock three, four, sometimes a
dozen times before he could settle into a chair. His heart would hammer at a car backfiring or a
neighbor’s knock—false alarms that left him trembling with fear and a gnawing shame. The more the
fragmented memories broke through, the more convinced he became that dangerous people were
everywhere, and that he had to stay ready. At the courthouse, where he had worked as a public
defender for forty years, he now scanned every visitor’s face for hidden threat, avoiding eye
contact with men who wore hoodies or kept their hands in their pockets.

Feeling this constantly on edge exhausted him, and Thong had developed his own ways of coping. He
would stop at a fast‑food place on the way home and eat an extra burger and fries, not because he
was hungry but because the chewing and tasting blunted the sharp edges of the day. He stopped
watching the evening news; crime dramas and even courtroom movies were out of the question because
they could trigger a memory fragment that would leave him staring at the wall. Yet these strategies
sometimes backfired. When he overate and then felt sluggish, he would become even more vigilant,
convinced that his dulled attention made him an easy target. And when he avoided a television show,
he would later find himself hypervigilant in a quiet room, jumping at the creak of a floorboard—a
false alarm that dragged the memory of the gunshot back into his mind with full force.

His hypervigilance toward people often created a self‑reinforcing cycle: by constantly scanning for
threats, he began to see danger in harmless strangers, which in turn heightened his sense of an
ever‑present menace. That increased threat feeling then pulled up more memory fragments—the smell of
gunpowder, the wet sound of her breath stopping. The more he tried to push the memories away with
overeating or avoidance, the more they seemed to intrude anyway, each fragment sharpened by the
guilt and grief that were now inseparable from the event itself. He had lost count of how many
evenings he spent pacing the house, checking the locks again, then sitting down to a meal he did not
taste, all while the memory of her head turning toward him before the shot played on a loop in his
mind.

fear and his anger at the man who shot Lan are poisoning the years he has left, and he hopes—though

him to feel something other than vigilance and grief.
