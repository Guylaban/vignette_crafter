period of civil conflict when she was a young radiologist in her early thirties. She remembered the
day vividly, or rather, she remembered the cacophony and the visceral terror: the sudden, earth-
shattering impact of a mortar shell striking the hospital wing where she worked, the screams, the
dust, the scent of blood and burning. She had been trapped for hours amidst the chaos, witnessing
unspeakable injuries and the desperate struggle for survival. The experience left her with a
haunting legacy; when she tried to deliberately remember what happened, she found it incredibly

to put the frightening event into words – it remained a collection of fragmented images, sounds, and
sensations – began to subtly reshape Eleanor’s life. The sheer effort of trying to recall, coupled
with an intense shame on recall whenever a fragment did surface, led her to avoid anything that
could cause negative or positive feelings. She found herself retreating, not just from painful
memories, but from the full spectrum of emotional experience, believing that to feel too deeply was
to risk a return to that raw, exposed vulnerability. This emotional avoidance, in turn, began to
manifest as a habit of spending long hours on her phone, playing games, or browsing the internet, a
form of screen escape she used specifically to avoid thinking about anything too challenging or
upsetting.

sense of the world’s inherent danger. She started to constantly scan her surroundings for signs of

radiology department, she’d find herself subtly checking exits, assessing new faces, and positioning
herself with her back to a wall. This hypervigilance meant that when she encountered unfamiliar
places, she would avoid going to them entirely, convinced she couldn’t properly assess the danger.
The feeling of vulnerability was too overwhelming, and she had come to believe that any new
environment harboured unseen threats.

posed a risk, further complicated her relationship with her own past. The persistent high alert made
it even harder for her to relax her guard enough to consciously process the trauma. When she did try

stomach, a physical manifestation of her vigilance, which would tighten whenever a fleeting image of
the hospital attack crossed her mind.

Over time, Eleanor’s maladaptive strategies solidified into entrenched patterns. Her avoidance of
all feelings and her reliance on screen escape actively prevented her from engaging with or
integrating the traumatic event. This meant the memory continued to be hard to recall deliberately,
and she still couldn't seem to put the frightening event into words, maintaining its power over her.

but Eleanor’s engagement with her tablet was a deliberate shield, a way to keep her mind from
wandering to the shadows of her past, making it difficult for them to make any plans for the future
together.

These same maladaptive strategies also reinforced her perception of threat. By avoiding feelings and
retreating into her digital world, Eleanor inadvertently cut herself off from experiences that might
have challenged her belief that the world was dangerous. She avoided going to unfamiliar places, and
this lack of exposure meant she never had the opportunity to disconfirm her fears. Consequently, she
still felt threatened by strangers approaching her, even in ordinary situations like grocery
shopping or meeting new patients at the clinic. The cycle had tightened around her, making her world
smaller and her internal landscape more fraught.

keep her memories at bay. David had gently suggested she seek help, noticing her increasing
isolation and the way she’d flinch at sudden noises. What frightened her most was the thought that

connect with the future she still, deep down, longed to share with her husband. She hoped, with a
fragile optimism, that it wasn’t too late to finally understand and integrate the event that had
shaped so much of her life.
