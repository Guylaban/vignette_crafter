Michael, a 45-year-old journalist, returned from an assignment abroad two years ago, forever altered
by the three weeks he spent in captivity. He had been captured while reporting on a conflict zone,
held in a cramped, dark room. He still vividly remembered the rough burlap sack placed over his
head, tightening around his neck, and the repeated physical shoves and prods from his captors. The
acrid smell of cheap alcohol, often on their breath during interrogations, had become indelibly
linked to terror.

Upon his return, Michael tried to resume his life with his wife, Sarah, but the world felt
different. His memories of the frightening event were like a film that played over and over in his
mind, often against his will. These memories forced their way into his mind when he did not want
them to, bringing back the same terror he had felt at the time. He would find himself back in that
room, the pressure on his neck, the intrusive touch, the suffocating smell, even when he was sitting
safely at his kitchen table.

This constant replay of terror meant that even the slightest reminder could send him spiraling. A
new ache in his neck, a familiar stiffness from long hours at his desk editing articles, would
instantly trigger a full-blown memory replay, overwhelming him with the sensation of the burlap
sack. Similarly, the innocent physical touch from Sarah, a comforting hand on his arm, now often
triggered a surge of panic, his mind instantly recalling the rough hands of his captors. The smell
of alcohol, even from a passerby on the street or a colleague after work, would instantly transport
him back, his breath catching in his throat.

Michael found himself living in a state of perpetual readiness for danger. He was on high alert when
he heard unexpected or loud sounds, his body tensing, his gaze darting around the newsroom or his
home. This constant hypervigilance meant he was always scanning his environment, looking for threats
that weren't there. He often reacted as if he were in danger even when nothing was actually wrong,
mistaking a dropped pen for an explosion or a car backfiring for a gunshot. This heightened state of
threat response, in turn, made his memory even more accessible and intrusive. When he was superalert
and watchful, his mind seemed to be primed to recall the most dangerous moments of his captivity,
making the "film" of his trauma even more prone to playing, intensifying the terror he felt.

His hypervigilance also intensified his reactions to potential triggers. Because he was always on
guard, his senses were acutely tuned to anything that might signal danger. This meant that the
subtle scent of alcohol from a colleague's breath in the office, or a casual touch from his wife, or
even a sudden neck pain, were not just reminders but felt like immediate threats, triggering intense
physiological and emotional responses. He began to avoid social gatherings where alcohol might be
present, making excuses to Sarah, who grew increasingly concerned by his withdrawal and reluctance
for physical intimacy. His work as a journalist, which once required him to be out in the field,
became almost impossible. He struggled to focus, constantly distracted by perceived threats, and
found himself declining assignments that required travel or interaction in unfamiliar settings,
impacting his career trajectory.

The cumulative effect of these experiences had become unbearable. Michael's constant state of high
alert, fueled by the relentless replay of his traumatic memories, left him exhausted and isolated.
He knew he couldn't continue this way, living a life dictated by fear and avoidance. The terror on
recall, the false alarms, and the ever-present triggers had created a suffocating cycle that felt
inescapable. He sought help, hoping to finally break free from the invisible chains of his past, but
deeply afraid that the terror was now an inextricable part of him.
