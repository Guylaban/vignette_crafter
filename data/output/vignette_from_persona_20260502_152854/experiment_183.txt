Sarah, a 48-year-old social ministry worker, remembers the accident with chilling clarity. It had
been a multi-car pile-up on a congested highway, a sudden screech of tires, then the jarring impact
that crumpled her vehicle and left her trapped, her body twisted and in searing pain. She had been
utterly unable to move, unable to help herself, experiencing a profound loss of autonomy as rescuers
worked to extract her. The feeling of being physically unwell, the acute pain and nausea, had been
overwhelming, and the crash imagery of twisted metal and shattered glass was seared into her mind.
The experience left her with a deep-seated belief that she could no longer trust her own body; it
had failed her, and she was certain it would again.

In the months that followed, Sarah found herself increasingly isolated. The accident had instilled
in her a pervasive sense of injustice, a conviction that what happened to her was deeply unfair and
nothing had been done to truly rectify it. This appraisal made her acutely sensitive to any
situation where she felt even a minor loss of autonomy, transforming routine requests at her
ministry into sources of disproportionate anxiety. She also harboured a profound sense of
helplessness, believing she simply couldn't stop bad things from happening to her, a thought that
became a constant shadow. This deep-seated conviction made her unable to trust safety, even when
others would assure her she was out of harm's way. This pervasive feeling of vulnerability fueled
her chronic threat monitoring; she now found herself scanning for exits as soon as she entered any
room or building, a habit she couldn't shake.

The persistent crash imagery, particularly of cars hitting each other, made the thought of driving
unbearable. Consequently, these potent triggers directly led her to avoid driving altogether, first
as a passenger, then as a driver, severely impacting her ability to carry out her duties as a social
ministry worker, which often required home visits. Her belief that her body was unreliable also
contributed to her maladaptive strategies, causing her to avoid physical stress and any situation
that might put her body under strain, even simple exercises. Furthermore, her pervasive sense of
helplessness reinforced her avoidance of driving and also made her avoid relaxation, as she felt
letting her guard down would leave her vulnerable to further harm.

Even minor instances of feeling physically unwell, such as a sudden twinge or headache, would
immediately activate her threat system. These internal triggers would send her into a state of
hypervigilance, causing her to jump or startle at ordinary sounds that wouldn't bother most people,
and further intensified her need to scan for exits. This constant state of threat, characterized by
her hyper-alertness and preoccupation with scanning for exits, made relaxation feel not just
difficult, but dangerous, solidifying her avoidance of relaxation. Paradoxically, her decision to
avoid driving and other situations that put her body under stress, while intended to keep her safe,
only reinforced her inability to trust safety, making her feel even more vulnerable in the long run.
The very act of avoiding driving meant that when she did encounter crash imagery, perhaps on
television or from a distant siren, the trigger felt even more potent and overwhelming, as she had
fewer opportunities to habituate. This cycle tightened, with her maladaptive strategies, like her
general social withdrawal, deepening her chronic threat responses, making her even more prone to
startling and scanning.

Sarah's once fulfilling career began to fray. She relied heavily on colleagues to ferry her to
client appointments, or navigated public transport with white-knuckled dread, her constant scanning
for exits making every journey exhausting. The profound sense of injustice lingered, making her feel
isolated and misunderstood. She came to therapy because she could no longer bear the tightening grip
of her fear, desperate to find a way to trust her body and the world again, and reclaim the autonomy
she felt had been stolen from her.
