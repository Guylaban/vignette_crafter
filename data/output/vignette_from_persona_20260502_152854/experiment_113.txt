demeanor. His life had been irrevocably altered five years prior when a Category 5 cyclone,
unprecedented in its ferocity, ripped through his coastal community in the dead of night. He

failed. The sudden, absolute darkness plunged them into chaos as the storm surge tore through their
home. The memory of the event was so extreme it often felt unreal to Koa, difficult to believe it
had actually happened to him. Yet, when the memories did intrude, they were intensely sensory; he
could still experience a vivid olfactory recall of the damp, salty air mixed with splintered wood
and decay, and sometimes even taste the metallic fear and seawater he had tasted during the event.

The loss of his wife and the sheer destructive power of the cyclone left Koa with a profound and
unshakeable belief that he couldn't stop bad things from happening to him, a pervasive sense of
helplessness that now colored every aspect of his life. This core conviction, coupled with the
equally strong appraisal that the world was an inherently dangerous place, meant Koa’s internal
alarm system was perpetually activated. Indeed, his deep conviction that the world was dangerous
made him constantly vigilant for signs of peril, a state of hyper-arousal that was exhausting. He
felt dead inside, an emotional numbness that had settled over him like a shroud, making it difficult
to connect with others or feel joy.

This relentless monitoring of his surroundings, fueled by his negative appraisals, made him highly
susceptible to intrusive memories. His mind would conjure fragmented images and sensations of the
storm, which in turn could instantly amplify his feeling of impending doom. These vivid
recollections often served to prime his system, making him even more reactive to his established
triggers. For instance, the simple act of waking up in the dark at night would immediately plunge
him back into the terror of the storm. Physical locations, especially those near water or damaged

memories, as that was exactly how he felt just before the disaster struck. Each time a trigger hit,
it felt like confirmation of his belief that bad things were inevitable, reinforcing his profound
sense of helplessness. His overarching sense that the world was dangerous also made him more
susceptible to being triggered by mundane stimuli.

maladaptive strategies. He started smoking heavily, a habit he hadn't had before, using it as a
crude form of self-soothing when the anxiety became unbearable. He also began to avoid relaxing
because it felt dangerous, fearing that any moment of peace would inevitably precede another
catastrophe. This was a direct consequence of his belief that he couldn't stop bad things from
happening. His job as an auditor often required him to travel to unfamiliar places, but now he
actively avoided these assignments, convinced he couldn't adequately assess the danger in new
environments. Furthermore, he found it nearly impossible to talk with others about the event or its
aftermath, retreating into himself. These strategies, while offering fleeting relief, ultimately
reinforced his core beliefs about his helplessness and the world's danger. By avoiding situations

to cope with them. Furthermore, his avoidance behaviors prevented him from ever truly testing the
perceived dangers, thus maintaining his heightened sense of threat.

The insidious cycle tightened. At work, an unusual silence in the office would make him feel
something dangerous was about to happen, and if a report took longer than expected to arrive, he
would immediately assume something bad was occurring. His constant vigilance, born from his belief
that the world was dangerous, kept him in a state of high alert, and this heightened state easily
gave way to intrusive memories, further fueling his sense of threat. The cumulative effect of these
cycles had become unbearable. Koa sought help because the emotional numbness had grown so profound
that he feared he was losing himself entirely, perpetually trapped in a world where danger lurked in
every shadow and happiness felt like a prelude to disaster.
