David, a 58-year-old African American man, had always prided himself on his steady hands and keen
eye, skills that had served him well for decades as a beloved hairdresser in his vibrant
neighborhood salon. Married for over thirty years, he was known for his calm demeanor and warm
banter. However, six months ago, his world fractured. Walking home late one evening after a long
shift, a man reeking of cheap alcohol accosted him in an alleyway. The assailant was verbally
aggressive, sneering, and harshly critical, accusing David of being "useless" as he violently
snatched his wallet. The stark visual memories of the assailant’s face, contorted in anger, and the
specific sounds and smells of that dark alley became indelibly seared into David's mind.

In the aftermath, David tried to push the incident away, attempting to suppress thoughts of the
trauma, believing that if he didn't dwell on it, it would eventually fade. But the harrowing scene
refused to be dismissed. Instead, the visual memories of the frightening event returned
relentlessly, playing out like a film over and over in his head. What made it worse was his
inability to articulate the full terror of it; he knew what happened, but found himself unable to
find words to describe the visceral fear and humiliation to his wife or the police. This constant,
vivid memory replay left him perpetually on edge, making him hyper-vigilant and acutely sensitive to
his surroundings. Consequently, any person acting even slightly suspiciously in his periphery would
instantly trigger a surge of fear, directly linking his internal memory struggles to external cues.

The cycle soon tightened its grip. Now, even the faint whiff of alcohol from a client’s breath in
his salon, or his wife’s gentle criticism about leaving a chore unfinished, or the fleeting sight of
someone perceived as acting suspiciously on the street, would instantly plunge David back into the
harrowing visual memories of the mugging. The scene would replay with agonizing clarity, forcing him
to relive the fear. These relentless memories, particularly their agonizing replay, inevitably led
to intense self-blame rumination. He would dwell endlessly on what he had done wrong, convinced he
should have fought back, or chosen a different route, or somehow prevented the attack.

To escape this torment, David redoubled his efforts to suppress these intrusive thoughts and
memories. He would consciously try hard not to think about the trauma, pushing the images and self-
recriminations away whenever they arose. Yet, this strategy proved counterproductive. The very act
of trying to suppress these thoughts, coupled with his heightened state of anxiety, paradoxically
made him more attuned to potential threats, transforming even minor observations into new triggers.
He found himself constantly scanning for danger, interpreting neutral actions as hostile, creating a
feedback loop where his maladaptive strategies inadvertently amplified his triggers.

The fear of being triggered, combined with his pervasive self-blame and escalating hypervigilance,
led David to increasingly avoid other people. His once bustling salon, a place he loved, became a
minefield of potential threats. He started cancelling client appointments, citing vague excuses, and
spent more time isolated at home. When a long-time client once offered mild criticism about a
haircut, David perceived it as a direct personal attack, barely managing to conceal his rising
panic. This avoidance of people, meant to protect him, only deepened his isolation and reinforced
his belief that the world was an inherently dangerous place. The more he avoided, the more potent
his triggers became when he inevitably encountered them, solidifying a self-reinforcing pattern that
felt impossible to break. His wife, witnessing the profound change in her once jovial husband,
gently urged him to seek help. David arrived in therapy exhausted, trapped by the constant replay of
the past and the fear that he was losing himself to a future he could no longer control.
