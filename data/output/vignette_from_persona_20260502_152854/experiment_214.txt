Minh, a 72-year-old married man of South Eastern Asian heritage, sought therapy reporting a
constellation of distressing symptoms that had increasingly constrained his life as a nanny. For
years, he had been haunted by the memory of a severe physical assault by a romantic partner. It had
happened late one evening in their home, the dim light of the living room doing little to cut
through the sudden, brutal shock of his partner’s unexpected violence. The attack had been swift and
unprovoked, leaving Minh disoriented and deeply traumatized, particularly by the sudden movements
and the oppressive darkness that had enveloped him.

In the aftermath, Minh found himself struggling to process what had occurred. The memories of the
event would often flash through his mind, and each time, a profound terror would return, feeling as
intense as it had been that night. What was particularly unsettling was that when these memories
came to mind, he would feel his body was in the exact same position as when the frightening event
occurred, a visceral reenactment that left him gasping for air. This terror on recall directly led
to a pervasive sense of threat; he became jumpy and easily startled, reacting with alarm to
unexpected movement nearby, even from the children he cared for during the day. He found himself
unable to relax, even when the children were asleep and he knew he was in a safe place.

The profound sense of threat, the constant feeling of being jumpy and unable to relax, had a
powerful impact on Minh's coping mechanisms. He became acutely aware that he was unable to relax
even in safe places, and this constant internal tension made him deeply wary of anything that could
be stressful. As a result, he began to actively avoid anything that could potentially raise his
stress levels, from difficult conversations with the children’s parents to watching news reports.
This persistent state of hypervigilance also reinforced a long-standing maladaptive strategy: he
became even more rigid in his resolve to never allow himself to feel or express anger, believing it
would only escalate his internal turmoil and make him more vulnerable.

The terrifying memories, often accompanied by the bodily reenactment, directly fueled his
maladaptive strategies. The sheer terror of remembering the assault made him instinctively suppress
any flicker of anger, convinced it was a dangerous emotion. He also started actively avoiding
circumstances that reminded him of the event, most notably being in the dark. Reading bedtime
stories to the children became an ordeal, as he insisted on bright lights, which sometimes confused
them. Over time, his avoidance of anything stressful, coupled with his rigid suppression of anger,
began to shape his daily life.

Paradoxically, these very attempts to cope inadvertently kept the cycle turning. His deep-seated
belief that he must never allow himself to feel or express anger, and his constant efforts to avoid
any potential stressors, seemed to prevent the memories from truly integrating. The memory began to
feel alien to him, as if it belonged to someone else rather than to him, yet it still brought back
the same terror. Furthermore, his inability to relax and his constant startle response, particularly
to the children’s sudden movements while playing, frequently triggered those vivid and terrifying
memories, making him relive the event.

The consequences of this self-reinforcing cycle began to accumulate. His wife, concerned by his
increasing withdrawal and his inability to truly rest, noticed his growing agitation and his
insistence on keeping lights on late into the night. His work as a nanny, once a source of quiet
joy, became a minefield of potential triggers. The children’s boisterous play and sudden movements
would frequently cause him to react as if he was in danger, making him tense and irritable. His
avoidance of stressful situations meant he was less able to advocate for the children's needs or
engage fully with their parents. Minh had come to therapy seeking relief, exhausted by the
relentless internal battle, and desperately hoping to find a way to escape the tightening grip of
his past that now dictated his present.
