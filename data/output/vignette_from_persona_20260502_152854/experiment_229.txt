Stefan, a 49-year-old fashion designer, once found immense joy in the vibrant chaos of his studio,
the intricate dance of fabric and form, and the meticulous planning of upcoming collections. His
world shifted irrevocably six months ago when his wife, Elara, was involved in a severe car

replays endlessly, convinced he could have, should have, driven her himself.

In the immediate aftermath, Stefan was consumed by a profound sense of horror and helplessness. When
the fragmented, overwhelming images of the crash would surface, especially the vivid flash of
twisted metal followed by a blank space where his mind refused to go, he found himself desperately
trying to push them away. He would actively suppress thoughts of the accident, sometimes even
muttering "no, not now" under his breath, and would then intentionally immerse himself in his design
work or scroll endlessly on his phone, anything to keep his mind occupied all the time. Yet, this
relentless effort to suppress thoughts and keep his mind occupied seemed to backfire. The memories,
denied proper processing, remained stubbornly fragmented and partial, returning as vivid, isolated
flashes that were all the more disorienting. The deliberate mental distraction, while offering
temporary relief, prevented him from ever truly integrating the event, leaving the trauma trapped in
his mind, ready to ambush him.

These intrusive memories, particularly the profound guilt that washed over him – a crushing
certainty that he should have been the one driving, or prevented her from leaving – would
immediately trigger a cascade of strong negative feelings: intense fear for Elara, burning anger at
himself, and a pervasive sense of dread. The emotional storm often bled into his nights, leaving him
with trouble falling or staying asleep, his mind racing with 'what ifs' and the horrifying images he
couldn’t banish. He experienced strong physical reactions too; the shimmer of a particular silk,
similar to the fabric Elara was supposed to pick up that day, could send a jolt of fear through him,
his heart rate accelerating as if he were back at the accident scene himself.

a distant ambulance or the sight of shattered glass on the street – seemed to make the traumatic
memories even more insistent and overwhelming. It was as if his body, primed for danger, would drag
the fragmented images back to the forefront of his mind with renewed force, making it impossible to
escape the internal replay. The constant undercurrent of fear and guilt, coupled with the exhaustion
from chronic sleep problems, made the idea of looking forward unbearable. Stefan, who once thrived

or even just dinner next week. It felt safer to live in a perpetual present, to prevent anything
else from potentially going wrong, or to avoid having to confront the emptiness he sometimes felt
when imagining a future without the weight of his guilt.

observing Stefan's retreat into himself with growing concern. He understood her worry, yet he was
certain that if he allowed himself to plan, to hope, he would only invite another catastrophe. What
frightened him most was the possibility that the raw, overwhelming emotion on recall would never

help, hoping there was a way to untangle the knot of his mind without losing himself entirely.
