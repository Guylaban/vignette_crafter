of struggling with the aftermath of his youngest son, Linh’s, life-threatening illness. The memory
of Linh, then just four, fighting for breath in the sterile confines of the ICU, remained
terrifyingly vivid. Minh had spent weeks by his son’s bedside, watching every flicker of his small
body, every line on the monitors. He recalled the specific pallor of Linh’s skin, the thin, blueish
veins visible on his temples, and the tangle of wires and tubes that seemed to consume his tiny
frame. These specific body parts, etched into his mind, became potent reminders of the helplessness
he felt. The long, silent nights in the hospital, punctuated only by the rhythmic beeping of
machines and the hushed whispers of nurses, imbued the very concept of nighttime and darkness with a
profound sense of dread.

Now, years later, Minh finds himself perpetually haunted. When he encounters any of these cues –
perhaps a fleeting glimpse of a child’s thin wrist, or as the sun dips below the horizon and the
fields darken around his farmhouse – these *triggers* instantly plunge him back into the past. He
experiences the frightening event as if it is happening right now, the air thick with the smell of
disinfectant, the sound of Linh’s ragged breathing filling his ears. These sudden, overwhelming
intrusions, where fragments of the memory appear without warning and then disappear, make the memory
feel terrifyingly *present*, not something that is over.

The constant reliving of his son’s suffering, and the pervasive feeling that the memory is happening
right now rather than something that is past, has led Minh to adopt a range of desperate coping
mechanisms. He finds himself actively trying to *avoid sleep*, especially in the dark, because lying
still in the quiet allows the terrifying images and sensations to flood his mind unchecked. He’s
taken to *delaying sleep* until very late, often staying up until 2 or 3 AM, watching television or
aimlessly scrolling on his phone, anything to postpone the inevitable confrontation with the night
and its terrors. These efforts to avoid the distressing memories, ironically, leave him chronically
exhausted and on edge.

His exhaustion has a profound impact, making him even more susceptible to his *triggers*. A simple
lullaby or pop song, perhaps one he heard faintly from a nurse's radio during a particularly
harrowing night at the hospital, can now instantly bring back those terrifying mental images, making
*certain songs* unbearable to hear. The memories themselves also sharpen his sensitivity to
potential *triggers*; because the event still feels so present, he is hyper-vigilant, scanning his
environment for any sign that might herald a repeat of the trauma, transforming innocuous details
into harbingers of dread.

The continuous cycle of reliving the event and the constant presence of his *triggers* have also
pushed Minh into isolating himself. He has started to *avoid other people*, particularly neighbours
and friends from his community, because he fears they might inquire about Linh or bring up general
health topics, which he knows would inevitably lead to him *reliving the event* once more. This
avoidance, while offering a temporary respite from potential triggers, has left him feeling
profoundly lonely and disconnected. He often skips community gatherings and even avoids the local
market unless absolutely necessary, preferring the solitude of his fields. His wife, worried, has

cannot continue like this, caught in a tightening loop of fear and avoidance, and it was this
realization that finally brought him to seek help, desperately hoping to find a way out of the ever-
present nightmare.
