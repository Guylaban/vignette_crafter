Michael, a 48-year-old shopowner, had always prided himself on his steady composure, a quality that
had served him well in his bustling hardware store. But that was before the phone call, before the
unimaginable news that his son, Liam, had been the victim of a drunk driving accident. The world
fractured that day, and though two years had passed, Michael lived with the constant, unsettling
feeling that it was still happening. His memories of the frightening event were incredibly vivid,
playing out like a film that replayed over and over in his mind, often feeling as if it were
happening right now rather than something that was over and done. The gruesome details, the frantic
calls, the sterile hospital waiting room – all were etched with a horrifying clarity that made true
peace unattainable.

In the aftermath, a pervasive sense of threat began to settle over Michael. He found himself jumping
or startling at ordinary sounds in his shop, the clang of a dropped tool or a sudden car horn
outside causing him to react as if he were in immediate danger. Unexpected movements nearby, a
customer reaching for a shelf or a sudden turn from his wife, would make him flinch, his heart
seizing in his chest. This heightened state of internal alarm meant that even the most innocuous
comments about loss or the sight of a passing hearse would trigger an immediate, acute sense of
danger and grief, instantly pulling him back to the raw pain of Liam’s death. These strong negative
feelings—fear, horror, and a crushing guilt—were his constant companions, and they intensified the
feeling that the vivid, replaying memories were truly happening again, right now.

To cope with this relentless internal assault, Michael developed a series of maladaptive strategies.
He started smoking heavily again, a habit he’d kicked years ago, finding a fleeting, false calm in
each drag. What truly became central, however, was his desperate attempt to avoid thinking about the
event. He would throw himself into work, staying late, burying himself in inventory, anything to
keep his mind from drifting. He also started actively avoiding anything that reminded him of someone
he had lost. If a customer at the counter began to talk about people losing loved ones, Michael
would abruptly cut them off or feign an urgent task in the back, his heart hammering. These
triggers, whether a chance remark about grief or the somber sight of a funeral procession passing
his shop, invariably led him to light another cigarette or retreat into the store’s back office,
trying to shut out the encroaching darkness.

The more Michael tried to push away the images of that day, the more they seemed to force themselves
back. His efforts to avoid thinking about the event paradoxically made the memories feel even more
present and vivid, replaying the scene with a horrifying clarity that refused to be dismissed. His
reliance on smoking and his relentless avoidance of any grief reminders, while offering momentary
respite, ultimately amplified his underlying threat response, leaving him more on edge, more prone
to startling, and steeped in a deeper well of negative emotions. By actively avoiding anything that
might evoke Liam’s memory or the pain of his loss, Michael found himself becoming even more
sensitive to potential triggers. A quiet conversation about a friend’s ailing parent, a brief
glimpse of a cemetery from the car window, or even just the word "victim" on the radio, could send
him spiraling. These constant reminders, coupled with his persistent state of hypervigilance and the
intrusive memories, made him increasingly dependent on his maladaptive strategies, creating a tight,
self-reinforcing cycle.

Lately, the cycle had tightened its grip. Michael found it harder to be present with his wife, and
his once-thriving shop felt more like a cage than a livelihood. His customers, once a source of
community, now often felt like potential sources of triggers. He knew, deep down, that his smoking
was terrible for him, and that his constant avoidance was pushing people away, but he felt trapped,
unable to break free from the patterns that had become his only defense. What truly frightened him
was the prospect of a future where Liam’s memory, once a source of immense joy, remained an
inescapable torment, keeping him forever stuck in the shadow of that terrible day. It was this
creeping despair, this fear of a life perpetually defined by loss and vigilance, that finally led
him to seek help, hoping against hope that he could find a way back to a life where memories could
be cherished without feeling like a present danger.
