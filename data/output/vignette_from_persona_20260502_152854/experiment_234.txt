protest. Caught in a volatile crowd and mistaken for someone else, he was severely beaten by
officers, sustaining a broken arm and several fractured ribs. The brutal assault left him with
chronic pain in his left side, and the vivid images of others around him suffering similar fates
were seared into his memory. This single event shattered his once-firm belief in order and justice,
replacing it with a pervasive sense of dread.

In the immediate aftermath, Ricardo found himself wrestling with a landscape of internal and
external reminders. A dull, persistent ache in his left side, a somatic pain that never truly
vanished, became a potent trigger, instantly transporting him back to the moment of impact and the
feeling of helplessness. He also became acutely sensitive to any visible injuries or wounds on
others; a client's bandaged hand or a character's bruise on a TV show would trigger a wave of nausea
and a tightening in his chest. Perhaps most distressing were the victim memories that would flood
his mind whenever he encountered news of police brutality or a memorial, bringing back not just his
own terror but the haunted faces of those who suffered alongside him.

These recurring triggers began to profoundly reshape Ricardo's perception of himself and his place
in the world. When he encountered a visible injury or felt that familiar ache in his side, he would
often find himself spiraling into the conviction that there was something intrinsically faulty about
him, a fundamental weakness or defect that had made the event happen, or at least made him uniquely
susceptible to it. He started to feel profoundly isolated and set apart from others, believing that
no one could truly understand the horror he had endured or the constant internal battle he faced.
This growing sense of alienation, coupled with the persistent reminders of his trauma, led him to
question if his life was even worth living anymore, feeling trapped in a world that no longer felt
safe or meaningful.

This deep-seated belief that his life might not be worth living, and the internal conviction that he
was intrinsically faulty, fueled a pervasive sense of vulnerability. Ricardo became convinced that
he was constantly in danger, an easy target in an unpredictable world. As a result, his threat
monitoring escalated dramatically. He began to avoid going to unfamiliar places, explaining to
himself that he simply couldn't assess the danger effectively in new environments, and it was better
to stick to the known, however small his world became. Before he could feel comfortable anywhere,
even in a familiar client's office, he would always identify potential escape routes, mentally
mapping exits and choke points in an unconscious but relentless ritual.

internal alarm system, always on high alert for danger, would immediately seize upon any minor

visible injury—and interpret it as a direct threat or a potent reminder of the violence he
experienced. The physical pain in his side, often dull, would sharpen under this intense scrutiny,
becoming a more intrusive and immediate reminder of the trauma.

Over time, this relentless threat monitoring and the associated avoidance behaviors began to
reinforce his negative appraisals. The very act of always identifying escape routes and needing to
check behind him made him feel more and more isolated. He found himself unable to relax in social
settings, constantly scanning the room, which further cemented his belief that he was fundamentally
different from others, unable to experience normal life. This growing isolation and the feeling of
being perpetually on guard deepened his conviction that his life was not worth living, as it was now
defined by fear and vigilance rather than connection and purpose. His work as a tax consultant,
which once involved meeting a diverse range of clients, became increasingly challenging. He found
himself declining new clients whose offices were in unfamiliar parts of the city, preferring to work
remotely or only with established clients in his known circuit, which began to impact his
professional growth and income.

Ricardo recently realized the extent to which his life had shrunk. His divorce, finalized two years
prior, left him living alone, and his social circle had withered as he pushed away friends who
suggested outings to new restaurants or unfamiliar parks. He now finds himself spending most
evenings alone, questioning the value of his existence. It was this profound sense of emptiness,
coupled with a growing fear that he would never feel safe again, that finally compelled him to seek
help, hoping to reclaim a life that felt meaningful beyond the constant shadow of danger and his own
perceived brokenness.
