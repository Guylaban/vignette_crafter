Priya, a 31-year-old South Asian woman, had always prided herself on her meticulousness, a trait
that served her well as a middle school teacher. Her life took an abrupt, terrifying turn eight
months ago when, momentarily distracted by her phone’s navigation, she failed to see a pedestrian
step into the road. The impact was sickening. She remembered a flash of movement, the screech of
tires, and the acrid, metallic smell of burning rubber that seemed to cling to everything even days
later. The pedestrian, a young student from a neighbouring school, sustained serious injuries. The
guilt was immediate and suffocating.

From that moment, Priya became convinced that the event happened because of the way she acted. This
profound self-blame quickly led to the belief that she was no longer capable of doing the things
that defined her. How could she, a teacher entrusted with guiding young minds, have been so
careless, so destructive? She felt she had forfeited her right to her former identity, and a deep
sense of being permanently an outsider now settled over her. She would look at her colleagues, her
husband, and feel a chasm between them, certain they couldn't possibly understand the weight of her
actions.

These pervasive negative appraisals profoundly influenced her experience of the traumatic memory.
Whenever the incident came to mind, it wasn't a recollection of something past, but rather a
terrifying re-enactment; the memory felt like it was happening right now, rather than something that
was over. It manifested as a series of vivid snapshot memories, frozen pictures in her mind of the
impact and its immediate aftermath. The crushing guilt and the belief that she was no longer capable
made these intrusions feel like deserved punishment, trapping her in a perpetual loop of distress.

Consequently, everyday occurrences began to morph into potent triggers. Even seemingly innocuous
conversations about minor mishaps in the staff room, or news reports about accidents on the morning
commute, would immediately plunge her back into that moment. These crash discussions would ignite
the olfactory recall of burning rubber, and suddenly the snapshot memories of the event would flood
her awareness, making it feel present, not past. The memory itself, with its vivid sensory details
and "happening now" quality, then became a trigger for further distress and hypervigilance.

What frightened Priya most, however, was how positive emotions themselves became fraught with
danger. A moment of joy or laughter with her husband would swiftly be replaced by a wave of dread
and shame. This positive emotion confusion stemmed from her belief that she was no longer capable of
deserving happiness, that her happiness was a betrayal of the injured student. Her persistent self-
blame and the conviction that she was an outsider also rendered any form of positive attention
unbearable. When her principal praised her innovative lesson plan, or a parent commended her
dedication, Priya would feel a surge of intense anxiety and self-loathing. Being praised became a
powerful trigger, a stark reminder of the capable teacher she felt she no longer was, exacerbating
her sense of worthlessness.

Her work as a teacher, once a source of immense pride, was now a constant battleground. Standing in
front of her class, the fear of making another mistake, of failing her students, was overwhelming.
She found herself avoiding eye contact, hesitant to offer extra help, convinced that her touch was
tainted. She began to isolate herself during lunch breaks, eating alone in her classroom,
reinforcing her belief that she was permanently an outsider now. The children, sensing her distance,
sometimes became boisterous, which, in turn, would heighten her anxiety, making her feel even less
capable of managing her classroom.

Priya finally sought help when her husband, worried by her increasing withdrawal and the constant
shadows in her eyes, gently but firmly insisted. She arrived, weary and guarded, hoping for some
relief from the relentless cycle of guilt and fear, but secretly convinced that no one could truly
understand, and that she might never escape the feeling that she was forever broken.
