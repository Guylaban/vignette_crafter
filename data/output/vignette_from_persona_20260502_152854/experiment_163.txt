Shani, a 47-year-old architect, remembers the metallic taste in her mouth most vividly, even now. It
was the dry, dusty air of the construction site, amplified by the sudden, sickening clang of steel
and the worker's sharp cry as a heavy beam, swinging unexpectedly in a sudden gust of wind, struck
him. She had been overseeing the critical lift, her design, her responsibility. Though the worker
survived, his injuries were severe, and the accident carved a chasm in Shani’s life.

In the months that followed, the world outside her office became a minefield. The spontaneous
auditory recall of the impact, the worker's cry, would suddenly flood her, making her jump or
startle at ordinary sounds that would not bother most people – a phone ringing, a door closing, the
distant rumble of a truck. This constant state of hyper-alertness meant she often reacted as if she
was in danger even when nothing was actually wrong, feeling a perpetual sense of unease. These
frequent false alarms, the constant feeling of being on edge, contributed significantly to her
feeling isolated and set apart from others, a deep chasm opening between her and her usually
supportive husband. She had come to believe that nobody could truly understand the persistent dread
she lived with.

This profound sense of isolation, coupled with the conviction that the person she had become was
unrecognisable to her, led Shani to develop elaborate mental rituals, a desperate form of magical
prevention. She would meticulously replay the sequence of events in her mind, imagining alternative
outcomes, convinced that if she could just perfect this mental rehearsal, she could somehow prevent
future catastrophes. These rituals, while intended to provide control, paradoxically made it harder
for her to deliberately recall what happened in a coherent narrative, as she was constantly
manipulating and re-ordering details. When memories of the incident did return, particularly the
intense taste vividness of the metallic dust, she would find herself dwelling relentlessly on what
she had done wrong. This self-blame rumination reinforced her belief that the person she had become
was unrecognisable to her, a stark contrast to the confident, meticulous architect she once was.

The spontaneous auditory recall of the accident often triggered this self-blame rumination, sending
her into loops of replaying the scene over and over, trying to find fault in herself. These intense
periods of rumination, alongside the constant internal pressure to perform her mental rituals for
magical prevention, became a source of intense anxiety itself. If she missed a step or felt a ritual
wasn't "right," it would trigger a wave of false alarms, making her feel acutely in danger again.
Her difficulty concentrating, a direct consequence of her heightened state of threat, further
impaired her ability to deliberately remember what happened, contributing to the fragmented nature
of her memories.

The self-blame rumination, the endless replaying of the accident, solidified her conviction that
somebody else would not have gotten into this situation, further deepening her sense of
worthlessness and reinforcing the feeling that the person she had become was unrecognisable to her.
In an attempt to manage the constant anxiety, she would often remind herself it could have been
worse, a downward comparison she used to try and calm her frayed nerves, though it rarely brought
true comfort. The belief that the person she had become was unrecognisable to her, coupled with the
shame, also made it hard to deliberately recall what happened, as if her mind was protecting her
from the full impact of her perceived failure.

Shani’s work suffered. Her concentration, once her sharpest tool, was fragmented, making complex
design tasks overwhelming. She began to avoid site visits, the sounds and smells too potent, too
likely to trigger a false alarm or a vivid memory. What frightened her most was the tightening grip
of these cycles, the sense that she was losing herself completely. It was this fear, the fear of
forever being this unrecognisable, isolated person, that finally compelled her to seek help, hoping
there was a way to reclaim the woman she used to be.
