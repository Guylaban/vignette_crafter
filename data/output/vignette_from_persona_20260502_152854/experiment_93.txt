Mai, a 54-year-old electrician of South Eastern Asian descent, had always prided herself on her
resilience and practical approach to life. That changed five years ago when a sudden, severe
respiratory illness landed her in the ICU, fighting for breath for weeks. The memories of that time
were vivid: the sterile, metallic tang of antiseptic permeating the air, the constant, mechanical
hiss and whir of the ventilator assisting her strained lungs, and the terrifying, heavy sound of her
own desperate breathing, which often felt like a death rattle. She survived, but the experience left
an indelible mark, subtly reshaping her world piece by piece.

In the months that followed her discharge, Mai found her once-familiar environment increasingly
fraught with hidden dangers. The subtle whir of a ventilation system in a client's building, or a
particularly heavy breath from a co-worker on a cramped job site, could instantly send a jolt
through her, her heart beginning to pound and her breath catching in her throat. These auditory
triggers, alongside the distinct smell of hospital disinfectant she sometimes encountered in public
restrooms or even certain cleaning products, would plunge her into a state of intense physical
reactivity. Her body would feel constantly tense and primed for danger, a pervasive sense of being
on alert that she couldn’t shake, often leading her body to react with a surge of adrenaline before
her mind could even register the source of the perceived threat.

The intensity of these physical responses was so overwhelming that Mai began to develop strategies
to cope. When her body felt constantly on alert, a relentless tension settling in her shoulders and
neck, she often found herself retreating into thoughts of how different her life would be if she
hadn't gotten sick, a form of counterfactual thinking that offered a momentary escape from the
relentless physical unease. She would also mentally distance herself from the experience, pretending
the entire ordeal happened to someone else, as if watching a harrowing movie starring a stranger.
This dissociation offered brief respite, but it also meant that Mai never truly processed the
reality of her near-death experience. By consistently pretending the illness wasn't hers, the
potency of the anniversary date of her hospitalization or the smell of antiseptic remained
undiminished, ready to ambush her at any moment, triggering the familiar cascade of dread.

Her coping mechanisms, though seemingly helpful in the short term, began to tighten the grip of her
distress. The constant mental re-writing of history, coupled with her tendency to dissociate,
paradoxically kept her body feeling perpetually on edge, as if the danger was still present and
unresolved, contributing to her pervasive sense of being on alert. She found herself increasingly
avoiding medical cues: she’d take circuitous routes to avoid passing hospitals, turn off the
television if an ambulance siren wailed on screen, and had postponed her own annual physical for two
years. This deliberate avoidance of anything medical, from doctor's offices to even televised
hospital dramas, meant that the very cues she sought to escape remained sharp and terrifying,
triggering intense physical reactions whenever they inevitably appeared, further reinforcing her
need to avoid.

The cycle had become self-reinforcing and exhausting. Mai, now divorced and living alone, found her
work as an electrician, which once brought her such satisfaction, increasingly challenging. A
client’s home might have a faint antiseptic smell, instantly transporting her back to the ICU, and
Mai would find herself mentally replaying the 'what if' scenarios, wishing she could undo that
period of her life, or just pretend she was watching a movie about someone else's ordeal. This loss
of focus, combined with her constant vigilance and frequent need to step away from situations that
triggered her, had started to impact her reliability and income. She came to therapy feeling
trapped, desperate to understand why her body and mind seemed to be conspiring against her, and
hoping to reclaim the sense of safety and capability that had defined her before the illness.
