Ricardo, a 64-year-old jeweler, carried the weight of a childhood trauma that still cast a long
shadow over his life. He was a small boy when he bore witness to his father’s frequent, explosive
rages directed at his mother. The vivid, almost photographic clarity of those moments—his father's
contorted face, the way his mother cowered, the deafening shouts that filled their small
apartment—remained with him. When he remembered the event, these clear, detailed snapshots brought
with them an intense, crushing guilt that he, as the eldest son, had been unable to protect her.
This pervasive guilt solidified his belief that he had failed in his most important role, leading
him to appraise himself as a weak person. The raw, unfiltered memories also instilled in him a deep,
pervasive sense that the world was an inherently dangerous place, where harm could erupt without
warning.

To cope with the overwhelming guilt and the intrusive nature of these clear, detailed memories,
Ricardo developed a strong habit of trying hard not to think about the trauma. He would push the
images away, suppressing any thought that threatened to bring them forward. Ironically, this
constant effort to suppress thoughts about his past only reinforced his internal narrative of being
a weak person; he felt he couldn't even control his own mind, further deepening his sense of
failure. His deeply held conviction that he was a weak person, someone who had failed, made him feel
perpetually vulnerable, leading to a constant, internal sense of threat. This pervasive feeling of
threat was further exacerbated by his coping strategies. Ricardo found himself constantly monitoring
his own thoughts for signs of "going crazy" under the pressure, a vigilant internal watch that kept
his nervous system on high alert. He also avoided anything that felt like relaxation, seeing it as
dangerous, a moment when his guard might drop, leaving him exposed and vulnerable, which further
intensified his sense of threat.

In his jewelry workshop, the everyday sounds and interactions could be jarring. A sudden shout from
a delivery person, or a heated discussion between colleagues, would immediately activate his
internal alarm, making him notice potential weapons or dangerous objects in the environment, like
the heavy tools on his workbench. Such confrontations, or even just the presence of other men if he
was alone with them in the workshop, would immediately make him try to suppress any thoughts about
his past trauma, fearing they would overwhelm him. These triggered moments also brought back the
familiar sting of his perceived failure, reminding him of his weakness, and further cementing his
appraisal that he had failed in his life's most important roles. His belief that he was a weak
person, coupled with his conviction that he had failed, made him interpret even minor interactions
as potential confrontations, thus increasing the frequency and intensity of his triggers. The
constant watching of others for signs of harm, born from his inability to trust his safety, made him
hypersensitive to any perceived threat, turning innocuous situations into triggers.

The constant feeling that he could not trust his safety, even when others reassured him, deepened
his conviction that he was isolated and had lost his connection to his community and culture,

memories of his childhood violence unintegrated and potent.

Ricardo’s marriage had ended years ago, his ex-wife citing his emotional distance and constant
anxiety. He had become increasingly isolated from his family and the vibrant Latin American
community he once cherished, feeling he had lost his connection to his culture. He found it
