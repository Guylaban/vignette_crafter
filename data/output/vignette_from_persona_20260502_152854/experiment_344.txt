Lani, a 20-year-old South Eastern Asian soldier, had been married for only a year when the assault
occurred during a remote training exercise. She was alone, tasked with securing a perimeter, when a
fellow soldier approached, then violated her trust and her body. In the immediate aftermath, the
event itself felt strangely disjointed. Her memories of the frightening encounter were mostly
fragmented pictures or images – a flash of uniform, the cold ground, the distant sounds of the base
– rather than a coherent narrative. This visual, piecemeal quality made the story feel unreal, as if
it was so extreme it was hard to believe it actually happened to her.

As weeks turned into months, these fragmented memories continued to plague Lani. They often intruded
as vivid, unsettling images, making her feel as though the memory belonged to someone else rather
than to her own experience. This detachment from her own past fed a profound sense of self-doubt and
confusion. She began to believe that the person she had become was unrecognisable to her, a stark
contrast to the confident soldier she once was. What haunted her most was the gnawing conviction
that somebody else, anyone else, would have stopped the event from happening, or at least reacted
differently than she had.

These negative appraisals of herself and her actions directly fueled a range of maladaptive
strategies. Convinced of her own failure and feeling alien to herself, Lani found herself
increasingly turning to food. She would eat more than usual, not out of hunger, but to distract
herself from the painful thoughts or to numb the overwhelming feelings of shame and unreality. When
the visual memories of the assault would flare up, or when the sheer disbelief that the story felt
unreal became too much to bear, she would consciously try to think of something else, redirecting
her attention to trivial tasks or daydreams. Yet, even as she tried to escape, another part of her
felt compelled to go over and over the details of the event, trying desperately to make sense of
what had happened, searching for a different outcome in the endless replay.

Ironically, these attempts to cope only tightened the grip of her distress. Her constant redirection
and overanalysing, far from providing clarity, seemed to further fragment the traumatic memory,
making it feel even more alien and less integrated into her personal history. The more she tried to
piece together the details, the more elusive a coherent narrative became, reinforcing the feeling
that the memory belonged to someone else. Furthermore, her persistent overanalysing, replaying every
moment, only deepened her conviction that somebody else would have intervened or handled the
situation better, solidifying her negative self-appraisals. She withdrew from her unit, skipping
communal meals and avoiding social gatherings in the barracks, fearing that her new, unrecognisable
self would be exposed. This isolation, a direct consequence of her coping mechanisms, led to a
profound sense of having lost her connection to her community and culture, both within the military
and with her family back home. Her marriage, once a source of comfort, now felt strained as her
husband struggled to understand her increasing distance and her reliance on overeating to manage her
distress.

Now, Lani finds herself trapped in a cycle where the fragmented, unreal quality of her memory fuels
her belief that she is irredeemably changed and that she failed. These painful appraisals then drive
her to overeat or redirect her thoughts, which in turn prevents her from processing the memory,
leaving her feeling more detached and lost. She came to seek help, exhausted by the constant
internal battle, hoping to find a way back to the person she was before the assault, desperate to
stop feeling like a stranger in her own skin and her own life.
