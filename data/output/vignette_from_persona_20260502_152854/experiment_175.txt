Ravi Sharma, a 61-year-old married paramedic, had always prided himself on his calm under pressure.
But lately, the pressure felt internal, relentless, and suffocating, leading him to seek help with a
PCL-5 score of 35. It had been nearly two years since the incident that shattered his composure: a
late-night call to an industrial accident involving a ruptured chemical tank. The scene was plunged
into near darkness, lit only by the flashing emergency lights and Ravi’s flashlight beam. The air
was thick with a pungent, sickly sweet odour, reminiscent of industrial-strength alcohol, that
burned his nostrils and throat. He’d seen the immediate effects on the workers, their desperate
gasps, the rapid onset of disorientation. He had narrowly escaped significant exposure himself, but
the memory had clung to him like the chemical residue on his uniform.

Now, any dark space could instantly transport Ravi back to that night. Just last week, while
navigating a dimly lit stairwell at his station, the familiar gloom instantly brought back the full
memory of the chemical spill without warning. He could feel the acrid burning in his throat, the
metallic taste in his mouth, and the same terror he felt at the time, his heart hammering in his
chest. When the memory returned, he felt so overwhelmed by emotion that he had to stop, leaning
against the cold concrete wall, struggling to catch his breath. This visceral re-experiencing made
him desperately seek ways to avoid such moments.

In an attempt to manage these overwhelming intrusions, Ravi found himself constantly surrounded by
people, needing to be active and engaged so he was never alone with his thoughts. He’d pick up extra
shifts, volunteer for community events, anything to keep his mind occupied. He also began to
ruminate endlessly about how the event could have been prevented, replaying every detail in his
head, searching for a different outcome. This constant rumination, far from offering resolution,
kept the memory active and vivid, making it harder to process and leaving it raw and easily
triggered. The act of constantly socialising, while providing a temporary distraction, meant he
never truly confronted the core distress, leaving the memory potent and unaddressed.

The persistent and terrifying memory of the chemical spill, particularly the burning smell and the
darkness, made Ravi hyper-vigilant for anything that might bring it back. He found himself scanning
every dark corner, subtly flinching at the faintest whiff of alcohol – even the hand sanitizer in
the ambulance – making these everyday environmental cues more readily perceived as threats and thus
more potent triggers. This vigilance extended to his work environment. His paramedic occupation,
once a source of pride, now felt fraught with danger. The ambulance itself, and the hospitals he
frequented, became increasingly associated with the trauma. He started to avoid medical cues,
declining routine physical examinations and feeling deeply uncomfortable when around other
ambulances or in hospital waiting rooms, even when off duty. This avoidance, intended to protect
him, paradoxically made his workplace itself a more potent trigger for distress.

When he encountered a trigger, like the distinct smell of alcohol from a patient’s breath or a dark
stretch of road on a night call, the immediate rush of anxiety and the certainty that the memory
would return pushed him further into his maladaptive strategies. He would immediately launch into a
new round of rumination, trying to exert some control over the uncontrollable, or make an urgent
phone call to a colleague, ensuring he wasn't alone with his thoughts. Ravi’s wife, Priya, had
noticed the change; he was rarely home, always "busy," and his once-boisterous laugh had become
brittle. He knew he was pulling away, but the thought of facing the darkness in his own mind, alone,
was what frightened him most. He was tired, perpetually exhausted by the effort of avoidance, and it
was this exhaustion, coupled with Priya’s gentle urging, that finally brought him to seek help,
hoping to find a way out of the tightening grip of his past.
