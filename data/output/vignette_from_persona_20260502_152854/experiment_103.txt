Rohan, a 39-year-old interior designer of South Asian heritage, sought therapy feeling increasingly
overwhelmed and disconnected, five years after the unexpected death of his younger brother, Anil.
The news of Anil’s fatal car accident had arrived like a physical blow, shattering Rohan’s world. He
recalled the surreal haze of the funeral, the hushed condolences, and the profound, aching finality
of it all. What stuck with him most was the suddenness, the lack of warning, and the way life had
irrevocably changed in an instant.

Initially, Rohan grappled with immense grief, but as months turned into years, his sorrow twisted
into something more insidious. He noticed that specific moments would plunge him back into that
unbearable state of shock. The anniversary of Anil’s death, for instance, became a day of profound
dread. As the date approached, Rohan would feel a growing panic, leading him to meticulously plan
elaborate distractions or take the day off from his busy interior design practice, ensuring he was
nowhere near any reminders of the event, a deliberate strategy to avoid anniversaries. Similarly,
during client consultations, a casual mention of someone losing a loved one, or even a design brief
touching on themes of remembrance, would immediately make Rohan change the subject or decline
projects that felt too close to themes of loss, clearly avoiding grief reminders.

These triggers often had an immediate and visceral impact. The first time a client spoke about their
recently deceased mother, Rohan felt his heart begin to pound, his breath catching in his chest, his
body reacting to danger before his mind could even process the words. This sudden physiological
response would often be followed by vivid flashbacks of the accident scene, replaying in his mind
with terrifying clarity – not the actual event, but the horrific images he’d conjured, the phone
call, the empty space Anil left behind. The intensity of these flashbacks and the unsettling
sensation of his body reacting before his mind convinced Rohan that he needed to be constantly
prepared for potential catastrophe. This led him to mentally rehearse worst-case scenarios, playing
out imagined tragedies involving his wife or parents, believing that if he anticipated the pain, it
wouldn't catch him off guard again.

The pervasive sense of threat began to manifest in his daily life. The constant mental rehearsal of
worst-case scenarios, intended to prepare him, ironically kept his nervous system perpetually
activated, making him more susceptible to sudden flashbacks and the unsettling feeling of his body
reacting before his mind to the slightest perceived threat. Rohan found himself increasingly
irritable, snapping at his wife, Priya, over trivial matters or having angry outbursts with his
design assistants when deadlines loomed. This frequent irritability and the fear of losing control
made Rohan withdraw further, reinforcing his desire to avoid any social situation where a trigger
might arise, lest he snap at someone he cared about.

His persistent efforts to avoid grief reminders and significant dates meant he never truly processed
his loss. Instead, this avoidance perpetuated a state of hypervigilance, leaving him constantly on
edge, his body poised to react to danger, often manifesting as sudden, inexplicable irritability
towards his wife or colleagues. His work, once a source of creative fulfillment, became fraught with
anxiety. He started turning down projects that required him to engage deeply with clients’ personal
stories, fearing the inevitable conversations about family and loss. Priya, noticing his increasing
emotional distance and unpredictable temper, urged him to seek help, concerned about the growing
chasm between them. Rohan arrived for his initial consultation, exhausted and guarded, admitting he
was terrified that if he ever truly allowed himself to feel the grief, he would simply shatter.
