demeanor, qualities that served him well in his demanding career. That changed irrevocably nearly

smell of burning oil and rubber filled the confined space, mingling with the terrifying sounds of
screeching tires and mangled steel. For what felt like an eternity, he lay in the sudden darkness,
listening to the groaning of his car and the distant, muffled shouts, certain he would not survive.

In the months that followed, the world Ratu once knew began to feel deeply unsafe. The faint smell
of exhaust fumes in a parking garage, for instance, or even the scent of burnt toast, could
instantly pull him back into the crushing darkness of that underpass, the memory feeling like it was
happening right now. He would sometimes hear the frantic shouts and the sickening crunch of metal in
his head, just as he had heard them during the frightening event. These sudden jolts, triggered
without warning by something in his surroundings, only solidified Ratu’s conviction that he had to
be on guard all the time. He had come to believe that he needed to be especially careful because you
never know what can happen next.

This constant vigilance, born from the conviction that he had to remain hypervigilant, manifested
physically. His body reacted to danger before he even had time to think, a jolt of adrenaline
coursing through him at the slightest provocation. He found himself constantly scanning for exits as
soon as he entered any room or building, whether it was a busy conference hall or a quiet
restaurant. Indeed, being in this constant state of threat seemed to prime his mind for the past,
with any sudden jolt or surge of adrenaline bringing fragmented but terrifying memories to the
forefront. When these memories flared, feeling like they were happening right now, his body would
instantly respond, heart racing and muscles tensing as if the danger was imminent. The vividness of
these flashbacks also left him incredibly raw, making him even more susceptible to anything in his
surroundings that could instantly bring back the full memory without warning. His hyper-alert
physical state meant that even a distant car sound, once innocuous, now felt like a direct warning,
instantly grabbing his attention and amplifying his anxiety.

His heightened state of alertness, fueled by the belief that he needed to constantly scan for
danger, made him acutely sensitive to potential triggers, turning innocuous daily occurrences into
sources of profound distress. His fear was that if he allowed himself to think about the event, he
would not be able to handle it, a thought avoidance strategy that paradoxically seemed to make
memories feel more intrusive and harder to control. He began to avoid driving after dark, making
excuses to his wife, Lena, for why he couldn’t pick up dinner or attend evening social events. At
work, his office on the 20th floor of a downtown high-rise became a source of anxiety; the constant
hum of city traffic below, punctuated by car horns and sirens, was a pervasive trigger. He found
himself unable to concentrate during meetings, his eyes darting around the room, always looking for
the quickest way out. Lena, too, was impacted; Ratu would wake during the night to check whether he
was safe, often startling her awake in the process. He had lost significant weight, his once sharp
focus dulled by exhaustion and constant fear.

The tightening grip of these cycles eventually became unbearable. Ratu was tired of living in a
state of perpetual alarm, exhausted by the relentless vigilance and the sudden, unbidden incursions
of the past. He came to therapy, hoping to find a way to quiet the constant noise in his head and
the terror in his body, though a part of him still worried that confronting the accident directly
would shatter what little control he felt he had left.
