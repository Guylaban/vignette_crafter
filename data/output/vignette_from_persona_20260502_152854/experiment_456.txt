Imani, a 48-year-old African American Rabbi, sat across from me, her posture rigid, her eyes
scanning the room even as she spoke. Her journey to this office began nearly two years ago, on a day
that shattered the peace of her synagogue and her life. She had been leading a community prayer
gathering when a coordinated attack, intended to cause mass casualties, began with a deafening
explosion just outside the main sanctuary. In the ensuing chaos, with congregants screaming and
debris flying, Imani found herself frozen, grappling with the impossible choice of who to help
first, feeling responsible for every soul present. Though she eventually helped usher many to
safety, the memory of her initial paralysis and the devastation around her left an indelible mark.

In the immediate aftermath, a profound sense of guilt settled over Imani. *“I will never forgive
myself for not doing more to stop what happened,”* she confessed, her voice barely a whisper. This
**inaction guilt** became a constant companion, twisting her perspective on the world. She found
herself increasingly certain that *“nothing good can happen to me anymore,”* a bleak appraisal that
began to color every aspect of her life. This pervasive sense of impending doom and the belief that
she had to be especially careful because *“you never know what can happen next”* fueled her
escalating **threat monitoring**. She began to find it impossible to believe others when they told
her she was safe, especially as darkness fell; *“I find it harder to feel safe at night than during
the day,”* she admitted, the night amplifying her anxieties. Her hyper-alertness meant she was
constantly scanning her surroundings for signs of danger, an exhausting and relentless task.

This persistent sense of threat began to subtly alter her perceptions of the world around her,
impacting her daily life as a spiritual leader. The *“explosion or bang sounds”* that were once
innocuous now sent jolts of panic through her, as did the feeling of *“being watched or observed”*
during services or community events. Her constant internal alarm, a deep-seated feeling that she
*“cannot trust safety,”* meant her mind was primed to interpret any sudden noise or lingering gaze
as a potential precursor to another attack.

Driven by her negative appraisals—the belief that she had to be *“especially careful because you
never know what can happen next”* and that *“nothing good can happen to me anymore”*—Imani started
to adopt a range of **maladaptive strategies**. She began to actively avoid situations where she
might be reminded of the event, often excusing herself from conversations with congregants who
wanted to process their shared trauma. *“Being reminded of the event by others”* felt like reopening
a wound she desperately wanted to keep closed. This avoidance, initially a coping mechanism, only
served to reinforce her belief that she was perpetually unsafe, deepening her conviction that
*“nothing good can happen to me anymore.”* The distance she felt from her community, her inability
to engage with their grief, only intensified her guilt and isolation.

Her constant scanning of her surroundings for danger, a form of **checking surroundings**, meant she
was perpetually on edge. While leading services, her eyes would dart to the doors, to the faces in
the pews, always searching for an anomaly. This made her more susceptible to triggers; any loud,
unexpected noise or the feeling of someone’s prolonged gaze would immediately activate her alarm
system, pulling her back to the day of the attack. Over time, these avoidance behaviours and
heightened vigilance began to impact her closest relationships. She found herself withdrawing from
her husband, and intimacy became impossible. *“I just can’t,”* she explained, referring to
**avoiding sex**, feeling too vulnerable, too afraid, and too consumed by her internal world of
threat. The constant mental energy spent *“scanning for danger”* and her inability to feel safe even
in her own home had left her exhausted and disconnected. When news reports related to similar
incidents appeared, she would quickly change the channel or turn away, unable to bear the weight of
the information.

Imani finally sought help when the toll on her marriage became unbearable, and her ability to lead
her congregation with the spiritual presence she once possessed felt like a distant memory. She felt
trapped in a cycle of vigilance and despair, desperately wanting to reclaim her life but uncertain
how to escape the tightening grip of her fears.
