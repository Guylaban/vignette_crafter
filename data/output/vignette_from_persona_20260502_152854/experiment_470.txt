Aisha, a 32-year-old South Asian woman, presented for therapy following her divorce, describing a
profound and debilitating struggle with the aftermath of torture she endured years ago. She worked
as a chaplain, a role that once brought her deep fulfillment, but now felt increasingly untenable
due to the persistent grip of her past.

The trauma itself remained a fragmented, visceral experience. Aisha recalled being held in a stark,
windowless room, the air thick with the metallic scent of fear. She remembered the coarse fabric of
a blindfold, the muffled sounds of unfamiliar voices, and the terrifying sensation of being
physically restrained and held down, her limbs pinned, her body utterly helpless. Uniformed figures
moved in and out of her periphery when the blindfold was briefly lifted, their presence a cold,
stark symbol of her captivity. Though she survived, the ordeal left her with a memory that felt
incomplete and elusive. She knew what happened, a terrifying truth etched into her being, but she
found herself unable to find words to describe it, the narrative slipping away whenever she tried to
grasp it. When she attempted to explain the event, she would lose the thread, unable to finish,
leaving her feeling profoundly isolated and misunderstood. This nonverbal memory, a collection of
raw sensations and images without a coherent story, became the lens through which she experienced
the world.

Because her memory of the event was so fragmented and nonverbal, Aisha found herself hyper-
responsive to any sensory input that remotely resembled her past ordeal, turning even benign
elements of her daily life into potent triggers. The sound of a distant siren or the clatter of
heavy boots on a hard floor, eerily similar to sounds from her captivity, would instantly plunge her
into a state of panic. The feeling of a seatbelt pressing against her, or a friendly hand resting on
her shoulder, could evoke the terrifying sensation of being physically restrained, sending shivers
of fear through her body. Even the sight of military or police uniforms, whether on television or
worn by security guards in the hospital where she worked, would bring a rush of dread, the uniform
serving as a stark visual reminder of the figures who had controlled her fate. These intense, often
inexplicable reactions were overwhelming, springing from a past she couldn't articulate.

The profound distress caused by these constant triggers, coupled with her inability to coherently
process or explain her traumatic memory, pushed Aisha towards a series of maladaptive strategies to
cope. When the intense, nonverbal flashbacks would seize her, making her feel as if she were back in
that room, she would sometimes hurt herself physically. The sharp, immediate pain served as a brutal
distraction, a way to pull her mind away from the terrifying mental images and sensations that words
couldn't capture. The feeling of vulnerability she experienced during the torture also made her
deeply afraid of open or exposed spaces. Walking through the large, open common areas of the
hospital, or even a park, would fill her with a sickening dread, making her feel utterly exposed and
unsafe. This fear began to significantly impact her work as a chaplain, limiting her ability to
minister to patients in various hospital settings. Furthermore, her struggle to articulate her
experiences and the fear of being triggered in front of others led her to avoid social contacts,
particularly with people she had socialised with before the event. She felt she couldn't explain
what she was going through, and the effort of maintaining a façade of normalcy was exhausting. This
increasing isolation was a significant factor in the breakdown of her marriage.

Unfortunately, these maladaptive strategies, while offering fleeting relief, inadvertently prevented
Aisha from processing her traumatic memory more fully. By hurting herself to distract from the
overwhelming feelings, she avoided confronting the raw sensations and images that constituted her
nonverbal memory, reinforcing its fragmented nature. Similarly, her avoidance of open spaces and
social contacts kept her from engaging with the world in ways that might have challenged her sense
of constant threat or provided opportunities to incrementally integrate her experience. Each act of
avoidance solidified the belief that the memory was too dangerous to touch, too overwhelming to
face, ensuring that she continued to struggle to find words and explain clearly what had happened.
This cyclical dynamic, where her fragmented memory made her highly susceptible to triggers, which in
turn fueled her use of maladaptive strategies, further entrenched her memory difficulties, had
become a tightening knot in Aisha’s life.

Now, Aisha sought help, exhausted by the constant vigilance and the ever-present fear. Her PCL-5
score of 68 reflected the severe impact on her daily functioning. She was tired of feeling like a
prisoner to her past, but the thought of confronting the wordless terror of her memory filled her
with an overwhelming dread, a fear that if she truly opened that door, she would be lost forever.
Yet, a part of her, however small, hoped that she might finally find a way to quiet the echoes of
that room and reclaim her life.
