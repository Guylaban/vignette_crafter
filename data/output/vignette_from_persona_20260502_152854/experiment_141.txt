Mark, a 53-year-old social worker, walked into the clinic with a posture that spoke of constant
vigilance, his eyes scanning the waiting room before settling uneasily. His life had been
irrevocably altered two years prior when, leaving a late client meeting in an unfamiliar part of
town, he was cornered and sexually assaulted in a narrow, poorly lit alleyway. The assailant
vanished into the night, leaving Mark with not just physical wounds, but an invisible scar that had
reshaped his entire world.

From that night onward, the memory of the event became a relentless torment. When Mark tried to
piece together what happened, the recall brought back the same terror he felt at the time, a
visceral re-experiencing that left him breathless and shaking. An overwhelming sense of shame on
recall also consumed him, making him feel profoundly tainted and isolated, even from his wife.
Despite knowing what happened, he often felt unable to find words to describe it, as if the horror
was too immense for language. The intense shame and terror that flooded Mark whenever he tried to
revisit those moments made him desperate to push these thoughts out of his mind; he would try to
suppress them, but they always returned, unbidden and unwelcome. This vivid, terrifying recollection
instantly put him on high alert, convinced that danger was always lurking just beyond his periphery.

His constant vigilance, the gnawing certainty that something bad was about to happen, drove Mark to
drastically change his routines. He began to avoid enclosed or confined spaces, preferring the
stairs at his social work office building to the elevator, or arranging client meetings in more
open, spacious rooms. He also found himself frequently looking behind him to check if someone was
following him, especially when walking to his car after work, a habit that grew out of an
unshakeable feeling that he was being watched. He avoided going to unfamiliar places, turning down
invitations to new restaurants or social gatherings, because he felt he couldn't adequately assess
the danger there. Ironically, this pervasive sense of threat only served to make the memory of his
assault feel more immediate and real, as if it could happen again at any moment. The fear he felt in
his daily life would summon the terror of that night, making the past feel like a constant, looming
present.

While these avoidance tactics offered fleeting relief, they ultimately reinforced his belief that
the world was a dangerous place, making him even more certain that something bad was happening
whenever there was a delay, like a late bus or a client running behind schedule. He found himself

internal battle made him irritable and distant, straining his marriage as his wife struggled to
understand his withdrawal. His capacity for empathy, once a cornerstone of his social work practice,
suffered as his own internal turmoil and hypervigilance overshadowed his ability to connect with
clients.

Mark’s wife eventually insisted he seek help, noticing how much he had changed and how his fear had
begun to define their lives. He came to therapy exhausted, trapped in a relentless cycle of fear and
avoidance, deeply ashamed of his struggles, but desperate for a way to escape the tightening grip of
his past.
