new life into the world. A beloved midwife in her close-knit community, her hands had delivered
countless babies, her presence a source of calm in moments of intense vulnerability. This
steadfastness was shattered one late evening, nearly two years ago, as she drove home from a
difficult delivery. Tired but satisfied, she was on a familiar country road when a deer suddenly
bounded into her path. The swerve was instinctual, but the old sedan skidded violently, striking a
sturdy oak. The impact was deafening, the world tilting before crashing into a sickening stillness.
Esther remembered the acrid smell of burning oil, the twisted metal pinning her left leg, and the
terrifying sensation of being utterly trapped. Lying there, disoriented in the wreckage, the distant
wail of sirens grew louder, a chilling sound that promised help but also felt like an omen of
impending doom. As paramedics worked to free her, she could see the crumpled hood of her car, a
stark image of destruction that etched itself into her mind.

The physical injuries from the accident eventually healed, but the emotional scars deepened with
time, transforming her once-vibrant life. Esther had always been strong, but now she had come to
believe that her body had been permanently damaged by what happened, a constant ache in her leg a
cruel reminder. This profound sense of physical vulnerability, coupled with the forced curtailment
of her midwifery practice, led her to a devastating conclusion: her life had been destroyed by the
trauma. She found herself constantly replaying the accident, convinced that somebody else, someone
more careful, would not have gotten into this situation, a relentless self-blame that gnawed at her
peace. This pervasive belief that her life was irrevocably broken and that she was somehow to blame
made her acutely sensitive to anything that reminded her of that night, almost as if she was
constantly searching for confirmation of her fears.

Now, any confined space, like the small examination rooms at the clinic she used to frequent, or
even her own utility closet, instantly felt like the crushing confines of her wrecked car,
triggering a suffocating wave of panic. These moments of being in locked spaces, or the sudden blare
of sirens from the nearby highway, would plunge her into a state of intense threat. Her heart would
pound, her breath would quicken, and she would become superalert and watchful, her eyes darting
around as if bracing for another impact. The sight of crash imagery on the news or even a dented
fender in a parking lot could send a jolt of fear through her, making her instantly hypervigilant.
This constant state of being on guard, the persistent feeling of threat, profoundly reinforced her
conviction that her body was indeed permanently damaged and that her life had been irrevocably
altered for the worse. She often thought, "See, I can’t even relax. This is my life now, always
waiting for something bad to happen."

These triggers, whether the sound of sirens or the feeling of confinement, directly fueled her
negative appraisals, solidifying her belief that her life had been destroyed. "This is what my life
has become," she'd lament to her husband, "constantly reminded of that moment, unable to escape."
What frightened her most was that her pervasive state of threat, this unrelenting hypervigilance,
made her even more susceptible to perceiving things as triggers. She was so keyed up that ordinary
sounds, like a neighbor's car door or a distant dog bark, would make her jump or startle, further
confirming her sense of vulnerability. It was a vicious cycle: her deeply held beliefs about her
damaged self and ruined life made her seek out and react to triggers, which in turn intensified her
threat response, which then confirmed her worst fears about herself and her future.

Sleep had become a nightly battle; she would lie awake for hours, her mind racing, replaying
fragments of the accident, or scanning her quiet bedroom for imagined dangers. When she finally
drifted off, she woke frequently, startled by the creaks of the old house. The joy she once found in
her family and community had dimmed, overshadowed by this relentless internal turmoil. Her husband,
seeing her withdraw, gently suggested she seek help. Esther finally agreed, exhausted by the
constant vigilance and the gnawing sense that her life, even at 80, was over, defined only by that
single, terrifying moment of impact. She longed for a return to some semblance of the peace she once
knew, but underneath, she was deeply afraid that it was simply too late.
