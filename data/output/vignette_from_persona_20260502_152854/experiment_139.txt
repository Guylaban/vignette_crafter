Isabella, a 22-year-old accountant, sought therapy feeling increasingly overwhelmed by a pervasive
sense of dread that had taken root after her infant son, Mateo, suffered a life-threatening illness
two years ago. The memory of those weeks in the pediatric ICU, watching Mateo struggle for every
breath, remained a raw wound. She remembered the terrifying stillness of the hospital room late at
night, the hushed voices of the nurses, and the agonizing wait for doctors to deliver test results
or confirm a diagnosis. Each protracted moment of silence felt like a precursor to tragedy, every
delay in a doctor’s arrival a confirmation that something catastrophic was unfolding.

Now, these echoes from the past manifested as a constant, low-level hum of anxiety in her daily
life. Working as an accountant, Isabella found the quiet hum of the office particularly unsettling.
A colleague’s unusual silence, or the unexpected stillness of the breakroom, would make her heart
pound and her breath catch, convinced that something dangerous was about to happen. Similarly, when
a report took longer than expected to load, or a client failed to return a call precisely when
anticipated, she would immediately assume something bad was happening, her chest tightening and a
wave of nausea washing over her. These moments of perceived threat, whether from silence or delays,
frequently triggered strong physical reactions, leaving her shaky and exhausted.

What Isabella found most distressing was how these intense feelings of threat would pull her back
into the harrowing memories of Mateo’s illness. A quiet afternoon in the office, her colleagues away
at lunch, could suddenly transport her to the sterile hospital room, the faint metallic taste of
fear, or the bland, tasteless hospital food, vivid on her tongue. These memories, though incredibly
potent in their sensory details, were often fragmented, and she found herself pushing them away
before she could fully recall the sequence of events, a desperate attempt to protect herself from
the overwhelming pain. It was as if her mind couldn't quite put the frightening event into words,
leaving her with a jumble of images and sensations rather than a coherent narrative.

These fragmented, nonverbal memories, even as she tried to block them, solidified a deep sense of
despair within Isabella. She had come to believe that she couldn't stop bad things from happening to
her, a crushing helplessness that permeated every aspect of her life. This conviction led her to
feel increasingly isolated and set apart from others; how could anyone understand the constant
vigilance, the fear that lurked just beneath the surface of everyday life? The Isabella who used to
laugh easily and find joy in simple things felt utterly gone, replaced by a person who was always
miserable, perpetually bracing for the next catastrophe. She yearned for the carefree happiness she
felt she had lost forever.

Her job, once a source of pride, had become a minefield. The need to avoid situations that could
trigger her anxiety meant she often avoided quiet areas of the office or became visibly agitated
when projects ran over schedule, leading to tension with colleagues. She found herself declining
social invitations, preferring the predictable, albeit lonely, confines of her apartment to the
unpredictable interactions that might expose her to unexpected silences or delays. Isabella finally
sought help, her exhaustion and increasing sense of despair outweighing her reluctance to revisit
the past. She hoped to find a way to reclaim the happiness she felt slipping away, but secretly, she
feared that the world was simply a dangerous place, and she was powerless to protect herself or
Mateo from its inevitable harms.
