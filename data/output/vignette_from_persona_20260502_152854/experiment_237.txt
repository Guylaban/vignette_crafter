Linh, a 61-year-old sculptor of South Eastern Asian heritage, found herself increasingly withdrawn
and consumed by a persistent unease that had eroded the vibrant creativity once central to her life.
For years, she had meticulously shaped clay and stone in her studio, a converted space in an old
industrial building, but now the echoes of a terrifying night resonated through every aspect of her
existence, leaving her feeling perpetually on edge.

The incident occurred late one evening, nearly three years ago, when Linh was finishing a complex
piece. She had been alone, save for a few other artists in their separate studios. Suddenly, a
violent commotion erupted from a neighboring space – a crash, then muffled cries. Peeking cautiously
from her doorway, Linh witnessed a brutal assault on a young artist she knew slightly. The attacker,
a large man whose strong, distinctive cologne filled the corridor, moved with terrifying
unpredictability, his actions swift and merciless. Linh froze, hidden, a silent observer to the
horror. When the assailant finally fled, his heavy footsteps thudding away into the night, Linh
remained paralyzed, the scent of his cologne lingering in the air, the memory burned into her mind.

From that night forward, seemingly innocuous sensations began to trigger an overwhelming sense of
dread. The sudden, unexpected sound of footsteps behind her, or even nearby in her studio building,
would immediately send a jolt through her body, her heart racing and muscles tensing, a classic
startle response. Similarly, catching a whiff of a specific perfume or cologne, even one vaguely
resembling the assailant’s, wouldn't just make her jump; it would plunge her back into the scene, a
flash of the violence unfolding before her eyes. These powerful external triggers would frequently
activate vivid, fragmented memories of the assault, making her feel as if she were reliving the
trauma in agonizing detail.

Even when no actual threat was present, the vivid, disjointed fragments of memory alone would ignite
a powerful physical reaction. Linh found herself experiencing strong physical reactivity – sweating,
trembling, and a racing pulse – simply from the intense, unbidden images that felt more real and
visceral than memories of ordinary things. The inability to construct a clear narrative, the way
these memories kept returning with such raw intensity, solidified her agonizing belief that there
was something fundamentally wrong with her as a person; she couldn't even tell herself a coherent
story of what had happened, let alone explain it clearly to her husband. This profound mistrust she
now harbored towards others, and her growing sense that she was somehow defective, made it almost
impossible to revisit the event consciously, contributing to the jumbled, incoherent quality of her
recollections.

Her deep-seated conviction that the world was a dangerous place kept her perpetually on edge, making
her jumpy and vigilant, especially at night when she found it harder to feel safe than during the
day. This constant state of hyperarousal and physical reactivity she experienced made it incredibly
difficult to process what had happened, ensuring that her memory of the event remained fragmented
and without a coherent story. Her persistent startle response and the relentless physical reactivity
reinforced her growing certainty that the world truly was a dangerous place and that people couldn't
be trusted, a belief that solidified with each jolt of fear. This pervasive sense of danger, the
feeling of being perpetually on guard, sharpened her sensitivity to her environment, causing
seemingly innocuous sounds or smells to register as potent triggers. The very realness of these
recurring memories, feeling more vivid than actual present moments, began to act as internal
triggers, almost conjuring up the external sensations that would then bring on a wave of panic.

Linh’s once vibrant studio, now a place of heightened anxiety, often stood empty. She found herself
unable to focus on her sculpting, the unpredictable nature of working with new materials or the
arrival of clients becoming unbearable. The constant vigilance meant she rarely felt truly present,
even with her husband, who noticed her increasing withdrawal and jumpiness. The world, she was
certain, was a dangerous place, and she, somehow, was ill-equipped to navigate it. It was the
growing chasm between her old self and this new, fearful existence, and her husband's quiet concern,
that finally compelled her to seek help, hoping to reclaim some semblance of the peace she had lost.
