Minh, a 24-year-old painter, first sought therapy after a particularly jarring incident at his art
studio. The physical abuse he endured almost a year ago at the hands of his then-partner had
shattered his sense of safety and left deep, invisible wounds. The relationship had escalated
quickly, moving from emotional manipulation to unpredictable outbursts of violence, often when Minh
least expected it. He remembered one night, after a quiet dinner, when a sudden, unprovoked push
sent him sprawling, followed by a barrage of blows. The memory of the event was not a clear
narrative; instead, it felt like a jarring collection of snapshots where some moments, like the
glint of light off a thrown object, were extremely clear, while others were completely blank,
leaving him with a confused sequence of what exactly happened. This fragmented and disorienting
memory of the event often made him feel as if it were happening right now, even when he was clearly
safe, plunging him into a state of immediate, overwhelming threat. The fragmented and disorienting
nature of these memories also made certain everyday occurrences—like unexpected sounds or moments of
quiet—potent triggers, pulling him back into the internal chaos.

This pervasive sense of danger was further amplified by his internal world. He had come to believe
that the event happened to him because of the sort of person he was, a painful conviction that
fuelled a deep sense of inadequacy. What frightened him most was the gnawing uncertainty that now
permeated his life; when something took longer than expected, he immediately assumed something bad
was happening, and this constant assumption of threat made him scan for exits as soon as he entered
any room or building, even his own studio. This feeling that he was under constant threat not only
made him perpetually on edge but also reinforced his belief that he was inadequate, unable to
protect himself, and fundamentally flawed. The confusing nature of his memories, coupled with his
self-blame, cemented his conviction that he no longer belonged anywhere, a belief that made him even
more vulnerable to the feeling that he was constantly being watched or observed.

These beliefs about his inadequacy and inherent flaw made him acutely sensitive to triggers. He
found that intrusive thoughts about the abuse would often surface without warning, especially when
he was alone, painting. These unwanted thoughts, combined with the feeling of being watched, would
instantly plunge him into a state of intense threat, often bringing back the partial clarity and
confused sequence of the event, making him feel like it was happening right now. In response to such
overwhelming feelings of danger, Minh found himself freezing completely when feeling threatened,
unable to move or speak, a maladaptive strategy that felt like the only way to survive in those
moments. He also began to actively avoid anything that reminded him of his abuser, meticulously
removing objects, changing routes, and even avoiding certain genres of music he once shared with his
ex-partner, hoping to escape the pain.

However, these avoidance behaviours, while offering momentary relief, had unintended consequences.
His efforts to escape perpetrator cues and the general uncertainty paradoxically made him even more
sensitive to potential triggers, as if his nervous system was primed to detect any hint of danger.
The constant hyper-vigilance and the need to scan for exits meant he was always feeling threatened,
a state that made it harder to engage fully with his painting, his passion, and his livelihood. This
pervasive sense of threat made him acutely sensitive, so that even minor cues could now easily
become triggers, pulling him back into the cycle. The relentless pressure of living under constant
threat also made it incredibly difficult for Minh to process or organize his memories, often leaving
them even more muddled and disjointed. He started noticing that the more he tried to avoid dreams by
staying up late or using substances, the more fragmented his memories became, making it harder to
process the event and reinforcing his sense of inadequacy. This cycle of avoidance, heightened
threat, and self-blame had created a tight, suffocating loop.

Minh's preoccupation with intrusion monitoring—listening for sounds that might mean someone was
entering his home—was a constant drain, leaving him exhausted. The constant threat he perceived had
also started to impact his relationship, as his partner struggled to understand why Minh was always
on edge, why he would sometimes freeze mid-conversation, or why he insisted on checking the locks
multiple times before bed. He was certain that his inability to trust, his constant anxiety, and his
self-blame were slowly eroding the foundations of his current relationship, confirming his fear that
he no longer belonged anywhere. It was this creeping isolation, coupled with the realization that
his painting career was suffering due to his inability to focus and his avoidance of public
exhibitions, that finally brought Minh to seek help. He desperately wanted to break free from the
invisible chains that bound him, but he was terrified that the person he had become was now his
permanent reality.
