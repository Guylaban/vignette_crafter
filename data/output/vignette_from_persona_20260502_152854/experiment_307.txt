small boy, he endured physical abuse from his caregiver, a period etched into his memory with
chilling clarity. He recalled the suffocating dread that settled with the onset of darkness, the
rough, scratchy texture of the cheap blanket he was often thrown onto, and the muffled
cries—sometimes his own, sometimes those of a younger child—that filled the terrifying nights. These
experiences made him feel like an object, utterly dehumanized and helpless.

Years later, the darkness of night continued to haunt Ethan. Waking up in the dark, his heart would
pound, his breath catching in his throat, a sudden physical reactivity that left him drenched in
sweat and hypervigilant, scanning his room for danger that wasn't there. His job as a housekeeper
often required him to clean in dimly lit areas, like storage rooms or basements, and these spaces
frequently became potent triggers, plunging him into overwhelming flashbacks where he was suddenly
back in that childhood room, the terrifying past feeling completely real across all his senses at
once. The tactile memory of that rough texture, encountered on a cleaning cloth or a worn rug, could
instantly bring on these intense physical reactions, his body tensing, ready to flee. And the sound
of children crying, especially in the homes he cleaned, would send a jolt through him, making him
freeze, his mind racing to understand what threat it signaled.

These triggers, particularly the dark and the feel of certain fabrics, often led to his memories of
the frightening events playing over and over in his mind, like a film on an endless loop. When these
memories returned, he would feel so overwhelmed by emotion—the fear, the shame—that he had to stop
whatever he was doing, sometimes collapsing onto the floor until the intensity subsided. This
constant replaying of the abuse, feeling so completely real and sensory, reinforced his deeply held
belief that he was still fundamentally dehumanized, an object to be used and discarded, not a person
deserving of respect or safety. The vividness of these memories also solidified his fear that others
could somehow see what had happened to him just by looking at him, a profound sense of being
exposed. This continuous re-experiencing of his past, through memory replay and full sensory
reliving, kept the traumatic content fresh, making him acutely sensitive to environmental cues, so
that even a slight change in lighting or a particular sound could instantly become a potent trigger
for further re-experiencing.

To cope with the relentless intrusion of these memories and the overwhelming emotional response they
provoked, Ethan developed a number of maladaptive strategies. He would often drift off into a world
of his own, a fantasy escape that provided a temporary reprieve from the internal turmoil, though it
left the raw memories untouched and prone to return. When a memory became too intense, or a trigger
too potent, he found himself dropping important responsibilities, abandoning tasks midway through
his cleaning routes, rationalizing it as necessary to stay safe, even if it meant risking his job.
This habit of giving up responsibilities created more idle time, inadvertently leaving him more
vulnerable to the intrusion of the very memories he was trying to escape. His reliance on fantasy
escape, while offering temporary respite, also subtly reinforced his negative appraisal of being
dehumanized, as it implied his current reality was unbearable and he was powerless to change it.

His deep-seated belief that you can never know who will harm you, fueled by the constant re-
experiencing of his past, kept him in a perpetual state of hypervigilance, making him acutely
sensitive to potential threats in his environment, causing more innocuous elements of his day, such
as a sudden noise or a fleeting shadow, to register as potent triggers. This fear of harm also made
him fiercely avoid being alone with another person, even colleagues or clients. This strategy, while
offering a perceived sense of safety, also inadvertently contributed to his feeling of being exposed

and overwhelming physical reactions, which then prompted his escape into fantasy or the abandonment
of his duties, only deepened his conviction that he was damaged and perpetually at risk.

Ethan sought help after a particularly bad week where the crying of a child in a client's home,

eventually lose everything, still believing that the world was an inherently dangerous place where
harm could strike at any moment.
