Jian, a 72-year-old refugee aid worker, arrived for his session with a weary politeness that belied
the profound distress he carried. For decades, his life had been defined by tireless service in the
most volatile corners of the world, but now, a quiet terror had begun to constrict his existence.
The turning point had been an incident three years prior, while he was part of a humanitarian convoy
delivering supplies in a war-torn region. As their vehicle navigated a dusty, unpaved road, a
deafening explosion had ripped through the air, instantly engulfing the lead jeep in a fireball. The
sheer force of the blast, the screams, and the subsequent chaos had been overwhelming. Even now, the
memory is so extreme, he often feels it’s hard to believe it actually happened to him, as if it were
a scene from a horrific film rather than a lived experience.

In the immediate aftermath, Jian found himself grappling with a relentless assault of his senses.
His memories of the frightening event were mostly vivid visual pictures or images: the flash of
light, the debris flying, the faces of his colleagues. When he had these memories, he also sometimes
heard things in his head that he had heard during the frightening event – the shriek of metal, the
cries, the distant gunfire. These intrusive visual and auditory recollections quickly began to
reshape his world. The constant, unbidden playback of the attack meant that Jian felt perpetually on
edge, jumpy and unsettled. This constant intrusion of the past quickly led to a profound shift in
his present experience of safety. He became hyperalert, watchful, and on guard, scrutinizing every
shadow and movement, particularly in unfamiliar surroundings. He found himself startling violently
at ordinary sounds that would not have bothered him before, like a car backfiring or a door slamming
shut, his heart hammering in his chest as if the blast were happening all over again.

Over the ensuing months, these vivid, intrusive memories, coupled with his heightened state of
alert, began to dictate his behaviour. The recurring visual images of the explosion, the faces of
the injured, and the sequence of events leading up to the blast led Jian to repeatedly blame himself
for what happened. He would replay the scene endlessly, convinced he had missed a crucial sign, a
flicker of movement, something that could have prevented the tragedy. This relentless self-reproach,
fueled by the persistent mental replays, made him increasingly unwilling to engage in activities
that might trigger similar sensations. He found himself avoiding driving altogether, or even being a
passenger in a vehicle, especially on unfamiliar routes or in busy traffic, as the enclosed space
and the sounds of the road too closely mirrored the convoy. His work, which once brought him immense
purpose, became increasingly challenging; he could no longer participate in field missions requiring
vehicle travel, limiting his effectiveness and forcing him into an administrative role he found
stifling.

The pervasive sense of threat only amplified the power of his memories. His hypervigilance, the
constant scanning for danger, meant that his senses were always primed, ready to latch onto anything
that might signal a repeat of the trauma. A sudden, unexpected noise, a visual flicker in his
peripheral vision, or even the rumble of a passing truck, would immediately trigger an auditory
recall of the blast, or a vivid visual memory of the explosion. This, in turn, reinforced his belief
that danger was ever-present, further shrinking his world. He came to believe he only felt truly
safe in a very limited number of places – his home, his garden – and even then, he was superalert.
He also found himself actively avoiding any situation that might involve disagreement or
confrontation. The thought of engaging in conflict, even a minor one with his wife, felt too risky,
too volatile, as if it might somehow unleash the chaos he had experienced. His wife, worried by his
increasing withdrawal and the limitations he placed on their life together, finally encouraged him
to seek help. Jian came to therapy hoping to find a way to quiet the relentless images and sounds,
to regain some sense of safety, and to understand why he couldn't shake the feeling that he was
somehow responsible for the terror he had endured. He was tired of his life being dictated by a past
he couldn't escape, afraid that these memories would consume him entirely.
