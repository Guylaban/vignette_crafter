her, or corner her, his face inches from hers, shouting. She remembered the terror of his grip, the
way his eyes would bore into hers, demanding attention, demanding submission, rendering her frozen.

Even after his death, the echoes of that terror lingered, transforming into a pervasive sense of
unease. Lani had come to believe, with a chilling certainty, that her intense reactions to everyday
interactions meant she was a truly "bad coper," a thought that solidified into a conviction that
there was "something fundamentally wrong with her as a person." This deep-seated appraisal of a
"defective self" made any spontaneous physical contact feel like an unbearable exposure, leading her
to instinctively pull away from even the most innocent touch. At work, she found herself flinching
when a colleague, discussing a complex query, might reach across her desk to point at her screen, or
recoil from the brief brush of a shoulder in the hallway. Similarly, the memory of her husband’s
piercing gaze made sustained eye contact feel like a threat, causing her to avert her gaze from
colleagues’ eyes, convinced they would see her perceived brokenness and the shame she carried.

Yet, this very strategy of withdrawing, of keeping others at arm’s length by avoiding eye contact
and any physical proximity, only deepened her internal conviction. The awkward silences, the missed
connections, and the growing sense of isolation that followed reinforced her belief that she was
indeed a "bad coper." She would ruminate on these interactions, replaying them in her mind,
convincing herself that her inability to connect normally was proof of her inadequacy. This constant
self-critique further eroded her sense of who she was – "I feel like I don't know myself anymore,"
she would often lament – confirming her fear that her reactions had irrevocably altered her
identity, leaving her a stranger to herself.

lights. The quiet of her bedroom, the darkness, often brought on a flood of intrusive images from
her past, particularly the moments of being cornered or touched violently. The resulting exhaustion,
while debilitating, was almost preferred to the vulnerability she felt when trying to sleep. This
chronic delay of sleep left her perpetually fatigued, making it harder to concentrate on her
demanding database administration tasks and further fueling her appraisal that she was a "bad coper"
who couldn't even manage basic self-care.

The cumulative effect of these habits began to show. Her colleagues, once friendly, now kept a
polite distance, interpreting her avoidance as aloofness or disinterest. Social invitations
dwindled, and Lani, already convinced of her own defectiveness, found it easier to retreat further
into her shell. She came to therapy feeling profoundly alone, exhausted, and desperately wanting to
understand why she felt so broken, yet terrified that any attempt to change might only confirm her
deepest fear: that there was truly no hope for her.
