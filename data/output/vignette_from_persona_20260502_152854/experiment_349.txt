Robert, 62, a bank teller for over forty years, found his life irrevocably altered one Tuesday
morning. He was carefully wheeling a heavy cart of coin bags across the bank lobby when an elderly
customer, Mrs. Henderson, a woman he knew well from her weekly visits, unexpectedly stepped out from
behind a pillar, distracted. Robert tried to stop, but the cart struck her, sending her sprawling.
The sound of her head hitting the marble floor, the sudden pool of blood, the sirens, and the
frantic scramble of his colleagues became an indelible mark. Mrs. Henderson survived, but with a
permanent brain injury, and Robert was left with an unbearable weight of guilt.

In the months that followed, when fragments of the memory appeared suddenly, often without warning,
they brought back the same terror he felt at the time, an overwhelming surge that left him
breathless and his heart pounding. This visceral recall immediately plunged him into a state of
intense threat, making him feel hyperalert and unable to relax, even when he knew he was in the
safety of his own home. He began to struggle with sleep, lying awake for hours, convinced he needed
to stay alert, constantly scanning his quiet bedroom as if danger lurked in the shadows.

This pervasive state of threat, of being superalert or watchful, made Robert acutely sensitive to
his environment, turning once innocuous daily occurrences into potent triggers. The general stress
of a busy workday at the bank, the pressure of a long line of customers, now felt like an unbearable
burden, immediately tightening his chest and putting him on edge. The familiar faces of related
people, his regular customers, could unexpectedly activate this internal alarm, their presence a
silent reminder of Mrs. Henderson. What truly unnerved him, however, was being touched unprepared; a
colleague’s hand on his shoulder, a customer reaching across the counter, would make him jump
violently, his heart racing.

These sudden jolts from triggers quickly fed into Robert’s negative self-appraisals. Each time he
flinched or felt overwhelmed by stress, he’d feel a profound surge of shame, reinforcing his belief
that he was inadequate and a lousy coper. He had come to believe that he had permanently changed for
the worse, that the man he once was—calm, competent, in control—was gone, replaced by someone
fragile and perpetually on edge. His internal monologue would confirm this: "See? I can't even
handle a simple touch. I'm broken."

His pervasive sense of inadequacy and the conviction that he was a bad coper deepened the emotional
charge of his traumatic memories, making them even more distressing. When he remembered the event,
the guilt on recall was overwhelming, and he would dwell on the specific details of how his actions
led to Mrs. Henderson’s injury, intensifying the terror he felt and making the memory feel almost
unbearable. This entrenched belief that he was permanently damaged and inadequate also made him
anticipate further failures and threats, subtly making him even more prone to being triggered. He
found himself bracing for stressful situations at work, which, paradoxically, only made them more
likely to activate his anxiety.

The constant state of being superalert and watchful, especially at night, significantly impacted the
quality of his memory recall. His difficulty falling asleep because he felt he needed to stay alert
would make the sudden fragments of the memory appear more vividly in the quiet dark, often
accompanied by the intense terror and guilt, trapping him in a cycle of rumination and further
disrupting his sleep. This unending vigilance, the inability to relax even in safe places, also
meant that his body was always primed for danger, making him even more susceptible to being
triggered by the smallest unexpected touch or the most minor stressor.

Over the past year, Robert’s world had shrunk considerably. He stopped taking lunch breaks in the
staff room, preferring to eat alone at his desk to avoid the unpredictable interactions with
colleagues and the possibility of being touched unexpectedly. His withdrawal had begun to strain his
marriage, as his wife struggled to understand his newfound detachment and constant edginess. He knew
he couldn't continue like this; the tightening grip of vigilance and guilt was suffocating him. He
finally sought help because his wife’s quiet despair mirrored his own, and he was terrified that the
man he had become would completely destroy the life he had built. He desperately hoped to find a way
back to a life where he could relax, where his past didn't constantly dictate his present.
