Mateo, a 22-year-old HVAC technician, first sought help after a year of increasing distress
following a devastating accident at a commercial job site. He had been performing routine
maintenance on a large rooftop unit when a heavy, poorly secured component he was working on
unexpectedly detached, crashing down and severely injuring a co-worker below. The metallic shriek of
the falling equipment, followed by the sickening thud and the sudden, chilling silence that
descended afterwards, were seared into his mind. He remembered the coppery scent of blood mixing
with the acrid smell of ozone from the damaged electrical wiring, smells that would later become
potent triggers.

In the immediate aftermath, Mateo was overwhelmed by a crushing sense of guilt. He knew he could
never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened, a thought that gnawed at him
relentlessly. His body, once a reliable instrument for his demanding trade, now felt like it had
betrayed him, prone to sudden tremors and sweats, leading him to believe he could no longer trust
his own physical reactions. When he looked in the mirror, he saw a stranger; the person he had
become was unrecognisable to him, a hollowed-out version of his former self. These profound negative
appraisals of himself and his actions quickly began to shape his behaviour. Because he felt he could
never forgive himself and that his body was unreliable, Mateo started to avoid anything that could
be stressful, fearing his reactions would lead to another disaster.

His avoidance became a rigid pattern. He found himself cancelling plans around specific dates or
anniversaries connected to the event, often calling in sick to avoid the emotional burden. This
constant avoidance of stressful situations, particularly at work, meant he often left tasks
unfinished or delegated them, creating a general sense of unease and making him feel perpetually
vulnerable to something dangerous happening. He also began overanalysing the details of the
accident, going over and over what happened trying to make sense of it, replaying every second in
his mind. Far from providing clarity, this obsessive overanalysing only solidified his belief that
the person he had become was unrecognisable to him, trapping him further in his guilt and
reinforcing his negative appraisals.

Despite his best efforts to suppress it, the memory of the accident would force its way into his
mind when he least wanted it to, often triggered by the metallic clang of tools or the distinct
smell of freon from a compressor, bringing back the olfactory recall of the pungent coolant and the
coppery scent of blood from that day. These intrusive memories, especially when accompanied by the
vivid smells, would immediately trigger strong physical reactions; his heart would race, his palms
would sweat, and he'd feel a surge of adrenaline, convincing him something dangerous was about to
happen again. The constant state of hypervigilance and the physical reactivity he experienced kept
the memory of the accident raw and vivid, making it feel alien, almost like it belonged to someone
else, yet undeniably present and terrifyingly real. His deep-seated inaction guilt made the memory
feel even more detached and alien, as if his mind was trying to distance itself from the unbearable
reality of his perceived failure.

Mateo started to only feel safe in a very limited number of places – his small apartment, his
mother’s house. Even a sudden, unusual silence at a job site, reminiscent of the silence after the
crash, could make him feel that something dangerous was about to happen, sending a jolt of fear
through him. His job, once a source of pride, became a minefield of triggers. He found himself
unable to work in confined spaces, constantly on edge, his senses acutely tuned to any unexpected
noise or smell, perpetually bracing for the next catastrophe.

The tightening grip of these cycles eventually brought Mateo to therapy. He was exhausted, isolated,
and losing his ability to function at work. He feared he would never be able to trust himself again,
that this unrecognisable self was permanent, and that he was destined to live a life haunted by the
past, forever trapped by the memory of what he couldn't stop.
