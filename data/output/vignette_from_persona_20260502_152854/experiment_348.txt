with the rich harmonies of her community orchestra. Her life took a devastating turn three years ago
when she contracted a severe, rapidly progressing lung infection. She spent weeks in intensive care,
intubated and fighting for every breath, surrounded by the clinical smells of disinfectant and the
incessant beeping of machines. The memory of that relentless struggle for air, the invasive medical
procedures, and the constant awareness of her body failing her, became deeply etched into her
experience.

In the aftermath, Leilani found herself irrevocably changed. Her nervous system remained perpetually
on edge, as if still caught in the ICU. She would frequently wake during the night to check whether
she was safe, her heart pounding with an irrational fear that the illness was returning. This
constant state of internal alarm, where her body seemed to betray her with its sudden, unbidden
reactions, only reinforced Leilani's deep-seated belief that she was inadequate and a lousy coper,
unable to control even her own physical responses. She felt a profound sense of self-doubt, certain
she couldn't trust herself to do the right thing, especially when panic seized her.

Living in this hypervigilant state, where her nervous system was constantly primed for danger, meant
that even a faint whiff of antiseptic or a momentary catch in her breath would instantly register as
an urgent threat. These otherwise innocuous sensations became potent triggers, immediately plunging
her back into the terror of her illness. The slightest difficulty breathing or feeling short of
breath, even from climbing a short flight of stairs, would send her into a spiralling panic.
Similarly, the specific, sterile smells she associated with the hospital became unbearable, causing
her to freeze or flee.

The relentless feeling that her body was always poised for danger, reacting before her mind could
even process, drove Leilani to avoid any setting that might trigger such an overwhelming physical
response, especially medical environments. She began to cancel routine check-ups, avoiding physical
examinations and even driving detours to avoid the sight of ambulances or hospitals. Her conviction
that she was a lousy coper, unable to handle the emotional and physical onslaught of her memories,
often led her to ruminate on how different her life would be had she not fallen ill, a torturous
counterfactual thinking that only solidified her self-judgment. She would sometimes tell herself she
deserved to suffer because of what happened, a harsh form of punishment thinking that kept her
trapped in a cycle of self-blame.

Her world began to shrink. Leilani only felt safe in a very limited number of places—her home, and

control and public performance, suffered immensely. She found herself unable to sustain the long
notes, and the stress of potential breathlessness during performances became an insurmountable
hurdle, leading her to withdraw from her orchestra. This further fueled her sense of inadequacy and
deepened her self-doubt. The accumulation of these changes, the growing isolation, and the constant
internal battle eventually led Leilani to seek help. She arrived for her first appointment,
exhausted and hopeful, yet still deeply afraid that she might not be able to trust herself to
navigate the path to healing.
