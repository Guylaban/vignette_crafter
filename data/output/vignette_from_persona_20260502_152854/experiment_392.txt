Rajesh, a 64-year-old South Asian man, presented for therapy with a quiet weariness that seemed to
settle deep in his bones. For decades, he had worked diligently as a warehouse worker, his life
marked by routine and a close-knit marriage. However, an incident from years ago, when he was
ambushed during a late-night shift and held captive for three days in a dark, cold storage unit, had
fractured his sense of security. His captors had taunted him with stories of venomous snakes lurking
in the building's forgotten corners, and the memory of the absolute darkness and the chill that
permeated his confinement remained vivid. The precise moment of his eventual release, early in the
morning, had also etched itself into his mind, becoming a silent, internal alarm.

In the years following, Rajesh found that the world around him was no longer benign. As the sun
began to set, and especially when the clock approached the early hours of the morning that mirrored
his release, a profound unease would descend upon him. These specific *triggers* — the time of day,
the encroaching darkness of night, or even just a conversation about snakes he might overhear at
work — would immediately plunge him into a state of heightened alert. He would find himself feeling
less safe at night than during the day, his senses acutely sharpened, constantly scanning his
surroundings. This *night vigilance* and *hypervigilance* became an automatic response; he was
always on guard, constantly looking behind him, convinced that someone might be following him, even
in the aisles of his familiar warehouse.

To cope with this relentless anxiety, Rajesh developed a series of *maladaptive strategies*. The
thought of lying in the dark, vulnerable and exposed, became unbearable, so he began to actively
*avoid sleep*, often staying up late with the lights on, hoping to outrun the fear that darkness
brought. He also found himself constantly checking his own body for signs of danger or injury, a
habit born from the terror of his captivity, and he would frequently ask his wife for reassurance,
needing to hear her confirm that he was okay, that they were safe. Yet, these efforts to control his
fear proved to be a double-edged sword. By avoiding sleep, he spent more hours awake in the very
darkness that triggered his anxiety, inadvertently making him more vulnerable to the *triggers* he
was trying to escape.

The exhaustion from his sleep avoidance, coupled with the persistent anxiety, slowly began to erode
his inner strength. He started to believe, with a profound sense of *helplessness*, that he simply
couldn't stop bad things from happening to him. This pervasive feeling that he was powerless, along
with the conviction that if he allowed himself to think about the traumatic event, he wouldn't be
able to handle it, became deeply entrenched *negative appraisals*. These beliefs, in turn, fueled
his *threat monitoring*. If he truly couldn't prevent harm, then he *had* to be hypervigilant; if he
couldn't handle the thoughts, he *had* to be constantly on guard, scanning for the inevitable
danger, especially at night when his perceived vulnerability was highest.

This vicious cycle tightened its grip over time. The cumulative effect of his *maladaptive
strategies* — the constant body monitoring and the endless seeking of reassurance — left him feeling
utterly drained. He began to question the value of his existence, thinking, "I am not sure my life
is worth living anymore," a stark realization born from the relentless struggle and the perceived
failure of his coping mechanisms. This deep-seated conviction that his life was no longer meaningful
then intensified his need to be constantly vigilant, making him even more sensitive to potential
threats. At work, the dim lighting in certain sections of the warehouse or the sounds of machinery
would trigger his *hypervigilance*, making him frequently pause to check behind him, impacting his
efficiency and causing concern among his colleagues. His wife had also grown increasingly worried
about his sleepless nights and his repetitive questions, feeling the strain on their once-steady
relationship.

Rajesh finally sought help, arriving at therapy feeling trapped and exhausted. He described a life
now defined by a perpetual state of readiness, a constant battle against an unseen enemy, fearing
that he was irrevocably broken and that true peace was forever out of reach. He hoped, though he
barely dared to articulate it, that someone might finally be able to help him escape the darkness
that had followed him home.
