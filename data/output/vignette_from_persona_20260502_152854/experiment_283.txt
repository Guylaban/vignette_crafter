anchoring her family since their parents immigrated from El Salvador. Her days were a rhythm of
repetitive tasks on the assembly line, a rhythm that had been violently shattered eleven months ago
with the unexpected death of her younger brother, Mateo. He was hit by a speeding car one evening,
just blocks from their shared apartment. Maria had been finishing a late shift when she heard the
distinct, rising wail of sirens approaching her neighborhood, a sound that had instantly filled her
with a nameless dread. Minutes later, the flashing red and blue lights painted the street outside
her window, and then the phone call came, confirming her worst fears. The funeral that followed was
a blur of raw grief, a somber procession that marked the end of her world as she knew it.

Since that night, the world had become a minefield. The blare of a siren, whether from an ambulance
on a distant street or a police car passing her factory, was enough to send a jolt of icy terror
through Maria’s body, instantly transporting her back to that horrifying night. Each time this
happened, she would launch into an obsessive cycle of *overanalysing* the details of Mateo’s
accident, replaying every fragment of information she had, searching desperately for a different
outcome, a moment where things could have been changed. This relentless mental re-enactment never
brought peace; instead, it left her exhausted and increasingly convinced that *her life had been
destroyed by the trauma*.

Her internal landscape, once marked by a quiet contentment, was now dominated by a profound sense of
loss. When a siren would cut through the air, or she would catch sight of a funeral procession on
her way to work, it wasn't just a momentary fright; these triggers solidified her deep-seated belief
that *she used to be a happy person but now she was always miserable*. The contrast between her past
self and her current despair felt stark and insurmountable. This pervasive feeling that *her life
had been destroyed* also made her hyper-alert to any potential reminders of loss, almost as if she
were waiting for the next catastrophe to confirm her bleak outlook. She found herself constantly
scanning her environment, making her even more susceptible to the sudden jarring sound of sirens.

To cope, Maria had developed an elaborate set of strategies. She began *avoiding anything that
reminded her of Mateo*, meticulously planning her routes to steer clear of the cemetery where he was
buried, and declining invitations to family gatherings where his memory might be discussed. At the
factory, the loud, clanking machinery often made her jumpy, and if a coworker, meaning well, ever
began to talk about people losing loved ones, Maria would abruptly change the subject or find an
excuse to leave the breakroom, unable to bear the conversation. This constant *avoidance of grief
reminders*, while offering fleeting relief, only served to deepen her sense of isolation and
reinforced the conviction that *she no longer belonged anywhere* in a world that continued to move
on without Mateo. She often found herself asking her older sister, "Am I going to be okay? Really?"
and while the temporary comfort of *reassurance seeking* was fleeting, the return of her anxiety
would only confirm her internal appraisal that she was not okay, that she truly *no longer belonged
anywhere*.

The relentless cycle had taken its toll. Maria struggled with concentration at work, often making
small errors on the assembly line, and her once-close relationships with her family had frayed under
the weight of her grief and avoidance. She felt perpetually tired, her mind a battlefield of
replayed moments and desperate analyses. It was this profound weariness, the feeling of being
trapped in a loop of fear and despair, that finally led her to seek help. She walked into the
clinic, her shoulders slumped, expressing a desperate hope for relief, even as she admitted her deep
fear that her life, as she knew it, had truly been irrevocably destroyed.
