Leena, a 35-year-old physical therapist, once found immense satisfaction in her work, guiding her
patients through recovery with a gentle yet firm touch. Now, her life felt like a tightrope walk,
every step fraught with the invisible mines laid by a traumatic past. Married and living in a quiet
suburban home, she carried the heavy burden of physical abuse inflicted by a previous romantic
partner, an experience that had reshaped her reality in profound and terrifying ways.

Her memories of that night were not like normal recollections; they felt like they were happening
right now, vivid and terrifying. When she tried to articulate what had happened, the overwhelming
emotion would seize her, making her lose the thread and unable to finish, leaving her breathless and
disoriented. This fragmented, present-tense recall cemented a deep-seated belief: there was
something fundamentally wrong with her as a person, leaving her with a crushing sense of permanent
humiliation.

Convinced that the event happened because of the way she acted and imbued with this profound sense
of a defective self, Leena found herself constantly dwelling on how she used to be before the abuse,
replaying past decisions. She also became consumed by prevention rumination, endlessly replaying
scenarios in her mind, desperate to figure out how the event could have been prevented. In her daily
life as a physical therapist, the constant need for physical touch with patients, or the subtle
pressure of guiding a limb, often served as a trigger for a profound sense of loss of autonomy. When
these moments arose, she would immediately become hypervigilant towards people around her, scanning
the room for danger, her senses on high alert.

A seemingly innocuous specific temperature on a patient's skin, or a brush of a colleague’s arm,
would send a jolt through her, making her flinch, and in that instant, a wave of self-blame would
wash over her, reinforcing the belief that the event happened because of the way she acted, renewing
her deep humiliation. Her pervasive sense of being a defective self and the permanent humiliation
she carried made her acutely sensitive to any situation that might hint at vulnerability or a repeat
of her trauma. This heightened sensitivity meant that even the slightest physical touch or a
perception of being forced to do something would now register as a potent trigger, confirming her
fears.

This constant mental vigilance—her dwelling on past possibilities and her hypervigilance towards
people—kept her nervous system in a perpetual state of alert. Consequently, she found herself more
easily triggered by everyday occurrences, such as a casual touch from her husband or a slight change
in room temperature, which she might once have ignored. At work, she found herself flinching from
patients who needed deep tissue work, her hands hovering, unable to make firm contact, especially if
their skin felt unusually warm or cool. Guiding a patient through range-of-motion exercises, a task
that required her to gently manipulate their limbs, often felt like she was being forced to do
something, evoking a panic that made her withdraw. Her colleagues noticed her increasing reluctance
to take on certain cases, leading to strained professional relationships. At home, her husband’s
attempts at intimacy were met with a rigid body and averted gaze, leaving them both feeling
isolated.

Leena had spent months trying to manage these feelings alone, but the tightening grip of her
symptoms had become unbearable. She was exhausted by the constant vigilance, the relentless
rumination, and the shame that permeated every interaction. The chasm between who she was and who
she used to be felt insurmountable, and the fear that she would never escape this cycle finally
compelled her to seek help, desperate for a way to reclaim her life from the shadows of the past.
