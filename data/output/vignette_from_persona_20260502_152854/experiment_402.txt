Koa, a 65-year-old Project Manager, still vividly remembered the day his son, Leilani, was diagnosed
with a rare and aggressive form of leukemia. The frantic hospital visits, the endless days and
nights in sterile rooms filled with the sterile scent of antiseptic, the incessant beeping of
machines, and the heart-wrenching sight of his child struggling for each breath – it had been a
nightmare from which he never truly woke. Leilani’s eventual passing left Koa with a profound sense
of failure, a belief that had slowly poisoned his life. He was certain that he should have protected
his son, and that he had failed him in the most fundamental way, a conviction that echoed in his
mind, "I should have protected others and I failed." This deep-seated self-blame, the persistent
thought that "the event happened because of the way I acted," underpinned much of his subsequent
distress.

These negative appraisals about his actions and character began to drive his coping. Convinced that
he was inherently flawed and couldn't be trusted to do the right thing, Koa instinctively turned to
maladaptive strategies to manage his overwhelming guilt and fear. He tried hard not to think about
the trauma, suppressing any memory of Leilani’s suffering, and began to keep his mind occupied all
the time, burying himself in complex project schedules and endless tasks at work. This constant
mental distraction, however, only served to reinforce his belief that he was too weak to confront
his past, strengthening his negative appraisals that he couldn't trust himself.

The world around Koa became a minefield of triggers, particularly those tied to the medical world.
The sight of an ambulance siren flashing past his office window, the mention of a hospital, or even
the subtle scent of antiseptic could instantly plunge him back into the terror. His negative
appraisals also made him acutely sensitive to these triggers, as if his perceived failures made him
more vulnerable to reminders. When he encountered these event-related triggers, such as hearing
about other parents whose children were ill, Koa would be overwhelmed by a strong physical
reactivity. His heart would race, his palms would sweat, and he would feel a terrifying difficulty
breathing or feeling short of breath, just as he had watched Leilani struggle. These strong physical
reactions and negative emotions, like intense fear and horror, were a constant companion, stemming
directly from his conviction that he was culpable and incapable.

The threat response, once activated, made him hyper-vigilant. Any slight delay in a project
deadline, any unexpected silence on a phone call, or even a moment of quiet reflection, would lead
him to assume something bad was happening. This heightened sense of physical reactivity, in turn,
primed him to perceive even more situations as triggers, keeping him trapped in a cycle of alarm. As
a result, Koa started to actively avoid medical cues. He skipped his annual physicals, cancelling
appointments repeatedly, and even rerouted his commute to avoid passing the local hospital, despite
it adding significant time to his drive. This avoidance, while offering temporary relief, only
reinforced his underlying belief that he was too fragile to face anything related to his son's
illness, further solidifying his self-doubt that he couldn't trust himself to do the right thing.

His role as a Project Manager, once a source of pride, became a battleground for his avoidance. When
a critical project faced a delay, Koa would experience intense physical reactions, his chest
tightening with the familiar breathlessness, convinced that this delay signified an impending
disaster, mirroring the agonizing waits for test results during Leilani's illness. He'd throw
himself into work, endlessly reviewing plans, trying to keep his mind occupied and suppress the
rising panic, but the underlying fear that he would fail again, that he couldn't trust his own
judgment, persisted.

Now, at 65, widowed and increasingly isolated, Koa found himself at a crossroads. The constant
mental distraction and avoidance had left him emotionally drained and disconnected. The fear of
experiencing strong physical reactions and negative emotions had made him withdraw from friends and
even his surviving family, who, in their own way, were related to the event and thus became
triggers. He finally sought help, exhausted by the relentless cycle, hoping to find a way to escape
the tightening grip of his past, but deeply afraid that his perceived failures meant he was beyond
help.
