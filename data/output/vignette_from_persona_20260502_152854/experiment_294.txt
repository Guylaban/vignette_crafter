Nia, a 63-year-old financial analyst, carried the echoes of a past trauma that had, over decades,
subtly reshaped the contours of her present life. Her journey to America had been born of necessity,
fleeing her home country as a young woman during a period of brutal conflict. She vividly recalled
the terror of being forced to trek for days through dense, unforgiving natural bushland with her
family, constantly hiding, the rustling leaves and snapping twigs underfoot amplifying their fear.
The most searing memory, however, was their encounter with armed military police who intercepted
them. They were aggressive, confiscating their meager possessions, and Nia watched helplessly as her
family was separated and forced to comply with demeaning orders under threat of violence, leaving
her with an enduring terror of being controlled and a profound sense of powerlessness.

In the controlled environment of her high-rise office in downtown Chicago, these distant memories
often felt startlingly close. Even a potted fern in the reception area, or the carefully tended
trees in the park she sometimes glimpsed from her window, could evoke a flash of the primal fear
she’d felt hiding in the unforgiving bush, making her breath catch in her throat. But what truly
tightened the knot in her stomach was any situation that hinted at a loss of autonomy. A new, rigid
corporate directive from senior management, dictating precise, unchangeable procedures, would
immediately trigger that old terror of being forced to do something against her will. This feeling,
so reminiscent of her past helplessness, would then lead her to avoid conflict at all costs. She
found herself agreeing to unreasonable deadlines or taking on extra, unwanted tasks without
objection, rather than risk a confrontation with her supervisors. This ingrained avoidance of
conflict, while seemingly keeping the peace, often left her feeling even more controlled and
resentful, ironically feeding back into her deep-seated fear of losing autonomy.

The presence of authority figures also cast a long shadow over Nia’s days. The sight of a police
patrol car on her commute, or even a security guard in uniform in her building’s lobby, could
instantly transport her back to that harrowing encounter in the bush. This particular trigger
frequently spiraled into an intense period of catastrophising about others. As a single woman with
family scattered across continents, she worried obsessively about bad things happening to her nieces
and nephews, imagining the worst possible scenarios unfolding for them, often calling them multiple
times a day to check in. Her anxiety about authority figures also meant she would go to great
lengths to avoid them. She might take the stairs instead of the elevator if she knew the CEO was on
a particular floor, or choose a longer route to work to bypass the police station. Yet, this very
strategy of avoiding authority figures sometimes led to situations where she felt even more hemmed
in. Missing an important meeting because she was avoiding the manager, or being unable to voice her
perspective on a project, often resulted in her being given directives she had no say in, thereby
amplifying her sense of lost autonomy.

This cycle had tightened its grip over the years. Her constant avoidance of conflict had led to her
taking on an increasingly heavy workload, as colleagues learned she would rarely push back, leaving
her perpetually overworked and feeling trapped by her commitments—a profound occupational
consequence. Now, at 63, the exhaustion of constantly managing these internal battles had become
overwhelming. Nia came to therapy feeling perpetually on edge, caught in a self-reinforcing loop
where every perceived threat to her independence or safety, no matter how small, compounded her
anxieties and drove her further into strategies that, while providing momentary relief, ultimately
left her feeling even more helpless and alone. She hoped to find a way to break free from the
invisible chains that still bound her to a past she had physically escaped decades ago.
