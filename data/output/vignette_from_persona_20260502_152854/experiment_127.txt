Mai, a 38-year-old single woman of South Eastern Asian descent, presented to therapy feeling trapped
in a cycle of overwhelming guilt and isolation. Her life, once defined by the precise and demanding
work of welding, had been irrevocably altered by a traumatic incident six months prior. Mai had been
on a construction site, meticulously welding a crucial support beam for a scaffolding structure. A
moment of intense focus, perhaps too intense, had led to a subtle error in her technique, a weakness
she hadn't detected. Later that day, she watched in horror as a section of the scaffolding, the very
part she had worked on, buckled under the weight of a colleague. The sound of groaning metal,
followed by his pained cry as he fell, was seared into her mind. In the immediate aftermath, as she
rushed to help, she distinctly remembered the acrid, metallic taste in her mouth, a visceral flavour
of dread that still returned with chilling clarity.

The memory of the accident, particularly the intense guilt that overwhelmed her upon its recall,
became an unbearable burden for Mai. She found herself plagued by an incomplete picture of what
happened; she could vividly recall the metallic taste and the sound of the collapse, but crucial
details felt missing, like trying to grasp smoke. This fragmentation, combined with the crushing
guilt, led her to adopt maladaptive strategies to cope. She started to avoid other people at work,
especially her colleagues, convinced that they blamed her for the accident, or that she would
inevitably betray her own culpability through conversation. She tried desperately to avoid thinking
about the event, pushing the images away whenever they surfaced, but this only seemed to make her
dwell more intensely on what she had done wrong, replaying the sequence of events in a painful self-
blame rumination.

These intrusive, guilt-laden memories, particularly the recurring metallic taste and the fragmented
images, profoundly impacted Mai's sense of threat. She began to experience severe sleep problems,
often lying awake for hours, tossing and turning as her mind replayed the incident. During the day,
she struggled with having difficulty concentrating, her thoughts constantly drifting back to the
site, to her colleague, to her perceived failure. Her chronic sleep deprivation and difficulty
concentrating meant her mind felt perpetually on edge, making her more susceptible to sudden,
intrusive images of the accident, creating a vicious loop where her heightened threat response
intensified her painful recollections.

Mai’s attempts to avoid thinking about the event and her withdrawal from colleagues created further
problems. By actively avoiding conversations about the incident and pushing away any thoughts of it,
she inadvertently kept the memory from consolidating properly, leaving her with an incomplete, yet
intensely painful, picture that she couldn't escape. This incomplete memory fueled her self-blame,
convincing her that she needed to keep the full horror at bay. Her occupation as a welder, once a
source of pride, became a minefield of triggers. The smell of welding fumes, the specific sound of
grinding metal, or even seeing scaffolding on other projects would cause strong physical
reactions—her heart would pound, her breath would quicken, and a wave of nausea would wash over her.
These physical reactions, born from the constant threat she felt, further cemented her belief that
she was perpetually unsafe and responsible, making it even harder to escape the grip of her
fragmented, guilt-ridden memories.

Mai's once-reliable work performance suffered significantly due to her inability to concentrate and
her avoidance of team projects. She started taking on less complex welding jobs, isolating herself
in her bay, and missing deadlines, which was completely out of character. This decline in her
professional life, coupled with her growing social isolation, finally brought her to seek help. She
felt exhausted, constantly on guard, and utterly alone, desperately hoping to find a way to process
the event, to quiet the relentless voice of self-blame, and to escape the feeling that she was
constantly living in the shadow of that terrible day.
