David, a 36-year-old married retail worker, remembers the accident as if it were yesterday, though
it happened five years ago. He had been working a busy holiday shift in a large department store
when a poorly maintained display structure collapsed, sending splintered wood and rough fabric
cascading down. He was near the registers, helping a customer, when the chaos erupted. People
panicked, pushing and trampling each other in a desperate scramble for safety. David found himself
caught in the crush, surrounded by debris, witnessing the raw fear and selfishness of others. He
remembers it in vivid, frightening snapshot memories, each a stark picture in his mind, feeling an
intense shame on recall that he hadn't been able to help more, or perhaps that he had frozen.

Now, his daily life at work is a minefield. The physical proximity of customers, especially during
busy periods, acts as an immediate trigger, instantly conjuring his memories of the collapse and
making them feel more real and vivid than memories of ordinary things. The way people sometimes push
or cut lines, a minor moral violation, will immediately bring him back to the panic he witnessed,
feeling a wave of that familiar shame on recall for his perceived inaction. These triggers make his
memories of the event so potent that they then frequently trigger an urgent need to escape, forcing
him to find excuses to retreat to the back room or step outside.

To cope with these overwhelming memories and the distress from triggers, David has developed several
maladaptive strategies. He tries hard to suppress thoughts about the trauma, pushing them away
whenever they arise, believing that if he doesn't think about it, it can't hurt him. This
suppression, however, paradoxically makes the memories feel even more persistent, sometimes leading
to them feeling even more real than normal later on, as if his mind is trying to force him to
acknowledge what he's pushing away. When these memories become too intrusive, or when he encounters
a new trigger like the tactile texture of a rough cardboard box or a splintered wooden pallet at
work, he sometimes tells himself that the event did not really happen or was not that bad. This
magical thinking is a desperate attempt to distance himself from the reality of the past, but it
only offers fleeting relief, leaving him unprepared for the next trigger.

The constant battle against these triggers and memories often exhausts David. The shame on recall
associated with his vivid snapshot memories then makes him hyper-vigilant to potential triggers,
causing him to perceive even innocent interactions as signs of people misbehaving, further
tightening the loop of his distress. When the internal pressure from these intrusive memories and
the constant vigilance becomes unbearable, he sometimes resorts to self-harm distraction, hurting
himself physically to stop thinking about the event. This self-harm momentarily provides a perverse
sense of control, but it ultimately reinforces the idea that the memories are too dangerous to
confront directly, making him even more sensitive to triggers. His attempts to suppress thoughts
about the trauma mean he avoids processing the event, which keeps the memories fragmented and vivid,
ready to be reactivated by the smallest trigger.

His marriage has grown strained; his wife, confused by his sudden withdrawals and unpredictable
emotional states, feels increasingly shut out. At work, his performance suffers. He avoids certain
aisles, struggles with moving stock, and finds himself snapping at colleagues, creating a tense
atmosphere. David recently sought help after a particularly bad shift where the crush of holiday
shoppers felt too real, too overwhelming, and he found himself resorting to self-harm again. He
fears that the memories are slowly consuming him, certain that if he doesn't get help, he'll lose
everything he still holds dear.
