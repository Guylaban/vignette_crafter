Arthur, a 74-year-old retired history teacher who now tutors high school students, presented for
therapy feeling exhausted and perpetually on edge. His struggles began five years prior, following
Hurricane Agatha, which had made a direct hit on his coastal home. He remembered the terrifying roar
of the wind, the water creeping relentlessly higher, and the sickening crunch as a section of his
roof collapsed, trapping him in the living room for nearly a day. The putrid smell of damp plaster,
salt water, and a faint, acrid scent of gas had permeated everything, and the agonizing delay in
rescue had left him certain he would not survive.

Since then, Arthur found that the memory of Agatha would force its way into his mind when he least
expected it, often triggered by something as innocuous as a news report about a storm or the distant
wail of a siren. These moments of uncontrolled access to the memory were deeply unsettling. When the
vivid images of the roof giving way or the rising water intruded, Arthur would immediately feel a
profound sense of unease, which quickly escalated into a heightened state of threat. He found
himself constantly scanning his surroundings for signs of danger, his eyes darting from window to
door, scrutinizing every shadow and movement, convinced that something catastrophic was always on
the verge of happening. This pervasive feeling of being unsafe meant he couldn't relax unless his
back was firmly against a wall, allowing him to see the entire room, a habit that made social
gatherings and even sitting in his favourite armchair at home feel like a strategic manoeuvre.

To cope with these intrusive memories and the overwhelming sense of threat they brought, Arthur
developed several strategies. Often, when the memory forced its way in, he would try to mentally
distance himself, pretending it happened to someone else, as if he were recounting a story from a
book rather than his own lived terror. This dissociation offered a fleeting reprieve from the
emotional pain, yet it also meant that sometimes he could recall what happened but feel completely
numb, as if it happened to a stranger, leaving him with a sense of unreality about his own
experience. His constant vigilance and the assumption that something bad was always about to happen,
particularly when anything took longer than expected, also fueled a relentless rumination about how
the event could have been prevented. He replayed scenarios endlessly, searching for a flaw in his
past actions, a way he could have outsmarted the storm.

This persistent rumination and the ever-present sense of danger led Arthur to systematically avoid
anything that could be stressful. He stopped watching the news, declined invitations to social
events that felt too "unpredictable," and even limited his tutoring hours, fearing that unexpected
questions or a student’s emotional distress might push him over the edge. While these maladaptive
strategies offered a superficial sense of control, they inadvertently tightened the grip of his
distress. His attempts to suppress the memory through dissociation, for instance, seemed to
ironically make the memory more fragmented and elusive, yet it still found ways to intrude, often
accompanied by the phantom smell of damp and gas. Moreover, his avoidance of stressors, while
intended to keep him safe, prevented him from learning that many situations were not, in fact,
dangerous, thus maintaining and even exacerbating his heightened perception of threat. He was
trapped in a self-reinforcing cycle, where the memory fueled the threat, which in turn drove his
coping strategies, only for those strategies to keep the memory and the threat alive.

His partner, Eleanor, had noticed Arthur's increasing withdrawal and his peculiar habit of always
positioning himself with his back to a wall, even in their own home. She had also gently pointed out
his growing agitation whenever a student was late for a tutoring session at the local community
center, his assumption that something terrible must have befallen them. Arthur, once a vibrant and
engaged tutor, now struggled to focus, his mind constantly darting to potential dangers, and his
students sometimes picked up on his distant, emotionless recall of historical events when his own
past felt too close. He sought help because he realized his world had shrunk to a fraction of its
former size, and he was terrified that he would spend his remaining years locked in a constant,
exhausting battle against an invisible enemy.
