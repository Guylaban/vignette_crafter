shadowy figures moving through the trees, their intentions unclear, but undeniably menacing. She had
survived, but a part of her remained trapped in that terrifying night.

Now, at 72, Asha carried the weight of that night with her daily, even in the serene forests of her
adopted home in the United States. Her occupation as a forester, which she deeply loved, became a
constant battleground. The deepening twilight, or waking up alone in her dark home, would instantly
plunge her back into that primal fear, her heart pounding with the memory of the village under
siege. Any sudden, loud bang – a distant backfire from a logging truck, the crack of a branch under
strain, or even thunder – would send a jolt of terror through her, making her freeze, scanning
frantically for the source, convinced for a split second that the war had returned.

Her days were consumed by an almost ceaseless vigilance. When working deep in the forest, Asha would

shadow. This checking, while an attempt to secure her safety, often heightened her sensitivity,
making her more prone to interpreting ambiguous sounds as immediate threats. She was hypervigilant

dangers. She had come to believe that trusting anyone too readily was a perilous mistake, a lesson
etched into her by the betrayal and violence she witnessed.

the intrusive images that often surfaced. Yet, even as the noise filled the air, a deep part of Asha

trying to escape, like a distant backfire that would send her heart racing. Her constant scanning of
the dense forest around her, while intended to ensure safety, sometimes made her particularly
susceptible to the rustle of leaves or the snap of a twig, which could instantly escalate into a
perceived threat.

This relentless state of alert had taken a significant toll. Asha, though physically robust for her
age, felt perpetually exhausted. Her social life, already diminished since her husband’s passing,

danger, always on guard. Her work, once a source of peace, now felt like a high-stakes mission,
demanding every ounce of her mental energy. The cumulative burden of decades living in this state of
hyper-arousal finally brought her to seek help. Asha yearned for a night of undisturbed sleep, for a

never truly feel safe again.
