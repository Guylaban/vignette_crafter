sorrow etched into the lines around his eyes. The genesis of his struggle lay decades in the past,
when his infant son, Minh, was diagnosed with a life-threatening congenital heart condition. The
hospital became their second home, a place of sterile terror where the scent of antiseptic hung
heavy in the air, mixing with the metallic tang of fear. Bao and his late wife spent countless
nights by Minh’s incubator, watching him undergo one medical procedure after another, each one a
fresh assault on their fragile hope. The initial joy of Minh’s birth was brutally usurped by an
overwhelming sense of horror, as they navigated a world of doctors, nurses, and the constant, high-
stakes drama of pediatric intensive care. Though Minh ultimately survived, thriving into adulthood,
the experience never truly left Bao.

In the years that followed, certain aspects of daily life became landmines. Even now, encountering
specific **triggers**, like the unmistakable hospital or antiseptic smell, or the sight of medical
instruments during a routine check-up, would instantly flood Bao with strong negative feelings such
as fear and horror. He’d feel a sudden rush of adrenaline, his heart pounding in his chest, a wave
of physical reactivity that made him want to bolt from the room. These triggers didn’t just provoke
strong emotions; they had a profound effect on his internal world, immediately causing his
**memories** of the frightening event to replay like a film that played over and over. He would find
himself back in the sterile corridors, reliving the agonizing wait outside the operating room, the
terror of seeing his tiny son hooked up to machines.

The constant replay of those terrifying scenes, feeling as if they were happening right now rather
than something that was over, meant that his mind was always on high alert. This vivid, present-
tense quality of his **memory** made him acutely sensitive to anything that even remotely resembled
a hospital setting, effectively broadening the range of what could act as a **trigger**. A news
report about a new medical technology, or even an advertisement on his computer screen for a baby
product, could plunge him back into that unbearable past, reminding him of Minh’s early fragility.
When these powerful memories took hold, they often manifested as full-blown flashbacks, where he
would suddenly feel or act as if the stressful experience were actually happening again. During
these intense episodes, his strong negative emotions and physical reactivity would surge, further
intensifying the vividness of the **memory**, making it feel even more immediate and terrifying.

Working as a cybersecurity analyst from his home office offered Bao some refuge, allowing him to
largely control his environment. Yet, even there, the pervasive nature of his past trauma would
intrude. A brief online video about a new hospital wing, or the unexpected sight of a pregnant woman
on his screen, could halt his focus entirely. The persistent internal replay of his son's illness,
the feeling that it was happening right now, meant that despite his best efforts, he often lost the
thread when trying to explain what had happened, unable to finish his thoughts as the past asserted
its dominance. After his wife passed away a few years ago, the isolation deepened, leaving him with
no one to share the burden of these relentless internal battles. He felt increasingly trapped, the
once-faint echoes of his son’s illness now a constant, deafening roar. It was this tightening grip
of the past, the pervasive sense that the trauma was still unfolding, that finally led Bao to seek
help, hoping to find some way to live in the present without constantly reliving the most terrifying
chapter of his life.
