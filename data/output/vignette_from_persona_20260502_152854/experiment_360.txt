by a persistent unease that had taken root after a life-threatening motor vehicle collision six
months prior. The accident had been a brutal, unexpected event: driving her work van to a job site
one Tuesday morning, she was T-boned by a speeding vehicle that ran a red light. The impact was
deafening, crushing the side of her van and pinning her in the driver’s seat. Though she eventually
recovered physically from a broken arm and severe bruising, the mental scars ran deeper. The other
driver had fled the scene, leaving Chloe with a profound sense of violation and a legal battle that
felt futile, cementing her belief that what happened to her was deeply unfair and nothing had been
done about it.

In the months since, Chloe found herself increasingly on edge. Her work, which she once loved, had
become a minefield of apprehension. Even the simple act of driving her work van to different sites,
a core part of her job, now brought with it a gnawing anxiety. If an apprentice or a more senior
colleague joined her in the vehicle, she would become acutely aware of every turn, every brake
application, every perceived risk on the road. This specific trigger of driving with others would
immediately flood her with the terrifying thought, "I can't trust that I will do the right thing to
keep us safe." She was certain that her ability to react, to protect, had been compromised, leaving
her with an overwhelming sense of self-doubt. This pervasive feeling that she could no longer trust
her own body to respond appropriately, a direct echo of her helplessness in the crash, made her
dread every journey she had to share, often inventing excuses to drive alone.

Her deep-seated conviction that she could no longer trust her own body had a profound impact on her
daily existence, subtly amplifying her triggers. Any unexpected jolt or slight disorientation while
on a ladder at a job site would instantly make her hyper-vigilant, priming her to perceive danger
more readily when she later encountered the crash imagery that dotted her commute. Seeing a mangled
car on the shoulder of the highway, or even just a news report about an accident, would ignite a
visceral memory of her own collision. This trigger of crash imagery would then immediately bring to
the forefront her deeply held negative appraisal: "What happened to me was deeply unfair and nothing
has been done about it." The sight of destruction and the perceived lack of justice in the world
would leave her feeling enraged and helpless, reinforcing the idea that she was a victim of an
uncaring system.

This cycle extended beyond the immediate reminders of the accident. Chloe’s pervasive self-doubt,
the feeling that she couldn’t trust herself to do the right thing, made any conversation about her
future career path or personal goals feel overwhelming. When her supervisor asked about her long-
term aspirations as an electrician, or friends inquired about her plans after her initial
apprenticeship, this trigger of talking about the future would surface a paralyzing anxiety. She
felt a profound sense of injustice that her future, which had once seemed so clear, now felt
irrevocably tainted by the accident. "How can I plan anything when I can’t even trust my own body,
or that I’ll make the right choice when it matters?" she would think, the two appraisals feeding
into each other, making the very idea of a future feel like a cruel joke. This made her withdraw
from social situations where such topics might arise, isolating herself further.

Chloe decided to seek help after she realized her avoidance of driving with others was beginning to
affect her career progression; she was turning down opportunities that required her to carpool to
distant sites or mentor junior apprentices. She felt stuck, caught in a tightening loop of fear and
injustice, acutely aware that her life was shrinking around her. What frightened her most was the
thought that she might never regain the confidence she once had, that this fractured sense of self
would become her permanent reality.
