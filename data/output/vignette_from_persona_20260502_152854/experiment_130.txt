pharmaceutical plant six months prior. He remembered the metallic tang in the air, the sudden
burning in his throat and eyes, and the frantic scramble to find the emergency shut-off after a pipe
burst, spewing an acrid, unknown chemical into the enclosed ventilation shaft where he was working.
He’d been alone, the alarm systems failing, and for what felt like an eternity, he was certain he
was going to suffocate, his body convulsing in the toxic haze. Though he was eventually rescued and
medically cleared, that harrowing experience had irrevocably altered his sense of the world.

Now, Bao’s body felt constantly on alert, a pervasive tension gripping his shoulders and stomach,
ready to react to danger that wasn't physically present. This internal alarm was relentless, making
it impossible for him to relax. He found himself actively avoiding any situation that might lead to
a sense of calm, convinced that lowering his guard, even for a moment, would leave him vulnerable to
another unseen threat. If his body wasn't tense, he reasoned, he wasn't being vigilant enough. This
constant state of physiological arousal, his body perpetually poised for fight or flight, profoundly
undermined his ability to trust safety. Even when his wife, Linh, assured him that their home was
secure, or when his colleagues confirmed a worksite was clear, he found it impossible to truly
believe them. He would nod, but inside, a knot of dread would tighten, certain that danger lurked
just beyond his perception.

This profound inability to trust safety, coupled with his body’s incessant readiness for threat,
directly fueled a series of maladaptive strategies that had begun to dominate Bao’s life. He
developed a compulsive habit of repeatedly checking that doors and windows were locked, sometimes
returning to the same lock three or four times before he could move on, his mind cycling through
imagined scenarios of intrusion or contamination. The thought of being alone, especially in a quiet
room or building, now filled him with overwhelming dread, echoing the suffocating isolation he’d
experienced in the ventilation shaft. He would linger in the garage until Linh was home, or make
excuses to stay in the living room if she was elsewhere in the house, unable to shake the feeling
that being by himself was a precursor to disaster.

His constant vigilance and the deeply ingrained belief that safety was an illusion also led him to
avoid anything that could be stressful. This wasn’t just about major life events; it extended to
everyday interactions. He started declining overtime shifts, even though the extra income was
important, because the thought of encountering a new or challenging repair job felt too
overwhelming, too unpredictable. He stopped attending family gatherings, finding the noise and the
need for social interaction too taxing, fearing that any unexpected turn in conversation or event
might trigger his internal alarm. Linh noticed him withdrawing, becoming quiet and distant, and
their once easy conversations were now strained, punctuated by his distracted gaze and his frequent
checks of the front door. He knew his avoidance was creating distance, but the perceived danger of
engaging, of letting his guard down, felt far more immediate and pressing.

The cumulative effect of these changes had been debilitating. Bao, who once took pride in his
meticulous work and his calm demeanour, now felt trapped in a cycle of fear and avoidance. He came
to therapy, not entirely sure what could be done, but desperate for a reprieve from the tightening
grip of his own mind. What frightened him most was the possibility that this hypervigilance and
distrust would never truly subside, leaving him forever isolated and on edge, unable to reclaim the
sense of security he had lost.
