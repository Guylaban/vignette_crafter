when the improvised explosive device detonated near their MASH unit. The concussive blast threw her
against a triage tent pole, the air thick with dust and the metallic tang of blood. She'd been
kneeling over a young soldier, trying to staunch a major femoral bleed with a rough, canvas-like
bandage, when the world dissolved into noise and chaos. The distinct, rhythmic thud of an
approaching Black Hawk, meant to be a sound of rescue, instead became intertwined with the rising
panic as she struggled to maintain pressure on the wound amidst the tremors and shouts. That coarse
texture of the bandage, now soaked and gritty, fused with the terror of that moment, an indelible
sensory mark.

In the years that followed, Aisha’s return to civilian life, and her demanding surgical residency,
was shadowed by an invisible war. The memory of the explosion, the patient, and the frantic
helicopter overhead didn’t fade. Instead, the full memory would often return without warning, not as
a coherent story she could articulate, but as a visceral surge of the same terror she had felt at
the time, paralyzing her for moments. It was this persistent, nonverbal memory, the sheer raw
feeling of it, that made her so acutely sensitive to certain aspects of her daily environment. A
loud bang from a dropped tray in the operating room, or the distant thrum of a news helicopter
passing overhead, could instantly yank Aisha back to that moment, triggering a cascade of dread.

These sudden, overwhelming triggers then compelled Aisha into a relentless vigilance. If a
helicopter sound or an unexpected explosion-like bang pierced the relative quiet of the hospital, or
if she encountered a specific rough texture—like the coarse weave of a new hospital gown or a
scratchy blanket—her mind would immediately pivot to threat assessment. She had come to believe that
danger could re-emerge at any moment, and so, before entering any new space, be it a crowded
cafeteria or a quiet consultation room, Aisha would meticulously plan an escape route. She’d scan
the exits, note potential obstacles, and mentally rehearse her movements. This constant checking of
her surroundings became an ingrained habit, a silent ritual she performed even in the most benign
settings.

But this very vigilance, meant to keep her safe, ironically tightened the grip of her fears. Her
maladaptive strategies, particularly the constant scanning of her environment, made her hyper-
attuned to potential threats, amplifying her perception of triggers. The distant hum of the
hospital’s HVAC system, which most people filtered out, became a nascent helicopter drone to Aisha,
demanding her immediate attention. The click of a door latch, amplified by her heightened state,
could sound like a precursor to another bang. She found herself always sitting with her back against
a wall in the staff lounge or during conferences, needing to see the entire room, convinced that if
she didn't, she'd miss a crucial sign of impending disaster. This meant she often appeared withdrawn
or unapproachable to colleagues, and her social interactions dwindled.

The cumulative effect began to impact her professional life. Her focus, once razor-sharp in the
operating theatre, was now frequently split. During complex procedures, a specific tactile sensation
from a surgical mesh or a sudden, unexpected sound from an instrument falling could briefly flood
her with the terror of her past, making it challenging to maintain concentration. Aisha knew
intellectually that the hospital was safe, but her body and mind didn't agree. She felt exhausted,
always on edge, and the joy she once found in her demanding work was slowly being eroded by a
constant, low-level hum of anxiety. It was this erosion, the fear that she might one day make a
critical error due to her internal battles, that finally brought her to seek help. She desperately
hoped to regain control, but what frightened her most was the idea that these memories and fears
might be an inseparable part of her now, forever diminishing the surgeon she aspired to be.
