Priya, a seasoned diplomat of fifty, had always prided herself on her composure and sharp intellect,
qualities that served her well in high-stakes international negotiations. But that changed
irrevocably during a site visit in a volatile region, where a sudden, unexpected breach in a
chemical storage facility left her gasping, the acrid, metallic taste of the fumes searing her
throat and lungs. She remembered the panic, the disorientation, and the crushing certainty that she
was about to die.

Months later, the physical effects had subsided, but the psychological scars were deepening. The
memories of the event no longer felt like a past experience; instead, something in her surroundings
could instantly bring back the full memory without warning. A sudden, sharp smell in the embassy
cafeteria, or the metallic tang of a new pen, would flood her senses, and she would find herself
back in that choking moment, the taste of the chemical vivid on her tongue as if it were happening
all over again. These intrusive flashbacks were profoundly distressing, and in their wake, Priya
would instinctively recoil, telling herself that the event did not really happen or was not that
bad, desperately trying to mentally erase the terror she had just re-experienced. This effort to
avoid thinking about the event became a primary coping mechanism, a way to fend off the overwhelming
sensations.

Each time a memory was triggered, Priya’s body would instantly respond as if the danger were
imminent. Her muscles would tense, her heart would race, and she would feel constantly on alert, her
body ready to react to danger. This heightened physiological state often manifested as irritable
behavior, leading to angry outbursts at colleagues who, to her, seemed oblivious to the hidden
threats she perceived everywhere. She found herself constantly scanning her surroundings for signs
of danger, her eyes darting across rooms, her ears straining for any unusual sound. This constant
vigilance, ironically, made her more prone to being triggered; the perpetual state of readiness for
threat meant that even minor sensory inputs could pull her back into a full, horrifying memory,
creating a relentless cycle.

As the months wore on, Priya’s attempts to avoid anything that could be stressful became
increasingly pervasive. She started declining assignments that involved travel to unfamiliar places
or visits to industrial sites, fearing that new environments would present unforeseen triggers. She
found herself struggling to articulate the traumatic event to her therapist; when she tried to
explain what happened, she would lose the thread and simply could not finish, the narrative
fragmented by her own efforts to suppress it. This deliberate avoidance of thinking about the event,
while providing momentary relief, seemed to keep the memories raw and unintegrated, making them more
difficult to process or recount coherently. Her persistent avoidance of stressors, driven by the
fear of further triggers, also kept her body locked in a state of hypervigilance. She believed that
by keeping her world small and predictable, she could protect herself, yet this very strategy
ensured her body remained perpetually tense and ready for an attack that never came, leaving her
exhausted and isolated.

Now, almost a year later, the impact on her diplomatic career was undeniable. Her colleagues noticed
her increasing irritability and her reluctance to engage in challenging assignments. Priya found
herself at a crossroads, the constant tension and the unpredictable nature of her memories making
her once-vibrant life feel brittle and unmanageable. She came to therapy seeking a way out of the
relentless cycle, exhausted by the effort of constantly being on guard and terrified that the taste
of fear would be with her forever.
