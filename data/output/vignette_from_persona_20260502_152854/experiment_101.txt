Arthur Miller, a 67-year-old widower and a retired military medic, found his life irrevocably
altered after a horrific motor vehicle collision just over a year ago. He had been driving home from
a volunteer shift when an impaired driver swerved into his lane, causing a devastating multi-car
pile-up. Though he survived with physical injuries that eventually healed, the memory of the mangled
vehicles, the acrid burnt smell of oil and rubber, and the pervasive reek of alcohol from the other
driver seared themselves into his mind. These sensory details became potent triggers, and now, even
a faint whiff of stale beer or the sight of a crumpled fender on the side of the road would
immediately send a jolt through him, making his heart race and his muscles tense with a familiar
physical reactivity. These sudden jolts often dragged him back to the scene, fragmented images and
sounds flashing before his eyes, sometimes including the distinct auditory recall of screaming and
grinding metal.

In the aftermath, Arthur struggled immensely to make sense of what had happened. When he tried to
explain the event, he would often lose the thread and simply couldn't finish, leaving him feeling
frustrated and isolated. The whole memory, in fact, began to feel alien, as if it belonged to
someone else entirely, a detached horror he couldn't fully claim. This jumbled, incoherent nature of
his memories, particularly his inability to clearly explain what happened, only solidified his
belief that he was a weak person, unable to even articulate his own experience. He became convinced
that the accident was his fault because of the way he'd acted, constantly replaying his reactions in
the moments leading up to and immediately following the crash. This persistent self-blame cast a
strange filter over his recollections, making them feel increasingly detached. His conviction that
he was to blame seemed to sharpen his senses, making him acutely aware of any potential reminder of
the crash, turning everyday occurrences into startling triggers.

Convinced that the accident was his fault and deeply doubting his ability to react correctly in a
crisis, Arthur began to meticulously monitor his own thoughts, terrified he was losing his mind. He
found himself constantly checking for signs of "going crazy," a maladaptive strategy that consumed
much of his mental energy. He also tried hard not to think about the trauma, pushing away any
intrusive images or sounds with all his might. The more he avoided driving, opting for taxis or
asking his daughter for rides instead of getting behind the wheel of his own car, and the more he
tried to suppress his thoughts, the more his self-doubt intensified; he felt like a coward, unable
to face his own past, reinforcing his belief that he was a weak person. This relentless mental
effort, coupled with his avoidance, kept his nervous system on high alert, ensuring that any minor
startle to ordinary sounds quickly escalated into full-blown physical reactivity.

His belief that he was fundamentally a weak person, someone who couldn't be trusted to do the right
thing, left him in a constant state of hyper-vigilance, anticipating danger around every corner.
This heightened state of arousal meant that even the slightest sound or smell could easily become a
potent trigger, sending him into a panic. When the fragmented memories of the crash would surface,
Arthur would immediately try to suppress them, pushing the images and sounds away with all his
might. The constant physical reactivity and feeling jumpy pushed Arthur further into avoiding
driving, convinced it was the only way to keep himself safe. Paradoxically, his constant monitoring
of his thoughts and attempts to suppress them made him hyper-vigilant for any potential trigger,

sounds, turning them into fresh triggers. His attempts to suppress thoughts about the trauma, while
offering temporary relief, seemed to further fragment his memory, making it even harder to piece
together what truly happened, leaving him adrift in a sea of uncertainty.

Arthur finally sought help after his daughter expressed significant concern about his increasing
isolation and persistent jumpiness. He yearned to feel like himself again, to drive to the grocery
store without a crippling fear, and most of all, to understand what had truly happened that day,
instead of living with the constant, terrifying feeling that he was unraveling.
