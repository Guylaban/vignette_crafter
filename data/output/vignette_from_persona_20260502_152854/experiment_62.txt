Marcus, a 45-year-old African American man, presented for therapy, describing a life increasingly
constrained by an event from his childhood. He works diligently as an agricultural worker, his days
often spent in vast, open fields, yet his inner world feels anything but free. He lives with his
partner, but even in their shared space, a deep sense of unease has taken root. The trauma he
endured was witnessing severe domestic violence involving a beloved family member when he was just
ten years old. He recalled the terrifying sounds and images, the feeling of helplessness as he hid,
unable to intervene. This core memory has left him with an enduring "Inaction Guilt"; he often
thinks, "I will never forgive myself for not doing more to stop what happened," a thought that
shadows his every quiet moment.

In the years that followed, this profound guilt, coupled with the raw brutality he witnessed, led
Marcus to a crushing realization that "I have lost my faith in people, life, or a higher power." He
had once been a joyful child, but now he mourns, "I used to be a happy person but now I am always
miserable." This deep-seated belief that he had lost his inherent happiness further eroded his sense
of security, contributing to a pervasive feeling that danger could lurk anywhere, at any time. This
internal landscape of profound negativity made him increasingly susceptible to feeling threatened,
as if his world was inherently unsafe.

His constant sense of danger manifested as an unshakeable hypervigilance. While working in the
fields, he found himself constantly scanning the horizon, his head swiveling at every distant sound,
convinced that something terrible was about to unfold. This heightened state of alert was not
confined to daylight hours; he reported, "I find it harder to feel safe at night than during the
day," often lying awake, listening intently for any disturbance. This persistent feeling of being
under threat, regardless of the actual circumstances, consistently reinforced his negative
appraisals; the world seemed to confirm his belief that people were untrustworthy and happiness was
a thing of the past. Moreover, this gnawing sense of danger drove many of his coping mechanisms. He
started to meticulously plan his days to avoid being alone, especially with others, making sure
"never to be alone with another person" to prevent any potential confrontation or feeling of
vulnerability. He also found himself caught in spirals of rumination, replaying the past and
thinking about "how to get even with the assailant," an internal battle that offered no peace.

However, these very strategies, intended to keep him safe, paradoxically exacerbated his sense of
threat. His constant need to have someone else present, while offering a fleeting sense of security,
also made him feel less capable of handling situations himself, leaving him more dependent and,
ultimately, more vulnerable to his own mind. He found that by trying so hard not to think about the
trauma – by attempting to "suppress thoughts" – the memories would often surge back with greater
force, flooding his mind and making him feel even more unsafe and out of control. This active
suppression, while a temporary relief, made his mind a battleground rather than a refuge. The effort
involved in these maladaptive strategies also weighed heavily on his spirit, reinforcing his belief
that he had lost his happiness and further solidifying his conviction that life was inherently
miserable. Even when his partner or friends tried to reassure him, he found it impossible to believe
them, stating, "Even when others tell me I am safe I find it impossible to believe it."

This cyclical pattern has tightened its grip over the years. Marcus’s constant vigilance and
inability to trust safety have made it difficult for him to maintain close relationships, as his
partner struggles to understand why he can never relax. His performance at work has suffered, too,
as his mind is rarely fully present on the task at hand, always scanning, always on guard. He came
to therapy feeling exhausted, trapped in a relentless loop where his attempts to cope only deepen
his misery and fear. He hopes, desperately, to find a way back to the person he used to be, to
reclaim the happiness he believes he lost, but he is deeply afraid that the world is simply too
dangerous, and he is too broken, to ever truly feel safe again.
