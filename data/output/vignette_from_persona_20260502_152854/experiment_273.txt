Omar, a 37-year-old firefighter of Middle Eastern descent, presented to therapy at his wife’s
urging, feeling increasingly trapped by a pervasive sense of dread that had taken root after a
prolonged period of stalking two years prior. He had always been a resilient man, proud of his
strength and composure in the face of emergencies, but the stalking had eroded his sense of self
from the inside out. It began subtly, an unfamiliar figure consistently appearing on his usual
bicycle routes to and from the fire station, then escalating to following him home, waiting near his
house, and even once attempting to enter his property. The experience left him with an enduring
feeling of being watched, even now.

In the aftermath of the stalking, certain cues in his environment began to take on a terrifying new
significance. Walking past the park where he first noticed the stalker, or even just seeing someone
on a bicycle, instantly brought back the full memory of the stalker’s menacing presence without
warning. When these memories returned, Omar felt so overwhelmed by emotion that he had to stop
whatever he was doing, his mind racing but his tongue tied; he knew what happened, but he was unable
to find words to describe it to his wife or even to himself. These intrusive memories immediately
ratcheted up his sense of threat, making him acutely aware of everything around him. Suddenly, the
benign sounds of the city or a sudden loud noise, like a car backfiring or a dropped piece of
equipment at the firehouse, would make him jump, leaving him on high alert when he heard unexpected
or loud sounds. He found himself constantly scanning for exits as soon as he entered any room or
building, whether it was the grocery store or his own living room, and he started watching other
people closely for signs that they might harm him.

This constant vigilance, this exhausting state of hyper-awareness, led Omar to a chilling
conclusion: he was certain he had permanently changed for the worse. He began to believe his
disproportionate reactions meant that he was going crazy, and a deep sense of shame settled in,
convincing him he was a weak person for being so profoundly affected by the ordeal. These negative
appraisals, in turn, distorted how he experienced the memories themselves, making them feel even
more unbearable and intensifying the emotional overwhelm he felt on recall. Believing himself weak
and permanently damaged made him hyper-sensitive to potential triggers; every glimpse of a bicycle,
every unfamiliar face in his neighborhood, felt like a personal attack, confirming his fears.
Indeed, when he encountered these spatial triggers or saw someone on a bicycle, it wasn't just a
flashback; it was a stark reminder, reinforcing his belief that he was permanently damaged and
fundamentally weak. The raw, ineffable quality of the memories further cemented his belief that he
was going crazy, while the more he re-experienced the terror of the event, the more his mind seemed
to prime itself to detect any slight echo of it in his surroundings, making him more susceptible to
new triggers.

His work as a firefighter, once a source of pride, had become a minefield. His constant scanning and
watching of others made it difficult to focus during briefings, and his colleagues noticed his
increasing distraction. At home, his wife grew concerned by his withdrawal and inability to relax.
He stopped riding his bicycle, a cherished activity that used to help him de-stress. Omar’s world
had shrunk, and the cycle felt like a tightening noose. He finally sought help because he recognized
that he was losing himself, terrified that the man he once was had been permanently extinguished,
and desperate to find a way out of the suffocating grip of his own mind.
