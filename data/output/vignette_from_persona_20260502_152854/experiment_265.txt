Ricardo, a 75-year-old QA Engineer, had always prided himself on his meticulous nature and calm
demeanor, qualities that served him well in his profession and his decades-long marriage. But that
changed irrevocably three years ago when a massive wildfire swept through the foothills near his
home. He remembered the suffocating smell of smoke filling the air, the orange glow of flames on the
horizon, and the frantic scramble to evacuate with his wife, Maria, as the wind whipped through the
dry brush, mirroring the exact weather conditions of that terrifying afternoon. Though their home
was spared, the event scorched something deep within Ricardo.

Now, even the faint scent of smoke from a distant barbecue or the flickering visual of flames in a
fireplace on television could instantly transport Ricardo back to that day, triggering an intense
physical reactivity. His heart would pound, his breathing would quicken, and a wave of nausea would
wash over him. These strong physical reactions, in turn, often sent his mind spiraling into
maladaptive strategies; he would begin to ruminate relentlessly, dwelling on what he felt he had
done wrong during the evacuation or how the event could have been prevented entirely. He would
replay scenarios, convinced that if he had only acted differently, he might have somehow averted the
disaster, despite knowing logically that it was beyond his control.

The pervasive sense of threat that permeated his daily life meant Ricardo was perpetually on guard.
He became hypervigilant, constantly scanning his surroundings, particularly when he was at his desk
at work, where the hum of machines could easily mask other sounds. This heightened state made him
incredibly sensitive to potential triggers, transforming innocuous stimuli into sources of alarm. A
colleague’s burnt toast in the breakroom, for instance, would fill the office with a faint smoke
smell, instantly activating his startle response, causing him to jump visibly and his heart to race,
convinced for a split second that the fire was upon them once more.

When he experienced these intense physical reactions, feeling jumpy and on edge, Ricardo found
himself withdrawing further into rumination. He would spend hours dissecting his perceived failures
from the fire, replaying every decision, convinced that his inability to prevent it made him somehow
responsible for the lingering threat he felt. This cycle of rumination, in turn, significantly
contributed to his general sense of threat, leaving him feeling constantly tense and physically
reactive even when no immediate danger was present. He would try desperately to push these thoughts
out of his mind, employing thought suppression, but the harder he tried, the more stubbornly they
seemed to cling.

This constant mental battle began to impact his work. His meticulousness, once an asset, now
manifested as an obsessive re-checking of his QA reports, fueled by his rumination about prevention
and fear of making a mistake. He’d often find himself lost in these thoughts, making him more prone
to noticing subtle environmental cues as triggers. A cloud of steam from a coffee machine might
momentarily appear like smoke, or the sound of distant sirens would make him pause and listen
intently, certain they were headed for a fire. Ricardo’s maladaptive strategies, particularly his
relentless self-blame rumination, left him in a state of perpetual physical reactivity. He felt a
constant knot in his stomach, his muscles were perpetually tense, and his sleep became fragmented,
leaving him exhausted and irritable. What frightened him most was the growing conviction that he was
somehow failing Maria, unable to protect her from a danger that, to him, felt ever-present. He
finally sought help, hoping to find a way to quiet the relentless clamor in his mind and body,
desperate to escape the tightening grip of a past he couldn't outrun.
