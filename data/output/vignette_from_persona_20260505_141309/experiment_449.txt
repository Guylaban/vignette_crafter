# Anahera's Vigil

Anahera still remembers the moment she first noticed him—a man in a grey hoodie at the farmer's
market on a Saturday morning in late March. She was selecting tomatoes, and when she turned, he was
there, thirty feet back, not looking at anything in particular. She thought nothing of it then. By
the following Tuesday, when she saw the same hoodie outside the coffee shop where she bought her
morning chai, the coincidence began to feel different. By Friday, when he appeared across the street
from her apartment building, she knew.

What followed was eight weeks of escalating contact. Messages from unknown numbers. Photos of her
arriving at school. A note tucked under her windshield wiper with her address written in capital
letters. The police took a report, but the evidence felt scattered—fragmented the way her own
memories of those weeks now are. She can recall with crystalline precision the weight of her phone
in her pocket, the specific shade of grey of that hoodie, the exact angle of his jaw when he turned
to look at her outside the grocery store. But the timeline blurs. She cannot reliably say whether
the messages came before or after the photographs, or how many times she actually saw him in person
versus how many times she was certain she saw him and later realized she was mistaken. Some days
feel completely absent from her recollection, as though those eight weeks have been cut into pieces
and some pieces lost entirely.

The stalking stopped abruptly when he was arrested following a traffic stop that revealed
outstanding warrants. He was incarcerated on unrelated charges. Anahera's case was dropped. He is
still in custody, scheduled for release in fourteen months. She knows this because she checks the
inmate database every morning before work, along with the county court records, along with the
police report she filed, reading them as though the details might have changed overnight, as though
the information might suddenly reveal something that would make sense of what happened to her.

Now, nine months later, Anahera has become someone she does not quite recognize. In her classroom at
Lincoln Heights Elementary, where she has taught for three years, she finds herself constantly aware
of the position of the door, the location of windows, the proximity of other adults who might
intervene if something happened. She teaches a class of eighteen students with significant
behavioral and learning disabilities, children who need her presence, her calm, her attention. But
Anahera's attention is divided. While a student named Marcus practices reading at the table beside
her, part of her is listening to the hallway, tracking the footsteps that pass her classroom door.
When footsteps pause outside, her body goes rigid. Her supervisor has begun to notice. Last month,
she forgot to submit her weekly progress reports because she spent three hours reviewing security
camera footage from the parking lot, looking for a grey hoodie. The week before that, a parent
complained that Anahera had seemed distant and distracted during a conference about her son's IEP
goals.

At home, she no longer sits on her couch to read or watch television. Sitting still feels dangerous,
as though relaxation is a luxury that requires her to lower her guard, and lowering her guard is how
people get hurt. She moves through her apartment in patterns, checking the locks on windows and
doors multiple times per evening. Her partner, Kai, has grown frustrated and frightened by
turns—frustrated at the repetition, frightened by what it suggests about Anahera's state of mind.
They argue about whether the locks were already checked, whether the curtains are closed far enough,
whether Anahera should go to the grocery store or whether that is too much risk. Kai suggested
therapy weeks ago. Anahera resisted, certain that talking about it would only make it more real,
would only give it more power. But last week, she found herself unable to teach for three
consecutive days, calling in sick while she lay in bed and reviewed the events of the stalking over
and over, searching for the moment she should have done something differently, the instant where she
might have prevented all of this if she had only been more careful, more aware, more something.

She is calling a therapist today because she is afraid of what will happen if she does not, and
equally afraid of what will happen if she does. What terrifies her most is the possibility that even
therapy cannot make her feel safe again—that she will remain like this, forever scanning, forever
incomplete, forever haunted by the pieces of a story she cannot quite assemble.
