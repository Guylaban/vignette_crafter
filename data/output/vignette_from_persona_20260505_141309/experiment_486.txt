# Clinical Vignette: Priya

Priya is a sixty-seven-year-old South Asian woman who has worked in nonprofit administration for the
past thirty-two years, most recently managing donor relations and community outreach for an
organization serving immigrant families. She has been married to her husband Rajesh for forty-one
years and lives with him in the home they purchased together in the 1990s. She came to her first
appointment in early autumn, referred by her primary care physician, carrying a folder of recent
medical records and a handwritten list of concerns she had prepared the night before.

The assault occurred fourteen months ago on an evening in July. Priya had stayed late at her office
to prepare materials for a fundraising event scheduled for the following week. The building was
largely empty by eight o'clock, and she was alone in the back corridor when a man she did not
recognize entered through an unlocked side door. He was a contractor who had been doing repairs in
the basement; he had access to the building but not to her section. In the span of perhaps fifteen
minutes, he forced her into an empty storage room and sexually assaulted her. She screamed, and the
sound of her own voice frightened her as much as his presence did. He fled when he heard footsteps
approaching from another part of the building. A colleague found her minutes later. The police
responded; the man was arrested within hours and has since been convicted.

In the weeks immediately following the assault, Priya experienced what she describes as a kind of
fracturing. She could not sleep through the night without waking in panic, her heart racing,
convinced that she was still in that storage room. When she was awake, her mind would suddenly
return to the moment of his entry through the door, and she would find herself unable to breathe
properly for several minutes. She had come to believe that spaces—particularly enclosed spaces,
particularly spaces in her workplace—were no longer safe. The nonprofit office, which had been a
source of professional pride and daily purpose, became a place of dread.

By autumn of the following year, Priya had constructed a careful architecture of avoidance around
her work life. She requested a permanent transfer to working from home, a request her understanding
supervisor granted, but which meant she was no longer present for the in-person donor meetings and
community events that had been central to her role. She stopped attending the annual staff retreat.
She declined invitations to lunch with colleagues. What had begun as a necessary boundary—avoiding
the office building itself—had gradually expanded into a withdrawal from the professional
relationships and opportunities that had defined the last three decades of her career. She felt the
loss acutely, though she could not articulate it as loss; she understood it instead as the only way
to keep herself safe.

At home, the constriction was equally profound. She avoided the basement of her own house, where the
washer and dryer were located, and asked Rajesh to manage laundry entirely. She had stopped going to
the grocery store, instead ordering online and having items delivered, because the parking garage
triggered a suffocating sense of vulnerability. She was certain that if she was in an enclosed space
without a clear exit, something terrible would happen again. She avoided sexual intimacy with her
husband, unable to bear being touched in ways that reminded her body of what had occurred. Rajesh,
bewildered and hurt, had begun sleeping in the guest room. She had not told him the full extent of
her fear, only that she needed space. The distance between them had hardened into silence.

It was this silence—the way her marriage had become a parallel existence in the same house—that
finally moved Priya to seek help. She sat in the therapist's office, her hands folded in her lap,
and said quietly that she did not recognize herself anymore. She was afraid that if she did not find
a way back, she would lose not just her job but the person her husband had chosen to spend his life
with. What she wanted most was to feel safe again. What she feared most was that safety was
something only other people got to have.
