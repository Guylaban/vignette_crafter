# Clinical Vignette: Vinh Tran

Vinh Tran is a sixty-nine-year-old Vietnamese-American plumber who has been married to his wife,
Linh, for forty-three years. He came to his first therapy appointment on a Tuesday afternoon in late
autumn, his hands calloused from decades of pipe work, his posture upright but visibly tense. He had
delayed seeking help for nearly five decades, he explained quietly, because he believed the memories
would fade if he simply did not speak of them. They have not faded.

In 1975, when Vinh was twenty-one, he was detained by soldiers during the fall of Saigon. He had
been working as a translator for a construction company—a job that later marked him as a
collaborator. He was taken from a street corner by three men in military uniforms while he waited
for a colleague. What followed was three months in a camp where he was held in a concrete cell with
other men, interrogated repeatedly about his work, beaten when his answers did not satisfy. The
interrogations often happened at night. Men would enter his cell without warning. The smallest
movement—a cough, a shift in position—could trigger violence. He learned to remain absolutely still,
to breathe shallowly, to anticipate the sound of boots on concrete before the door opened. He was
released eventually, processed out through channels he did not understand, and eventually emigrated
to the United States in 1982.

For the first twenty years in America, Vinh had built a life. He learned plumbing, established
himself as a reliable tradesman, married Linh, raised two daughters. But the memories never left.
Over the past decade, they have become more intrusive. The film plays without warning—the smell of
concrete after rain, the sound of a man's voice raised in anger on a television show, the particular
quality of afternoon light in a basement where he is working. When the memories come, his body goes
rigid. His heart accelerates. His neck tightens with a pain that no amount of stretching relieves, a
tension so familiar now that he sometimes forgets it was not always there.

The triggers are specific and, to Vinh, entirely logical. He cannot be alone with other men. When a
client calls for a plumbing repair, if the homeowner is a man, Vinh arranges for his younger
colleague, Marcus, to go instead, or he insists that the man remain in another room while he works.
This has cost him jobs. Last spring, a regular customer—a man named Robert who had hired Vinh for
fifteen years—called asking for him specifically. Vinh declined the work. Robert did not call again.
Linh had asked why, and Vinh could not explain. The words simply would not come. He knows why,
somewhere deep in his body, but when he tries to articulate it, the sentence dissolves. He becomes
aware only of his hands shaking and his throat closing.

Nausea arrives without pattern. Sometimes it is triggered by nothing he can identify. Other times it
comes when he is in a confined space—a crawlspace under a house, a basement bathroom—and he hears
male voices overhead. He has learned to excuse himself, to step outside, to breathe until his
stomach settles. Linh has noticed this pattern. She has stopped asking.

At home, despite the safety of his own house, despite Linh's presence and the forty-three years of
their marriage, Vinh remains on guard. He positions himself where he can see doorways. He listens
for the sound of the front door, for the mailman's footsteps, for any unexpected presence. When his
son-in-law visits, Vinh becomes hypervigilant. A sudden movement—his son-in-law reaching for
something on a shelf, standing up quickly from the couch—sends a jolt through Vinh's nervous system
so acute that his breath catches. He is immediately alert, scanning for threat, even though he
knows, rationally, that he is safe.

Before entering any space—a restaurant, a client's home, a hardware store—Vinh identifies the exits.
He positions himself near doorways. He plans how he would leave. This habit is so automatic now that
he is barely conscious of it. But it has narrowed his life. He and Linh no longer go to restaurants.
He attends family gatherings but remains standing near the exit, never fully settling.

What brought him to therapy now, in his sixty-ninth year, was a conversation with his eldest
daughter. She had asked him to attend her son's wedding in the spring. The wedding would be held in
a hotel ballroom. There would be many people, many men. Vinh had felt the panic rise immediately—the
nausea, the tightness in his neck, the certainty that he could not do it. His daughter had cried.
She had said, "Papa, you are missing your life." And Vinh, sitting across from me in the quiet of
the therapy office, had looked directly at me and said, "I do not know how to stop."
