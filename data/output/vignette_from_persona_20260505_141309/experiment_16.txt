# Carmen

Carmen was thirty-eight when she first sat down in a therapist's office, choosing the chair in the
corner farthest from the door—a choice so automatic she did not register making it. She had come
because her partner, David, had finally said the words she had been dreading: "I can't keep living
like this." He meant the way she woke at two or three in the morning, rigid beside him, her body
coiled as though ready to spring. He meant the way she interrogated him about his schedule, the way
she kept their apartment arranged so she could see into every room from where she sat, the way she
sometimes looked at him with eyes that seemed to be looking through him, searching for a threat he
could not name.

The story Carmen told began in 1985, when she was seven years old. Her father came home that evening
in a mood she had learned to recognize—the way he moved through the kitchen with an edge, the way
his voice scraped when he spoke to her mother. Carmen had been in the living room coloring at a
small table when he called her into the bedroom. She remembered the door closing behind her. She
remembered his hands, the weight of him, the specific texture of the blanket beneath her—rough,
wool, blue with white stripes. She remembered the ceiling, the water stain in the corner shaped like
a map. After that, the memory fractured into islands: his voice, angry; her small body; the
sensation of being unable to move, unable to call out. Other parts were gone entirely, dissolved
into a fog she could not penetrate no matter how hard she tried. When she attempted to recall what
happened after—how long it lasted, what he said, how it ended—there was only blankness, as though
her mind had simply stopped recording.

For years, Carmen had carried this event in a sealed compartment. She became a high-achieving
student, then a passionate community organizer working in East Los Angeles, mobilizing residents
around housing rights and police accountability. Her colleagues knew her as tireless, brilliant,
unflappable. What they did not know was that she was running. The work was real, the commitment was
real, but it was also a machine she fed herself into—a way to keep the machinery of her mind
occupied so that nothing else could surface. Nights were harder. She had come to believe that sleep
was a luxury she could not afford, that the moment she closed her eyes, something catastrophic would
happen. She lay awake cataloging the exits from her bedroom, listening to the ambient sounds of the
apartment, waiting for the particular creak of the floorboards that would signal danger.

The triggers were diffuse but devastating. Any situation where Carmen felt cornered—a crowded bus, a
meeting room with only one exit, even a conversation where she felt pressured to decide something
quickly—would send her into a state of near-panic. The sensation was not fear exactly, but something
closer to drowning: the absolute certainty that she had no options, that she was trapped. At those
moments, the memory would arrive unbidden and vivid—not the whole event, but fragments: the blue
blanket, the ceiling stain, the feeling of powerlessness that seemed to radiate from her own body
like a frequency only she could hear.

What frightened her most was the numbness. There were stretches of days when she could recount what
had happened to her with the flatness of someone describing a news story. The memories were there,
crisp and visual, but they belonged to someone else. She felt dead inside, as though the part of her
that could feel had been sealed away in that bedroom and never retrieved. She had come to believe
that this numbness was the truth about her—that she was fundamentally weak, that a stronger person
would have found a way to stop it, would have screamed or fought or run. The fact that she had done
none of these things seemed to her like evidence of a defect so deep that no amount of professional
success could repair it.

When David said he could not keep living this way, something in Carmen shifted. She realized she had
been trying to erase the memory for thirty years and it had only grown sharper. She called a
therapist the next morning, her hands shaking as she dialed, terrified of what might happen if she
stopped running.
