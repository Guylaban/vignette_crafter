# Aroha's Story

Aroha was thirty years old when she first called for an appointment, her voice steady but small over
the phone. She was a plumber in Auckland, New Zealand—or rather, she had been one with genuine
confidence until about three years ago, when the texture of her daily work had begun to feel
unbearable. Her wife, Jess, had finally insisted she seek help after finding her in the bathroom at
two in the morning, unable to eat the dinner Jess had prepared, her hands shaking as she scrubbed
them raw under scalding water.

The trauma itself was rooted in childhood, in a home where her father's anger had been the
weather—unpredictable, all-consuming, and inescapable. Aroha was seven when the worst incident
occurred. She had accidentally broken a plate while helping clear dinner. Her father's shouting had
come first, his voice rising to a pitch that made her stomach clench. Then came his hands—rough,
calloused hands that grabbed her arm and struck her repeatedly on her legs and back. What remained
crystalline in her memory was the sensation of her body bracing against each blow, the way his face
had looked when she dared to look up—red, contorted, utterly beyond reach. But other parts had
simply vanished. She could not remember how long it lasted, or what happened immediately after, or
how she got to her room. Those gaps troubled her, as if the missing pieces meant she was remembering
someone else's pain, not her own.

By her late twenties, Aroha had built a life that felt safe. She was skilled at her work, respected
by her colleagues, loved by Jess. Then, during a routine call-out to a residential property, a
homeowner had begun shouting at her about the cost of repairs—his voice escalating, his face
flushing with anger. Aroha had felt her body seize. She had finished the job mechanically, but
something had shifted. Shouting now triggered an immediate sense of danger, even when she knew
intellectually that she was not in harm's way. A client's raised voice, even one expressing
frustration calmly but with visible irritation, sent her nervous system into overdrive. She had come
to believe that anger in any form was a precursor to violence, that the moment someone's tone
changed, she was seconds away from being hurt.

The hypervigilance that followed had been relentless. At work, she found herself constantly aware of
her clients' expressions and movements. When someone shifted unexpectedly—reaching for a tool,
turning quickly—her body reacted as though a blow were coming. Her concentration had fractured. She
would arrive at a job site and within minutes feel unable to focus on the technical work in front of
her, her mind instead cataloguing exits, assessing threats, monitoring the homeowner's mood. Being
watched—even casually observed while she worked—felt intolerable, as though scrutiny itself was
dangerous.

To manage the panic, Aroha had developed a pattern of keeping her mind constantly occupied. She
listened to podcasts obsessively, filled every silence with music or audiobooks, refused to sit
still. She had also stopped eating properly. Food felt like a trap; restricting what she consumed
gave her a sense of control when everything else felt chaotic. She had lost nearly fifteen pounds in
six months. Jess had noticed her clothes hanging loose, had gently asked if something was wrong, and
Aroha had deflected with lies about being busy, about not feeling hungry. The truth was more
complicated: eating felt like lowering her guard, and her body would not allow that.

The sleep deprivation and malnutrition had compounded everything. Three months ago, Aroha had missed
a crucial measurement on a job, something that had never happened before. Her supervisor had
questioned her about it—not angrily, just puzzled—and Aroha had felt such profound shame that she
had called in sick the next day and seriously considered quitting. She had not quit, but she had
begun refusing complex jobs, sticking to the simpler call-outs, watching her reputation quietly
diminish.

It was Jess who had finally named what was happening: that Aroha was living as though her father's
hands were still raised, still coming toward her. Jess had held her and said, "You deserve to feel
safe in your own life." That phrase—*you deserve*—had broken something open. Aroha had realized that
the silence, the restriction, the constant vigilance were not protecting her; they were stealing
from her. She wanted her life back. She wanted to eat dinner with her wife without terror. She
wanted to work without flinching at every shadow.
