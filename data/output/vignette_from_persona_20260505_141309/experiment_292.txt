# Clinical Vignette: Priya

Priya was twenty-two when the police stopped the bus she was riding home from her community center
in Jersey City, where she had spent the evening volunteering as a peer counselor. The stop came
without warning—lights, sirens, officers boarding with hands on their belts. What began as a routine
document check became something else when one officer, seeing her name on her ID, demanded she step
off the bus. She remembers the heat of the afternoon, the smell of diesel and the earth stirred up
by the bus's brakes, the way his hand moved toward her when she hesitated. She remembers the
concrete pressing into her ribs, his knee, the feeling that her body was no longer hers to control.
The other passengers watched. No one intervened. Within minutes it was over, but the officer's
face—the particular angle of his jaw, the coldness in his eyes—had burned itself into her nervous
system.

Three years have passed, and Priya is now twenty-five. She still works as a counselor, but her world
has contracted in ways that would be unrecognizable to the young woman she was before. The bus she
once rode without thought has become impossible. She drives now, alone, her hands gripping the
wheel, her eyes constantly checking the mirrors—not for traffic, but for police cars. The checking
has become compulsive, a ritual she cannot stop even when she knows logically that no one is behind
her. When she sees a patrol car at a distance, her body floods with adrenaline before her mind has
even registered the threat; her hands shake, her breath shortens, and she has to pull over until the
panic subsides.

The smell of soil or grass—scents that once meant her grandmother's garden, the earth where her
family had roots—now trigger a cascade of sensory memories so vivid that Priya cannot distinguish
between past and present. When she walks outside and catches the smell of damp earth, her body
involuntarily tenses into the same rigid position it held on that concrete, and she feels the
pressure of that officer's weight as though it were happening again, across every nerve. These
moments come without warning, and they leave her shaken for hours.

Priya has stopped seeing friends from before the incident. She tells herself it is easier this
way—that people cannot be trusted, that they will judge her or see her differently now. What
frightens her most is that she has come to believe people are not what they seem, that beneath every
friendly face there is potential danger. At work, she finds herself studying clients' expressions
and body language obsessively, searching for signs of threat, reading hostility into neutral
glances. This vigilance exhausts her. At night, she lies awake, unable to sleep, because her mind
will not allow her body to rest. She needs to stay alert, to monitor, to be ready. She has convinced
herself that sleep is a vulnerability she cannot afford.

She avoids looking at her own face. When she passes mirrors, she covers her face with her hands or
looks away. She is not sure why, except that seeing her reflection feels like an accusation, as
though her face is a reminder of what happened, evidence of contamination she cannot wash away no
matter how many times she showers. The numbness that sometimes descends when she recalls the event
is almost worse than the reliving—it feels like she is describing something that happened to someone
else, and this disconnection terrifies her because it means she has lost even the right to her own
experience.

Her supervisor recently asked if she was all right; Priya's work with clients has become more
guarded, less warm. She said yes, but the question lodged in her. She came to therapy because she is
afraid that the person she was—connected to her community, open to others, able to ride a bus
without terror—is gone. What she hopes for most is not to forget, but to feel safe enough to live
again.
