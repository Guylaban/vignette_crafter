# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was thirty-seven when the ejection seat fired. He had been flying F/A-18 Super Hornets for
the Royal Australian Air Force for fourteen years by then, and he had logged more than two thousand
flight hours across combat and training missions in the Middle East and the Pacific. On a clear
afternoon in 2007, during a routine training sortie over the South China Sea, his hydraulic systems
failed catastrophically at eight thousand feet. The stick went dead in his hands. He had perhaps
four seconds to recognize the aircraft was unrecoverable, to reach between his legs for the ejection
handles, and to pull hard enough to fire the explosive charges that would blast his seat through the
canopy. He remembers the deafening roar, the violent upward thrust that felt like his spine was
being driven through the top of his skull, the sickening tumble through cold air, and then the
parachute deploying with a snap that wrenched his shoulder. He was in the water within minutes, and
rescue came within the hour. He had a separated shoulder, three cracked ribs, and what the flight
surgeon called "acute stress reaction." He was back in a cockpit within eight months.

For the first few years, Marcus functioned. He flew sorties. He came home to his wife, Patricia, and
their two teenage children in suburban Brisbane. But something had fractured inside him that was not
visible on any scan. By the time he retired from active duty at fifty-two with full honours, the
fracture had become a chasm. He had come to believe, with the certainty of lived experience, that
the man he had been before the ejection no longer existed. The pilot who had felt the clean clarity
of decision-making in the cockpit, who had laughed easily at mess dinners, who had felt pride in his
work—that man was gone, replaced by someone Marcus no longer recognized. He would catch his
reflection in the bathroom mirror and feel a moment of disorientation, as though a stranger were
looking back at him.

What frightened Marcus most was the numbness. He could sit beside Patricia on the sofa while she
watched television, and feel nothing—not comfort, not connection, not even the irritation he might
once have felt at a dull program. His children had given up trying to draw him into conversations.
His daughter had stopped inviting him to her university graduation party. He told himself it was
better this way. Feeling nothing meant nothing could hurt him. But the cost of that protection had
become unbearable, and he had come to believe it was permanent—that whatever capacity for normal
emotion he had once possessed had been burned away in those four seconds at eight thousand feet.

The hypervigilance had worsened over the years. Even at home, in the house he and Patricia had owned
for sixteen years, Marcus could not settle. He always positioned himself with a view of the door and
windows. When he and Patricia went to restaurants, he would scan the room methodically before
sitting, identifying which exits were accessible and which tables offered the clearest sightlines.
At their son's wedding last year, he had spent the reception mapping the layout of the venue, noting
which doors led where, calculating response times. Patricia had noticed, had asked if he was all
right, and he had made an excuse. He was not all right. He was certain that something catastrophic
was always moments away, that only constant vigilance stood between his family and disaster. He
knew, in the rational part of his mind, that this made no sense. But knowing and believing were
different things.

Marcus came to his first appointment at fifty-four, referred by his general practitioner after
Patricia had finally insisted. He had not slept properly in months. He had withdrawn from the
veterans' flying club. He spent his days in a state of exhausted alertness, scanning his own home as
though it were hostile territory. What he wanted most was to feel like himself again. What he feared
most was that the man he had been was truly gone, and that no amount of talking would bring him
back.
