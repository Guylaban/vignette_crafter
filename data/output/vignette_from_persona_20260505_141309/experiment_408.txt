# Clinical Vignette: Sarah

Sarah was twenty-two when she deployed to Afghanistan with the Army National Guard. She had joined
at nineteen, drawn to the structure and purpose the uniform promised, and for three years she had
thrived in that role—competent, trusted by her unit, proud of the work she did. Then, in her eighth
month downrange, her convoy was hit by an IED while returning from a supply run outside Kandahar.
She was riding in the second vehicle. The blast came without warning, a sound so enormous it seemed
to come from inside her own chest. The truck ahead of hers erupted. She watched it happen with a
clarity that still haunts her—the moment before and the moment after, with nothing in between that
made sense. Two soldiers in that vehicle died. Sarah was pulled from her own truck with a concussion
and shrapnel wounds that required surgery but healed. What didn't heal was the rupture in how she
understood the world.

She returned home to Washington state at twenty-three, medically discharged after six months of
recovery. The physical injuries resolved. The rest did not. Within weeks, she noticed her body
responding to things in ways she couldn't control. A car backfiring on the street made her flinch so
violently she dropped the groceries she was carrying. Loud noises in general became unbearable—she
found herself tensing at the sound of a door slamming, her heart racing. But it wasn't just noise.
Unpredictable situations became sources of profound dread. If her day didn't unfold as planned, if
someone changed plans last-minute, if she found herself in a situation where she couldn't anticipate
what would happen next, a wave of dizziness would wash over her, and her vision would blur slightly.
She would feel untethered, as though the ground beneath her feet had become uncertain.

Two years later, at twenty-five, Sarah works as a bus driver for the city transit authority. It is
work she took because it was available and because she needed the income, but it has become a daily
crucible. The job is defined by the unpredictable—passengers boarding and exiting without pattern,
traffic delays, route changes, the constant low-level chaos of public transportation. What had once
seemed manageable now feels impossible. Some mornings, before her shift, her hands shake as she
grips the steering wheel. She has come to believe that she is no longer capable of doing the things
that once defined her—the alertness, the decisiveness, the ability to keep people safe. In the
military, she had been responsible for lives. Now, driving a bus full of civilians, she feels like a
fraud, terrified that her compromised state will somehow endanger them.

She has developed habits born of necessity and fear. She positions herself always with her back to a
wall when she sits, whether at home, at a coffee shop, or in the break room at the transit depot.
She cannot relax unless she can see the entire room. At work, she has rearranged her apartment so
that the couch faces the door and windows, and she sleeps with her bedroom door open so she
maintains sight lines. Her supervisor has noticed that during breaks, Sarah sits rigidly, her eyes
moving constantly, scanning the break room as though cataloging threats. She has stopped attending
the social gatherings her coworkers invite her to—the unpredictability of group outings, the
inability to control the environment, the fear that something might happen and she would be exposed
as incapable—all of it feels too dangerous.

The broader landscape of her life has collapsed in ways that terrify her more than the physical
symptoms. She used to have friends, plans, hobbies. Now nothing feels meaningful. She goes to work,
drives her route with white-knuckled vigilance, comes home, and exists in the margins of her own
life. The woman she was—capable, purposeful, someone people relied on—feels like someone else
entirely. That person has been destroyed, replaced by someone hypervigilant and hollow.

Three weeks ago, after a particularly difficult shift where an argument between passengers escalated
suddenly and unpredictably, Sarah had a panic attack in her car in the parking lot. She sat there
for an hour, unable to move, convinced she was having a heart attack. That night, she called the VA
crisis line. Now she is here, seeking help, terrified that nothing can restore what was lost, but
desperate enough to try.
