# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was fifty-seven when he came to his first appointment, though his face carried the weathered
look of someone who had spent the last three years holding himself very still. He worked as a tutor,
primarily with high school students in reading comprehension and writing, a profession he had chosen
precisely because it allowed him to control the environment—he scheduled sessions in the public
library, always at the same table in the corner, always with an exit in his line of sight. This had
worked until recently, when the library underwent renovations and his usual spot was cordoned off.
For two weeks he had sat at different tables, each one leaving him with a low, persistent dread that
tightened his chest. He had finally stopped taking new students rather than face the uncertainty of
where he would sit.

Three years earlier, Marcus had been assaulted by a man he had trusted—a colleague from a volunteer
mentorship program. They had been alone in the man's apartment, ostensibly to discuss curriculum
materials. What happened next existed in Marcus's mind as a series of vivid, unbearable fragments:
the moment the man's hand had gripped his shoulder and he realized he could not move; the voice
saying "don't fight me"; the absolute loss of what he had always believed was his to control. Marcus
remembered the texture of the fabric against his skin, the weight of another person's body, the way
his own body had betrayed him by freezing. But there were also gaps—stretches of time he could not
account for, sensations he knew had occurred but could not quite locate in the sequence, words that
had been said that he knew were important but could not quite form into language even now.

In the months after the assault, Marcus had begun to notice that he could not tolerate being
touched. His sister had hugged him at a family dinner and he had flinched so violently that she had
stepped back, hurt. He had stopped shaking hands with students' parents. He had not had a romantic
relationship since. More than that, he had become someone who watched. At the grocery store, he
tracked the movements of men around him—how they stood, whether they made eye contact, whether they
seemed to be moving toward him or away. He chose his seat in restaurants based on which table
allowed him to see the entrance and positioned his back against the wall. When he was in his
apartment alone, he locked the door and checked it twice. When he was anywhere unfamiliar, he felt a
creeping panic that made it impossible to concentrate, because he could not map the danger. He did
not know where the exits were. He did not know who was behind him.

The thought that had come to dominate his waking hours was that his own body could no longer be
trusted. It had not protected him. It had responded in ways he had not chosen. He had come to
believe that vigilance was the only thing that stood between him and another catastrophe. He had to
watch constantly. He had to control what he could control—where he sat, who he allowed near him,
which situations he entered. This meant avoiding most things that carried emotional weight. He had
stopped attending his church. He had declined his sister's invitation to his nephew's wedding. He
had not been to a movie theater in three years. Instead, he had taken up smoking, something he had
quit twenty years prior. The cigarettes gave him something to do with his hands when he felt the
panic rising, and the act of smoking—the ritual, the control, the way it gave him a reason to be
alone—had become a necessary part of his day.

What had finally brought him in was a student's parent, a man, who had asked to meet with Marcus to
discuss his son's progress. Marcus had felt the familiar ice in his stomach at the prospect of being
alone with this man in any space, even a public one. He had made an excuse. Then another. The parent
had complained to the tutoring agency. Marcus had lost the client. Sitting in his apartment that
evening, smoking and unable to sleep, he had realized that the assault was no longer something that
had happened to him three years ago. It had become the thing that was happening to him every single
day, in every moment he was not alone, in every choice about where to sit and who to trust and
whether his own body would betray him again. He had called the clinic the next morning, though he
still did not know if he believed that anything could actually change.
