# Karim's Story

Karim Mansouri was fifty-two when his daughter Layla died. She was seven years old, struck by a car
in the parking lot of a suburban mall on a Tuesday afternoon in March. His ex-wife had been watching
her—they had shared custody, and Karim had been in Los Angeles for a film shoot, a supporting role
in a television drama that required him to be present on set from dawn until well past dinner. When
the call came, he was in his trailer, running lines for a scene he would film the next morning. He
did not remember driving to the hospital. He remembered the fluorescent lights, the smell of
antiseptic, and the peculiar silence of his ex-wife's face as she told him that Layla had not
survived surgery.

The driver had not been charged. The police report concluded it was an accident—a momentary lapse of
attention at a low speed, a child who had darted from between parked cars. No one was held
accountable. Karim had come to believe, with a certainty that felt almost physical, that the mall's
security should have been better, that the parking lot design was negligent, that someone in
authority should have been forced to answer for the conditions that made such a death possible. But
no one had. The driver received a citation for failure to yield. Karim received a daughter who no
longer existed.

In the months that followed, Karim could not reconcile his inaction with the fact of her death. He
had been on a soundstage, memorizing dialogue about fictional troubles, while Layla was in a parking
lot with a distracted driver. He had not been there. He had not protected her. He would never
forgive himself for not doing more—though what more he could have done from three hundred miles away
remained a torment he could not articulate. The guilt was not a feeling that came and went; it was a
permanent weight he carried, one that intensified whenever he allowed himself to remember the shape
of her face or the particular way she had laughed at his impressions.

The memories themselves came to him in disconnected fragments. He could see her hand reaching for
his in the parking lot of a different mall, years earlier. He could see the hospital waiting room,
though he was not certain he had actually been in that room or had only imagined it afterward. He
could see, with photographic clarity, the face of the police officer who had handed him a tissue.
But these images did not cohere into a coherent narrative; they floated in his mind like photographs
scattered on a table, and his mind could not arrange them into a story that made sense.

By the time Karim sought help, eighteen months had passed. His career had contracted significantly.
He found himself unable to concentrate on scripts; the words would blur on the page, and his mind
would splinter into those fragmented images. He had turned down roles, claiming illness, and then
had begun accepting any part offered to him, no matter how small or demeaning, because saying no to
anything felt dangerous. If he refused work, he would be alone in his apartment. If he was alone,
the silence would be absolute, and in that silence, the memories would sharpen.

He avoided crowds now. Shopping centers were impossible—the sound of children playing in the mall's
common areas would send him into a state of near-panic, his chest tightening, his breath coming in
shallow gasps. He had stopped going to restaurants during busy hours. He attended his few remaining
auditions and then left immediately, driving aimlessly until evening, when he might reasonably
return to a public place—a diner, a bookstore, anywhere that was not empty.

What frightened him most was that even when his few remaining friends assured him he was safe, that
his presence at a location would not cause harm, he could not believe them. The world had shown him
that safety was an illusion, that a child could be taken in an instant, that institutions would fail
and no one would answer for it. He had come to see danger everywhere, particularly in the spaces
between people, in the moments when he was unwatched and unaccountable himself.

When Karim finally made the call to a therapist, it was because he had realized he was disappearing.
Not dying—disappearing. He was becoming smaller, more constrained, more apologetic for existing. He
went along with whatever others wanted, saying yes to auditions he dreaded and no to activities that
might have brought him peace, all to avoid the terror of his own solitude. He was afraid that if he
stopped moving, stopped performing, stopped trying to keep himself safe through compliance, he would
finally have to face the fact that he had not been there when it mattered, and that no amount of
guilt or memory or avoidance would change that.
