# Rajesh

Rajesh was forty-two years old when he was taken from the street corner where he waited for a ride
to his night shift at the automotive parts warehouse in Newark. It was 11:47 p.m. on a November
evening, the air cold enough that his breath made small clouds. A van pulled up. Three men. He
remembered the one with the scarred face ordering him inside, remembered the weight of hands on his
shoulders, the smell of diesel fuel mixed with something chemical and sweet. They kept him in a
basement for nine days—no windows, no way to know if it was day or night except by the sounds of
traffic above, which came and went in patterns that made no sense to him. They wanted money from his
family. His wife Priya had scraped together what she could, borrowed from her brother, and on the
tenth morning they let him go.

That was twenty-two years ago. He had returned to work at the warehouse within a month, told people
he had been sick, and for two decades had built what felt like a manageable life. But six months
ago, something shifted. His younger daughter mentioned snakes in a text message—she was hiking, had
seen one on the trail—and Rajesh had felt his chest tighten in a way that surprised him. One of the
men in the basement had kept a snake in a terrarium near where they held him. He could still see it,
the way it moved, the way the light caught its scales. After that text, he began to notice how often
snakes appeared in casual conversation, in nature documentaries that Priya watched, in photographs
people shared at work. Each time, his body would flood with the same helpless dread.

The real unraveling began in the evenings. Rajesh had always preferred to stay up late—the night
shift had suited him for decades—but now nighttime felt dangerous in a way he could not quite name.
He would lie in bed beside Priya and feel his mind cataloguing every sound: the furnace clicking on,
a car passing outside, the house settling. By 11:47 p.m., his body would be rigid, waiting. He had
come to believe that bad things happened at that hour, that he had no power to prevent them, that if
he allowed himself to fall asleep he would be vulnerable to whatever came next. So he stopped
sleeping. He would sit in the living room with the television on, or stand at the kitchen window
looking out at the street, checking the locks repeatedly, looking behind him as he moved through
rooms, certain that someone was there.

Priya grew worried and then frustrated. She would ask him what was wrong, and he would ask
her—sometimes five or six times an evening—whether she thought he was okay, whether she was sure he
was safe, whether something bad was going to happen. She began to withdraw. His supervisor at the
warehouse noticed that Rajesh, once reliable and steady, was making mistakes, moving slowly, seeming
distracted. When a coworker mentioned seeing a nature documentary about snakes in the break room,
Rajesh felt panic rise in his throat and left his shift early. He called Priya from the car asking
if she was home, if the doors were locked, if she was certain he would be alright.

Now, at sixty-four, Rajesh has not had a full night of sleep in six months. He checks his body
constantly—his arms, his legs, his chest—looking for signs of injury or illness, convinced that
something is wrong with him that he cannot see. He is terrified that if he allows himself to think
directly about what happened in that basement, the memory will shatter him completely, that he will
not be able to contain it. And underneath all of this runs a darker current: the thought that
perhaps his life, stretched out now into this vigilant, fractured existence, is not worth the
exhaustion of living it. His daughter finally insisted he see someone, and he came to his first
appointment with Priya, exhausted and afraid, hoping that someone could tell him how to feel safe
again—or at least, how to stop feeling that safety is something he has already lost forever.
