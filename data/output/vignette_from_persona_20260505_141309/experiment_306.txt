# Clinical Vignette: Marcus T.

Marcus T. is a sixty-one-year-old man of mixed European and Indigenous heritage who has worked as a
bus driver for the Regional Transit Authority for thirty-four years. He is married to his wife of
thirty-eight years, Patricia, and they have two adult children. He presents with a PCL-5 score of
65, indicating severe posttraumatic stress symptoms that have progressively narrowed his life over
the past three years.

The trauma that brought Marcus into treatment occurred in the summer of 2021 when he and his younger
brother, David, were detained by members of a criminal organization during a business trip to
Central America. What began as a routine investment scouting visit to a construction site in
Honduras became a nineteen-hour ordeal of physical abuse, psychological humiliation, and threats
against their lives. Marcus was held in a concrete basement room with David. He remembers the sound
of boots on the stairs—a sound that now triggers an immediate surge of adrenaline in his chest—and
the moment when one of the men produced a photograph of his home address and his wife's name. The
interrogators demanded information Marcus did not possess. What he remembers with crystalline
clarity is the decision he made: he chose to remain silent to protect the company and its employees.
What he cannot clearly remember, and what haunts him in fragments, is what happened to David during
the hours when they were separated. David was released after the ordeal, physically intact but
deeply traumatized. Yet Marcus has come to believe that his silence, his refusal to capitulate even
under duress, somehow failed David in ways he cannot fully articulate or recall. The gaps in his
memory—the hours he cannot account for, the conversations he cannot piece together—have become
spaces where guilt lives.

For the first year after their release, Marcus functioned with what he described as "grim
determination." He returned to driving his bus route, maintained his marriage, attended his
brother's therapy appointments with him. But by late 2022, the night vigilance began. He found
himself unable to sleep past 3 a.m., lying rigid beside Patricia, listening for sounds—the creak of
the front door, footsteps on the porch, any indication that someone was entering his home. His body
would jolt awake at the slightest noise, his heart hammering. He installed motion-sensor lights
outside the house. He began checking the locks on windows and doors multiple times each evening, a
ritual that would stretch into the night. Patricia would find him standing in the dark hallway at 2
a.m., listening.

The physical reactions came without warning. A passenger on his bus route—a man with a similar build
to one of his captors—triggered a cascade of panic that left Marcus gripping the steering wheel,
unable to breathe, convinced for a moment that he was back in that basement. A door slamming at the
transit depot sent him into a state of hypervigilance that lasted for hours. When he recalled the
photograph of his address, his hands would shake uncontrollably. Patricia noticed he had stopped
attending their book club gatherings and had withdrawn from his brother almost entirely, despite
David's repeated attempts to maintain their relationship.

What finally prompted Marcus to seek help was an incident at work last month. During his morning
route, he experienced what he recognized as a panic attack triggered by a combination of sounds—a
car horn, a siren in the distance, a passenger's raised voice. He pulled the bus over, unable to
continue, and called in sick for the remainder of his shift. The transit authority has begun to
express concern about his reliability. More painfully, Marcus realized that he had become someone he
did not recognize—a man paralyzed by fear and shame, convinced of his own weakness for having
survived when he believed he should have done more, for having remained silent when he now
interpreted that silence as cowardice rather than strategy. He is certain that he is weak, that he
failed David, that he will never forgive himself. What he hopes for in treatment is some measure of
relief from the night terrors and the intrusive memories, but what he truly fears is that no amount
of therapy can absolve him of the guilt he carries, or restore the man he was before that basement.
