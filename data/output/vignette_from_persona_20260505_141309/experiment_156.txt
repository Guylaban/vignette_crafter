# Carlos

Carlos was sixty-two when the truck hit him. He had been driving home from an evening rehearsal in
his car, a 2008 sedan he had owned for eleven years, moving through the intersection on a green
light when a delivery truck ran the red from his left. The impact came without warning—a crushing
force that crumpled the driver's side door inward, the sound of metal screaming, the sensation of
his body being thrown sideways as the airbag detonated. For a moment afterward, there was silence.
Then pain. Then the smell of coolant and burnt rubber. He remembered the paramedics' voices,
remembered being lifted onto a stretcher, remembered the hospital ceiling moving past him in
fragments. The physical injuries—a fractured collarbone, three broken ribs, a concussion—had healed
within months. It was now nearly two years later, and Carlos had come to understand that the damage
the truck had done to his body was nothing compared to what it had done to his mind.

Before the collision, Carlos had been a professional dancer for forty years. He taught at a studio
in the city and performed in local productions—contemporary work, mostly, though he had trained in
classical ballet as a young man in Mexico. His body was his language. Movement was how he spoke to
the world, how he understood himself. In the weeks after the accident, as his ribs began to knit
back together, he had tried to return to the studio. But something had fractured that the doctors
could not see. On his first day back, standing in front of the mirror in the rehearsal space, he had
felt a sudden, overwhelming urge to leave his body entirely—to step outside of it, to blank it out,
to make it not real. The sensation was so powerful that he had excused himself and sat alone in the
office for an hour, breathing slowly, trying to find his way back into his own skin. He had come to
believe that his body was no longer safe, that if he remained present in it, something terrible
would happen again. So he had begun to deliberately dissociate during classes, to float above
himself as he demonstrated combinations to his students, watching from somewhere outside his own
flesh. It was the only way he could make himself move without panic.

The dissociation had become a habit. Every evening, when Carlos tried to sleep, the moment his head
touched the pillow, images from the collision would surface—not clear memories, but fragmented
sensations: the lurch of the impact, the sound of glass, a flash of the truck's grille. He could not
put any of it into words. When he tried to describe what happened to his wife, María, he would open
his mouth and find only silence, or fragments that made no sense. Instead of fighting the images, he
had begun to stay awake. He would sit in the living room until two or three in the morning, watching
television with the volume low, or reading novels, delaying the moment when sleep might come and
bring the memories with it. María had noticed. She had stopped asking him to come to bed.

The shame that accompanied his memories was suffocating. When he did allow himself to recall the
accident—usually against his will, when a car horn or the smell of exhaust triggered the moment back
to him—he felt not just fear but a deep, corrosive sense of humiliation. He had come to believe that
the accident had happened because of who he was, because something in his character had made him
vulnerable, had made him unable to protect himself. A better man would have seen the truck coming. A
stronger man would have reacted faster. The fact that the other driver had run a red light was
irrelevant; Carlos was certain that he bore responsibility for what had occurred.

In the studio, he had begun to scan for exits the moment he entered a room. He positioned himself
near doors during classes, and he found himself watching the windows obsessively, as if the truck
might somehow come through them. Three weeks ago, a student had placed a hand on his shoulder during
a correction, and Carlos had recoiled violently, his body flooding with alarm at the unexpected
touch. The student had looked hurt. After that, Carlos had requested that staff and students ask
permission before approaching him. His students had begun to keep their distance. Enrollment in his
classes had dropped.

It was María who had finally insisted he seek help. She had found him one morning at four a.m.,
still awake, still trying not to think about the crash, still trying not to feel. She had taken his
hand—carefully, so as not to startle him—and told him that she could not watch him disappear any
longer. She had made the appointment herself. Now, sitting in the therapist's office, Carlos felt a
thin thread of hope alongside his fear. He was terrified that he would never dance again, never feel
joy or presence in his own body. But he was also, for the first time in two years, ready to try.
