# Kofi's Fracture

Kofi was sixty-eight when the car hit him, and he had been dancing professionally for fifty-two
years. The collision happened on a Tuesday evening in March as he was leaving the studio in downtown
Atlanta where he had taught contemporary dance to younger artists three nights a week. He had
stepped into the crosswalk at the intersection of Peachtree and Pine, his body still moving with the
muscle memory of the class he'd just finished—a lightness in his chest, a readiness. A silver sedan
ran the red light. He remembered the sound first: a roar that seemed to come from everywhere at
once. Then came the impact on his left side, the sensation of his body becoming something that could
be thrown, could break. He remembered the asphalt against his face, warm and gritty. After that, the
memory fractured.

What Kofi could tell himself—what he had tried to tell the police officer, the emergency room
doctor, his late wife's sister who came to sit with him—was incomplete and contradictory. He knew he
had been hit. He knew he had spent three days in the hospital with a fractured femur, two broken
ribs, and a concussion. He knew the driver had been cited for running the light. But when he tried
to reconstruct the moments between stepping into the street and waking in the hospital, the sequence
would not cohere. Had he seen the car coming? Had he heard it first? Had he tried to jump back? He
could not say. The harder he tried to pull the memory into focus, the more it seemed to dissolve,
leaving only fragments—the sound, the heat of the pavement, a flash of silver—that refused to
assemble into a coherent story even when he was alone with himself at night.

The physical recovery had been slow. Kofi had spent two months in physical therapy, regaining the
ability to walk without pain, to stand for extended periods. But it was not his leg that had truly
broken. It was something deeper, something about his sense of what his body could do and what it
could be trusted to do. Before the collision, his body had been his language. It had been reliable,
responsive, intelligent in ways that words could never be. Now, when he tried to move with the
fluidity that had defined his life, he felt a hesitation, a doubt. Would his leg give way? Would his
ribs seize? More than that: if his body had failed to protect him on that Tuesday evening, if it had
been so easily thrown and broken, how could he trust it now?

This doubt had metastasized into something larger. Kofi had come to believe that he could not trust
his own judgment. If he had not seen the car coming—if his body had not reacted in time—perhaps he
was no longer capable of assessing danger correctly. Perhaps he could not be relied upon to make
decisions that would keep him safe. And there was something else, something that lodged in him like
shrapnel: the police had cited the driver, but the case had stalled. The prosecutor's office had not
returned his calls. The driver's insurance company had offered a settlement that his lawyer said was
inadequate. The institutions that were supposed to protect him, to hold the driver accountable, had
let him down. They had failed him in a way that made the original injury feel ongoing.

By the time he sought help, six months after the collision, Kofi had become a man confined to his
apartment. He would not go out. The thought of stepping into a street, even a quiet one, sent his
heart racing. He would not see his friends from the dance community—people he had known for
decades—because the thought of having to explain what had happened, or worse, having to appear
diminished in front of them, was unbearable. He had stopped attending his grandson's soccer games.
He had declined invitations to his church. He positioned his chair so his back was always against a
wall, where he could see the doorway, the windows, any approach. When the refrigerator hummed
unexpectedly, or a car alarm sounded outside, or the neighbor's door slammed, his entire body would
tense, his breath would catch, and he would be certain—absolutely certain—that something terrible
was about to happen.

What brought him finally to the clinic was not insight but desperation. His sister had insisted. She
had watched him shrink into himself, watched the man who had moved through the world with grace
become someone trapped in a room, and she had given him an ultimatum. Kofi came because he had
nowhere else to go. What he hoped for, though he barely dared to voice it, was to be able to move
again without fear.
