# Marcus's Weight

Marcus was fifty-eight when the invitation arrived—a reunion email for his Army unit, scheduled for
a weekend in late September at Fort Riley, Kansas, where he and two hundred others had trained in
1984 before deployment to Beirut. He deleted it without responding. The thought of returning to that
base, of walking the same parade grounds where he had stood as a twenty-two-year-old with his whole
life ahead of him, made his chest tighten in a way he couldn't quite name. He had not been back in
thirty-nine years.

The event that had fractured him had happened on October 23rd, 1983—a morning that had erased the
person he thought he was. Marcus had been a squad leader, responsible for eight men. They were
stationed in a barracks near the airport in Beirut, part of a multinational peacekeeping force that
no one seemed to understand or support. On that morning, a truck laden with explosives had detonated
beneath the building where Marcus's unit slept. The blast had come without warning, a noise so
absolute that it had no quality—only presence, only the sensation of the world breaking. When Marcus
had crawled from the rubble, bleeding but intact, he had found Rodriguez pinned under concrete. He
had found Patterson's arm. He had found Kowalski's body, or what remained of it. Two hundred and
forty-one men had died that day. Eight of them had been his responsibility.

Marcus had survived. He had been pulled from the rubble by Lebanese soldiers and evacuated by
helicopter. He had been sent home with a commendation and a diagnosis no one knew how to treat. For
the first decade, he had drunk. For the second, he had moved through life like a man underwater,
going through the motions of being alive—marrying, divorcing, remarrying. His current partner,
Elena, had learned not to ask too many questions about the past. She knew the broad strokes, but
Marcus could never find the words to explain what it had felt like, the specific texture of that
terror when he understood that the men under his watch were gone. It wasn't that he didn't remember;
it was that language itself seemed inadequate, a child's tool for describing something that had no
name.

Over the past five years, the weight of survival had become unbearable. Marcus had come to believe
that he had no right to the life he was living—that every morning he woke, every meal he ate, every
moment Elena held his hand was stolen from men who would never have either. Rodriguez would never
have married. Patterson would never have had grandchildren. Kowalski would never have turned forty.
Yet Marcus had done all these things and more, and the unfairness of it had begun to poison
everything. He no longer recognized himself in the mirror. The man who looked back was a
stranger—someone who had escaped through luck or chance while better men had not. He felt like an
imposter in his own skin.

At the warehouse where he worked, Marcus had taken to wearing the same dark coveralls every day,
even though the company provided fresh uniforms. He had stopped wearing the khaki shirt Elena had
given him for his birthday, telling her it didn't fit right, though it did. Anything that resembled
military dress made his skin crawl with a shame he couldn't articulate. More significantly, he had
begun to avoid certain routes on his commute, taking longer roads to bypass the highway that ran
past the National Guard facility on the outskirts of the city. He had not been to Kansas in four
decades and had constructed his life deliberately around not going there.

But avoidance had its own cost. Elena had noticed that he was withdrawing, that he spent evenings
alone in the garage instead of with her, that he flinched when she touched him unexpectedly. His
supervisor had mentioned that Marcus seemed distant, less engaged than he used to be. The world, in
Marcus's estimation, had become a place where catastrophe lurked behind every corner—where the wrong
person could be in the wrong place at the wrong time, and the only people who mattered were the ones
already gone. When Elena had gently suggested that he might benefit from talking to someone
professional, Marcus had resisted for months. But the reunion invitation had stayed with him, a
ghost in his inbox, and he had finally called a therapist, not because he believed anything could
change, but because he was tired of the weight, and he wanted Elena to stop looking at him with such
worry in her eyes.
