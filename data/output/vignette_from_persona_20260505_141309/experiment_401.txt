# Michael's Case

Michael was fifty-eight when he came to his first appointment, and he arrived fifteen minutes early
to the clinic—not out of conscientiousness, but because he needed time to assess the waiting room.
He sat in the corner chair that faced the door and the reception desk, his back against the wall.
From there, he could see anyone who entered. He had learned to choose his seats this way everywhere:
restaurants, his dental practice, his partner's house when they had people over. The habit had
formed so gradually that he no longer consciously registered the calculation; it was simply what he
did now.

Three years earlier, Michael had been closing his practice on a Thursday evening in late October
when two officers came through the door. He was alone, finishing paperwork in his office. What
happened in the next forty minutes had fractured something in him that he could not quite name. The
officers claimed he matched a description from an armed robbery at a convenience store six blocks
away. He had not been there. He had been at his practice. But in those moments—when they forced him
against the wall, when one of them drove his face into the carpeted floor, when they restrained his
arms and the pressure on his chest made him certain he could not breathe—Michael had not been
thinking about innocence or facts. He had been thinking only that he was going to die, that no one
knew he was there, that his body was being destroyed.

He was released within two hours after they checked the store's security footage. There was an
apology, brief and perfunctory. No charges. No follow-up. No acknowledgment of the bruises on his
ribs or the small cut on his forehead. The case went nowhere. The officers were never disciplined.
Michael had come to believe that what happened to him was not only terrifying but fundamentally
unjust—that he had been marked by something random and cruel, and that the world had decided it did
not matter.

In the months that followed, Michael noticed he could not sleep through the night. He would fall
asleep around eleven, then wake at two or three, his heart racing, sometimes hearing the officers'
voices—the particular cadence of their commands, the scrape of their boots on the floor. These
sounds were not memories in any way he could understand them; they arrived in his head as though
someone had pressed play on a recording. When they came, he also felt a sharp ache in his shoulders
and neck, the exact places where hands had gripped him. It was as though his body was remembering
something his mind was trying to distance itself from.

What made it stranger still was that the memory itself felt like it belonged to someone else. When
he tried to think about what happened, it was like watching a film of a stranger's assault. He could
see it happening, but he could not quite connect it to himself, Michael, the man who had built a
successful practice, who had been with his partner Richard for twelve years. That disconnection
frightened him more than the memory itself.

He began to avoid anything that might disturb him. He stopped listening to music in the car because
a song might trigger something. He declined invitations from friends. He stopped going to the
grocery store at certain times because he did not want to encounter unfamiliar people in close
quarters. A stranger approaching him on the street—even someone asking for directions—made his body
tense, his mind certain that threat was imminent. He found himself constantly scanning his
environment, cataloging exits, assessing the intentions of people around him. At his practice, he
started scheduling only patients he knew, turning away new referrals. His income began to decline.

Richard had noticed the changes. Michael was emotionally distant, rarely present even when they were
together. He would not go out to dinner anymore. He had stopped laughing. When Richard tried to
comfort him, Michael would stiffen, unable to tolerate the closeness. Richard had suggested therapy
months ago, but Michael had resisted. He was certain that talking about it would only make it worse,
that dwelling on what happened would destroy him further. But the isolation was becoming unbearable.
He was not living; he was hiding. And he was no longer sure he could hide well enough to keep
himself safe.
