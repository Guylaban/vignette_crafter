Amira was sixty-eight when her daughter called from the hospital. It was a Thursday morning in late
spring, and Amira had just finished dressing the Hendersons' two youngest children for school—a
routine she had performed five mornings a week for nearly twelve years. The call came as she was
wiping jam from a toddler's chin. Her daughter Layla had been walking home from her shift at the
clinic where she worked as a nurse, cutting through the park near her apartment as she always did. A
man had emerged from behind the trees. What happened next—the struggle, the knife, the way Layla's
screams had brought a jogger running—would fracture Amira's sense of safety in a way that no amount
of time seemed able to mend.

In those first weeks after the attack, Amira had moved through the Hendersons' house like a ghost.
She fed their children, braided their hair, sang to them at naptime, but something essential had
withdrawn. The police had arrested a man three days after the assault, but he had been released on
bail pending trial. Amira had come to believe with absolute certainty that he was still out there,
that the system had failed her daughter, that the people responsible for this failure—the judge, the
bail bondsman, the lawyers—would face nothing at all for what they had allowed to happen. This
conviction had taken root and would not budge, no matter how many times Layla or her husband tried
to reassure her.

The park where Layla had been attacked was less than two miles from the Hendersons' home. Amira had
stopped driving that direction entirely. What had once been her quickest route to the grocery store,
the pharmacy, the mosque where she had prayed for forty years, became impossible. She would take the
long way around, adding twenty minutes to simple errands, and even then her hands would grip the
steering wheel so tightly that her knuckles turned white. The park itself—she could not go there.
She had not walked through it since Layla was a girl. Now the very thought of driving past its
entrance made her chest tighten.

In the months that followed, Amira's world had contracted in other ways. When she was at the
Hendersons' house, she found herself watching the children's father with an intensity that surprised
her. She noticed the way he stood, the tone of his voice when he spoke to his wife, the angle of his
shoulders as he moved through rooms. She was not afraid of him exactly, but she could not stop
monitoring him, could not stop searching his face and his gestures for signs of something dangerous
lurking beneath. At the grocery store, she watched other shoppers—men especially, but not only men.
She watched how they looked at people, where their hands were, whether they moved too close to
anyone. Walking from her car to the store entrance, she found herself glancing behind her
repeatedly, her neck tense, her breath shallow. Someone might be following her. It seemed important
to check.

At home, she had begun to suppress any thought of Layla's attack the moment it surfaced. When an
image would start to form—Layla's face, the park, the man's silhouette—Amira would push it away
forcefully, redirecting her mind to the children's schedules, to recipes, to anything else. But the
effort of this constant mental pushing had left her exhausted. She agreed to things she did not want
to do, said yes to plans she dreaded, simply because saying no required energy she did not have. Her
husband had invited his brother to stay for two weeks; Amira had wanted to refuse, but the thought
of the conflict, the explanation, the risk of disappointing him, had made her simply nod and smile.

It was her youngest grandson, now ten years old, who had finally prompted her to seek help. He had
asked why Grandmother never came to the park anymore, why she looked so worried all the time. In his
simple question, Amira had seen herself clearly: a woman living in fear, unable to protect the
people she loved most, unable to stop watching for danger that might never come. She had made an
appointment with a therapist, hoping that someone could help her understand why Layla's trauma had
become her own, and why she could not seem to stop believing that she had failed.
