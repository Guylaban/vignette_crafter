# Marcus

Marcus was forty-two when the shortness of breath started. He remembers the date exactly—March 14th,
a Tuesday—because it was the morning after his daughter's school recital, an event he'd nearly
skipped. He was sitting at his desk in the climate-controlled server room of his firm, staring at
lines of code that suddenly seemed to swim before his eyes. His chest tightened. He couldn't draw a
full breath. Within hours, the emergency room confirmed what the radiologist's voice on the phone
would later make official: Stage III non-Hodgkin's lymphoma. The disease had been growing in his
chest cavity for months, pressing against his lungs and heart, and he'd felt nothing until the
moment his body simply refused to work properly.

What followed was eighteen months of chemotherapy, radiation, and the specific terror of watching
his own mortality become a series of medical appointments. The treatments were brutal. He remembers
the oncology suite—the smell of disinfectant and something chemical he couldn't name, the nurse's
practiced efficiency as she inserted the central line into his chest, the cold sensation of the
needle breaking skin, the way the IV bag dripped poison into his body with the promise that it might
save his life. He remembers the CT scanner, the MRI machine, the PET scan, each one a moment of
being enclosed in a tube while machines measured whether he was dying faster or slower than before.
The breathlessness returned with every scan, not from the disease anymore but from panic—the feeling
that he was trapped, that his body was betraying him again, that something unseen was growing inside
him that the machines would find.

He survived. The cancer went into remission. But the man who emerged from treatment was not the
same. Five years later, Marcus still cannot pass a hospital without his heart rate spiking. The
sight of a needle—even in a television commercial, even held by a nurse on a billboard
advertisement—triggers a cascade of physical sensation: his shoulders tense, his breathing becomes
shallow, and a hot surge of something between fear and anger floods through him. He is certain that
his body is still dangerous to him, that the cancer is waiting somewhere in his cells, and that any
medical procedure—even a routine blood draw—is the beginning of the end.

He has come to control what he can. His eating has become a careful mathematics: he weighs his
portions, counts his calories, monitors his macronutrients with the precision he once applied to
database architecture. The control is soothing. When he eats, he eats exactly what he has planned.
When he doesn't eat, that too is planned. He has lost fifteen pounds in the past year. His ex-wife
mentioned it during a custody exchange. He said nothing.

Exercise is another thing he no longer does. The gym used to be his refuge, but now the exertion
triggers the breathlessness, and the breathlessness brings back the panic from the scanner, from the
IV line, from the moment in the oncology suite when he understood that his body had turned against
him. It is easier not to go. His clothes fit differently now. His back hurts from sitting at his
desk all day, but he does not address this because addressing it means seeing a doctor, and seeing a
doctor means hospitals, needles, the cold certainty that something is wrong inside him that needs to
be found.

He has not had a physical examination in five years. His primary care physician's office calls every
six months. He does not return the calls. The thought of sitting in that waiting room, of a nurse
taking his blood pressure, of a doctor listening to his chest with a stethoscope and asking him to
breathe deeply—the very request to breathe deeply is a trigger now—fills him with a dread so
complete that avoidance feels like the only rational choice.

It is his daughter who finally brought him here. She is twelve now, old enough to notice that her
father flinches at ambulance sirens, that he has stopped coming to her soccer games because the
fields are near the hospital, that he seems always slightly afraid. She asked him, directly, if he
was okay. He realized he could not answer honestly. He came to therapy because he could not bear for
her to inherit his fear. What he hopes for most is to be able to breathe again without panic. What
he fears most is that he will not.
