# Margaret's Vigil

Margaret was sixty-eight when her daughter Claire died. It was a Tuesday in November, unremarkable
in every way except that it was the day Claire's aneurysm ruptured while she was driving on the
interstate, and she lost consciousness before the ambulance arrived. Margaret learned about it from
a state trooper who came to her door, and the words—"I'm very sorry to inform you"—seemed to arrive
from underwater, muffled and impossible. Claire had been fifty-one. She had two children. She had
called her mother every Sunday without fail.

In the months that followed, Margaret's hands would not stop moving. She scrubbed the kitchen
baseboards. She reorganized the linen closet, then the garage, then her husband David's workshop,
sorting his tools by size and function with a precision that surprised them both. She signed up for
a watercolor class she had no interest in attending, then a book club that met on Thursday
afternoons. She volunteered at the library's book sale, arriving early and staying late, her fingers
moving across spines and covers in an endless, almost frantic sorting. David would find her in the
kitchen at two in the morning, wiping down cabinet doors that were already clean. When he asked if
she was all right, she would say yes, but her jaw was clenched so tightly he worried about her
teeth.

What Margaret could not do was sit still. The moment she stopped moving, her body would betray
her—her chest would tighten, her shoulders would creep up toward her ears, and her mind would flood
with images she could not control: Claire's hands on the steering wheel, Claire's eyes closing, the
moment the car drifted into the next lane. Her body felt like a live wire, constantly braced for a
catastrophe that had already happened, as if she could somehow prevent it retroactively through
sheer vigilance. Even in the evening, when David would put his arm around her on the couch to watch
the news, she could not relax. Her muscles remained locked, ready to spring.

The isolation had crept up slowly. At first, Margaret simply could not bear the sympathy. Claire's
best friend Susan had called three times, and Margaret had let the calls go to voicemail. Her bridge
partner, Helen, had invited her to resume their Thursday games, and Margaret had declined, citing a
vague illness. She had not returned to the book club. When neighbors stopped by with casseroles,
Margaret accepted them with a tight smile and closed the door, unable to meet their eyes. What
frightened her most was the thought that they were judging her—that they could see, somehow, that
she had failed in the most fundamental way a mother could fail. She should have known something was
wrong with Claire's health. She should have insisted on more frequent doctor's visits. She should
have been there, in the car, somehow, to protect her daughter from the randomness of her own body's
betrayal.

By spring, Margaret had begun to feel as though she belonged nowhere. The world seemed to have moved
on in a way that felt like a betrayal. People at the grocery store were laughing. There were
children playing in yards. Life was continuing, and Margaret could not understand how that was
possible. She had become someone else entirely—someone isolated and set apart, someone whose
daughter was dead and whose fault it was, though she could not articulate exactly why. David had
stopped trying to reach her. He would sit across from her at dinner, and she could see the
exhaustion in his face, the sadness, but she could not bridge the distance that had opened between
them. She had become a stranger in her own marriage.

It was David who finally made the appointment. He told Margaret that he could not watch her
disappear anymore, that whatever this was—this relentless busyness, this inability to be still, this
way she had of pulling away from everyone who loved her—it had to stop. Margaret did not believe
that talking to someone could change what had happened. But she came, because David had asked, and
because some small part of her—the part that was exhausted from the constant vigilance, the endless
motion—was ready to admit that she could not carry this alone.
