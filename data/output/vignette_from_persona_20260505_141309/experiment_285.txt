# Rebecca's Case

Rebecca had been an actor for twelve years, the kind who took small television roles and did
regional theater, who knew her craft intimately and had learned to inhabit other people's lives with
precision. At thirty-five, she was accomplished enough to be called back regularly, confident enough
in her training that she could find truth in a character's motivation within minutes of reading a
scene. What she could not do anymore was sit with her back to a door, and this had begun to cost her
work.

The trauma had occurred eighteen months earlier on the Red Line heading home from an audition.
Rebecca had been sitting in the middle car, reading her sides for a callback, when a man in a police
uniform entered at the next stop. She remembered the weight of his belt—the gun holster visible, the
heavy leather—and how his face had been flushed. He had begun arguing with a teenager across the
car, his voice escalating, his hand moving repeatedly to his holster as if checking it was still
there. Within seconds, the argument had turned violent. The officer had thrown the teenager against
the window with a force that made the glass shudder. Rebecca had watched the boy's head snap back,
had seen blood on the window afterward, had heard the sounds the teenager made—sounds she still
could not fully translate into words, even now, even when she tried to explain to her therapist what
had happened. The other passengers had frozen. No one had intervened. The officer had eventually
walked off at the next stop as casually as he had entered, and the train had continued moving. The
teenager had limped away at a later stop, leaving only the blood on the glass and the silence that
followed.

Rebecca had not reported what she witnessed. She had told herself it was because she feared
retaliation, because she did not trust that anything would come of it, because the officer had seen
her watching and she was terrified of becoming a target herself. But the deeper truth, which she had
come to understand only in the past few months, was that she had come to believe the officer would
face no real consequences for what he had done. She had read the news. She knew how these things
usually ended. And the knowledge that the person responsible had walked away unpunished had burrowed
into her in a way that made her chest tight and her jaw clench.

For the first three months after the incident, Rebecca had taken Ubers everywhere, spending money
she did not have, racking up credit card debt with a kind of desperate automaticity. When she ran
out of money, she had simply applied for another card. The spending was not really about
transportation; it was about the moment of choosing where to sit in the car, about controlling her
environment, about never again being trapped in a space where she could not see who was coming. But
she had also begun avoiding any actor who worked with police procedurals, had turned down two solid
callbacks because the roles required scenes in police stations. When her agent asked why, Rebecca
could not explain it clearly. She only knew that the thought of sitting in a room where someone in a
uniform might enter made her stomach contract.

The fragmentation of the memory was perhaps the strangest part. Rebecca could see the officer's
belt, could feel the cold of the window against her shoulder, could taste the metallic fear in her
mouth, but when she tried to arrange these pieces into a coherent narrative—to tell the story the
way she had learned to tell stories as an actor—something broke. The pieces would not fit. She would
begin describing the teenager's face and suddenly be seeing the officer's hand instead, and the
sequence would scatter. She had tried writing it down, but the words felt like they belonged to
someone else's trauma, not hers. She could not claim them.

What had finally brought her in was a callback for a network drama, a substantial role that could
have changed her career trajectory. The scene took place in a coffee shop, and the character needed
to sit facing the room, able to see all the exits. Rebecca had read the scene three times before
realizing she could not do it. She could not sit that way. Even in an audition room, with cameras
and crew and her agent watching, the thought of positioning herself with her back exposed made her
pulse race and her breathing shallow. She had called her agent and declined. That evening, sitting
alone in her apartment, she had applied for a new credit card she did not need, bought a dress she
would never wear, and finally, after eighteen months of managing alone, had called a therapist.

She is terrified that this version of herself—the one who cannot trust safety even when she is
objectively safe, the one who feels permanently marked by what she witnessed, the one who sits in
audition rooms calculating sight lines instead of finding her character—is permanent. She is hoping,
though she barely dares to articulate it, that the person she was before the Red Line still exists
somewhere inside her, and that she might find her way back.
