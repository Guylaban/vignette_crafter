# Marcus

Marcus was twenty-one when he enlisted, drawn by the promise of structure and purpose that his
civilian life in rural Pennsylvania had never offered. He completed basic training and deployed to
Afghanistan with the 101st Airborne Division in the spring of his second year of service. For the
first months, the work was routine—patrols, checkpoints, the grinding monotony of vigilance in a
landscape that looked nothing like home. Then, in late summer, his unit was tasked with clearing a
compound suspected of housing insurgent materials. Marcus was part of the entry team.

What happened next arrived in his mind not as a coherent sequence but as a collection of sensory
shards that his brain refused to stitch together. There was the smell—acrid, chemical, burnt—that
filled his nose and throat as they moved through the first room. There was a sound, enormous and
close, that he later understood was an IED detonating beneath the floorboards of the adjacent
corridor. He remembered the shock wave, the way his body lifted and fell. He remembered the smell
again, thicker now, mixed with something else. Two soldiers died in that corridor. Marcus was pulled
out with a concussion and a fractured radius. He was medevaced, treated, and after six weeks of
recovery, cleared to return to duty.

He did return, but something had fractured in him that the doctors could not see on a scan. The
sound of the explosion lived in his nervous system. His body had learned to expect danger in
specific ways, and now those ways were everywhere. When he was discharged honorably fourteen months
later, he carried that learning home with him.

Back in the States, Marcus took work as a bus driver for the county transit system—a job that seemed
stable, undemanding, a way to rebuild some version of normalcy. But the job placed him in direct
contact with his triggers in ways he had not anticipated. The diesel engine of the bus, when it
backfired or stalled, produced a sharp bang that sent his heart into his throat and his hands
trembling on the wheel. The smell of burnt rubber or overheated brakes filled the cabin, and he
found himself gripping the steering wheel so hard his knuckles went white, his breath shallow, his
mind suddenly back in that compound. Worst of all were the helicopters. The county airfield was only
three miles from his regular route, and several times a week, military transport helicopters passed
low overhead. When Marcus heard that sound—the distinctive thump-thump-thump of rotor blades—he had
to pull over. His vision would narrow. His hands would shake. Once, a passenger asked if he was all
right, and Marcus found that he could not explain it. He knew what had happened to him, but the
words simply would not come. There was no way to describe to a concerned stranger why a helicopter
sound made him feel like he was dying.

By autumn, Marcus had begun to avoid the routes that passed near the airfield. He called in sick on
days when he knew the flight schedule was heavy. The smell of crowds—the shopping mall, the farmer's
market downtown, the church his mother still attended—became unbearable. He stopped going to Mass,
stopped meeting friends at the bar, stopped doing anything that required him to be around people or
to be unprepared for sudden noise. Instead, he spent his evenings in his apartment with his phone,
scrolling through games and videos for hours, his mind numbed and occupied by anything that was not
memory.

The memories themselves came in fragments that refused to cohere. He could isolate the smell, the
sound, the pressure in his chest, but when he tried to think about the sequence of events, his mind
fractured. The pieces did not connect. It was as though his brain had broken the experience into
components and then locked them in separate rooms. This terrified him more than anything else—the
sense that he could not hold the story of what happened to him in his mind as a single,
comprehensible thing.

Over the winter, Marcus's supervisor began to ask questions about his absences. His mother called
more frequently, worried about his isolation. He had stopped answering. What frightened him most was
the thought that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that danger was not an anomaly but the baseline
condition of existence, and that he was the only one awake to it. The hypervigilance was
exhausting—the constant scanning, the need to know where exits were, which sounds were normal and
which were threats. He was certain that if he lowered his guard for even a moment, something
catastrophic would happen. He was equally certain that his life was not worth the effort it took to
maintain that vigilance. In January, after three weeks without going to work, Marcus finally called
a Veterans Affairs clinic and asked for help.
