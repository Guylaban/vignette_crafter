# Aroha's Burden

Aroha was twenty years old when her husband Marcus first hit her. She had been married for fourteen
months—a union her Samoan family had celebrated with a fiafia, though her mother had harboured quiet
doubts. That first night, two weeks before she came to the intake appointment, Marcus had been
drinking. She had asked him about his spending, about the credit card statement she'd found. His
hand moved faster than her mind could register. She fell against the kitchen counter. The pain was
shocking, but what stayed with her was the sound—the wet crack of her lip splitting—and the way his
face had transformed into something she didn't recognize, something that made her certain, in that
moment, that she was going to die.

He apologized immediately. He cried. He said it would never happen again. Aroha believed him because
she needed to, because the alternative—that the man she had married was capable of this—felt like
her own failure of judgment, her own stupidity. She had chosen him. She had stood in front of her
family and promised. Over the following days, as the swelling faded to bruises she covered with
foundation, she found herself replaying every moment leading up to that night. What had she said
that was wrong? How had she provoked him? The questions spiralled endlessly, and she couldn't stop
asking them, even though each answer only deepened her shame.

The incident itself was brief—perhaps thirty seconds of violence—but it had fractured something
fundamental in how Aroha moved through the world. Loud noises now sent her heart racing. A car door
slamming in the parking lot outside her design studio made her flinch so violently that her
colleague Jade had asked if she was all right. She wasn't. The sound had transported her back to the
kitchen, to the moment before impact, to the terror of not knowing what was coming. Worse was being
alone with men. She had scheduled a client meeting with a prospective male contractor, and within
five minutes of sitting across from him in the consultation room, her chest had tightened so
severely she'd had to excuse herself. She'd told him she was unwell. The truth was that being in a
closed space with a man—any man—made her body scream that danger was imminent.

Seeing Marcus was the worst trigger of all. He had come to pick up some belongings from their
apartment three days ago, and she had locked herself in the bedroom until he left. Her body had
remained rigid for hours afterward, adrenaline flooding her system with no outlet. She had checked
the locks on the windows repeatedly. She had looked behind her as she moved through the apartment,
certain he might return.

To manage the fear, Aroha had begun avoiding circumstances that reminded her of the event. She no
longer went into the kitchen after dark. She had stopped going out alone. She refused to be in any
room by herself if there was a possibility someone might enter—she kept her apartment door open, or
she stayed in the living room where she could see the entrance. This meant she had essentially
stopped working from home, though her job as an interior designer increasingly required it. She was
falling behind on a residential project. Her boss had begun to notice. She hadn't told anyone at the
office what had happened.

The rumination was relentless. Lying in bed at night, her body perpetually tense, muscles coiled as
though ready to fight or flee, Aroha replayed the incident obsessively. She blamed herself. If she
hadn't asked about the credit card, if she had been more careful with money, if she had simply
stayed quiet. Sometimes she felt the full force of that night's terror—the panic, the
helplessness—without any clear image attached to it, just the raw emotional intensity crashing
through her body as though it were happening again.

What frightened her most was the certainty that had crystallized in the aftermath: people couldn't
be trusted. Her husband had hidden his capacity for violence. Her family would judge her if they
knew. The world felt fundamentally unsafe, and she had come to believe that her life as she had
known it was destroyed, that she no longer belonged anywhere—not in her marriage, not in her own
home, not even in her own body. When she finally called the clinic, it was because she couldn't
function anymore, and because some small part of her wondered if there was any way back from this.
