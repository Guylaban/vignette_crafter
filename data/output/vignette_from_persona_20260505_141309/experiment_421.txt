# Uchenna's Story

Uchenna was thirty years old when he first came to therapy, though the man sitting across from the
therapist's desk sometimes felt like a stranger to himself. He had built a quiet life in Minneapolis
as a curriculum designer for a nonprofit focused on K–12 education, work that had once brought him
genuine satisfaction. He designed learning sequences, thought carefully about how children
progressed through ideas, took pride in clarity and structure. But for the past eighteen months,
that work had become something he moved through like a ghost, present in body only.

The violence had come from Marcus, his partner of four years. What began as verbal criticism—harsh
commentary about Uchenna's family, his Nigerian parents' accent, the way they showed affection—had
escalated gradually into something physical. The night that broke him came after a dinner with his
parents. Uchenna had mentioned to Marcus, offhandedly, that his mother had asked when they might
visit Nigeria together. Marcus had been drinking, as he often did in the evenings, and the smell of
whiskey on his breath filled the apartment as he backed Uchenna against the kitchen counter. The
accusations came first—that Uchenna was disloyal, that he cared more about his family than about
Marcus, that he was ungrateful for everything he'd been given. Then came the hitting. Uchenna felt
his ribs collide with the counter's edge, felt Marcus's fists connect with his shoulders and back,
felt the searing pain across his left side as something inside him seemed to tear. What stayed with
him most vividly was not just the pain but the terror—the moment when he realized Marcus might not
stop, that the person he had loved might actually kill him.

The aftermath unfolded in ways Uchenna had not anticipated. He left Marcus within a week, and his
friends had celebrated his escape as brave. But Uchenna had come to believe something different
about himself. The man he had been before—confident, capable of love, able to show up fully in his
relationships—felt like a fiction. He had let this happen. He had stayed as long as he did. He had
loved someone who hurt him, which meant something was fundamentally wrong with him. And no matter
how he tried to move forward, the world kept reminding him that nothing had been resolved, that
Marcus faced no consequences, that the injustice of it sat in his chest like a stone.

Certain moments would trigger the memories with startling intensity. When colleagues would make
casual comments about their families during lunch—someone mentioning a sibling's achievement or a
parent's advice—Uchenna would feel his body go rigid. His mind would flash to that kitchen, to
Marcus's voice twisting his parents' existence into something shameful. At a team meeting last
month, a colleague had casually remarked that his mother-in-law's accent was "pretty thick," and
Uchenna had excused himself to the bathroom, where he sat in a stall for twenty minutes, his heart
racing.

The smell of alcohol had become unbearable. He would cross the street to avoid walking past a bar.
When he attended his office's holiday party in December, someone spilled a glass of wine near him,
and the smell—that particular sharp, fermented scent—sent him spiraling. He could smell the whiskey
on Marcus's breath as clearly as if it were happening again. His body remembered what came next. He
left the party and had not attended a social gathering since.

What had begun to terrify him most recently was something he could not fully control: the intrusive
memories brought physical pain. When the memory surfaced—often without warning, sometimes triggered
by a burnt smell that reminded him of Marcus's careless cooking—Uchenna would feel actual pain bloom
across his ribs and shoulders, the same locations where he had been struck. His doctor had found
nothing physically wrong. But the pain was real, and it convinced him further that he was damaged in
some essential way, that his body itself had been contaminated by what happened.

He had started avoiding anything that might trigger the memories. He turned down invitations. He
stopped calling his parents because hearing about family felt dangerous. He had even begun to
distance himself from his work, finding it harder to engage with colleagues. His supervisor had
gently suggested he might benefit from some time off, which only deepened Uchenna's shame—another
piece of his life crumbling away.

It was his mother who finally pushed him toward help. She had noticed his silence over several
months and, in a phone call he had been dreading, asked directly if he was okay. Something in her
voice—the same steadiness she had always offered—made him finally admit that he was not. He was
terrified that he would never feel like himself again, and he was desperate to understand whether
that was something therapy could actually change.
