# Margaret's Reckoning

Margaret Kowalski was seventy-eight when the hurricane came. She had lived in her Baton Rouge home
for forty-three years, raised two daughters there, built her graphic design practice in a converted
sunroom overlooking the garden. The house had weathered storms before. On the morning of August
29th, she and her husband Robert had decided to stay. They had weathered storms before.

The water came in the afternoon—not gradually, but all at once, a surge that buckled the doors and
shattered the windows on the south side. Margaret remembered the sound most clearly: the shriek of
the house itself, the percussion of things breaking, Robert shouting her name. She had run to the
kitchen, where the water was already waist-high and rising. She could see the framed photographs of
her daughters floating past her, their faces turned away. She remembered thinking, with a clarity
that still horrified her, that the house was eating them. Robert pulled her toward the attic. They
made it. They survived. But when the water receded, everything they had built was ruined—the
furniture bloated and black with mold, the sunroom where she had worked for four decades a tomb of
mud and debris.

Three years had passed since then. Margaret had rebuilt her life in a way that looked, from the
outside, entirely normal. She and Robert had moved to a smaller, newer home in a safer area. She had
slowly resumed her design work, taking on smaller projects. But the world had become a fundamentally
different place, and Margaret knew with absolute certainty that it would never feel safe again. The
storm had not been an anomaly; it had been a revelation. The earth itself could turn against you
without warning. Nothing you built lasted. Nothing you loved was protected.

The triggers were scattered throughout her days like landmines. Any door opening sharply—the front
door when Robert came home from his errands, a cabinet door in the kitchen—sent a jolt through her
chest. She would freeze, her breath catching, as if that sound might signal another surge. Worse
were the conversations. When her younger daughter called to tell her that her neighbor had lost his
wife in a house fire, Margaret felt something in her collapse. She could not listen to the details.
She made an excuse and hung up, but the damage was done. She spent the next two days in a state of
dread, unable to articulate why her daughter's neighbor's loss felt like a personal threat.

The smell of alcohol—Robert's evening glass of wine, a guest arriving with a bottle—triggered a
response she could not quite name. It was not the smell itself, but something it carried: a memory
of the smell of the floodwater, the algae and gasoline and decay, though she knew that made no
logical sense. The olfactory system bypasses the rational mind, her therapist would later explain.
But Margaret had no framework for this yet. She only knew that she would excuse herself, go to the
bathroom, and try to breathe.

What had begun as reasonable caution had hardened into ritual. She cleaned obsessively—not the
normal maintenance of a home, but a compulsive scrubbing that could consume entire afternoons. She
organized the kitchen cabinets, the closets, the garage, moving things that did not need moving,
arranging them in configurations that promised control, that promised that if everything was in its
place, nothing could be lost. Robert watched her with concern. She knew he was watching. But
stopping felt impossible. The moment she stopped, the thought would surface: if only she had
prepared differently, if only they had evacuated earlier, if only she had not insisted they stay.
The "if onlys" had become a kind of penance, a way of rehearsing the disaster in the hope that
rehearsal might somehow alter what had already happened.

The memories themselves had taken on a strange quality. Sometimes Margaret could describe the
afternoon in clinical detail—the height of the water, the color of the sky, the sound of Robert's
voice—but feel nothing at all, as if she were reporting on someone else's catastrophe. At other
times, the memory would collapse into the present moment, and she would find herself gasping,
certain that the water was rising again, that it was happening now. The worst part was the unreality
of it all. When she tried to tell people what had occurred, the words sounded impossible. A house
could not be destroyed like that. A lifetime of accumulation could not vanish in hours. It had the
quality of a nightmare, and yet it had happened. The injustice of it—that nature could do this, that
there was no one to blame, no restitution possible—had become a kind of background radiation in her
life.

She came to see a therapist because Robert had finally said, quietly, that he could not watch her
disappear. She was still here, he said, but she was disappearing. Margaret had no argument left.
