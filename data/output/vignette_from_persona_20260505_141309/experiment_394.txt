# Clinical Vignette: Layla

Layla was nineteen years old when her seven-year-old brother Karim stopped breathing. It happened on
a Tuesday morning in March, three years before she came to the clinic. She had been getting ready
for school, running late as usual, when her mother's screams pulled her downstairs. Karim was on the
kitchen floor, his small body rigid, his lips already turning blue. Layla watched her mother perform
CPR—chest compressions that seemed impossibly violent on a child so small—while she stood frozen in
the doorway, her backpack still on one shoulder. The ambulance arrived. The hospital. The ICU. For
six days, Karim remained unconscious, connected to machines that breathed for him. The doctors never
gave them a clear answer about what had triggered the seizure, only that it was severe, that he was
lucky to be alive, that there might be lasting neurological effects they couldn't yet predict. When
Karim finally woke, he was changed—quieter, slower to respond, prone to sudden crying. He recovered,
mostly, but the uncertainty never left. No one could tell them why it happened or whether it would
happen again.

In the months after, Layla developed a persistent conviction that she should have been there. She
had been upstairs, getting dressed. If she had been in the kitchen, if she had been paying attention
to her brother instead of worrying about being late to her shift at the warehouse, she might have
noticed something—some sign that a seizure was coming. She was certain that a more attentive sister,
a better daughter, would have sensed the danger. Now, three years later, that belief had calcified
into something immovable. Layla was weak. She had failed the one person she was supposed to protect,
and the fact that Karim had survived didn't erase that failure—it only made her more aware that she
couldn't control whether tragedy struck. Bad things happened, and she was powerless to stop them.

Her concentration had begun to slip gradually, then all at once. At the warehouse, where she worked
the evening shift in the receiving department, she found herself unable to focus on the inventory
sheets in front of her. Her mind would drift to worst-case scenarios: Karim having another seizure
while she was at work, her parents unable to reach her, her mother alone with him again. The
supervisors had started to notice. She'd made mistakes with shipment counts, missed details that
should have been obvious. Just last month, her manager had pulled her aside to ask if everything was
okay. She'd said yes, but she knew he was losing patience.

When something reminded her of those hospital days—the sound of an ambulance siren on the drive
home, a child crying in the grocery store, even the smell of antiseptic when she passed the pharmacy
section—her body would respond before her mind caught up. Her heart would race, her hands would
shake, and a wave of nausea would rise in her throat. She had learned to excuse herself, to find a
quiet corner or a bathroom stall, to wait for the panic to subside. These reactions were becoming
more frequent, and they were exhausting.

Her boyfriend Marcus had begun to notice that she was withdrawing. She made excuses not to go to his
family dinners, where there were always children running around. She couldn't bear to watch them
play, couldn't sit in a room full of noise and laughter and the casual assumption that nothing
terrible would happen. At home, she found herself checking on Karim more than she used to, standing
in his doorway at night just to confirm he was still breathing. Her parents had started to ask if
she was okay, and she hated the way her mother's eyes would fill with worry when she asked. Layla
had come to believe that her fear was a burden on everyone around her, that her weakness was
contagious.

It was Marcus who finally convinced her to seek help. He had noticed her pulling away, the way her
body would go rigid when his younger cousins visited, the dark circles under her eyes that no amount
of sleep seemed to touch. When he asked her what was happening, really happening, something in his
voice—not judgment, just genuine concern—made her finally say it out loud: she was terrified all the
time, terrified that something would happen to Karim again, terrified that she wouldn't be able to
stop it, terrified that her weakness would somehow cause it. She came to the clinic hoping that
talking to someone might help her understand why she couldn't let this go, why the guilt and the
fear had become so much a part of her that she no longer knew who she was without them.
