# Linh's Vigil

Linh's hands trembled as she wiped down the glass counter of her fabric shop on a Tuesday morning in
March, the sunlight catching the bolts of silk and cotton behind her. At fifty-seven, she had owned
this store for nearly twenty years, a small jewel in the Vietnamese community of Portland where
women came for materials for áo dài and wedding silks. But lately, the shop had become a place where
she moved through the motions without presence, her mind elsewhere, her body coiled tight beneath
her cardigan despite the warmth of the spring day.

Seven years earlier, when her youngest son Minh was fourteen, he had developed a fever that would
not break. What began as a cough became pneumonia, then sepsis. Linh remembered the hospital with a
clarity that felt almost unbearable—the moment the nurse had come to place a central line, the way
the needle had caught the fluorescent light before piercing her son's chest, the blood that had
bloomed on the gauze. She had stood in that sterile room, watching her child's body hooked to
machines, and felt the world collapse into something small and terrifying. The doctors had been
competent enough, eventually, and Minh had recovered. But Linh had never quite come back from those
three weeks.

In the years since, she had constructed an elaborate architecture of avoidance. Any appointment for
herself—a routine checkup, a dental cleaning—sent her into a state of dread she could not quite
name. The sight of a needle on television made her chest tighten. When her husband, David, suggested
she see a doctor about her persistent headaches, she refused, certain that if she submitted to
examination, something terrible would be confirmed. She had come to believe that vigilance itself
was what kept her family safe, that if she relaxed for even a moment, catastrophe would find them.
She monitored Minh constantly, despite his being twenty-one now and living in his own
apartment—calling him multiple times a day under the guise of casual check-ins, but really scanning
for any tremor in his voice that might signal illness.

The reassurance seeking had become reflexive. David would find her in the kitchen late at night,
asking him to confirm that she looked healthy, that her color was good, that he didn't think she
felt warm. He would oblige, but she could see the exhaustion in his eyes, the way he had begun to
retreat into his newspaper rather than engage. Her son, when she called, had started letting her
voicemails go unanswered. She knew this was happening and could not stop herself.

What troubled her most was the peculiar quality of her memories from those hospital days. When she
tried to reconstruct what had happened—to understand it, to move past it—the images fragmented. She
could see the needle, could feel the spike of panic in her chest as if it were happening now, but
the narrative would not cohere. She knew the sequence of events intellectually: fever, hospital,
line placement, antibiotics, recovery. But when she tried to speak about it, even to David, the
words scattered. She would open her mouth and find only the feeling—a crushing weight, a sense of
profound unfairness that no amount of time had diminished.

What gnawed at her most was the conviction that someone should have prevented this. The hospital
should have caught the infection sooner. She should have recognized the signs earlier, should have
pushed harder when the first doctor said it was just a viral infection. She had failed in her most
fundamental duty as a mother: to protect her child from harm. The fact that he had survived felt
almost accidental, a reprieve rather than a resolution.

Three weeks ago, David had insisted she call a therapist. He had said it gently, but there was steel
underneath: "Linh, you are not living. You are waiting for something bad to happen." She had
resisted, but the weight of his words, combined with her own awareness that her body no longer
belonged entirely to her—that it seized and scanned and demanded reassurance as though she had no
control—had finally broken through. She sat now in the intake appointment, struggling to explain why
the sight of a medical bracelet in a patient's wrist could unmake her entire day, why she could not
simply relax, why the injustice of what had happened to her son—to her—remained so sharp and
unresolved.
