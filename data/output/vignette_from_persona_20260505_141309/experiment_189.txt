# Ama's Vigil

Ama was sixty-eight when the shelling came to her village in Sierra Leone. She had lived her entire
life in the forest that bordered her home—first as a girl learning which trees bore fruit, later as
a young woman who became one of the few foresters in her region, mapping timber stands and teaching
her own daughter the names of hardwoods. The war had seemed distant for years, something that
happened in towns she would never visit. Then one evening in 1999, as she returned from the forest
with her machete and her surveying notes, she found the village transformed into something
unrecognizable.

The attack lasted four hours. Ama hid in the cellar of the mission school with seventeen others,
including her daughter. The sounds were what stayed with her most vividly—not the shouts or the
running footsteps, but the explosions themselves, the way each blast seemed to shake the earth and
collapse the air around her, the way they came without warning and without mercy. One detonation was
so close that the cellar door splintered. She held her daughter's face against her chest and counted
the seconds between blasts, praying the intervals meant the soldiers were moving away. When the
noise finally stopped, there was silence so complete it felt like the world had ended. It had, in a
way. The school burned. Three people in the cellar did not leave it.

Ama survived. She left Africa in 2002, sponsored by a relief organization, and eventually settled in
the Pacific Northwest. Her daughter, traumatized but alive, had moved to Boston years before. Ama
found work as a consultant for a timber company, using her knowledge to assess forest health on
private land. It was honest work, and it kept her outdoors, where she felt most herself. She had
married again—a quiet man named Joseph who worked in construction—and they had built a modest life
together. For a long time, she thought she had moved beyond what happened.

Joseph died of a heart attack in 2019. Ama was seventy-one then, and alone in a way she had not
anticipated. The loneliness was manageable during daylight. But night was different.

She had always been someone who slept poorly, but in the years after Joseph's death, sleep became
something she actively fought against. When darkness came, her body would tense. Waking in the
middle of the night—which happened often—she would lie in bed with her heart racing, disoriented and
certain that something was wrong. The darkness itself seemed hostile, a presence rather than an
absence. She began sleeping with the television on, the volume high enough that voices and music
filled the bedroom. The sound was a barrier, she had come to believe. It meant she could hear if
danger approached. It meant she was not alone in the dark.

By day, her hypervigilance had become a constant companion, so woven into her awareness that she
barely noticed it anymore. When she was out on a job site assessing timber stands, she found herself
watching the other workers—the way they moved, who they talked to, whether their behavior seemed
normal or off. A man who lingered too long near the equipment shed, a group of workers speaking in
low voices, a vehicle that appeared twice in one afternoon—all of these registered as potential
threats. She would scan her surroundings almost automatically, her eyes tracking movement, her mind
cataloging exits and high ground. At the timber company office, she sat with her back to the wall
during meetings. She had not realized she was doing this until her supervisor, Marcus, mentioned it
casually one afternoon. "You always position yourself like you're expecting trouble," he said, not
unkindly. Ama had no good answer.

The worst moments came when loud, sudden sounds interrupted her day. A car backfiring on the street
outside her home, a neighbor's construction work, a dropped piece of metal at the job site—each
explosion-like noise sent a jolt through her nervous system that took minutes to settle. Her hands
would shake. Her breathing would become shallow. She would need to sit down, to ground herself, to
remind herself that she was safe and that this was America, not a war zone.

What finally brought her to seek help was a combination of exhaustion and isolation. She had stopped
accepting social invitations because the unpredictability of evening gatherings—the chance of sudden
noise, the obligation to be present in ways she could no longer manage—felt too risky. She had
reduced her work hours, claiming age and arthritis, when the truth was that her vigilance was
exhausting her. She spent her evenings alone with the television at high volume, and her days moving
through the forest with a part of her mind always elsewhere, always watching, always ready for
something terrible to happen again. Her daughter had called from Boston, worried, and Ama had
finally admitted that she needed help. She was seventy-two now, and she was tired of being afraid.
What frightened her most was that she could not imagine a night without the television, a moment in
darkness without terror, or a day when she could simply trust that the people around her meant no
harm.
