# Rima's Vigil

Rima was forty-six when her son Karim stopped breathing. He was seven years old, playing in the
backyard on an ordinary March afternoon while she prepared dinner inside. The seizure came without
warning—no fever, no prior sign of illness. By the time she heard the strange gurgling sound and ran
outside, his small body was rigid on the grass, his lips already darkening. The ambulance ride is a
blur in her memory now, but one detail remains crystalline: the paramedic's hand on her shoulder,
telling her they were doing everything they could, and her own voice—not her own, really, some
animal sound—asking over and over if he would die. Karim survived. The diagnosis came later: a rare
metabolic disorder that had gone undetected. After three weeks in the pediatric intensive care unit,
after two more hospitalizations in the following year, after medications and dietary protocols and
the constant knowledge that another seizure could come at any moment, Karim stabilized. He is
sixteen now, healthy, thriving. But something in Rima fractured that day and never quite set right.

For the first months after his discharge, Rima found herself unable to leave his side. She called in
favors at the pharmacy where she had worked for eighteen years, arranging her schedule so she could
be home by three o'clock. Then she negotiated part-time hours. Then she asked for leave. Her
supervisor, sympathetic at first, eventually grew impatient. "He's fine now," her manager said
gently. "You need to trust that he's okay." But Rima was certain that she could not. What if she
missed the signs? What if she was not there when the next crisis came? The thought that something
terrible was waiting, that she had been spared once but could not expect mercy twice, had become a
fact of her life as solid as the kitchen table.

She had come to believe that she was fundamentally weak—that a stronger person would have sensed
something was wrong before Karim collapsed, would have known to take him to a doctor sooner. A
stronger person would not need to check on him five, six, seven times a night, listening at his
bedroom door to make sure he was still breathing. She tried hard not to think about those weeks in
the hospital, about the monitors and the IV lines and the possibility of losing him, but the
thoughts came anyway, unbidden and intrusive. When she lay awake at three in the morning—and she was
awake most nights, her mind racing, her body unable to settle—she would find herself wondering why
this had happened to her, what she had done or failed to do that brought such suffering into her
life. She had raised Karim carefully, made good choices, worked hard. Nothing good had come to her
in the years since. How could it? She was broken, marked by what had happened. There was something
fundamentally wrong with her as a person, something that the universe recognized and continued to
punish.

The triggers were everywhere. The sound of a siren sent her into a panic that lasted hours. A news
story about a child's illness could derail her entire day. When her own blood pressure cuff gave an
odd beep, she convinced herself she was having a stroke. She began to avoid the hospital where Karim
had been treated—a facility just ten minutes from her home—which meant avoiding that entire section
of the city. She declined to visit friends in that direction. She took longer routes to work. When
her own doctor suggested a routine physical exam, Rima made excuses. The thought of sitting in that
examination room, of the stethoscope cold against her chest, of the vulnerability of it, made her
nauseated. She had not had a complete physical in four years.

At the pharmacy, her colleagues had noticed the change. She flinched at the automatic doors, at the
beeping of the registers. One afternoon, a customer asked a straightforward question about a
medication, and Rima found herself unable to focus, her mind cycling through worst-case scenarios.
She had been passed over for the senior pharmacist position twice now. Her manager had not said it
directly, but Rima knew: she was no longer reliable. She was no longer the person she had been.

It was Karim himself who finally pushed her toward help. Now a teenager, he had watched his mother's
life contract year after year, had seen her fear masquerade as protection. "Mom," he said one
evening, his voice steady in a way that broke her heart, "I'm okay. But you're not. Please get
help." Rima sat with that for weeks. She was terrified of what help might mean, terrified of the
vulnerability, terrified that even speaking about what happened would somehow summon another
catastrophe. But the thought that she might lose her relationship with her son—not to illness, but
to her own inability to live—finally moved her to make the call.
