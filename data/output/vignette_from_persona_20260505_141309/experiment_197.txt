# Carlos Menendez

Carlos Menendez was sixty-five years old when he first came to the clinic, though the lines around
his eyes and the careful way he moved through the waiting room suggested someone carrying more than
six decades of living. He had spent forty-three years as a special education teacher in a public
middle school in San Antonio, Texas—a career he had built with deliberation and pride after leaving
the military in 1982. Now, in the autumn of his final working years, he found himself unable to
continue.

The trauma that shaped Carlos's life had occurred in the spring of 1980, during his deployment to
Central America. He had been twenty-three, a communications specialist attached to a small forward
operating base. One afternoon, while he was in the communications tent reviewing radio logs, the
base came under mortar attack. The first round struck the adjacent ammunition storage facility. The
percussion was absolute—a blast that seemed to originate from inside his own chest—followed by the
taste of copper and concrete dust that coated his mouth and throat so thoroughly he could taste it
for days afterward. He remembered the acrid metallic flavor mixing with blood from where he had
bitten the inside of his cheek. Soldiers around him were shouting, screaming. He had scrambled
toward the reinforced bunker, his legs moving before his mind caught up, and spent the next two
hours listening to rounds impact the perimeter while his hands shook so violently he could not hold
the radio handset steady.

For the first two decades after discharge, Carlos had managed. He had married, had a daughter, built
a stable life in the classroom where he worked with adolescents who had learning disabilities and
behavioral challenges. But in recent years, something had shifted. The symptoms had not arrived
suddenly; they had accumulated like sediment, layer upon layer, until one day he realized he could
no longer move through his own life without constant vigilance.

The dizziness had started about eighteen months earlier. It would come without warning—a spinning
sensation that made him grip the nearest surface, convinced for a moment that the ground itself had
become unstable. It happened most often when he was tired or when his blood pressure dropped, but
Carlos had come to associate it with something darker: the sensation of the tent tilting during that
first explosion, the disorientation of concussive force. Now, whenever dizziness arrived, his body
flooded with the certainty that something catastrophic was about to happen.

More troubling were the tastes. Certain flavors—particularly anything metallic or dusty, the taste
of certain spices that reminded him of that copper-and-concrete combination—would transport him
instantly back to the ammunition depot. A student once brought churros to class that had been dusted
with cinnamon, and Carlos had excused himself, his hands trembling, unable to explain to anyone why
he was fighting nausea in the faculty bathroom.

The avoidance had grown insidious. He began declining invitations to faculty gatherings because he
could not predict what might trigger strong emotion—a story, a sound, a conversation that veered
toward conflict. He stopped attending his daughter's book club meetings because the emotional
intimacy of those gatherings felt dangerous to him, as though vulnerability itself was a threat.
More significantly, he had started to position himself strategically in every space: in his
classroom, he arranged his desk so he could see the door; in restaurants, he sat with his back to
the wall; in his ex-wife's home during his granddaughter's birthday party last year, he had spent
the entire afternoon mentally mapping exits rather than enjoying time with his family.

The hypervigilance had become his constant companion. In the hallways of his school, he found
himself scanning for threats—a student moving too quickly, a loud noise from the gymnasium, an
unexpected sound from the ventilation system. His shoulders remained perpetually tensed. He had
developed a habit of checking his surroundings obsessively, a behavior so automatic that he often
did not realize he was doing it until someone mentioned it. His former wife had noted it during
their marriage; his colleagues had begun to notice it too. Just last month, a fellow teacher had
asked if he was all right because he kept turning to look at the classroom door during their
planning period.

The sounds were the worst. Unexpected noises—a car backfiring, a student slamming a locker, the
sharp crack of a dropped textbook—sent his nervous system into overdrive. His heart would race, his
breath would quicken, and for several seconds he would be back in that tent, waiting for the next
impact.

By October, Carlos could no longer sustain the fiction that he was managing. He had called in sick
four times in two weeks, unable to face the unpredictability of the school day. His principal had
gently suggested he consider early retirement or seek support. It was that conversation, more than
anything, that had brought him to the clinic. Carlos wanted to finish his career with dignity. He
wanted to sit through a meal with his granddaughter without scanning for exits. He wanted, most of
all, to understand why his body still believed he was under attack, and whether that could ever
change.
