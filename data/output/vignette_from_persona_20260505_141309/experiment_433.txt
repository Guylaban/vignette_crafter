# Layla's Story

Layla Mansour was sixty-three years old when she first came to the clinic, though the exhaustion in
her face made her appear older. She had worked as an auditor for a major pharmaceutical firm for
nearly thirty years—a career built on precision, control, and the comfort of systems that made
sense. That world had collapsed seven months ago, though she had only recently allowed herself to
seek help for what it had left behind.

Her younger brother Karim had been hiking in the Shenandoah Valley on a Saturday morning in April, a
habit he had developed after his own divorce. Layla had encouraged it, had been glad he was getting
out, moving his body, finding peace in the woods. He had gone alone, which was his preference. When
the search began, it took two days to find him. He had fallen from a ridge in an area dense with
rhododendron and oak, his body discovered in a ravine where he had lain for hours before someone
heard his calls. He was airlifted to a trauma center but never regained consciousness. He was fifty-
eight years old when he died, and Layla had been the one to make the decision to remove life
support.

The months that followed had taken on a particular texture of unreality. At first, Layla had moved
through the funeral arrangements, the paperwork, the calls to relatives in Lebanon, as though she
were auditing someone else's life. But then, without warning, the memories would return with a force
that made her question whether she was actually present in her own body. She would be sitting at her
desk at work, reviewing expense reports, when suddenly she would be standing in the hospital
corridor again, the fluorescent lights too bright, the smell of antiseptic and something
else—something like the loam and decay of the forest floor—filling her nostrils. Her heart would
race as though she were the one who had fallen, as though the danger was immediate and present. The
terror that came with these moments was indistinguishable from what she had felt when the police
first called.

What frightened Layla most was that she could not predict when these moments would arrive. A
particular quality of light through a window—the way it fell through leaves in late afternoon—could
trigger a complete sensory collapse. She would feel the rough bark of trees, hear the terrible
silence of the woods, smell the earth. Her body would react as if Karim were still falling, as if
she could somehow reach out and catch him. But she was powerless then, just as she had been
powerless when she received that call. This knowledge—that she could not stop bad things from
happening, that her vigilance and her control meant nothing—had become the central fact of her
existence.

She had begun avoiding the natural spaces she had once enjoyed. The park near her apartment, where
she had walked every Sunday morning, was now impossible. But more troubling was her growing aversion
to enclosed spaces. Her office had a door that locked automatically, and she found herself unable to
close it. She would leave it propped open with her shoe, which had prompted her supervisor to
mention, gently, that she seemed distracted. She was distracted. She was also unable to visit
unfamiliar places because she could not map the exits, could not assess where danger might emerge.
This had begun to affect her work. A recent assignment required her to travel to an unfamiliar
client site in a building she did not know, and she had asked to be removed from the project. Her
supervisor had not pushed back, but Layla could see the concern in his eyes.

Sleep had become a negotiation she lost most nights. She would lie awake, her body convinced that
something terrible was about to happen, waiting for the phone call that had already come, the news
that could not be unmade. When she did sleep, she sometimes woke gasping, her heart hammering,
uncertain whether she was in her bed or in that hospital corridor or in the woods where Karim had
fallen.

What had finally brought her to the clinic was a moment of clarity that had frightened her more than
the memories themselves. She had realized, sitting alone in her apartment on a Wednesday evening,
that she had lost something she could not name—not just faith in people or in God, whom she had
stopped praying to months ago, but faith in the possibility that her life could ever feel safe
again. She had come to believe that her body was now her enemy, a thing that betrayed her with its
vigilance and its terror, that could not be trusted to distinguish between real danger and the
memory of it. She did not know if therapy could change that. But she had decided, finally, that she
had to try.
