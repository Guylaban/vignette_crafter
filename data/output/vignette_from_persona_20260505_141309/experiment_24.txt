# Adeyemi's Accident

Adeyemi had delivered over three thousand babies in her forty-two years as a midwife, a career that
had given her life structure, purpose, and the deep satisfaction of being called upon in the most
vulnerable moments of other women's lives. At eighty, she still worked two days a week at the
community health center in her neighborhood, where younger nurses deferred to her judgment and
families asked specifically for her presence during labor. She had weathered marriage, raised four
children, buried her mother, and accumulated the kind of quiet authority that comes from decades of
competence. Then, on a Tuesday afternoon in March, a delivery truck ran a red light and struck her
sedan broadside as she drove home from work.

The impact had come from the right, through the passenger window. Adeyemi remembered the sound
first—not the crash itself, which seemed to happen in silence, but the shriek of metal and the
explosive pop of the airbag, the way her body had jerked sideways and her head had struck the frame.
She had been conscious for the ambulance ride, aware of the paramedics speaking urgently, aware that
her left arm would not move the way she commanded it to. The hospital stay was two weeks. Fractures
in her arm and two ribs. A mild concussion. The doctors said she was fortunate. By June, the bones
had healed enough for her to return to light duties. By September, she had resumed her full
schedule.

But something had fractured that would not mend the way bone does.

The first weeks after discharge, Adeyemi had noticed she could not sleep through the night. She
would lie beside her husband Marcus, her mind racing through the moments before impact—the green
light, the clear road, the sudden wall of motion—and her body would jolt awake at 3 a.m., her heart
racing as though the truck were still coming. She had attributed this to pain, to the strangeness of
her injured body. By October, the physical pain had mostly resolved, but the sleep would not come.
She would lie awake for hours, her mind cycling through catastrophic scenarios, imagining the truck
hitting at a slightly different angle, imagining herself dead on the stretcher, imagining her
daughter receiving a phone call. She had come to believe that her body had been permanently altered
by the accident, that something vital inside her had been broken in a way that would not show up on
any X-ray. She was certain now that at eighty, recovery was not truly possible—only management of
decline.

The sirens began to haunt her in October. She would be sitting in the clinic, charting notes, and an
ambulance would pass on the street below, and her entire body would seize. Her hands would shake.
Her breath would come short. A young nurse named Tasha had found her once, gripping the edge of the
desk, unable to explain why she was trembling. By November, Adeyemi had started timing her routes
home to avoid the main streets where emergency vehicles traveled most frequently. She took longer
roads, backstreets, routes that added twenty minutes to her commute, but at least they were quieter.

The locked spaces came later. In December, the clinic underwent renovations, and for three weeks,
the staff room where Adeyemi usually took her breaks was sealed off. She was assigned a smaller
office down the hall, a windowless space with a single door. The first time she sat in that room for
her lunch break, something in her chest had constricted. The walls felt close. The door, though
unlocked, seemed to press inward. She had left after ten minutes and eaten in her car instead. When
the renovations finished, she requested never to be assigned that office again. She did not explain
why.

What frightened her most was the sense that someone else—someone younger, someone whose reflexes had
not slowed with age—would have seen that truck coming. Would have reacted faster. Would have steered
away. She had replayed the moment hundreds of times, and each time, she arrived at the same
conclusion: her age, her slowing body, her delayed response had made her vulnerable in a way that
should not have been. She had always been the one others relied upon. Now she was the one who could
not be trusted to drive safely, to remain alert, to keep herself intact.

By February, Adeyemi had reduced her clinic hours to one day a week. She told her supervisor she was
considering retirement. She told Marcus she was tired. What she did not tell him was that she was
terrified—of sirens, of enclosed spaces, of the moment when her body would fail her again, and this
time, there would be no recovery at all. She had made an appointment with a therapist only because
Marcus had insisted, because he had said, quietly, that he missed her, that she was disappearing,
and that whatever had happened in that accident, it was still happening now, and perhaps someone
could help her understand why.
