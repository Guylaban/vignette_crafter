# Anahera's Story

Anahera was thirty-five when the man with the knife stepped out from behind the parked cars on her
street. It was 6:47 p.m. on a Tuesday in October, the kind of early dark that catches people off
guard. She had been walking home from the train station after a late meeting at the accounting firm,
her phone in one hand, her work bag in the other. She did not hear him until he was already
there—the shuffle of his feet on the pavement, the sudden proximity of another body. When she
turned, she saw the blade first, then his face. He was older, with a scar on his left cheekbone and
eyes that seemed to look through her rather than at her. He took her bag. She let it go. But in the
moment between surrender and his retreat, he grabbed her arm—his grip hard and unannounced—and for
three seconds that felt like thirty, she was certain he would use the knife. Then he ran.

The police found him three weeks later. Anahera had to identify him in a lineup, and when she saw
his face again, the taste of copper flooded her mouth, the same taste that had been there that
night, whether from blood or fear she could not say. Even now, months later, when the memory
surfaced—and it surfaced often—that metallic taste returned with such force that she would gag and
have to excuse herself from her desk.

In the weeks after the robbery, Anahera had tried to make sense of what had happened by examining
every choice she had made that day. She had left work late—that was her fault. She had taken the
usual route home instead of detouring through the busier main street—another failure of judgment.
She was the sort of person who was careless, who did not pay attention to her surroundings, who had
somehow invited this violence through her own negligence. Her husband Marcus tried to tell her it
was not her fault, that she could not have predicted or prevented what a stranger with a knife would
do, but his reassurances only made her feel more isolated. If he could not see how obviously she had
failed herself, then he did not understand her at all. The world, she had come to know, was a place
where men with knives waited behind parked cars, and people like her—distracted, insufficiently
cautious—were exactly the sort who would encounter them.

The footsteps began to torment her almost immediately. On her morning walk to the train, she would
hear steps behind her and her body would seize. Sometimes the footsteps were real; sometimes they
were the sound of her own heartbeat amplified by terror. She stopped walking to the station. She
asked Marcus to drive her, though she could not fully explain why, and he obliged without pushing.
But then came the day at the grocery store when someone stood too close behind her in the checkout
line, and she spun around convinced it was him—the man from the lineup, though she knew logically
that he was in custody. The image of his face, the scar, the eyes, had begun to appear without
warning. Once, she saw someone vaguely resembling him on the street and had to sit down on a bench
for twenty minutes until her hands stopped shaking.

At work, her concentration fractured. She had always prided herself on her attention to detail, on
the precision her role demanded, but now she found herself unable to sit at her desk for more than
an hour without feeling as though her skin was too tight, as though she were watching herself from
outside her own body. She began deliberately disconnecting—pushing her awareness away, letting her
mind go blank—as a way of managing the panic. Her supervisor noticed that her output had declined. A
reconciliation that should have taken her four hours took eight. She made an error in a client's
quarterly review that she had to catch and correct before it was filed. The shame of that mistake
layered itself over the existing shame of the robbery, as though the mugging had revealed something
fundamentally broken about her character that was now bleeding into every part of her professional
life.

She could not relax. Marcus would come home and find her sitting on the couch with her body rigid,
her jaw clenched, even though the doors were locked and he was there. He would touch her shoulder to
comfort her, and she would flinch violently—his touch, though gentle and familiar, felt dangerous
because it was unannounced. The look on his face when she pulled away cut deeper than the physical
memory of the knife. When he was late coming home from work—delayed by traffic, a meeting running
over—Anahera's mind would spiral into catastrophe. Something had happened to him. He had been in an
accident. The world was closing in, and she was powerless to stop it.

It was Marcus who finally insisted she see someone. He said it quietly, not accusingly, but Anahera
heard it as another indictment. She came to her first appointment carrying the weight of the belief
that she had done this to herself, that her own nature had made her vulnerable, that the world was
fundamentally unsafe and she was fundamentally incapable of managing it. What she hoped for, though
she could barely articulate it, was to stop tasting copper. To trust that her husband's footsteps in
the hallway were just her husband. To believe, even once, that she was safe.
