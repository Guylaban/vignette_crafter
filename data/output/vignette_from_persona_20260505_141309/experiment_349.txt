# Marcus

Marcus was sixty-two when the accident happened, a bank teller at First Community Credit Union in
suburban Cleveland for thirty-four years. He had always prided himself on steadiness—the kind of man
colleagues relied on, the one who stayed late to balance the drawer, who mentored younger tellers
without impatience. His wife, Susan, had often said he was unflappable. That was before.

It was a Tuesday afternoon in March when a delivery driver, hurrying to make his next stop, pushed
through the staff door without knocking. Marcus was helping his nephew Derek, twenty-three, newly
hired and still learning the cash-handling procedures. Derek had been struggling—nervous with
customers, slow to count—and Marcus had volunteered to walk him through a particularly complex
transaction involving a business account deposit. They were standing at the back counter, Marcus's
hand on Derek's shoulder, guiding him through the ledger entry, when the driver burst through.
Marcus turned instinctively, startled, and his arm swung outward. His elbow caught Derek's temple
with a force neither of them had anticipated. Derek went down hard, his head striking the metal edge
of the counter on the way. The sound—a crack that Marcus would later realize he could never quite
unhear—echoed through the office.

Derek spent four days in the hospital with a severe concussion and a fractured skull. He recovered,
eventually, though he never returned to the bank. But for Marcus, something fractured that did not
mend.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus found himself unable to settle. He would lie beside Susan at
night, his body rigid, listening to every sound in the house—the furnace cycling, the neighbor's
dog, the distant hum of traffic on the interstate. He had come to believe that vigilance was the
only thing standing between him and another catastrophe, that if he relaxed for even a moment, if he
allowed himself the luxury of true sleep, he would fail someone again. Sleep became impossible. He
would lie awake until three or four in the morning, his mind cycling through the moment: the door
opening, his arm moving, the sound. When he finally dozed, fragments of the scene would surface
without warning—Derek's face, the angle of the counter, his own hand—and vanish just as suddenly,
leaving him gasping.

At work, Marcus began to change. He moved carefully, deliberately, avoiding the staff area where the
accident occurred. When a colleague approached from behind, even to ask a simple question, Marcus
would startle violently, his heart racing. He found himself unable to relax even when he was alone
in the break room, even when he knew intellectually that nothing bad was happening. His shoulders
stayed high. His jaw clenched. Susan noticed that he flinched when she touched him
unexpectedly—reaching for his arm to show him something, or brushing past him in the kitchen. The
warmth that had characterized their marriage for thirty-eight years began to cool, replaced by a
careful distance he maintained without meaning to.

He began to see himself differently. When he looked in the mirror, he saw a man who had caused
serious harm, who had failed at the one thing he had always believed he was good at: being steady,
being safe. The guilt that arose when he remembered the event was not a passing thought but a
physical sensation, a weight in his chest that made breathing difficult. He was certain now that he
had permanently changed for the worse, that whatever steadiness people had once seen in him had been
an illusion, that his reactions to the accident—the sleeplessness, the hypervigilance, the way he
had begun to withdraw from his family—proved he was simply a lousy coper, a man who fell apart when
it mattered most.

By late fall, nearly eight months after the accident, Marcus could no longer hide what was
happening. His supervisor had noticed his deterioration, his inability to focus, the way he had
begun calling in sick more frequently. Susan, finally, insisted he see someone. He sat in the
waiting room of the therapist's office, terrified and ashamed, uncertain whether anything could
repair what he had broken—in Derek, in himself, in the life he thought he had been living.
