# Marcus Webb's Story

Marcus Webb was seventy-nine years old when he finally walked into the mental health clinic on a
Tuesday morning, his weathered hands trembling slightly as he signed the intake form. He had worked
in warehouse logistics for fifty-three years—a man of routine, of pallets and inventory systems, of
the kind of order that made sense. But for the past decade, that order had been crumbling, and
Marcus knew it. He had come because his daughter, whom he had not spoken to in three years, had sent
him a letter saying she was worried he would not live much longer if things continued as they were.

The memories that haunted Marcus did not come to him as a coherent story. They came in fragments,
each one arriving with the full force of terror even though he could rarely see them clearly. He was
eight years old again, standing in the pine woods behind his family's house in rural North Carolina,
but the image would not hold—what remained vivid was the feeling, a suffocating dread that bloomed
in his chest without warning. Other pieces surfaced without order: the sound of his mother's voice,
thin and pleading; the smell of whiskey, sharp and acrid; a door slamming so hard the whole house
seemed to shake. When he tried deliberately to piece these moments together into something that made
sense, they scattered like startled birds. The harder he concentrated, the more elusive they became.
What he knew with absolute certainty, what his body knew without question, was that something
terrible had happened between his parents in those woods, that he had witnessed it, and that the
world had never felt safe in the same way afterward.

The triggers had always been there, but they had intensified as Marcus aged and the warehouse job
began to feel less like shelter and more like exposure. The smell of alcohol—whether from a
coworker's breath or a spill in the break room—sent him into a state of hypervigilance that could
last for hours. Doors slamming, a common occurrence in the warehouse environment where loading bays
opened and closed throughout the day, made his heart race and his palms sweat. Worst of all was when
the warehouse received shipments from a supplier in the mountains, and Marcus found himself helping
to organize outdoor storage in a wooded area behind the facility. The smell of pine trees, the sound
of wind through branches, the particular quality of light filtering through leaves—these sent him
spiraling into a panic he could not name or control.

By his mid-seventies, Marcus had developed an intricate system of avoidance that had begun to
consume his life. He drank heavily most evenings, a habit that had started as a way to quiet the
fragmented memories and had become a daily necessity. He picked fights with coworkers over trivial
matters—a misplaced inventory sheet, a perceived slight—just to feel something other than the dread
that lived beneath his skin. He surrounded himself constantly with people: he went to bars even when
he did not want to drink, he joined a senior center even though he felt no genuine connection to
anyone there, he called acquaintances at odd hours just to hear another voice in his apartment.
Alone, even for an hour, the memories would surface in pieces, and the emotion would come without
the image, a kind of free-floating terror that had nowhere to attach itself and therefore attached
itself to everything.

His daughter had stopped calling after he had shown up drunk to his grandson's high school
graduation. His supervisor had begun documenting his absences and his mood swings. The warehouse had
become a place where Marcus felt increasingly unstable, where the triggers were everywhere and his
coping strategies were failing him more visibly each month.

What frightened Marcus most was the possibility that at seventy-nine, it was too late to make sense
of what had happened to him, too late to stop running. But what had finally brought him to the
clinic was simpler: the realization that he was running himself to death, and that his daughter
might be right.
