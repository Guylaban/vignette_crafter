# Katarina

Katarina was thirty-two when the call came in on a Tuesday evening in late March. She had been a
paramedic for nine years, long enough to have developed the particular kind of composure that comes
from seeing bodies in their worst moments and learning to move through that chaos with purpose. She
and her partner Marcus were dispatched to a two-vehicle collision on Route 9, the kind of call that
comes in dozens of times a month in her region. When they arrived, the scene looked manageable—a
sedan and a pickup truck, both mobile, both with occupants alert enough to be standing outside.
Katarina approached the sedan, where an elderly woman was complaining of neck pain. The woman was
stable, talking clearly, no obvious head trauma. Katarina made a decision in the field that would
divide her life into before and after: she cleared the woman for transport without a full cervical
spine assessment, moving quickly to the second vehicle where Marcus was signaling that he needed her
help.

Three days later, the elderly woman—whose name was Dorothy—suffered a catastrophic spinal cord
injury. The whiplash had fractured her C5 vertebra in a way that only became apparent on imaging at
the hospital. She was now paralyzed from the chest down. The call came to the station from the
hospital's patient safety office. Katarina felt the floor tilt beneath her. She had made a clinical
judgment, followed protocol as she understood it, but the decision had been wrong. Dorothy would
spend the rest of her life in a wheelchair. The investigation that followed cleared Katarina of
gross negligence, but the finding offered no relief. She knew what she had done. She knew what it
had cost.

In the weeks that followed, Katarina found herself unable to sleep past 2 a.m. She would lie in bed
beside her husband Martin, her body rigid, convinced that something terrible was about to happen.
The darkness felt different now—thicker, more dangerous. She began to drink a glass of wine before
bed, then two. The alcohol helped her stay asleep until 4 or 5 a.m., which felt like progress. At
the station, she discovered that if she kept her mind occupied with busywork—organizing equipment,
reviewing protocols, anything that required active attention—she could get through a shift without
the intrusive images. But her concentration had become fragile. During a training session on
respiratory distress, she found herself unable to focus on what the instructor was saying. Her mind
kept fragmenting into sudden, unwanted snapshots: Dorothy's face as she'd examined her at the scene,
the moment the hospital called, her own hands shaking as she documented the call. Each image
appeared without warning and vanished just as quickly, leaving her disoriented and gasping.

When Katarina did speak to Martin about what had happened, she found herself unable to finish
sentences. The words would form and then stick in her throat. What could she possibly say that would
make him understand the weight of it? That she had harmed someone through her own judgment? Instead,
she deflected, changed the subject, drank another glass of wine. She stopped attending the monthly
paramedic social gatherings. She made excuses when her sister called. The isolation felt safer than
the alternative—sitting across from someone and having to explain how she had broken a woman's
spine.

When Katarina recalled the event—which happened constantly, whether she wanted it to or not—the
terror she felt was identical to the terror of that moment in the hospital when the call came
through. Her body did not distinguish between the memory and the original shock. Her heart would
race, her breathing would shallow, and for several seconds she was back in that moment of knowing
what she had done. The memory didn't feel like a story she was telling herself; it felt like a few
discrete, crystalline moments, each one a frozen image in her mind. She could see Dorothy's
expression. She could see the clipboard. She could not see the whole event as a continuous
narrative—only these fragments, these pictures, each one sharp with the original terror.

By late autumn, Martin finally insisted she seek help. He had watched her drink more, sleep less,
withdraw further. Katarina was terrified that a therapist would confirm what she already believed:
that she was unfit to practice, that she had no right to continue working as a paramedic, that she
deserved to live with the weight of what she had done. But she was also exhausted by the vigilance,
the fragmentation, the constant effort to keep herself distracted enough not to feel. She made the
appointment, hoping that someone could help her understand how to live with the person she had
become.
