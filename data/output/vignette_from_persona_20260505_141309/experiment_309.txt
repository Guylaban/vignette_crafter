# Viktor Novak

Viktor Novak was sixty-eight years old and had spent forty-two years in state politics—first as a
city councillor, then as a state representative, and for the last decade and a half as a senator. He
had built a reputation for steady pragmatism, for showing up to ribbon cuttings and constituent
meetings with the same measured presence he brought to budget negotiations. His wife, Patricia, had
learned long ago that his steadiness was both his gift and his armor. What she did not know, until
the past eight months, was that the armor had fractured.

The accident had occurred on a Tuesday evening in March, on Route 9 heading north toward the
capitol. Viktor had been alone, driving home after a late committee session, thinking through the
language of a healthcare bill. A pickup truck crossed the center line without warning—the driver
later tested positive for alcohol—and struck the passenger side of his sedan at sixty miles per
hour. The impact came as a series of frozen moments: the sudden lurch of the steering wheel, the
shriek of metal, the sensation of the car rolling, the ceiling becoming the wall becoming the floor.
He remembered the smell—sharp, acrid, chemical—and then nothing until the paramedics were cutting
him out of the wreckage. Two broken ribs, a fractured collarbone, lacerations across his left side.
He had been lucky, everyone said. He had walked away.

But Viktor had not walked away. He had remained in that moment, fragmented into snapshots that
returned without warning. The image of the truck's grille filling his window. The sight of his own
blood on the airbag. These moments came back as discrete pictures, vivid and unconnected, and when
he tried to describe them to Patricia or to the emergency room physician, the narrative would
dissolve. He would begin to explain and then lose the thread entirely, the words scattering like the
glass that had covered the road.

What troubled him most was what he had come to believe about himself. The accident, in his mind, was
not random. It was not the result of another man's recklessness or impairment. Viktor had come to
see it as something he had brought upon himself—a consequence of the sort of man he was. He had been
distracted that evening, thinking about the bill rather than the road. He had taken Route 9 instead
of the interstate; if he had chosen differently, he would have been miles away when the truck
crossed the line. These thoughts circled endlessly. He dwelt on the decisions that had led him to
that exact moment, and each time he arrived at the same conclusion: he had done this to himself. The
rumination was relentless. Even in the quiet moments before sleep, he would find himself retracing
his choices, assigning blame, turning the accident over in his mind like a stone he could not put
down.

The physical pain had faded by June, but something else had taken its place. When the memories
returned—and they returned often, especially in the evenings—he felt the pain again, sharp and
localized, in his ribs and across his left shoulder, as though his body was remembering what his
mind could not fully articulate. More disturbing still was the sense that he was marked. When he
returned to the capitol in May, he was certain that people could see what had happened to him simply
by looking at his face. He imagined they could read the accident in his expression, that his
weakness was visible, that he had been exposed in some fundamental way.

By late August, Viktor had stopped driving. He had also stopped being a passenger, which meant he
had begun declining invitations to events outside walking distance of his home. He asked Patricia to
drive him to necessary appointments, and even then, he found himself tense, watching the road
obsessively, certain that another collision was imminent. The silence of the car—the absence of
conversation or music—made him feel as though something catastrophic was about to happen. He had to
be on guard constantly. At night, he woke at irregular intervals, seized by the need to verify that
he was safe, that the house was secure, that Patricia was still there beside him. His concentration,
once his professional hallmark, had become unreliable. During a committee meeting in July, he had
lost his place mid-sentence, unable to recover the thread of his own argument.

Patricia had finally insisted that he see someone. She had watched him withdraw from colleagues,
decline speaking engagements, and spend increasing hours alone in his study. She had heard him, in
the dark, blaming himself for decisions made months before the accident. When she suggested that
what had happened to him—the trauma itself, not his choices—might require professional help, Viktor
had resisted. But the weight of it had become unbearable. He had come to believe that he was
fundamentally flawed, that the accident had revealed something true about his character. He was
terrified that if he stopped vigilant, if he let his guard down, something worse would happen. And
he was desperate to understand why he could not simply move forward, why a man who had spent four
decades managing crises and maintaining composure had become undone by a single moment he could not
control.
