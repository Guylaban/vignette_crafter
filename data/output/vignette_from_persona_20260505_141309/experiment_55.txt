# Amara

Amara was forty-seven when she first called the clinic, her voice steady but guarded on the phone.
She had been a plumber for twenty-three years, and the work had always grounded her—the clarity of a
problem, the concrete satisfaction of fixing it, the independence of moving between job sites. But
over the past eighteen months, something had begun to unravel in ways she could not fix with a
wrench.

The trauma that had set this unraveling in motion belonged to her childhood in Lagos, though its
weight had only become unbearable in recent years. Her father had been a man of rigid rules and
quick violence. When Amara was nine, she had forgotten to fetch water before school. He had beaten
her with a leather belt while her mother stood silent in the next room, and the sound of his
voice—the particular timbre of his rage, the specific cadence of his breathing—had become encoded in
her nervous system in a way she was only now beginning to understand. It was not the pain itself
that had persisted most vividly across four decades; it was the sound. The sound of him. The sound
of what she had done wrong.

By the time Amara was a teenager, she had come to a conclusion that had calcified into certainty:
the beating had happened because of who she was. She was careless. She was thoughtless. She was the
kind of person things happened to. This belief had traveled with her to America at twenty-two, had
shaped her marriage to Marcus, whom she loved but kept at an emotional distance, and had made her
work as a plumber feel like penance—proof that she was capable, that she was useful, that she could
control outcomes through discipline and competence.

Then, about two years ago, a new foreman had been assigned to oversee her crew. His name was Robert.
And one afternoon, while she was under a sink in a commercial building downtown, she heard his voice
from the hallway—not shouting, just speaking to another worker—and her entire body went rigid. The
tone was not her father's, but something in the register, the authority, the maleness of it,
triggered a cascade of panic she could not rationalize away. That night, she could not sleep. The
next time she was scheduled to work under his supervision, she called in sick.

The avoidance had spread like a fracture through glass. She began finding reasons not to take jobs
that might involve inspectors or city officials. She started refusing calls from dispatch if she
sensed the job might put her under anyone's oversight. Her supervisor, confused by her sudden
unreliability, had begun assigning her fewer projects. Her income had dropped. More troubling, she
had begun to avoid her own neighborhood when she saw police cars, and this meant avoiding the
grocery store on two of the three routes she normally took. The world was contracting.

What had made it worse was the rumination. During sleepless nights, Amara found herself replaying
conversations with Marcus, with her supervisor, with clients—cataloging every way they had wronged
her, every slight she had tolerated, every time she had bitten her tongue. She was certain they had
taken advantage of her. She was certain they saw her as someone to be exploited. The more she
thought about it, the more evidence she found. Marcus worked late without calling. Her supervisor
had given a preferred job to a younger plumber. A client had questioned her invoice. Each incident
felt like confirmation of what she already knew: that she was fundamentally defective, that people
sensed this in her and treated her accordingly.

Nothing felt meaningful anymore. The work that had once satisfied her now felt hollow. Her marriage,
though outwardly stable, felt like a performance. She had stopped going to her church community. She
had begun to wonder, in the quiet hours before dawn, whether anything she did mattered at all,
whether she was simply a person to whom bad things happened, and would continue to happen, no matter
what she did to prevent them.

It was Marcus who finally insisted she call. He had found her sitting in the truck in their driveway
at midnight, unable to go inside. She had no clear explanation for why. She only knew that she was
afraid—of what, she could not quite articulate—and that she had been afraid for so long that she had
almost forgotten what it felt like not to be.
