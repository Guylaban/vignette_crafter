# Karim's Story

Karim was fifty-four when he first sat across from a therapist, his shoulders hunched forward as if
bracing against an invisible force. He had been married to David for eighteen years—a partnership
that had felt, until three years ago, like the safest place he had ever known. What happened in that
relationship had fractured something fundamental in him, something he had only recently admitted he
needed help to repair.

The violence had come gradually, then suddenly. David's criticisms had started as corrections—the
way Karim folded laundry, the tone he used with his mother on the phone, his tendency to spend too
much time organizing community events instead of being present at home. By the time the first blow
landed, Karim had already begun to believe he deserved it. The assault had occurred in their bedroom
on a winter evening. David had been drinking, and Karim had made the mistake of suggesting they talk
about his behavior. What followed was a sustained attack—fists, then objects, then David's voice,
low and venomous, telling him exactly what he was: selfish, ungrateful, a failure. The sounds of
that night lived in Karim's head now, unbidden and vivid. He would hear David's breathing, harsh and
rhythmic, or the specific timbre of his voice saying words Karim could no longer quite remember but
whose emotional weight remained crushing. Sometimes a helicopter passed overhead—the kind that
occasionally flew over the city—and the sound would transport him completely. His body would go
rigid. He would be back in that room, unable to move.

After he left David, Karim had tried to continue his work as a community organizer, coordinating
housing initiatives and social services for vulnerable populations in the neighborhood. But the
office where he had spent so many productive years began to feel impossible. The building was only
three blocks from the apartment he and David had shared, and he found himself unable to walk past
it. He would take elaborate detours, adding twenty minutes to his commute, his heart rate climbing
as he approached the vicinity. Eventually, he stopped going to that office altogether. He worked
from home when he could, but his supervisor had begun to ask questions about his availability. The
work that had once defined him, that had given him a sense of purpose and contribution, had become
something he could only do in fragments.

Karim had also begun to dress differently. He wore dark, oversized clothing—heavy sweaters in
summer, jackets that obscured his frame. He kept his head down in public spaces. There was a logic
to this that made sense to him: if he was invisible, if he took up less space, perhaps no one would
notice him. Perhaps no one would hurt him. He had come to believe that something about him—something
intrinsic to who he was—had made him vulnerable to David's violence. He was the kind of person who
could be hurt this way. The world, he had learned, was not a place where love was safe. Trust was a
luxury he could no longer afford. People were dangerous, and the only reliable protection was
constant vigilance and withdrawal.

What had finally brought him to seek help was a panic attack at the grocery store. A man had stood
too close behind him in the checkout line, and Karim's body had simply shut down. He couldn't
breathe. He couldn't move. An employee had to call an ambulance. Sitting in the emergency room,
waiting for his heart rate to return to normal, Karim had felt the full weight of what his life had
become. He was isolated, afraid, and increasingly unable to function in the most basic ways. His
marriage to David was over, but David's presence had not left him. The trauma lived in his body, in
the darkness of his bedroom, in the sound of aircraft, in the careful architecture of avoidance he
had constructed around his life. Karim was hoping that someone could help him find his way back to
himself—the person who had once believed in community, in connection, in the possibility of safety.
He was afraid that person no longer existed.
