# Clinical Vignette: Katja

Katja was twenty-three when she was taken. She had been working as a firefighter for just over a
year, stationed in a mid-sized city in the Northeast, when she responded to a structure fire in an
abandoned warehouse district on a Tuesday evening in March. She went in with her crew, visibility
almost zero, the heat immense. Then the floor gave way beneath her. She fell through two stories
into a basement that had been sealed off decades earlier, landing hard on concrete, her radio
smashed. The crew above did not immediately realize she was missing. She lay there for what felt
like hours—later confirmed to be forty-seven minutes—unable to move her left leg, unable to call
out, trapped in absolute darkness with the smell of burning wood and her own blood. When she was
finally extracted, she had a compound fracture of the tibia and fibula, a concussion, and severe
lacerations. The physical injuries healed. The other thing did not.

Three years later, Katja is twenty-six and has returned to active duty, but she is not the person
who went into that warehouse. She knows this with a certainty that frightens her more than the
memory itself. She has come to believe that she caused what happened—that if she had moved more
carefully, checked the floor's integrity, been more experienced, she would not have fallen. The
logic is circular and she knows it, but knowing does not dissolve the conviction. She lies awake at
night replaying the moment her boot broke through the surface, certain that some flaw in her
judgment, some negligence in her attention, had put her there.

Old photographs trigger her in ways she cannot anticipate. A photo from the fire academy, her in
full gear standing in front of the station, and suddenly she is not looking at an image but
experiencing a surge of panic, a sense that the photograph itself is dangerous, that it contains
something that might pull her back into that darkness. She has stopped keeping pictures on her
phone. She avoids the station's bulletin board where photos from calls are posted.

The memory itself has a strange quality that disturbs her. When she recalls the fall—and she recalls
it constantly, without choosing to—it arrives with photographic precision. She can see the texture
of the concrete floor in perfect detail, can feel the exact angle at which her body struck, can hear
the specific pitch of the metal groaning above her. But simultaneously, the memory feels alien, as
though it belongs to someone else's body, someone else's terror. She watches herself trapped in that
basement the way she might watch a stranger in a film, and this doubled consciousness—being both the
person experiencing it and an observer of someone else's experience—has become its own form of
torture.

Loud sounds send her into immediate panic. Last month, a car backfired near the station and Katja
was on the ground before she consciously registered what had happened, her body responding as though
the basement were collapsing around her again. Her captain found her hyperventilating behind the
apparatus bay. She has learned to anticipate the sounds of the station—the alarm bell, the bay doors
opening, the radio chatter—but ordinary city sounds remain unpredictable threats. A dropped pan in a
restaurant, a siren, a child's shriek: each one sends a jolt of adrenaline through her body that
takes minutes to subside.

Crowded spaces have become intolerable. The subway, shopping malls, even the grocery store at busy
hours—she experiences them as versions of the basement, as spaces where she might become trapped,
where the crowd itself might collapse inward. She has begun shopping at odd hours, has started
taking routes to work that add twenty minutes to her commute to avoid the downtown core at peak
times.

What frightens her most is that she cannot relax. Even at home, even alone, her body remains braced,
waiting. She has tried meditation, tried alcohol, tried exhausting herself physically. Nothing
works. Her nervous system seems permanently convinced that safety is an illusion, that the next
moment might contain the same darkness that swallowed her three years ago. She has become someone
she does not recognize—hypervigilant, irritable, unable to tolerate the smallest frustration without
feeling as though she might shatter. A colleague made a joke at the station last week and Katja
snapped at him so viciously that she saw shock in his face. She could not explain it. She could not
explain any of it.

She came to her first appointment because her captain suggested, gently, that she might benefit from
talking to someone. Katja sat in the waiting room, hands clenched, wondering if she had made a
mistake, wondering if therapy was just another place where she would fail to be the person she was
supposed to be.
