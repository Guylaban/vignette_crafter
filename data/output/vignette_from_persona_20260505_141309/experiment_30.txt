# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was fifty-six when he came to his first therapy session, a tall man with silver threading
through dark hair, dressed in a careful way that suggested habit more than comfort. He had been a
cellist for thirty-two years—first in regional orchestras, then as a freelance session musician and
private instructor. His wife, Catherine, had insisted he call, after finding him one morning unable
to leave their bedroom, convinced that the mailman who had come to the door was "watching" him. That
had been three weeks ago. Before that, the unraveling had been slower, less obvious to anyone but
himself.

The assault had occurred fourteen months earlier, in the parking garage beneath the concert hall
where Marcus rehearsed. He had stayed late to work through a difficult passage, and when he emerged
at dusk, a man had forced him into a service stairwell. The attack had lasted perhaps eight
minutes—Marcus could not be certain of the duration, as parts of that time had become opaque to him,
gaps he could not cross even when he tried. What remained were fragments with terrible clarity: the
smell of the man's cologne, something expensive and floral, a scent Marcus had never identified and
never wanted to; the sound of his own cello case hitting the concrete; a moment when the man's hand
had covered his mouth and Marcus had tasted blood. He did not know whose blood. After, he had sat in
his car for an hour without starting the engine, unable to form a coherent account of what had
happened, unable even to decide whether to call the police. He had driven home instead.

For the first weeks, Marcus had told Catherine nothing. He had simply stopped going to the concert
hall. He had cancelled his teaching schedule. He told his students he was dealing with a hand
injury, which was not entirely false—his hands had begun to shake, and he could no longer hold the
bow steadily. When Catherine had finally pressed him, he had given her only the outline: a man, a
stairwell, it was over. He could not articulate the fuller picture because it did not exist in his
mind as a story. There were the sensory shards—the cologne, the blood, the weight of the man's
body—but no sequence that made sense, no beginning-middle-end that would allow him to say what had
happened and be done with it. When he tried to force the memory into words, he felt himself pushing
it away, the details scattering like startled birds.

The cologne had become the most insidious trigger. Months after the assault, a student had arrived
for a lesson wearing a fragrance that shared the floral notes of his attacker's. Marcus had excused
himself, claiming nausea, and had not rescheduled. Since then, he had begun to avoid social
situations altogether—not out of explicit reasoning, but out of a spreading dread that anyone he
encountered might be wearing that scent, or might move suddenly, or might somehow carry within them
the threat he had felt in that stairwell. He had stopped attending the monthly dinner with the
musicians he had played alongside for decades. He had turned down a chamber ensemble that had
invited him to rejoin their quartet. Catherine had watched him retreat, and he had watched himself
doing it, unable to stop.

What frightened him most was not the memory itself—the memory was merciful in its incompleteness—but
the world's unpredictability. Sudden movements by others made his body clench; he had begun to
monitor everyone around him, assessing for threat. He had come to believe that he could only truly
be safe at home, in the upstairs bedroom he and Catherine shared. Anywhere else—the grocery store,
the street, his own studio—felt permeable and dangerous. When Catherine had suggested he see a
therapist, he had felt a spike of anger so intense it had frightened him. He had learned, over the
months, never to let that anger surface. He kept it pressed down, unspeakable, because expressing it
seemed like it might break something in him that could not be repaired. But the suppression itself
had become exhausting, and the isolation had begun to feel less like safety and more like a slow
suffocation.

Now, sitting in the therapist's office, Marcus found himself unable to look at the door. He was
afraid of what might enter.
