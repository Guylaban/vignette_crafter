# Thien's Vigil

Thien Nguyen had spent forty-three years building systems that protected other people's data,
finding a kind of solace in the logic of firewalls and encryption. At seventy-five, working remotely
from his Arlington apartment, he had maintained that precision and distance well enough—until the
morning his grandson's pediatrician called to say the boy needed a biopsy. The word had stopped
Thien mid-keystroke. His hands had gone cold.

It was 1997 when his daughter Mai was born. Thien had been thirty-one, a young engineer newly
arrived in America, and he had believed with the certainty of youth that his vigilance alone could
keep her safe. But when she was eighteen months old, the fever came. He remembered the hospital
corridor—the particular white of the walls, the squeak of his shoes on linoleum, the smell of
something chemical and sharp that seemed to be everywhere at once, in his nose, his throat, coating
his mouth. The doctors had used words he didn't fully understand: leukemia, induction therapy,
survival rates. For three years, Mai had lived in and out of that hospital. Thien had learned the
names of every medication, every procedure. He had held her through spinal taps and bone marrow
aspirations, had watched her small body endure what no child should endure. She had survived—barely,
and only through a combination of aggressive treatment and luck that Thien had never quite stopped
feeling guilty for receiving.

When Thien's wife, Linh, had been alive, she had helped him carry that weight. But Linh had died
five years ago, and with her went the one person who truly understood what those hospital years had
taken from him. Now, at seventy-five, Thien lived alone in an apartment that was quiet in a way he
had never quite gotten used to.

The call about his grandson had opened something in him that he thought had closed long ago. Within
days, the memories began to intrude—not as recollections he could examine and set aside, but as
vivid replays that seemed to happen in his body before his mind could catch up. He would be sitting
at his desk, reviewing code, and suddenly he was back in the pediatric oncology ward, the smell of
antiseptic overwhelming, his daughter's small hand in his, the beeping of monitors that sounded like
a countdown. The memory didn't feel like something from twenty-five years ago. It felt like it was
happening now, happening to him again, and he couldn't quite explain to himself—or to anyone
else—why his heart was racing or why his hands were shaking.

When strong emotions came, they came all at once: a surge of fear, a crushing weight of guilt that
he had somehow failed to protect his child, a rage at the universe that made no sense and that he
could barely articulate. He had taken to avoiding anything that might trigger these states. He
stopped visiting his grandson. He made excuses. When his cardiologist scheduled him for an annual
physical, he cancelled and rescheduled three times before finally going, his entire body tense
during the examination, unable to fully explain to the doctor why he was so anxious—the words simply
wouldn't come in the right order.

The hospital smell was the worst. He had begun taking different routes to avoid driving past the
medical center. He crossed streets to avoid pharmacies. When his neighbor mentioned she was
pregnant, Thien had felt something crack inside him, a flash of old terror that babies and
pregnancies meant hospitals, meant illness, meant the possibility of losing everything. He had found
himself withdrawing from his neighbor, from his grandson's mother, from the life that was still
happening around him.

It was his grandson's oncology nurse who finally reached out to Thien's daughter, expressing concern
about his absence. That conversation, delivered gently but directly, had finally broken through his
isolation. At seventy-five, Thien had come to understand that the memory of Mai's illness had never
truly finished happening inside him. He was seeking help now because he was terrified that his own
fear was costing him the chance to be present for his grandson, and because the weight of carrying
this alone—the weight he had carried since 1997—had become unbearable.
