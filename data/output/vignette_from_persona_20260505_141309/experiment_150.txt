# Marcus

Marcus was twenty-one when his younger brother Dominik died. It happened on a Tuesday evening in
March, in the parking lot behind the shopping centre where Dominik worked stacking shelves. A
dispute over a debt escalated into violence. Marcus was not there. He was at home with his wife,
Petra, when the police called. The details emerged slowly—Dominik had been struck repeatedly, had
fallen, had struck his head on the concrete edge of a parking barrier. The specific image that would
later haunt Marcus was something he never witnessed but could not stop seeing: the back of Dominik's
skull, the particular curve of it that Marcus remembered from childhood, now fractured against grey
concrete.

In the weeks after the funeral, Marcus found himself unable to be alone. Not because he feared for
his safety in any rational sense, but because the moment silence settled around him—when Petra left
for her shifts at the hospital, when he sat in the bathroom, when he woke at three in the
morning—the smell would come. Not a smell exactly, but a memory of one. Dominik's cologne, the cheap
drugstore kind he'd worn since he was sixteen. Mixed with something else. Copper. Concrete dust. The
smell was so vivid that Marcus would gag, would stumble into the living room to turn on the
television, would text Petra in a panic even though she was working and couldn't answer. Being alone
meant being trapped with that sensory ghost, so he stopped being alone. He took to sitting in the
corner of the break room at the warehouse where he'd worked, even on his days off, just to be near
people. Eventually he stopped going to the warehouse altogether, and then there was no reason to
leave the house except to follow Petra from room to room, waiting in doorways while she showered,
sitting on the bathroom mat while she used the toilet, his presence becoming a weight she never
complained about but couldn't ignore.

When people moved suddenly—when Petra reached for something on a high shelf, when a neighbour
appeared around the corner of the street—Marcus's body would jolt as though he himself were under
threat. His hands would clench. His breath would come shallow. He was certain, in those moments,
that violence was about to unfold, that he was about to witness something terrible again. His body
had become an enemy, responding to phantom danger with the certainty of actual peril. He had come to
believe that he could not trust his own nervous system, that it was broken, that it would betray him
at any moment.

The flashbacks were not dreams. They were vivid photographic moments that would erupt without
warning—not memories he recalled so much as scenes he suddenly inhabited. He would be standing in
the kitchen and find himself seeing, with absolute clarity, the parking lot at dusk, the sodium
lights coming on, the particular angle of the barrier. These moments felt entirely real, present-
tense, as though he were there and it were happening now. He could not put any of it into words.
When Petra asked what was wrong, what he was feeling, he could only shake his head. The experience
existed outside language, a raw sensory fact that had no grammar.

Comments about family—even casual remarks from Petra about her own brother, or a television
programme mentioning siblings—would trigger a panic that felt like drowning. He began avoiding all
media, turning off the television when unexpected scenes appeared, refusing to read news articles or
watch films. But avoidance had its own cost. His unemployment stretched from weeks into months. He
had been a competent warehouse supervisor before March, someone who took pride in managing inventory
and training newer staff. Now he could not imagine returning to that role, could not imagine being
responsible for anything, could not trust himself to function in the world. Petra had begun to
mention, gently, that they needed his income. That comment alone—the implication that he was failing
her—sent him spiralling into a darkness so complete that one night he found himself in the bathroom
with a razor blade, pressing it against his forearm until the physical pain eclipsed the unbearable
pressure in his chest. The blood, strangely, was a relief. It was real. It was something he could
control.

It was Petra who finally insisted he seek help. She had found the cuts, had cried, had said she
couldn't watch him disappear. Marcus came to his first appointment carrying the weight of a man who
no longer recognized himself, terrified that no one could help him, but more terrified still of
losing his wife to the wreckage of his own survival.
