# Clinical Vignette: Mariana

Mariana was nineteen years old when she first came to the clinic on a Tuesday evening in October,
her supervisor at the Metropolitan Transit Authority having quietly suggested that she might benefit
from talking to someone. She worked as a train conductor, a job she had taken at seventeen with a
fierce determination to establish independence and distance from her mother's apartment in the
Sunset Park neighborhood of Brooklyn. The irony was not lost on her: she had chosen work in enclosed
metal boxes hurtling through dark tunnels, a choice that now felt like a punishment she had designed
for herself.

The traumatic event itself had occurred when Mariana was seven years old. She had been asleep in the
bedroom she shared with her younger brother when her father's voice rose from the living room—not
angry at first, but insistent, accusatory. Her mother's responses came quieter, placating. Mariana
had learned by then to lie still and listen, to parse the temperature of her father's tone the way
other children might read a thermometer. That night, something shifted. His voice became something
else entirely, a roar that seemed to crack the walls themselves. There were sounds she could not
name—furniture moving, her mother crying out, the wet crack of impact. Mariana had gotten out of
bed. She had opened the door to the hallway just enough to see her father's silhouette over her
mother's body on the floor, his arm raised, and in that moment she had frozen. She was small enough
to hide, young enough that her presence might have meant nothing, but she had done nothing. She had
watched. When her father finally stormed from the apartment, slamming the door hard enough to shake
the building, she had run to her mother and found her bleeding from her mouth, one eye already
swelling shut. Her mother had told her to go back to bed. Mariana had obeyed.

Now, twelve years later, Mariana lived in a state of perpetual vigilance that had calcified into a
kind of architecture of control. At night, she could not sleep without checking every lock in her
small studio apartment—the front door twice, the windows, then back to the door again. She was never
quite certain the first time was real. The darkness itself had become a threat. She slept with the
television on, or sometimes with her phone's flashlight pointing at the ceiling, anything to keep
the black from pressing in. When unexpected sounds punctured the silence—a neighbor's door slamming,
a siren in the distance—her heart would race so violently she thought it might break through her
ribs, and she would sit rigid, listening, waiting for the shape of danger to become clear.

Her work as a conductor had begun as an escape but had become a minefield. The tunnels between
stations were long and dark. The rumble of the train, the screech of metal on metal—these sounds
triggered a state of hyperalertness that left her exhausted by the end of each shift. She had
started staying late after her routes ended, reorganizing the conductor's cabin, checking
maintenance logs, anything to delay going home to the quiet and the dark. Her supervisor had noticed
her working sixteen-hour days and the weight she had lost. Mariana barely ate anymore. Food felt
unnecessary, a distraction from the work of staying safe. She had come to believe that if she kept
herself small enough, busy enough, vigilant enough, she could prevent what had happened to her
mother from happening to anyone she might ever care about. She could not articulate this belief
clearly, but it moved through her like a current, driving every choice.

The memories themselves had become her constant companion, though they did not feel like memories at
all. When they returned—triggered by darkness, by a raised voice overheard on the platform, by the
metallic taste that sometimes flooded her mouth without warning—they felt present, immediate,
happening now. She could taste the copper and salt from that night, could feel the paralysis in her
limbs as if it were still occurring. The sequence of events was scrambled in her mind; sometimes she
remembered her father's face before his voice, sometimes she remembered running to her mother before
she had gotten out of bed. The disorder troubled her as much as the vividness, as if her inability
to keep the story straight meant she had failed to truly witness what had happened, and therefore
had failed even more completely.

What had finally brought her to the clinic was a moment on the A train three weeks ago when a
passenger had dropped a metal toolbox, the crash echoing through the car. Mariana's heart had
seized. She had frozen at the controls, unable to move, unable to think. A passenger had to alert
the dispatcher. She had sat in the dark tunnel for fifteen minutes, sweating, certain that she was
going to die, or that she had already died and was trapped in some in-between place. When she
finally moved the train forward, her hands were shaking so badly she could barely grip the controls.
She had called in sick for the next four days. On the fifth day, her supervisor had handed her a
business card for the clinic and told her gently that she was one of the best conductors he had, but
that something needed to change. Mariana had taken the card, and now she sat in the waiting room,
terrified that talking about what happened would make it real in a new way, and equally terrified
that she could not go on like this—caught between the past that would not release her and a future
she could not imagine.
