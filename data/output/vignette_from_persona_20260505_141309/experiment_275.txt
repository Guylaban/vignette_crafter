# Thida's Vigil

Thida was fifty-seven when she came to the office on a Tuesday in October, her appointment card held
tightly in both hands. She had been married to Robert for thirty-one years—a good marriage, she
said, though she had not let him touch her without warning for nearly two decades. The thought of
his hand on her shoulder when she wasn't braced for it still sent her into a state of alarm that
left them both shaken. He had learned to announce himself before approaching, to give her time to
prepare. It was simply how they lived now.

The trauma had occurred in 1997, during a business trip to Bangkok. Thida had been a younger woman
then, a rising designer traveling with her firm to source textiles and furniture for a high-end
hotel project. She had been in the lobby of the hotel when the bomb went off—not the main blast, but
close enough. The sound was not sound; it was the world breaking. There was smoke, and the smell of
alcohol from the bar's shattered bottles mixed with something else, something burnt. People were
screaming. She had been trapped briefly under a fallen beam, not injured severely, but long enough
to believe she would die there. Long enough to feel the weight of the building on her chest and
smell the alcohol seeping into the dust around her. She was pulled out by hotel staff, taken to a
hospital, assessed, and released. Physically, she recovered.

But the recovery had been incomplete. Within weeks, she found herself unable to look at photographs
from before the trip. Old photos—from vacations, from family gatherings, from any time before that
day—became unbearable. They seemed to belong to a person who no longer existed. When her daughter
asked why she wouldn't display pictures in the living room anymore, Thida couldn't articulate it.
The images felt like evidence of a world that had been safe, and safe worlds were lies. She began to
keep them in boxes in the basement.

The smell of alcohol became a trigger she could not manage. Wine at dinner parties, the hand
sanitizer her colleagues used in the office, even the faint scent of someone's cocktail at a
restaurant—any of it could pull her into a state where she was back in that lobby, the beam pressing
down, the acrid smell choking her. She began to decline social engagements. Robert stopped inviting
friends over. The isolation seemed like the only reasonable response to a world that contained such
dangers.

Talking about the event itself was impossible. When her therapist at the time had asked her to
recount what happened, Thida had felt the walls of her chest tighten, her breathing shallow and
fast. The words themselves seemed dangerous, as if speaking them aloud would make the event happen
again, or worse, would make her vulnerable to something else. She had stopped therapy after three
sessions. For twenty-five years, she had not spoken about it to anyone except Robert, and even then,
only in fragments.

The hypervigilance had become her constant companion. She had to be watching, always. In her design
studio, she positioned her desk so she could see the door. In restaurants, she sat with her back to
the wall. When clients came in for consultations, she watched their hands, their faces, their
posture—assessing, calculating, determining whether they posed a threat. It was exhausting and
automatic, like breathing.

The physical strategies had accumulated over time. Exercise had become compulsive. She would wake at
five in the morning and run for an hour, push through a gym session, come home and do yoga until her
muscles burned. The physical exhaustion seemed to quiet something in her mind, at least temporarily.
But when exercise wasn't enough, when the feelings of dread and disconnection became too large, she
would hurt herself—pinching the soft skin of her inner arm until it bruised, or holding ice against
her wrist until it burned. The pain was clarifying. It gave her something real to feel instead of
the vast, formless terror that otherwise consumed her.

Food had become another refuge. In the evenings, when Robert was watching television and she was
alone with her thoughts, she would eat—not from hunger, but from a need to fill herself with
something, to make the emptiness less vast. A box of cookies, a container of ice cream, leftovers
eaten standing at the counter. The fullness was temporary, but it was something.

Six months ago, a client had asked Thida to talk about a project she'd completed before 1997. It was
a simple question, a conversational opening. But the request to discuss anything from that era, to
potentially mention the trip itself, had sent her into a panic so severe that she'd had to excuse
herself from the meeting. She'd spent the rest of the day in her home office, unable to work, unable
to do anything but sit with the knowledge that she couldn't even perform the basic functions of her
job anymore. Robert had found her there at six o'clock, still in her chair, and had gently suggested
that perhaps it was time to seek help again.

Thida arrived at the appointment because she was afraid of losing everything—her marriage, her
career, her sense of herself as someone capable. But she was also terrified of what remembering
might cost her.
