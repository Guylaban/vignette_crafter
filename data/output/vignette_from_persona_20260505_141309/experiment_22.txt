# Layla's Vigilance

Layla was fifty years old when she first walked into the therapist's office, and she had spent the
last twenty-three years trying to make herself small. It was not a conscious strategy so much as a
grammar that had become her native language. She wore layers—dark cardigans over long-sleeved
shirts, even in summer—and kept her headscarf fastened tight, not for faith but for invisibility.
The concealment had become so habitual that she no longer registered choosing it; she simply could
not imagine stepping outside without this armor of fabric between herself and the world.

The reason lived in her body still, though she had tried to leave it behind in the camps of Jordan.
In 1991, when Layla was twenty-seven, the war had come to her neighborhood in Baghdad with the sound
of helicopters and the smell of burning. She had been at work at the primary school where she taught
when the first missile struck three blocks away. The ground had moved. Children had screamed. She
had gathered them into the basement and sat in the dark for hours, listening to the building groan
above her, waiting to know if she and the thirty-two children in her care would die. They had
survived that day. Many others had not. By the time the bombing stopped and the borders opened,
Layla's family had fled everything—her home, her career, her marriage (her husband had stayed
behind, and they had never reconnected). She had arrived in the United States with a sister and two
young nephews, carrying documents that no one recognized and a trauma that had no language in
English.

Now, in her social work practice, Layla spent her days helping other displaced families navigate the
systems that had once bewildered her. She worked in a community center in a neighborhood where many
refugees had resettled, and she was good at her job—patient, knowledgeable, fluent in the particular
pain of losing everything. But the office itself had become a minefield. Fluorescent lights hummed
overhead. Clients came in distressed, their voices rising as they described loss and fear, and Layla
would feel her chest tighten, her breathing shallow. She had come to avoid scheduling appointments
that involved strong emotion—the ones where families learned their asylum claims had been denied, or
where trauma survivors broke down during intake. She would find reasons to transfer those cases to
colleagues, telling herself it was better for the client, when the truth was that their anguish
activated something in her that she could not control.

At night, Layla woke. Not from nightmares, though those came too, but from a hypervigilance so
ingrained it had become her default state. She would jolt awake at two or three in the morning, her
heart racing, and she would rise and move through her apartment checking locks, looking out windows,
scanning the street below for anything unusual. A car parked too long. A shadow that moved wrong.
She had installed extra locks on her bedroom door. She slept with her phone within arm's reach,
though she could not have explained to whom she would call or what emergency she was preparing for.
The threat felt imminent and shapeless, always.

By day, her body remained in a state of constant appraisal. In the grocery store, she tracked the
positions of other shoppers, noting exits, assessing which ones looked dangerous. At red lights, she
scanned adjacent cars. Walking to her office, she was acutely aware of every person on the sidewalk,
every sound that deviated from the expected pattern. Her mind had become a threat-detection system
running perpetually at full capacity. She was certain—had come to be certain—that danger was always
present, that her vigilance was the only thing standing between her and catastrophe. When her sister
had suggested she seemed anxious, Layla had bristled. This was not anxiety. This was attention. This
was survival.

What had finally brought her to seek help was a moment of recognition that frightened her more than
any external threat. During a staff meeting, a colleague had raised her voice in frustration about a
funding delay, and Layla had felt her body flood with adrenaline, her vision narrowing, her hands
trembling. She had excused herself and spent twenty minutes in the bathroom, unable to breathe
normally. Afterward, she realized she could not remember what her colleague had actually said—only
that loud emotion had triggered an alarm in her nervous system that she could not turn off. She was
afraid, finally, not of the world outside but of her own mind, of the way it had learned to see
danger everywhere, even in the safe spaces she had worked so hard to build.
