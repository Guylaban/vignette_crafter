# Captain Isabelle Novak

Isabelle Novak was forty-five years old and had been flying combat missions for the U.S. Air Force
for nearly two decades when, on a routine patrol over a volatile region in the Middle East, her F-16
was struck by a surface-to-air missile launched by a terrorist cell. The impact came without
warning—a sudden, violent shudder that seemed to split the aircraft in half. Alarms shrieked in the
cockpit. She had perhaps four seconds to assess the damage before ejecting, watching the desert
floor rush toward her as her parachute deployed. She landed hard, dislocated her shoulder, and lay
in the sand for seventeen minutes before a rescue team reached her, seventeen minutes during which
she could hear voices shouting in a language she didn't understand, could see shadows moving across
the dunes, and was certain—absolutely certain—that she was about to be captured or killed. The
rescue came. She survived. But something fundamental had fractured.

In the weeks after her recovery, Isabelle noticed changes she couldn't quite name. Crowded spaces
began to feel suffocating—the officers' club, the flight line during shift changes, even the grocery
store near her apartment in Raleigh. Her chest would tighten, her breathing would shallow, and she
would find herself scanning the room for exits, for threats, for anything that might be about to
happen. She started smoking again after a decade of abstinence, lighting cigarettes in her car or on
her apartment balcony at two in the morning, needing something to do with her hands, some small
ritual of control. Loud noises made her flinch—the backfire of a truck, a door slamming, the screech
of landing gear on the tarmac—and she would find her body rigid, her hand instinctively reaching for
a weapon that wasn't there.

Sleep became a battlefield. She would lie awake for hours, her mind cycling through the ejection
sequence, the silence of free fall, the certainty of capture. When she did sleep, she woke gasping,
drenched in sweat, convinced that someone was in her apartment. She began checking behind her
constantly—while driving, while walking to her mailbox, while sitting at her desk reviewing flight
logs. The habit had become so automatic that her colleagues noticed. A younger pilot had asked,
gently, if everything was okay. She hadn't known how to answer.

The base had a small gymnasium, windowless, with low ceilings—a space she had used for years without
discomfort. Now she couldn't bring herself to enter it. Enclosed spaces triggered a panic she
couldn't rationalize away. She had begun avoiding them: the elevator in the command building, the
cargo hold of transport planes, even the cockpit itself had started to feel like a trap. This
avoidance was becoming a problem. Last month, she had turned down a training assignment that
required three days of simulator work in a confined pod. Her commanding officer hadn't said
anything, but she saw the concern in his eyes. She was a decorated pilot being sidelined by her own
body.

What frightened Isabelle most was the sense that she had lost something irreplaceable: the feeling
that she could handle anything. She had always been someone who could manage crisis, who could
compartmentalize fear and act. Now, a minor administrative frustration or a critical email from her
supervisor would send her into a spiral of catastrophic thinking. She would tell herself that she
couldn't cope, that even small upsets were too much, that she was coming apart. She had come to
believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous—that the missile had been proof of this, that
nowhere was truly safe, that bad things happened to good people and there was nothing she could do
to stop them. When her therapist suggested this was not fact but interpretation, Isabelle had
reminded herself that it could have been worse—she could have been captured, tortured, killed—as if
gratitude for survival was a form of inoculation against despair.

She had finally requested a mental health referral three weeks ago, after waking one morning to find
herself checking the locks on her apartment for the third time in an hour, her hands shaking, her
mind absolutely convinced that someone was coming. She was terrified that a diagnosis would ground
her permanently, that she would lose her career. But she was also terrified of what would happen if
she didn't get help—of becoming someone she didn't recognize, of losing not just her job but herself
entirely.
