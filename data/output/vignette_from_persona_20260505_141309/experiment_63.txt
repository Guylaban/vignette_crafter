# Clinical Vignette: Margaret

Margaret was thirty-four when her brother Thomas died. It was a Tuesday in March, late afternoon,
and she was working the lunch shift at O'Malley's on Fifth Avenue when her mother called. Thomas had
been hit by a car while crossing the street two blocks from his apartment. He was pronounced dead at
the scene. Margaret remembers the way the phone felt in her hand—the weight of it, the exact shade
of the bar's brass rail blurring as her manager guided her to the back office. What she cannot see
clearly is the moment between hanging up and sitting down, or how she got home that night, though
she knows she must have driven herself because her car was in her driveway the next morning.

The investigation revealed that Thomas had stepped into traffic without looking. He was thirty-one,
a careful person by nature, and the detective's preliminary assessment suggested he might have been
distracted—phone in hand, earbuds in. Margaret had texted him that morning. A casual message about
their mother's birthday dinner coming up. She had come to believe that if she had not sent that
text, if she had not created even that small moment of connection that might have drawn his
attention inward rather than outward, he would have seen the car. The thought had calcified into
certainty over the eighteen months since his death. She had acted. The consequence followed. This
was the architecture of what happened.

In the weeks after the funeral, Margaret returned to work at O'Malley's. The bar was her
territory—she had worked there for seven years, knew the regulars by drink order and life story,
moved through the evening service with the kind of muscle memory that required no thought. But
something had fractured. She found herself unable to look at the news on the television above the
register. A story about a pedestrian accident in another city would appear, and she would turn away,
feeling her chest tighten, her jaw clench. She stopped reading news on her phone entirely. When her
boyfriend Marcus tried to talk to her about current events, she would leave the room. The world felt
full of warnings she could not afford to hear—reminders that people died in ways that made no sense,
that a single moment of inattention, a single text message, could unravel everything.

What frightened her most was the idea that people were not what they seemed, that the safety she
thought she had constructed was an illusion. Marcus loved her, she knew this intellectually, but she
found herself unable to fully trust his reassurances. When he told her that what happened to Thomas
was not her fault, the words seemed to bounce off something solid inside her. He could not possibly
understand. No one could. She had begun to believe that relying on other people was a kind of
naiveté, a dangerous softness. She controlled her emotions at work with increasing
rigidity—maintaining the bright, attentive persona of a good bartender while feeling nothing
underneath, a performance so seamless that her regulars commented on how steady she was, how
unflappable. They did not know that she was holding her breath, that the clarity of certain moments
from that day—her mother's voice, the texture of the phone cord, the exact shade of the afternoon
light through the office window—had become so vivid and intrusive that she sometimes lost track of
where she was.

Three months ago, Marcus had asked her to see someone, a therapist. She refused. The thought of
speaking to a professional, of having her internal landscape examined and catalogued, felt like
another kind of vulnerability she could not permit herself. But last week, after she had snapped at
a regular customer and then spent twenty minutes in the walk-in freezer trying to regulate her
breathing, Margaret called her mother. She admitted that she was struggling, that the weight of what
she believed about herself—about her culpability, about the fundamental untrustworthiness of
safety—had become unbearable. It was her mother who found the therapist's number and placed it on
Margaret's kitchen counter. Margaret is thirty-eight now. She is here because she is exhausted,
because she is afraid that if she continues as she is, she will lose Marcus and her job and whatever
remains of her life. But she is also terrified of what might happen if she stops controlling
everything, if she lets herself believe that what happened was not her doing.
