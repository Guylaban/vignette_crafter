# Clinical Vignette: Robert Chen

Robert Chen had been making pottery for thirty-seven years. His studio sat on two acres outside
Portland, a converted barn where clay dust hung perpetually in the light and the wheels turned with
the rhythm of his hands. He had built a modest reputation—galleries in Seattle and San Francisco
carried his work, and he taught weekend workshops to a steady stream of amateur potters drawn by his
reputation for patient instruction and his distinctive glazes, deep blues and greens that seemed to
hold light. His wife Margaret had managed the business side of things for two decades, handling
orders, shipping, the website. They had been married for thirty-four years. They had no children.

On a Tuesday in March, Margaret went to the grocery store and did not come home. She was found in
the parking lot two hours later, slumped in the driver's seat of their car. The paramedics said it
was sudden cardiac arrest—massive, catastrophic, with no warning. She was sixty years old. Robert
was in the studio when the police came. He had been centering clay, his hands wet, his mind in that
meditative place where he lived most of his days. He remembered the officer's mouth moving,
remembered looking down at his hands, at the clay spinning slowly to a stop on the wheel.

The first weeks after Margaret's death passed in a blur of funeral arrangements and people in the
house. Robert moved through these things as though watching himself from outside his body. The grief
was immense but it felt distant, muffled. What he noticed more acutely was a strange sense of danger
that had entered his life. The studio, which had always been his sanctuary, began to feel unsafe. He
went there one morning about ten days after Margaret died and stood looking at the wheels, the clay
bins, the glazes she had helped him organize. The light came through the north windows exactly as it
always had. But Robert found himself thinking: something terrible can happen at any moment. You can
be making something beautiful and the world can collapse. He did not throw that day. He locked the
studio and went back to the house.

Over the following weeks, Robert found himself avoiding anything that might remind him of Margaret.
He could not bring himself to look at the kitchen, where she had made her morning coffee while
reading the news on her tablet. He could not open the file on his computer where they kept their
shared calendar. He stopped answering emails from the galleries, stopped responding to people who
wanted to sign up for workshops. When he thought about the future—about next season's orders, about
the kiln firing scheduled for May, about anything that stretched beyond the immediate day—he felt a
rising panic that he could not name. Planning felt like tempting fate. If he made plans, something
would happen to destroy them. The only safe thing was to remain still, to avoid the future entirely.

His sister called repeatedly. His doctor sent a letter. Margaret's sister came by the house and
found him in the kitchen, and Robert could see the shock in her face at his appearance—he had lost
weight, his hands trembled slightly. He told her he was fine, just grieving, and she did not believe
him. By July, four months after Margaret's death, the pottery workshops had been quietly cancelled.
The galleries had stopped calling. The studio sat locked, and Robert had begun to push away even the
small moments when grief seemed to lift slightly—a sunny afternoon, a friend's kind voice on the
phone—because those moments felt dangerous, like they were setting him up for another loss. He had
come to believe that safety meant feeling nothing at all, that the future was a threat, and that
anything that reminded him of the life he and Margaret had built together was something to be locked
away and forgotten. It was his sister's ultimatum, delivered in person on a gray afternoon in early
August, that finally brought him to a therapist's office. She had said: "Robert, you're
disappearing. You need help." He sat in the waiting room, terrified of what talking about this might
unleash, but more terrified still of the hollow life he was building in the ruins of the one he'd
lost.
