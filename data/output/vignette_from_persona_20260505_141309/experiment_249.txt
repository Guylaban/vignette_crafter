# Clinical Vignette: Thida

Thida was fifty-two when the monsoon came. She had lived in her neighborhood in New Orleans for
eighteen years, had weathered storms before—Hurricane Katrina when she was younger and far away,
smaller hurricanes that passed without catastrophe. She was not afraid of water the way some people
were. But on the afternoon the levee failed, she was at home alone, and the water rose faster than
the news reports had promised, faster than her mind could process as real.

She remembered the sound first: not wind, but a roar, something alive and purposeful. The water came
through the windows and under the doors simultaneously, cold and filthy and rising. She had run to
the attic, pulled herself up through the access panel, and sat in the dark for six hours before the
rescue boat found her. The water had reached her neck before the rescuers arrived. She could still
see the waterline on her bedroom wall, could still feel the texture of the contaminated water
against her skin when she closed her eyes without warning.

In the months afterward, Thida had tried to return to normal. She went back to her job at the
McDonald's on Magazine Street, where she had worked the morning shift for twelve years. But the
restaurant's ventilation system hummed and clicked in patterns that made her body tense. Unexpected
sounds from the kitchen—a metal tray dropped, the ice machine cycling on—sent her heart racing. She
found herself bracing for something, though she could not name what. By six in the evening, as the
sun began to lower, a heaviness settled into her chest that did not lift until morning. She had come
to believe that night was when bad things happened, when she could not protect herself, when the
world became unreliable. She would lie awake beside her husband Marcus, listening to the house
settle, cataloging every creak and groan, certain that another disaster was imminent, that her
vigilance was the only thing standing between her family and catastrophe.

The memories themselves had become fragmented and intrusive. Thida would be wiping down the counter
and suddenly feel the cold water on her skin, or she would smell something faintly metallic and her
mind would flood with disconnected images: her daughter's photograph floating face-down, the
darkness of the attic, a rescuer's gloved hand reaching toward her. These images did not come in
sequence or with narrative coherence. They arrived as sensory shards, overwhelming her with an
emotion so acute that she would have to excuse herself to the bathroom, where she would sit on the
toilet and wait for the feeling to subside enough that she could return to work.

Three months into her return to the restaurant, Thida's manager had asked her to cover an evening
shift. She had declined, then declined again, then stopped answering when he texted. She had not
worked an evening shift in eight months. The thought of being in the building as darkness fell, of
being responsible for closing procedures while her vigilance escalated, felt impossible. When her
manager finally stopped calling, Thida felt relief and shame in equal measure. She was not the
person she had been. She was certain now that she would never recover that version of herself—the
woman who could laugh easily, who did not flinch at sudden noises, who felt safe in her own home
after dark. That woman had drowned in the floodwater.

What frightened her most was not the memories themselves, but the belief that had crystallized in
the aftermath: that she had no power to prevent harm. The water had come regardless of her caution.
The levee had failed despite everyone's assumptions about safety. Bad things happened, and her
vigilance, her fear, her sleepless nights—none of it could stop them. She did not belong in a world
where such randomness existed. She belonged nowhere.

It was Marcus who finally insisted she see someone. He had noticed her sitting on the back porch at
two in the morning for the third consecutive night, and he had said, quietly, that he could not
watch her disappear. Thida had made an appointment with a therapist, not because she believed
anything could change, but because her husband's face had looked so afraid, and she could not bear
to be the cause of that fear on top of everything else.
