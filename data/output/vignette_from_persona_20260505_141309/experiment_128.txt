# Zahra's Vigil

Zahra was eighty years old when she came to the clinic, her silver hair pinned back with the same
careful precision she had maintained for sixty years, her hands folded in her lap with the composure
of someone who had spent a lifetime holding other people's crises at arm's length. She had been a
social worker for nearly five decades, the kind of woman who sat across from families in their worst
moments and helped them find the next step. Now, at eighty, she could barely leave her apartment.

Thirty-two years ago, her son Reza had fallen ill. He was seven years old, bright-eyed and full of
the kind of restless energy that drove her to distraction in the best way. One autumn morning he
complained of a sore throat. By evening, his fever had climbed to 104. By the next morning, he was
struggling to breathe. The hospital admitted him within hours, and what should have been a simple
case of epiglottitis became a three-week ordeal that nearly took his life. Zahra remembered the
moment the pediatrician pulled her aside in that fluorescent hallway, the smell of antiseptic and
something else—something organic and wrong—filling her nostrils as he explained that Reza might need
to be intubated. She remembered the needle going into his small arm for the IV line, the way his
eyes found hers as if asking her to make it stop. She had held his hand and promised him it would be
all right. She had lied.

Reza survived. He recovered fully, went on to university, to a career, to a family of his own. But
something in Zahra had not recovered from those three weeks. For years she had managed it well
enough. She continued her work, helped her clients navigate their own traumas, raised her other
children, built a life with her late husband Kaveh. But as she aged, as Kaveh died five years ago,
as the world seemed to grow smaller and more fragile around her, the weight of what she had lived
through began to surface in ways she could not control.

It started with conversations. When a colleague mentioned her sister's cancer diagnosis, Zahra felt
the ground tilt beneath her. When her granddaughter spoke casually about a friend whose mother had
passed away, Zahra found herself unable to breathe normally for the rest of the afternoon. She had
come to understand that hearing about anyone's loss—anyone's child sick, anyone's loved one
dying—was a trigger she could not manage. The talk of loss seemed to pull at something fundamental
in her, a thread that unraveled into panic and grief so overwhelming it surprised her with its
ferocity.

She began to avoid going out. First it was just the social events she had always attended—the weekly
gatherings with her women's group, the neighborhood walks she had loved. Then it was the grocery
store, because you never knew who you might run into or what they might tell you. Her daughter-in-
law had gently suggested a doctor's appointment for her blood pressure, and Zahra had found herself
unable to go. The thought of sitting in a waiting room, of a nurse approaching with a blood pressure
cuff, of that clinical smell—the same smell that had haunted her in the hospital all those years
ago—made her chest tight and her mind blank with refusal.

The memory itself had changed over the decades. Sometimes she would be sitting quietly, doing
nothing in particular, and suddenly she would feel the full weight of her terror and helplessness,
though the images of that hospital room would not come. The emotion would arrive unannounced and
complete, as if her body remembered what her mind had tried to forget. Other times, unbidden, the
memory would force itself into her consciousness—Reza's pale face, the sound of the monitors, the
terrible helplessness of watching your child suffer and being unable to stop it. When it came, it
brought grief so profound that she would sit in her bedroom for hours afterward, unable to do
anything but sit.

What frightened her most was the conviction that had settled into her bones: she had failed him. She
had promised to protect him and she could not. She had acted against everything she believed in—she
had abandoned her composure, her professional distance, her faith in her own competence—and in doing
so, she had become someone she no longer recognized. The worst part was knowing that she could not
have stopped it. She was eighty years old and she still could not accept that some things simply
happen, that no amount of vigilance or love or sacrifice can prevent suffering. She had spent her
life helping others come to terms with exactly this truth, and she could not apply it to herself.

Her daughter had finally insisted she see someone. Zahra sat in the clinic waiting room, avoiding
the antiseptic-scented hand sanitizer by the door, and wondered if at eighty, after three decades of
carrying this, there was any point in trying to change.
