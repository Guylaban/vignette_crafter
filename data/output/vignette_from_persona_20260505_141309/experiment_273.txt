# Karim's Vigil

Karim had been a firefighter for twelve years, and in that time he had learned to move through
crisis with the steadiness of someone trained to trust his own reflexes. At thirty-seven, married to
Yasmin for nine years, he had built a life that felt solid—the kind of life his parents, who had
emigrated from Lebanon before he was born, had worked hard to make possible for him. But the man who
showed up to the firehouse now was not the same one who had left it on a Tuesday morning in early
March.

The stalking had begun without announcement. Karim first noticed the bicycle in early February—a man
he didn't recognize, riding slowly past his house on Maple Street at odd hours. The man never
approached, never spoke. He simply appeared: at the corner when Karim left for work, parked outside
the station one afternoon, waiting on the sidewalk when Karim and Yasmin returned from the market.
When Karim tried to speak to him, the man would cycle away quickly, only to reappear the next day.
Karim had called the police, filed reports, changed his route home. Nothing stopped it. The man's
face—lean, expressionless, eyes that seemed to catalogue Karim's movements—had burned itself into
Karim's mind with the intensity of a threat he could see but not fully understand or control.

Then, on that Tuesday in March, the man had been waiting outside the station at dawn. When Karim
stepped outside to begin his shift, the man stood directly in his path, close enough that Karim
could see the detail of his worn jacket. "I know where you live," the man had said quietly. "I know
where your wife goes." Karim had moved toward him, but the man had melted backward into the pre-dawn
darkness, and by the time Karim reached the street, he was gone. The police arrested him three days
later after Yasmin spotted him outside their bedroom window. The man was charged; he was held. But
something in Karim had not been arrested with him.

In the weeks that followed, Karim's body became a instrument of vigilance. He could not enter a room
without cataloguing its exits—the door to the kitchen, the window in the break room, the side
entrance at the station. His colleagues had begun to notice. During calls, he was sharp, reliable,
but back at the station, something had shifted. He would sit in the common area with his back to the
wall, watching the faces of the firefighters around him, searching for the subtle signs of threat he
had learned to read in the stalker's blank expression. When a truck rumbled past the station or a
siren wailed in the distance, his entire body would tense. Loud, unexpected sounds made his heart
race in a way that embarrassed him—a grown man, trained for emergencies, jumping at noise.

What frightened Karim most was the conviction that had settled into his chest: that he had become
weak. A weak man would have been followed. A weak man would be afraid now. He had come to believe
that the stalking had exposed something true about him, something permanent. He was not the steady,
reliable person he had always thought he was. The evidence was in how he scanned rooms, in how he
could not sleep without checking the locks twice, in how the sight of a bicycle—any bicycle, ridden
by anyone—could send him into a state of panic so overwhelming that he had to step away from
whatever he was doing and breathe until the world stopped spinning.

Yasmin had suggested, gently, that he talk to someone. She had noticed that he was withdrawing, that
he had stopped attending the community dinners they used to enjoy, that he flinched when she touched
him unexpectedly. He had resisted at first. But three weeks ago, when he found himself unable to
describe to his captain why he had reacted so violently to a fellow firefighter's joke about being
followed, when the memory had crashed over him without warning and left him standing in the
apparatus bay unable to speak, unable to articulate what was happening inside him, he had finally
called. Now, sitting in a therapist's office, Karim was trying to find the words for what it meant
to have been changed by someone else's gaze, and to believe that the change was permanent.
