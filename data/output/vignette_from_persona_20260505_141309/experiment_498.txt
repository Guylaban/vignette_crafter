# Vinh's Story

Vinh Nguyen was forty years old when he finally made the appointment, though he had been carrying
the weight of what happened for nearly a decade. He was a journalist then, in 2015, working for an
independent news outlet in Southeast Asia, investigating corruption in local government. He had
built a reputation for meticulous reporting and fearless questions—the kind of reporter editors
trusted with sensitive stories. That reputation had made him a target.

The abduction happened on a Tuesday evening. He was leaving his office when three men in plain
clothes forced him into a van. What followed occupied a space in his mind that he could not fully
access. He remembered the smell of diesel fuel and the sound of a door slamming, remembered being in
a concrete room with a single window high on the wall. But the hours in between—the specific words
spoken, the exact sequence of what was done to him—remained fragmented, like a film with missing
frames. When he tried to reconstruct it deliberately, the details scattered. What he could not
forget, paradoxically, was the feeling: the absolute certainty that he would not leave that room
alive, that his body was no longer his own, that the person he had been before walking out of his
office no longer existed.

He was released after seventy-two hours. His captors never explained why. He was never formally
charged with anything. He returned to his apartment in Hanoi, filed a police report that went
nowhere, and tried to resume his work. But something had fractured inside him that did not repair.

Within weeks, Vinh noticed that ordinary sounds made him flinch violently—a car horn, a door
slamming, even the neighbor's television through the wall. His body responded to these sounds as
though they were threats, flooding him with adrenaline before his mind could catch up. He began
eating less, not consciously at first but as a kind of drift. Food tasted like nothing. His mouth
felt dry. Eating felt like an act that belonged to a person with a future, and he was no longer
certain he had one. Over months, he lost fifteen pounds.

He stopped answering calls from colleagues. A former friend from university, someone named Minh, had
texted him to meet for coffee; Vinh deleted the message without responding. The thought of sitting
across from someone who knew him before—who could see in his face what had been done to him—was
unbearable. He had come to believe that his torture was written on his skin, visible to anyone who
looked closely enough. More than that, he was certain there was something fundamentally broken in
him now, something that made him unworthy of friendship or ordinary human connection.

The memory of his captivity haunted him precisely because he could not fully remember it. He would
lie awake at night trying to reconstruct the sequence, trying to understand what he might have done
differently, how he might have prevented this. If he had left the office five minutes earlier. If he
had taken a different route. If he had been more careful, more aware, more vigilant. The rumination
was exhausting and futile, but he could not stop. The gaps in his memory felt obscene—as though his
mind was protecting him from something so terrible that consciousness itself rejected it.

Vinh eventually left journalism. He moved to the United States on a visa, telling himself it was a
fresh start. He found work as a freelance translator, a job he could do from home, alone. He lived
in Portland now, in a small apartment, and had been there for five years. He watched people closely
when he had to go out—the grocery store, the pharmacy—scanning faces for signs of danger, trying to
anticipate threats before they materialized. He had never told anyone in America what happened. The
secret sat in his chest like a stone.

What brought him finally to seek help was a child crying in the apartment above his. The sound—high-
pitched, sustained, utterly ordinary—had sent him into a panic so severe that he could not breathe.
He sat on his kitchen floor for two hours, trembling, unable to move. The next morning, he searched
for a trauma therapist and made an appointment. He was afraid of what remembering might cost him. He
was also afraid of what forgetting had already cost.
