# Thea

Thea was thirty-five when she came to her first appointment, her hands steady as she filled out the
intake forms but her jaw clenched tight enough that the therapist noticed. She was a physical
therapist herself—worked at a clinic in Portland, Oregon, specializing in post-operative
rehabilitation—and she moved through the waiting room with the composed efficiency of someone
trained to manage pain in others. What she did not say in the waiting room, but would say within the
hour, was that she could no longer touch her patients without her hands trembling.

The event had happened four years ago. Thea had been married to Marcus for six years at that point,
and the relationship had been deteriorating in ways she had not fully admitted to herself until the
night it became impossible to deny. They had been arguing about money—about her working too much,
about his unemployment, about the shape their life had taken—and the argument had moved from the
kitchen to the bedroom. Marcus had grabbed her wrist, hard enough that she felt the bone shift. When
she tried to pull away, he had forced her onto the bed, and what happened next existed in her memory
not as a sequence of moments but as a kind of eternal present tense, a sensation that would not
resolve into past.

She remembered the specific temperature of his hands on her skin—cold, deliberately cold because he
had held them under the bathroom faucet first, and she understood later that this was intentional,
that the cold was part of the control. She remembered being unable to move her legs because he was
sitting on them. She remembered saying no, and him saying that she had made him do this, that she
had pushed him to it, and believing, in the moment and for a long time after, that this might be
true.

They had stayed married for another year. Thea had not told anyone what happened. She had gone to
work, treated patients, moved through her days as though the person she had been before—the woman
who could touch another human being without fear—still existed. But by the time she finally left
Marcus, that woman was already gone.

Now, four years later, Thea found herself unable to work. The problem was not dramatic or obvious.
She could still perform the technical aspects of her job. But when a patient came in for a shoulder
assessment, and she moved her hands toward their neck to palpate the trapezius, her body would flood
with something that felt like the memory was happening again—not as something she was remembering,
but as something that was occurring in real time. The temperature of her own hands would suddenly
feel wrong, or she would become aware of her patient's trust in her, their vulnerability, and she
would become convinced that something was fundamentally broken in her, that she was dangerous, that
her presence in the room was a threat even though she knew rationally this made no sense.

She had started finding reasons to delegate the hands-on work. She told her supervisor she was
developing carpal tunnel. She suggested patients see her colleagues instead. Within six months, her
caseload had shrunk by half. Her supervisor had pulled her aside and asked if everything was all
right. Thea had not known how to answer.

The intrusive memories had worsened over the past year. She would be lying in bed next to her
current husband, David—a kind man, careful with her, whom she had married two years ago—and suddenly
she would be back in that bedroom, and the feeling of being trapped, of having no choice, would be
so overwhelming that she would have to get up and leave. David had stopped trying to touch her
without explicit permission. He had stopped trying to understand why.

What frightened Thea most was the thought that Marcus had been right, that something in the way she
was had invited what happened, that the defect was hers and not circumstantial. She spent hours
thinking about what she might have said differently that night, what she might have done. She would
replay the argument in her mind, searching for the moment where she could have prevented it all, and
the searching never ended because there was no moment where prevention became possible once the
violence had already begun. Yet she searched anyway, convinced that if she could only find the right
version of that night, she could undo not just the event but the person she had become—the woman who
flinches at temperature changes, who cannot allow herself to be touched, who no longer recognizes
herself in the mirror and believes she never will again.

It was David who had finally insisted she see someone. He had said it gently, without blame, but
with a kind of quiet firmness that she recognized as love. Thea had agreed, though she was not sure
what she hoped would happen. She was not sure she deserved to feel better.
