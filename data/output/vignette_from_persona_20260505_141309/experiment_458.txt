# Clinical Vignette: Amara

Amara was twenty-seven when the police stopped her car on a stretch of road outside Columbus that
she had driven a hundred times. She was on her way home from her mother's house, tired from a long
afternoon of cooking and family talk, when the lights appeared in her mirror. The officer who
approached her window smelled heavily of alcohol—a sharp, stale reek that filled the car when he
leaned down to speak. His movements were unpredictable, his speech slurred, and within minutes he
had pulled her from the vehicle, restrained her, and arrested her on a charge that would later be
dropped entirely. The arrest report would note that he had confused her vehicle with another. For
three hours she sat in a holding cell. For three years afterward, she would carry the weight of
those hours in ways she could not fully name.

By the time Amara came to seek help at thirty, the architecture of her daily life had contracted
around an effort to avoid the feelings that threatened to overtake her. She had come to believe that
there was something fundamentally broken in her—that a person who had been treated that way must
have done something to invite it, must have looked wrong or spoken wrong or existed in a way that
marked her as someone the world could hurt. This belief had hardened into a kind of certainty. She
isolated herself from friends who had once been close, declining invitations and, when pressed,
offering vague excuses. The truth was that being around people felt dangerous. Their sudden
gestures—a hand reaching across a table, someone standing up quickly from a chair—sent her nervous
system into high alert. She found herself scanning faces for signs of aggression, tracking the
movements of hands, positioning herself near exits in rooms she had sat in peacefully for years.

Her work as a school counselor had become an exercise in compartmentalization and exhaustion. Amara
had always been skilled at creating safety for adolescents in crisis, but now she found herself
avoiding the very situations that defined her role. When a student came to her office in emotional
distress, she would feel the panic rise in her chest—not because of the student, but because the
intensity of their feeling threatened to crack open something in her that she was terrified would
consume her if she let it. She began referring students to colleagues. She stopped attending staff
meetings where she might be asked to share observations or engage in the kind of emotional openness
that had once come naturally. The principal had noticed. Her evaluations, once glowing, now carried
language about "decreased engagement" and "concerning withdrawal from collaborative work."

Sleep had become impossible. Even in her own home, with her husband asleep beside her, Amara could
not relax. Her body remained on alert through the night, her mind cataloging sounds—a car passing,
the house settling, wind at the window. The smell of alcohol, which her husband occasionally drank
with dinner, would send her back to that roadside in an instant, her heart racing, her hands
shaking. She had asked him to stop drinking altogether. He had tried, but the restriction created
tension between them. She could see the hurt in his eyes when she flinched away from him, and this
only deepened her conviction that she was broken, that her damage was now damaging him.

What finally brought Amara to call a therapist was a moment of clarity that arrived during an
ordinary afternoon. She was so exhausted—bone-deep, soul-deep exhausted—that she realized she was no
longer living her life but merely surviving it, and not even doing that well. She was failing at her
job, retreating from her marriage, and increasingly convinced that the isolation she had chosen was
the only honest response to what she had learned about herself and the world. The thought that
frightened her most was not that she would have another encounter with police or authority, but that
she would spend the rest of her life like this: hypervigilant, alone, and utterly convinced that the
fault lay not with what had been done to her, but with who she was.
