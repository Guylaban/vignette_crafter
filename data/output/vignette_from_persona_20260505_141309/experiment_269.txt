# Vinh's Vigil

Vinh Tran was eighty-two years old when he first came to the clinic, though the lines around his
eyes and the careful way he held himself made him seem older. He had been a data scientist for the
past thirty years, work that suited his temperament—clean problems with logical solutions, minimal
human unpredictability. But in 1992, before the spreadsheets and the algorithms, he had spent
fourteen months as a relief worker in Cambodia, coordinating medical supplies and food distribution
in a region still fractured by the war's aftermath.

He did not speak of those months easily. What he knew was that something fundamental had shifted in
him during those weeks in late 1992, when he witnessed a village chief—a man he had come to trust,
who had helped Vinh navigate the distribution networks—take a bribe meant for a widow's family and
disappear into the countryside. The betrayal was not the worst thing Vinh saw there, but it was the
moment something inside him began to calcify. People could not be trusted. Authority could turn
predatory. The world revealed itself as fundamentally unsafe, and there was nowhere to hide from it.

By the time Vinh returned to the United States, he had already begun the habits that would define
the next three decades. He stopped watching news broadcasts after the first year—the reports of
political corruption, of violence in unstable regions, of people exploiting the vulnerable sent him
into a state of acute panic that felt indistinguishable from being back there. He would lie in bed
for hours afterward, his heart racing, his hands trembling. It was easier simply not to turn on the
television.

He married a woman named Lin in 1995, and for many years the marriage provided a kind of ballast.
But Vinh had become someone who rehearsed conversations obsessively before they happened. He would
sit at the kitchen table, running through what he might say to a colleague, a neighbor, a stranger
at the grocery store, imagining the ways the interaction could go wrong, the ways someone might
deceive him or take advantage. Lin learned not to interrupt these silent rehearsals. She learned to
be patient with the way his eyes would scan a room when they went out to dinner—the exits, the other
patrons, the staff, the windows. She learned that his anger, when it came, was sudden and
disproportionate, a flash of rage at something small that seemed to contain all the rage he had
never fully processed. He would apologize immediately, horrified at himself, convinced that he was
losing his mind, that his reactions were proof he was becoming unhinged.

After Lin died in 2016, the structure of his life loosened. He continued his work remotely, which
meant fewer interactions to rehearse, fewer reasons to leave his apartment. But the nights became
worse. His dreams were visceral replays of moments he had tried for thirty years to
compartmentalize—the chief's face, the widow's daughter crying, the sound of gunfire in a distant
village. He would wake gasping, his body drenched in sweat, and for several minutes he could not
orient himself to the present. The memory did not feel like something that had happened long ago; it
felt like it was happening now, in this moment, in his bedroom in Seattle. His body remained on high
alert for hours after waking, muscles tight, his breathing shallow, waiting for the next threat.

The constant scanning of his environment had become so automatic that he no longer noticed he was
doing it. But his colleagues had noticed. In video meetings, his eyes would dart away from the
screen. He had begun to isolate further, citing technical difficulties, until finally his supervisor
suggested he consider retirement. He was eighty, after all. He had earned it.

What finally brought Vinh to the clinic was a conversation with his daughter, who had found him one
morning after a particularly severe nightmare, sitting on his bed with his hands shaking, convinced
he was losing his ability to distinguish reality from memory. She had said, gently, "Dad, you need
help. Real help. Not just waiting for it to pass." He had agreed, though what frightened him most
was the possibility that professional help would confirm what he already believed—that the damage
was permanent, that the person he had been before Cambodia was gone, and all that remained was this
vigilant, angry, fragile man who could no longer trust his own mind.
