# Rajesh

Rajesh was forty-two when his father died, though he still thinks of himself as thirty-nine—the age
he was before everything changed. His father, Vikram, had complained of chest tightness one Thursday
morning in Mumbai while Rajesh was in his studio in Portland, Oregon, reviewing fabric swatches for
a high-end residential project. By the time Rajesh's mother called with the news, Vikram had already
collapsed in the kitchen. He was gone before the ambulance arrived. The unexpectedness of it—the
fact that his father had seemed fine just weeks earlier when they'd video-called, that there had
been no warning, no chance to say anything—had carved something out of Rajesh that he still could
not name.

The first year after his father's death, Rajesh had thrown himself into work with an intensity that
impressed his colleagues and exhausted his wife, Priya. He redesigned three commercial spaces and
two private homes, each project demanding his full attention in a way that felt necessary,
protective. But as the anniversary of his father's death approached—November 14th—something shifted.
Rajesh found himself unable to concentrate on a client meeting. His hands had begun to shake
slightly as the date drew closer, and he'd started mentally rehearsing what would happen if Priya
were to die next, if his mother's health would fail, if he would be utterly alone. These rehearsals
felt important, urgent, as though by imagining the worst he might somehow prepare himself to survive
it. He would lie awake at three in the morning constructing elaborate scenarios: a car accident on
the drive to work, a sudden illness, a fall down the stairs. Each imagined catastrophe felt like a
kind of armor, a way to inoculate himself against further loss.

By the second year, Rajesh had begun to avoid November entirely. He took vacation days when he
could, kept his calendar deliberately light, and found reasons not to be home if Priya suggested
plans. When his colleague Deepak mentioned that his mother had passed away and invited the team to
the memorial service, Rajesh felt his throat tighten and his chest grow hot. He made an excuse about
a client deadline and did not attend. The thought of being in a room where grief was being openly
acknowledged, where people spoke about loss and remembered the dead, felt unbearable. He could not
explain this to Priya without acknowledging that he was still undone by what had happened, and
admitting that felt like weakness.

The avoidance had consequences he had not anticipated. Clients began to notice that Rajesh was
unavailable in November and December. One important project had been reassigned to a junior designer
because Rajesh could not commit to a timeline that spanned the anniversary. His relationship with
Priya had grown strained; she had wanted to mark the anniversary together, to light a candle or call
his mother, but Rajesh had insisted on working late those evenings. When she persisted, he had
snapped at her with a harshness that shocked them both—his voice rising, his words cutting in ways
he did not recognize in himself.

The flashbacks had begun without warning. He might be sitting in a client's living room, discussing
paint colors, when suddenly he would feel the phone call happening again—the tremor in his mother's
voice, the moment when he understood that his father was not coming back. His body would react
first: his heart would race, his palms would sweat, and only then would his mind catch up to the
fact that he was safe in Portland, not in Mumbai, that this was memory, not present danger. He had
learned to excuse himself, to step into a bathroom or hallway, to breathe until the sensation
passed. But the irritability was harder to hide. Small frustrations—a traffic delay, a contractor's
miscommunication, Priya asking him a routine question about his day—would trigger an anger that felt
disproportionate and uncontrollable. He would feel it rising in his chest before he could think, his
voice sharpening, his words becoming weapons.

Three years after his father's death, Rajesh finally admitted to Priya that he needed help. The
anniversary was approaching again, and he could not bear the thought of another year of this—the
dread, the avoidance, the sudden rages that left him ashamed and isolated. He had come to believe
that if he allowed himself to grieve, if he acknowledged the loss directly rather than trying to
outrun it, he would somehow fall apart completely. But he was already falling apart, just more
slowly, in ways that were damaging his marriage and his career. Priya had gently suggested that
talking to someone might help him find a way forward that did not require him to disappear every
autumn. Rajesh was terrified of what might emerge if he stopped running, but he was more afraid of
losing what remained.
