# Kofi's Reckoning

Kofi Mensah was sixty-two when his son died, and he had not yet found a way to live in the world
that followed. The death itself—a sudden cardiac arrest on a Tuesday morning in March, his thirty-
four-year-old son Kwesi collapsing on the trading floor of a Manhattan office building—had arrived
without warning, without the mercy of time to prepare. Kofi had been at his own desk in midtown,
watching the markets, when his ex-wife's voice came through the phone. He remembered the sound of
her breathing, the pause before she spoke, but the words themselves seemed to exist in a fog. He had
driven to the hospital in a state that felt like dreaming, and when he arrived, Kwesi was already
gone. The body was still warm when Kofi saw it, but the person inside had vanished entirely, leaving
Kofi standing in a fluorescent hallway with no explanation for how the universe could have permitted
such a thing.

In the months that followed, Kofi found himself caught in an exhausting loop of questioning. Why had
Kwesi been on that trading floor at all? Why had Kofi not insisted that his son pursue something
safer, something less demanding? He replayed conversations from years past, searching for the moment
he might have changed the trajectory, the instant where a different word from him could have altered
everything. At night, he would sit at his kitchen table with a glass of scotch and reconstruct the
morning of Kwesi's death hour by hour, as though repetition might reveal some hidden causality, some
thread he could pull to unmake what had happened. The rumination was relentless. It felt necessary,
as though if he could only understand why, he could prevent it from happening again—to someone else,
to himself.

Yet even as he obsessed over the reasons, Kofi found himself desperately trying not to think about
it at all. When a memory would surface—the sound of Kwesi's laugh, the way he used to call his
father "the old man" with affection—Kofi would push it away with an almost physical force, clenching
his jaw and turning his attention violently back to his screens, to the numbers, to anything else.
He began avoiding the places and people that carried his son's presence. He stopped going to the gym
where Kwesi used to join him on weekends. He deleted his son's contact from his phone, then felt
ashamed and retrieved it, then deleted it again. His ex-wife had called several times to talk about
the memorial service, about how they might honor Kwesi's memory together, but Kofi couldn't bear it.
He made excuses and eventually stopped answering.

The work became a refuge and a recklessness all at once. Kofi had always been a careful analyst,
methodical in his approach to the market. But now he found himself making trades he would never have
considered before, taking positions with leverage that bordered on suicidal, gambling on movements
that defied his own analysis. His colleagues noticed. His manager pulled him aside and asked if
everything was all right. Kofi assured him it was, even as he felt himself operating at the edge of
something dangerous, as though part of him was testing whether he could be hurt further, whether the
universe had anything left to take.

Then came the flashbacks. They arrived without preamble—Kofi would be sitting at his desk and
suddenly he was in the hospital hallway again, his hands were shaking, his vision had narrowed, and
for a few seconds he could not remember where he was. Once, he was on the phone with a client when
it happened, and he found himself unable to speak, unable to explain the silence. The client thought
he had been disconnected. Another time, a fragment of the memory surfaced unbidden—not the full
scene, but just the image of Kwesi's face, pale and still, and then it was gone again, leaving Kofi
gasping and confused, unable to retrieve the rest of it even when he tried.

What frightened Kofi most was that he could not hold the memory together anymore. When he tried
deliberately to reconstruct what he had seen that day, pieces were missing. He knew he had seen his
son's body, but the details were scattered, incomplete, like a puzzle with gaps he couldn't fill no
matter how hard he concentrated. The harder he tried to remember, the more elusive it became. And
the not-knowing—the sense that something crucial had been taken from him and he couldn't even fully
access his own grief—had become almost as unbearable as the loss itself. He had finally called a
therapist because his manager had made it clear that another incident at work would mean his job,
and because he was beginning to suspect that the recklessness he felt, the sense that nothing
mattered anymore, might be leading him somewhere he could not come back from.
