# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus had been twenty-three when he met David at a construction site in Portland, Oregon. What
began as a casual friendship deepened quickly into something Marcus thought would last forever. For
the first two years, things felt solid. Then, gradually, David's drinking increased, and with it
came a shift—subtle at first, then unmistakable. By the time Marcus was thirty-one, the physical
violence had become routine. The worst incident happened on a Tuesday evening in their shared
apartment. David had been drinking since afternoon. Marcus, coming home from a job, mentioned
casually that he'd be late the following evening. David erupted. What followed was a blur of fists,
the sharp pain of being thrown against the kitchen counter, the smell of burnt food left on the
stove—something David had started cooking and abandoned—mixing with the metallic taste of blood in
Marcus's mouth. When it ended, Marcus lay on the linoleum floor, certain he might not get up. He did
get up. He left that night and never went back.

That was eighteen years ago. Marcus had married Linda five years later, and they had built a quiet
life together. He was a skilled plumber, reliable and well-regarded in his community. But the past
decade had seen something shift in him, a gradual tightening that neither he nor Linda could quite
name. It started small. Whenever there was a burnt smell—toast left too long in the morning, a
neighbor's grill, even the acrid scent of hot metal from a blowtorch at work—Marcus would feel his
chest constrict. His mind would snap backward to that evening, to the smell, to the helplessness. He
began to notice that he was avoiding certain jobs, particularly ones that required him to work in
kitchens or commercial spaces where cooking happened. He'd make excuses, pass the work to younger
plumbers. His boss had started to notice the pattern, though he said nothing.

The real problem was confrontation. Any confrontation, even the mildest disagreement with a customer
or a conversation with Linda that carried even a whisper of tension, would set something off in him.
Two months ago, a customer had raised his voice about a delayed invoice. Marcus had felt his entire
body go rigid. He'd excused himself, walked out, and sat in his truck for an hour, unable to move.
When Linda had gently asked him that evening what was wrong, something in her tone—not anger, just
concern—had made him snap at her in a way that shocked them both. He could see the hurt in her face,
and he hated himself for it, but he couldn't seem to stop the pattern.

Sleep had become its own battlefield. Marcus found himself unable to fall asleep at a reasonable
hour. Instead, he would stay up until one, two, sometimes three in the morning, scrolling mindlessly
on his phone, watching television with the volume low, anything to avoid the quiet darkness where
his mind would go. He was exhausted constantly, moving through his days like someone underwater.
Linda would find him on the couch at dawn, unshaven, hollow-eyed.

What terrified Marcus most was that he seemed to be becoming someone he didn't recognize. When his
mind drifted toward what had happened with David—and he fought hard to keep it from drifting
there—he would force his thoughts elsewhere, anywhere else. He wouldn't let himself think about it,
wouldn't let himself remember the specifics. But the avoidance had a cost. He'd turned down a
promotion that would have meant managing other plumbers because the idea of being in a position of
authority, of potentially having conflict with employees, made him nauseated. He'd started making
excuses to avoid social gatherings where there might be raised voices or heated discussion. Linda
was patient, but he could feel her confusion, her worry, the slow distance growing between them.

It was Linda, finally, who insisted he talk to someone. She'd found him at three in the morning,
awake again, and she'd simply said, "This isn't working anymore, Marcus. You're not working anymore.
Please get help." He'd agreed, not because he was ready, but because he couldn't bear to lose her
the way he'd lost everything else. Now, sitting in the therapist's office, Marcus was terrified that
naming what happened would somehow make it real again, that talking about it would give it power.
But he was also desperate to stop living like a man in permanent hiding.
