# Aroha

Aroha was seventy-eight when her husband Frank struck her across the face during an argument about
money. She had been married to him for forty-three years—long enough that she had learned to read
the tightness in his jaw, the way his breathing changed before his hand came up. That night, she did
not see it coming. He had seemed calm. They had been sitting at the kitchen table with the bills
spread between them, and when she suggested they might need to cut back on his golf membership,
something shifted in him. The force of the blow knocked her sideways off her chair. Her head hit the
corner of the cabinet. She felt the warm rush of blood down her temple, the sharp pain in her left
cheekbone, and something else—a sensation like breaking inside her chest, as though the architecture
of her body had suddenly become unreliable.

She did not leave. She told herself it was because she was eighty years old, because they had
grandchildren, because the shame of it would be unbearable. But the truth, which she could not quite
articulate even to herself, was that she no longer trusted her own judgment about what was safe. If
she had misjudged Frank that night—if she had failed to see the danger that was clearly there—then
how could she trust herself to make any decision about her life? She had spent decades reading him,
and she had been wrong. The thought terrified her more than the blow itself.

In the weeks after, Aroha stopped sleeping. She would lie beside Frank in the dark, her body rigid,
waiting for some signal that would tell her when danger was coming. Around two or three in the
morning, she would wake suddenly, her heart hammering, and she would slip out of bed and move
through the house, checking the locks on the doors, looking into the kitchen to see if Frank was
there, peering down the hallway to confirm she was still alive. Sometimes she would stand in the
living room for an hour, listening to the silence, trying to decode whether it meant safety or the
calm before something worse. Her welder's hands, which had spent fifty-two years steady and precise
at the torch, began to shake. She would grip the edge of the kitchen counter and feel her fingers
trembling against the laminate, and she would think: *This is what happens when your body betrays
you. It doesn't just hurt; it stops working.*

At the welding shop where she had worked part-time since Frank's retirement, Aroha found herself
unable to concentrate. The work required absolute steadiness—a moment's hesitation with a torch
could ruin a joint, could damage equipment, could hurt someone. One afternoon in November, as she
was preparing to weld a structural beam, her hands began to shake so violently that she had to set
the torch down. Her supervisor, a man she had known for twenty years, asked if she was all right.
She told him she was fine, but she did not pick the torch back up. She called in sick the next day,
and the day after that. By December, she had stopped going to the shop altogether. She told Frank it
was her arthritis, and he did not question it.

What frightened Aroha most was not Frank himself anymore—or rather, it was Frank, but it was also
her own body. She had come to believe that something fundamental had been broken in her that night,
something that could not be repaired. Her body no longer belonged entirely to her; it had become a
thing that could betray her without warning, that could fail to protect her, that could be used
against her. She would feel her heart rate spike at the sound of his footsteps on the stairs, and
she would interpret this not as fear but as proof that her nervous system had been permanently
damaged. The emotions she had once felt—joy in her grandchildren's visits, satisfaction in her work,
even affection for Frank on the good days—had flattened into a gray, numb awareness that she was no
longer the person she had been.

It was her daughter who finally insisted she come to see a therapist. Aroha had resisted, but her
daughter had been direct: *"Mum, you're not yourself. You're terrified all the time. You won't work.
You won't leave the house. Something happened, and you need help."* Aroha sat in the therapist's
office two weeks ago, her hands folded in her lap to keep them from shaking, and she said the words
aloud for the first time: "He hit me. And now I don't know if I can ever trust myself again."
