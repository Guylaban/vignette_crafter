# Clinical Vignette: Vinh

Vinh was thirty-two when he first noticed the tremor in his left hand. A minor thing—a flutter that
came and went—but it persisted through the autumn, and by December his wife Linh insisted he see a
doctor. He went reluctantly, already sensing something was wrong in a way he could not name. The
neurologist's office was sterile and bright. Within two weeks, after an MRI and a spinal tap, he was
told he had multiple sclerosis. The diagnosis arrived not as relief but as a sentence. For the first
time in his adult life, Vinh understood that his body was not his own, that it could betray him
without warning, that medicine could not guarantee he would walk or think clearly in five years'
time.

The year that followed was a cascade of medical appointments. Spinal taps, infusions, blood draws,
the constant surveillance of his own failing body. Vinh began to notice that hospitals smelled like
something between bleach and fear, and that waiting rooms filled him with a suffocating dread—not of
the procedures themselves but of the time spent waiting, the liminal space where bad news seemed to
accumulate. He started finding reasons to skip appointments. When Linh scheduled his next infusion,
Vinh would develop a tension headache the morning of, or convince himself that the neurologist's
office had called to reschedule. He had come to believe that if he did not enter a hospital, did not
sit in those chairs, did not let them draw his blood, then perhaps the disease itself might pause,
might reconsider its claim on him. The logic was desperate but it made a kind of sense: his body had
already proven itself defective, already shown him that he was fundamentally weak in ways that other
men were not. To submit to the medical system was to accept this verdict as permanent.

At work, Vinh's role as a UX designer required him to attend meetings, to present wireframes, to
collaborate with developers and product managers. But three months after his diagnosis, he began
working from home as much as possible. Meetings that ran over schedule sent him into a state of
sharp internal alarm—if the meeting was delayed, something was wrong, something was slipping out of
control, and he would find himself checking his heart rate on his watch, feeling the muscles in his
neck and shoulders lock tight. His manager, who had been supportive initially, began to express
concern about his presence on the team. Vinh was missing brainstorms. His responses to Slack
messages came hours late. When colleagues suggested he join them for lunch, he declined; the
restaurant felt too public, too exposed, a place where his body might betray him in front of
witnesses. By the time he was thirty-five, he had been quietly demoted—not officially, but everyone
knew. He was now the designer who worked alone, who did not come to the office, whose contributions
were solid but increasingly marginal.

What frightened Vinh most was not the disease itself, but the possibility that he had been right all
along: that he was fundamentally broken, that his weakness was not a product of his illness but
something intrinsic to who he was. Linh had stopped asking him to go to appointments. Instead, she
attended them herself, collecting information about his health while Vinh stayed home, deliberately
not thinking about what the neurologist had said, not imagining what the next MRI might show. He
found that if he did not allow himself to think about the diagnosis, if he kept himself still and
quiet and away from anything that might stress his body, then he could almost pretend he was still
the man he had been before. But the pretense was exhausting. His body remained tense, perpetually
braced for catastrophe, and no amount of avoidance seemed to quiet that vigilance.

It was Linh who finally insisted he seek help. She told him that she could not watch him disappear
anymore, that his fear had become bigger than the disease, and that he needed to talk to someone who
understood what had happened to him. Vinh came to his first session uncertain whether talking would
change anything, but also desperately hoping that someone might tell him he was not, in fact, as
broken as he believed.
