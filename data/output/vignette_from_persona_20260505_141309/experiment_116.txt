# Marcus

Marcus was thirty-four when Hurricane Helene made landfall near Charleston, South Carolina, where he
had been stationed as a Navy officer for two years. The storm surge came at night. He had been
helping with evacuation efforts, moving through the naval base in the dark with a small team, when
the water rose faster than anyone had predicted. One moment he was in the operations building on the
second floor; the next, the power failed and the emergency lighting flickered out. The water rose
through the stairwell with a roar that sounded almost alive. He remembered the cold, the absolute
blindness, his uniform becoming heavy, the smell of diesel fuel and salt and something
else—someone's spilled bourbon from the officers' club on the first floor, mixing with the
floodwater. He had clawed his way up to the roof with two other men. They stayed there for six hours
in darkness, unable to see anything, listening to the wind and the water below, not knowing if
rescue was coming. The third man beside him—Lieutenant Chen—had kept saying they would be fine, kept
talking about his daughter's birthday party the next day. Chen didn't make it off that roof alive.

In the weeks after rescue, Marcus had returned to his duties. He told himself this was what he had
trained for, that survival was what mattered. But something had fractured in a way his mind kept
trying to make sense of. The memories came as vivid images—the black water, the shape of Chen's face
in the darkness—but they felt impossible, like something he had watched happen to someone else
rather than experienced himself. When the images came, his body would seize into the exact position
he had held on that roof, crouched and shaking, his muscles tensing as if the water were still
rising around him.

By autumn, Marcus had begun to withdraw. He stopped attending the weekly dinners with the other
officers and their spouses. His wife, Jennifer, would ask why he no longer wanted to see his closest
friends, and he could not fully articulate it. Being around them meant being in rooms where he felt
exposed, where the casual closeness of bodies in a restaurant or a bar made his breath shallow.
Worse, he had begun to tell himself that he deserved to feel this way, that Chen was dead and he was
alive and there was no fairness in that equation. He deserved to be miserable. He deserved to be
alone. When Jennifer would suggest they go out, he would find reasons not to. The smell of alcohol
at any gathering—even the faint scent on someone's breath—would transport him back to that rooftop,
to the diesel and bourbon mixing in the floodwater, and he would feel his body lock into that
crouched position, his chest tight, the darkness pressing in even though the room was well lit.

He had taken to spending evenings in the garage, away from the house, away from Jennifer's worried
face. He had stopped attending base functions. His commanding officer had begun to notice a change
in his performance, a flatness that was unlike the Marcus of before. He had come to believe that he
no longer belonged anywhere—not at the base, not at home, not among the people he had once called
friends. The world had divided itself into two groups: those who had been in the dark water, and
everyone else. He was set apart now, isolated by something no one else could see or understand.

It was Jennifer who finally insisted he speak to someone. She had found him in the garage one
evening, sitting in the dark by choice, and she had said something that broke through his reasoning:
"You're still here. You survived. But you're acting like you're still drowning." He had come to the
clinic reluctant, uncertain whether talking about what happened could change the fact that Chen was
gone, or that he no longer recognized himself as the person he had been before the storm. What
frightened him most was the possibility that this new version of himself—isolated, convinced of his
own unworthiness, trapped in images his mind insisted were unreal even as his body experienced them
as present—might be permanent.
