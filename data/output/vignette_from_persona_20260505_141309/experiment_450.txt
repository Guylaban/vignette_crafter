# Amara's Case

Amara Okonkwo was twenty-five when she came to the clinic, referred by her hospital's occupational
health department after her third request for a schedule change in six months. She had been a
surgeon for just over two years—a distinction she had worked toward since childhood, driven by a
need to fix what was broken, to be the person others could rely on. The trajectory had been linear
and deliberate until the summer she spent deployed with a military medical unit to a conflict zone
in West Africa.

She had volunteered for the deployment. Her family had been horrified, but Amara had felt called to
it in a way she could not fully articulate. She was there for three weeks before the incident that
would reshape her entire life.

The attack came on a Tuesday morning at a field hospital where she had been assisting with trauma
surgery. A mortar struck the compound. The sound—not just the explosion itself but the particular
metallic *crack* of it, followed by the rush of air—remained locked in her nervous system. What
followed was chaos that her mind could only partially hold. The texture of the blood-soaked surgical
drape she had gripped as the building shook, the acrid dust that filled her lungs, the screams of
patients and staff, the weight of a colleague's body she had tried to shield—these fragments existed
in her memory not as a coherent narrative but as sensations that invaded her without warning.

When she tried to speak about it in the clinic, her voice would flatten. She would describe the
date, the location, the number of casualties, but these facts sat separate from the lived horror of
the moment. The actual terror—the animal certainty that she was about to die, the smell of smoke and
blood, the specific texture of that surgical drape—these things could not be put into words. They
existed only as physical memory, as something that lived in her body rather than her mind.

Back in the United States, Amara had returned to her surgical rotation at a major teaching hospital.
For the first three weeks, she functioned. Then, one afternoon, a helicopter passed low overhead on
its way to the rooftop landing pad. The sound—that particular *thump-thump-thump*—had sent her into
an immediate panic. She had stepped away from the operating table, her hands shaking, her heart
racing as though she were back in the compound. A colleague had noticed. She had made an excuse
about fatigue.

The avoidance had begun almost immediately after that. Before entering any room—the OR, a patient's
bedroom, the hospital cafeteria—Amara would pause and identify the exits. She needed to know where
she could go if something happened. In the operating room, she had begun asking to position herself
where she could see the entire space. Her attending surgeon had initially accommodated this,
chalking it up to preference, but when she began refusing certain cases or requesting different
stations, questions were raised.

At home, she could not sit on the couch with her back to the room. She repositioned her bed so she
faced the door. When she was at a restaurant with friends, she would arrive early and select a
table, always with a wall behind her. Her friends had stopped inviting her out. They had noticed her
scanning the room constantly, her tension, the way she seemed to be listening for something beneath
the normal sounds of conversation. She had told one friend it was stress from work. She had told
another nothing at all.

The sound of a car backfiring on the street near her apartment had sent her into a state of panic
that lasted for hours. She had hidden in her bedroom, her heart pounding, waiting for something she
could not name. A motorcycle with a loud engine had triggered the same response. She had begun
planning her routes through the city to avoid roads where she had heard such sounds before.

What had finally brought her to the clinic was a moment in the operating room three days ago. A
surgical instrument had dropped and struck the metal tray with a sharp, percussive *bang*. Amara had
frozen. For a moment, she was not in the hospital. She was in the compound, and the world was
collapsing. She had excused herself, ostensibly to use the restroom, and had spent fifteen minutes
in a bathroom stall trying to breathe, trying to remember where she was. Her hands would not stop
shaking. When she returned to the operating room, the attending surgeon had taken her aside and made
the referral.

Amara was terrified that she would lose her career. She was also terrified of what might happen if
she allowed herself to feel the full weight of what had occurred. She did not know which fear was
larger.
