# Margot

Margot was thirty-four when the diagnosis arrived—not gradually, not through a series of concerning
symptoms, but all at once, in a radiologist's office, in a single sentence that seemed to stop time.
Invasive carcinoma. The words hung in the sterile air between her and the doctor, who was already
turning to the next slide on the screen. She had gone in for what she thought was a routine
mammogram, the kind she'd been putting off for months because her dental practice was busy and she'd
convinced herself she was young enough, healthy enough, that it could wait. The wait had cost her
something she could not yet measure.

The surgery came first, then chemotherapy, then radiation. For eighteen months, her body became a
landscape she no longer recognized—scarred, weakened, marked by treatments that left her nauseated
and bald and uncertain whether she would live to see forty. She had come to believe that her body
was fundamentally untrustworthy, that it had betrayed her without warning and might do so again at
any moment. Even now, five years later, that belief had not loosened. Every ache, every unexpected
sensation, every moment her energy dipped sent her into a spiral of dread. She checked herself
obsessively—then stopped checking at all, because looking at the surgical scar or the asymmetry of
her chest triggered a cascade of panic that left her shaking.

The chemotherapy smelled like metal and antiseptic. That smell had filled the infusion center where
she spent hours tethered to an IV, watching her own blood circulate through a machine and back into
her veins. She had sat in those recliners alongside other patients, some of whom did not survive.
She had watched a woman two chairs over decline over the course of months, and then one day that
woman's chair was empty. Now, whenever Margot encountered that particular combination of smells—the
sharp bite of hospital-grade disinfectant, the faint metallic undertone—her heart would race and her
hands would go cold. She had stopped visiting her dentist for her own cleanings, despite her
profession. The smell of the office, so similar to the infusion center, made her feel as though the
cancer was returning, as though she were back in that chair with no options, no escape, no control
over what was happening to her body.

What frightened her most was the sense that she had no way out, that her illness had trapped her in
a version of herself she did not recognize and could not shed. She had become someone weaker,
someone damaged, someone fundamentally changed. Her partner, David, had tried to be supportive
during treatment, but over the years his patience had worn thin. She had stopped letting him touch
her, had taken to sleeping in separate rooms because she could not bear the feeling of being seen in
her altered body. She had also stopped talking to him about how she felt, stopped talking to most
people about the illness or its aftermath. When friends asked how she was doing, she gave short
answers and changed the subject. When David tried to discuss her avoidance, her isolation, the way
she had withdrawn from their life together, she shut down entirely.

The photographs were worst of all. Old pictures from before the diagnosis—images of herself with
hair, with symmetry, with a body that felt like it belonged to someone else entirely—triggered a
profound sense of loss and anger that she could not articulate. She had deleted most of them from
her phone. The ones that remained, in boxes in the closet, she would not look at.

At thirty-nine, Margot had returned to her dental practice, but something fundamental had shifted.
She found herself increasingly unable to be present with her patients, unable to tolerate the close
physical proximity that her work required. She had begun scheduling fewer appointments, referring
patients away, watching her practice shrink. David had finally asked her, quietly, whether she
thought she might need help. She had resisted for months. But last week, when she realized she was
eating lunch alone in her car in the parking lot because the thought of sitting in the staff
room—with its fluorescent lights and its faint smell of sterilizing solution—made her feel trapped
and suffocating, she had finally called a therapist.
