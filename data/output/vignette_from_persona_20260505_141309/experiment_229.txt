# Marcus

Marcus was forty-nine when his younger brother Stefan died. The call came on a Tuesday afternoon in
March—Stefan had been found unresponsive in his apartment in Portland, an overdose, the police said,
though the toxicology would take weeks. Marcus had been at his studio in Manhattan when his mother
phoned, her voice already fractured. He remembered the exact moment: standing in front of a mood
board of Italian fabrics, swatches of charcoal and cream pinned to the wall, and then the world
tilting sideways as his mother spoke words that didn't seem to belong in language.

Stefan had been forty-three. He had struggled with addiction for years—a fact that lived in the
background of family conversations, acknowledged but somehow never quite real until it was. Marcus
had tried to help, had paid for treatment twice, had offered Stefan money and shelter and the kind
of practical support that felt like it should matter. But Stefan had always drifted back. In the
months before his death, Marcus had begun to distance himself, telling himself it was better to have
boundaries, better not to enable. He had stopped calling as frequently. When Stefan reached out,
Marcus sometimes didn't respond right away. These were the facts that, when Marcus allowed himself
to remember them at all, filled him with a shame so acute it felt like a physical weight.

The funeral was in late April. Marcus sat in the pew next to his wife, Catherine, and felt
nothing—or rather, felt everything so intensely that it registered as numbness. He could see his
mother's shoulders shaking. He could see the casket. He could see Stefan's friends from high school
sitting in the back, people Marcus hadn't thought about in decades. But the moments had a strange,
fractured quality. Later, Catherine would tell him what he had said during the reception, and he
would have no memory of saying it. There were gaps—whole stretches of time that simply weren't
there.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus threw himself into work with a ferocity that surprised even his
colleagues. He arrived at the studio before dawn and left after dark. He took on additional
projects, volunteered for client presentations he normally would have delegated, kept his mind
moving constantly so that it couldn't settle. Catherine mentioned it once—gently, the way she did
things—and he had snapped at her, something he rarely did. The studio became the only place that
made sense. At home, in the quiet of the evening, the thoughts came: memories of Stefan as a boy, of
conversations they'd had, of the times Marcus hadn't answered the phone. The guilt would rise in his
chest like something trying to claw its way out, and he would find himself unable to breathe, his
heart racing in a way that felt dangerous.

Sleep became impossible. He would lie in bed next to Catherine, his body rigid, his mind cycling
through the same terrible loop. He tried to suppress the thoughts, to push them away, but they had a
momentum of their own. The only relief came from distraction—he began staying up late, scrolling
through his phone, turning on the television, anything to keep his mind occupied. By June, he was
functioning on four hours of fragmented sleep most nights.

Catherine noticed that he had stopped planning anything. When she mentioned taking a trip in the
fall, or talked about renovating the kitchen, Marcus felt a surge of panic. How could he make plans?
How could he think about a future when Stefan didn't have one? The thought felt like a betrayal. He
would change the subject abruptly, or tell Catherine they could discuss it later, and later never
came.

By August, Catherine's frustration had begun to show. She loved him, she said, but he was
disappearing. She didn't recognize him anymore. Marcus knew she was right, but he couldn't seem to
stop. When he tried to explain—tried to tell her about the guilt, about the way his body would
suddenly flood with terror and shame when something reminded him of Stefan, about the gaps in his
memory of the funeral that terrified him—the words came out wrong. He sounded defensive. He sounded
like he was blaming her for not understanding.

It was Catherine, finally, who insisted he see someone. She made the appointment herself and told
him the date and time. Marcus sat in the therapist's office on a gray September morning, his leg
bouncing, his hands clenched, and admitted that he couldn't go on like this. He was afraid of what
would happen if he stopped moving, stopped distracting himself, stopped trying not to think. He was
afraid that if he allowed himself to fully remember that day, to feel what he had been trying so
hard to suppress, he would break entirely. And underneath all of that was something deeper: the
certainty that he had failed his brother, that his distance, his boundaries, his refusal to keep
rescuing Stefan had somehow contributed to his death. He didn't know how to live with that
knowledge, and he didn't know how to live without trying to escape it.
