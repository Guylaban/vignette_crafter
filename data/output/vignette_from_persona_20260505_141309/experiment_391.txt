# Thien's Vigil

Thien Nguyen was fifty-eight when his eldest son drowned during the monsoon season off the coast of
Galveston. The boy—Duc, who was twenty-three—had gone out on a commercial fishing boat with two of
Thien's regular crew members, men Thien had known for nearly thirty years. The boat capsized in a
sudden squall. The other two men were pulled from the water by the coast guard. Duc was found four
hours later, face-down in the churn, already cold.

Thien had been on the dock that morning, mending nets as he had done for forty years. He watched Duc
walk toward the boat, saw his son wave back at him with that careless confidence of the young. The
last thing Duc said was, "Don't worry, Papa. We'll be back before dark." They never came back. When
the coast guard called, Thien's hands had still been tangled in the net. He had to cut himself free.

In the days after the funeral, Thien's wife, Linh, noticed he barely slept. He would lie beside her
in the dark, staring at nothing, his body rigid. When he did drift off, he would jerk awake,
gasping, his heart hammering so hard she could feel it through the mattress. Sometimes he would call
out Duc's name. Within a week, Thien had stopped trying to sleep in the bedroom at all. He moved to
the living room couch, where he would sit upright until dawn, waiting for something he could not
name.

The physical reactions came without warning. A car door slamming in the driveway sent a spike of
adrenaline through his chest so violent he thought he was having a heart attack. The sound of
water—even from the shower, even from the kitchen faucet—made his palms sweat and his breathing
shallow. Once, Linh turned on the television and a news report about a fishing accident in Louisiana
came on. Thien's whole body went rigid. His face drained of color. He stood up, walked to the
bedroom, and closed the door. He didn't come out for two hours.

What had begun to crystallize in Thien's mind, in the weeks that followed, was a conviction that the
world was fundamentally unsafe and that the people in it could not be trusted. The two crew members
who survived—men he had called friends—had come to the house to pay respects. Thien had listened to
their account of what happened, and something in their words felt false to him. Why had they been
pulled from the water so quickly? Why had Duc not been found until later? He had come to believe,
though he could not articulate it clearly, that there was something they were not telling him.
Perhaps they had not tried hard enough. Perhaps they had chosen themselves. He stopped answering
their calls.

His wife had begun to feel like a stranger too. She would try to comfort him, to sit with him on the
couch at night, and he would feel nothing—no warmth from her presence, no solace in her touch. She
seemed to exist on the other side of a thick glass wall. He loved her, he knew this intellectually,
but the feeling itself had drained away, leaving only a hollow space where his capacity for
connection used to be. "I feel dead inside," he told her once, and she had wept. He had not been
able to comfort her.

The guilt had become a constant companion. Why had he sent Duc out that day? Why had he not warned
him about the weather, which he—a man who had read the ocean his entire life—had sensed was turning
dangerous? Other fishermen's sons had survived. Other families still had their boys. The unfairness
of it, the randomness, made no sense to him. He had come to believe that his survival, his continued
breathing and walking and eating, was somehow unjust. He did not deserve to be here when Duc was
not.

By his sixtieth birthday, Thien had stopped going to the docks entirely. His wife had tried to coax
him back, saying the business needed him, that the crew was struggling without him. But the thought
of being near the water, of hearing the boats, of seeing the nets—all of it felt like a punishment
he could not bear. He had effectively retired, though no one had said it aloud. The income had dried
up. Linh had taken a job at a grocery store to keep them afloat. She came home exhausted, and Thien
sat in the living room, waiting for sleep that would not come, waiting for the guilt to somehow
transform into something he could survive.

It was Linh who finally insisted he see someone. She had found a therapist who specialized in grief
and trauma. Thien had resisted, but his wife's quiet desperation had worn through his resistance. He
had agreed to one appointment, telling himself it would change nothing. What he did not yet
understand was that the wall between him and the rest of the world had become so thick that he could
no longer see through it—and that somewhere, beneath the numbness and the guilt and the fear, a part
of him still wanted to.
