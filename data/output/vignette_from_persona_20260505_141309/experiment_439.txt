# Clinical Vignette: Aroha

Aroha was sixty-five years old when a reversing delivery truck pinned her against the loading dock
of the tech firm where she had worked for seventeen years. The impact came without warning on a
Tuesday afternoon in March. She had stepped back to make room for a colleague, and in that moment
the truck's backup alarm—which she later realized had been sounding—became white noise against the
rumble of the engine. The driver never saw her. Her left side took the full force. She remembered
the smell of diesel fuel and hot rubber, the sensation of her ribs compressing, and the strange
clarity of watching her own arm bend at an angle that arms should not bend. The pain came in waves,
each one more insistent than the last, until the paramedics arrived and something clinical happened
to her body.

The hospital stay lasted three weeks. She recovered, medically speaking. The fractures healed.
Physical therapy restored most of her mobility. But in the months that followed, Aroha found herself
caught in a loop of questions that had no satisfactory answers. Why had she stepped back at
precisely that moment? Why had the driver not checked his mirrors? Why had a routine Tuesday
afternoon been chosen to remake her life? She would lie awake at night, retracing the sequence of
events, looking for the point at which she might have changed course—the moment she should have
stayed in her office, or taken a different route, or simply not existed in that space at that time.
The rumination became as much a part of her day as breathing. She would be at her desk analyzing
network logs and suddenly find herself back at the loading dock, replaying the seconds before
impact, searching for the flaw in herself that had allowed this to happen.

Sleep became impossible in the way it once had been—a simple transition into rest. Instead, Aroha
lay rigid in her bed each night, her body tense with the need to monitor for danger. She had come to
believe that sleep meant vulnerability, that the moment she relaxed her vigilance something else
would come for her. When she did manage to doze, she would wake abruptly in the darkness, her heart
hammering, needing to verify that she was still intact, still safe in her bedroom. The nights were
worse than the days. In daylight, she could see threats coming. At night, everything was potential
danger.

The memories themselves were vivid and embodied. When they surfaced—and they surfaced constantly,
unbidden—she saw the truck's grille, the shocked face of her colleague, the geometry of the loading
dock tilted at an impossible angle. But seeing was only part of it. The smell of diesel would arrive
without warning, visceral and complete, as though she were standing at the dock again. And her ribs
would ache, a phantom echo of the original injury, a pain that no X-ray could explain because it
lived in her nervous system's memory.

Over the following year, Aroha began to withdraw from anything that might disturb her emotional
equilibrium. She declined invitations from friends. She stopped attending the book club she had
enjoyed for a decade. She even reduced her contact with her daughter, telling herself that isolation
was safer than connection. If she felt nothing—neither joy nor sorrow, neither anticipation nor
disappointment—then she could not be hurt. The problem was that the numbness had spread like
frostbite. Her work, which had once engaged her intellectually, became mechanical. Her colleagues
noticed that she was different, more distant, less collaborative. She had begun to work from home
whenever possible, and when she was in the office, she positioned herself with her back to the wall
and her eyes on the exits.

What frightened her most was the conviction that her body had become a liability, something she
could no longer trust. It had failed to protect her. It had broken. And worse, she was certain that
others could see this failure written across her face, could sense her fragility just by looking at
her. She imagined that people at work were watching her with pity, or worse, with a kind of morbid
curiosity about the woman the truck had nearly killed.

By the time Aroha made her first appointment with a therapist, eighteen months had passed. She was
exhausted in a way that sleep could not touch. The rumination had worn grooves into her thinking.
She was hoping, though she hardly dared to articulate it, that someone could help her understand why
this had happened, and perhaps—though this seemed impossible—help her sleep through the night again.
