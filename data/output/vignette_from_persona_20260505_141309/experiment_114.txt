# Chinedu

Chinedu was nineteen when the men came through the warehouse door. He had been working the night
shift at the logistics facility in Newark, restocking inventory in the back section, when three
figures in dark clothes forced him into a storage room at gunpoint. What followed was thirty-six
hours of captivity—bound to a chair, a cloth stuffed in his mouth, the sound of their voices cycling
between threats and laughter, the metallic click of a pistol being cocked repeatedly, sometimes
followed by the cold press of the barrel against his temple. One of them had a laugh that seemed to
come from deep in his chest, almost a bark. Another kept asking him questions he couldn't answer
because of the gag, then would hit him across the face for his silence. The third barely spoke, but
his footsteps—heavy, deliberate—would approach and recede, approach and recede, and Chinedu never
knew which moment would bring violence.

He was found when a supervisor came looking for him. The police arrested two of the men within a
week; the third was never located. Chinedu gave his statement to detectives, and something about
their calm, methodical questioning—the way they helped him organize the chaos into a coherent
narrative—made him feel less alone in it. Within months, despite his parents' concerns, he applied
to the police academy. He wanted to be the person who found people, who brought order to chaos. He
wanted to understand how to make sense of the senseless.

But in the year since the captivity, Chinedu has discovered that his mind refuses to cooperate with
his intentions. When he tries to write reports about cases, he finds himself staring at the screen,
knowing exactly what he witnessed or learned, but the words simply will not come. His supervisor has
noticed the incomplete paperwork. His partner has begun to file reports without him. He tries hard
not to think about what happened in that warehouse—he pushes the thought away the moment it
surfaces, forces his attention elsewhere—but the effort itself is exhausting, and it has not made
the memories less powerful.

The nights have become unbearable. Chinedu will not go out after dark anymore. He tells people he is
tired, that he has early shifts, but the truth is that darkness feels like a return to that room.
The streetlights, the shadows between them, the sound of footsteps behind him—all of it sends his
body into a state of vigilance that leaves him drained. He has started taking the day shift, which
has cost him seniority and respect among his colleagues, but he cannot bring himself to explain why.

His girlfriend, Amara, has become another casualty of his captivity. She reaches for his hand, and
he pulls away. She tries to embrace him, and his body goes rigid. She has asked him repeatedly what
is wrong, whether he is still attracted to her, whether the relationship means anything to him
anymore. He cannot tell her that it is not her—that any touch, even the gentle brush of her fingers,
sends him into a state of panic that feels like drowning. The feel of someone's hands on him, even
in tenderness, resurrects the sensation of the rope around his wrists, the grip of hands forcing him
down. He has come to believe that if anyone touches him, something terrible will happen, though he
cannot articulate what. Amara has stopped trying.

What troubles Chinedu most is that sometimes, without warning, he will be sitting at his desk or
driving to work, and a wave of pure terror will crash through him—the full emotional weight of those
thirty-six hours—but without any image, without any clear memory to anchor it to. He will hear the
bark-like laugh echoing in his head, or the metallic click of the pistol, sounds so vivid they seem
to be happening in the room with him. He will feel the panic in his chest and have no idea what
triggered it, which makes him feel even more powerless.

He came to the clinic because his captain suggested it, told him that his performance was
deteriorating and that he needed to get himself sorted out. Chinedu agreed, though what he is really
hoping for is that someone can help him reclaim the night, reclaim touch, and reclaim the ability to
speak about what happened without his voice disappearing into silence.
