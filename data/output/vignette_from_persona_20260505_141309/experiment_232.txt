# Rodrigo

Rodrigo was twenty-six when his younger brother Marco died. The death was sudden—a massive stroke on
a Tuesday morning while Marco was at work, no warning, no illness that anyone had noticed. One
moment Marco was texting Rodrigo about weekend plans; the next, his coworker was calling 911. By the
time the ambulance arrived, Marco was already gone. Rodrigo had been at the county detention
facility where he worked as a corrections officer, processing intake paperwork in the administrative
wing, when his mother called. He remembered the fluorescent lights humming overhead, the smell of
industrial cleaner, the way his hands had gone numb as she spoke.

In the weeks after the funeral, Rodrigo had tried to return to his routine. His wife, Carmen, had
been patient at first, bringing him coffee in the morning, letting him sit in silence after his
shifts. But something had shifted inside him that he could not name. By the time he came to his
first therapy session at twenty-eight, he had begun to understand that he was no longer the person
he had been before Marco died. The happiness that had defined him—his quick laugh, his ease with
others, the way he used to joke with Carmen about everything—felt like something that belonged to
someone else now. He had come to believe with a kind of certainty that he would never feel that
lightness again, that some part of him had died with Marco and would not return.

The triggers came unexpectedly. A news story about sudden cardiac death on the radio would send him
into a state where the world seemed to flatten and recede, as though he were watching his own life
from behind glass. These moments of unreality frightened him more than the grief itself. He would
feel his sense of self dissolving, and he would panic, unsure whether he was actually present in the
room or if he was slipping away entirely. When Carmen tried to talk to him about her own
sadness—about how much she missed Marco's visits, how hard it was to see his empty chair at family
dinners—Rodrigo would feel something sharp rise in his chest. The weight of her grief on top of his
own felt unbearable, and so he had begun to start arguments with her over small things: the way she
loaded the dishwasher, money they didn't have, plans she wanted to make. The fights gave him
something to focus on, a temporary escape from the hollow ache beneath everything. Carmen had
stopped trying to talk to him about Marco after the third or fourth argument. She had stopped
talking to him about a lot of things.

At the detention facility, Rodrigo had become hypervigilant in a way that exhausted him. He
monitored the faces of the inmates more closely, watching for signs of medical distress, convinced
that if he looked away for even a moment, someone would collapse on his watch. He had begun to
believe—with a logic that made perfect sense to him in those moments—that he should have seen
Marco's stroke coming, that there must have been some sign he had missed, some way he could have
protected his brother if he had been more attentive. This certainty had metastasized into a
conviction that he was failing in his duty as a corrections officer, that his inattention had
already cost lives. His supervisor had noticed the change. Rodrigo had become argumentative with
colleagues, quick to escalate minor conflicts with inmates. Three weeks ago, he had been pulled
aside and told that his performance was slipping, that he needed to get himself together or they
would have to move him to a different assignment.

What finally brought Rodrigo to therapy was a moment of dissociation so profound that he had lost
several minutes of time on the drive home from work. He had come to, hands gripping the steering
wheel, parked on the shoulder of the highway with no memory of how he had gotten there. The fear
that seized him in that moment—the terror that he was losing his mind—had been sharper than anything
else he had felt since Marco died. He was afraid now that he had no future, that he was broken in
some fundamental way, and that his marriage, his job, everything he had built, was slipping through
his fingers because he could not stop being miserable. He wanted to feel like himself again. More
than anything, he wanted to believe that was still possible.
