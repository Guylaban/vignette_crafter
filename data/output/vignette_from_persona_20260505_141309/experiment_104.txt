# Aroha's Vigil

Aroha Parata was fifty-three when her husband Michael died. It was a Tuesday in March, unremarkable
in every way except that he collapsed on the kitchen floor while making his morning coffee, and by
the time the ambulance arrived, he was already gone. A massive stroke, the paramedics said. There
was nothing anyone could have done. Aroha had been in the back room of their small grocery on Queen
Street, counting inventory, when the police came to find her. She had not been there. She had not
heard him fall, had not held his hand, had not said goodbye.

In the weeks that followed, Aroha moved through the motions of grief that were expected of her—the
funeral service at the local Catholic church, the gathering of family and friends, the careful
arrangement of flowers and casseroles in her home. But something in her had fractured in a way she
could not explain to anyone. When people spoke to her about loss, about how they understood what it
was to lose someone, to miss them terribly, she felt a strange and terrible vertigo. The
conversations themselves became dangerous. A cousin mentioning the death of her father, a customer
at the shop talking about her mother's passing—these moments sent Aroha's heart racing, made her
palms sweat, made her certain without knowing why that something catastrophic was about to happen.
She came to dread these conversations, began to excuse herself when she sensed them coming,
sometimes invented reasons to step away. Over time, she found herself avoiding people altogether,
keeping interactions at the shop brief and transactional.

What frightened her most was the smell of alcohol. Michael had kept a bottle of whisky in the
cupboard above the sink. She had never paid it much attention before his death, but now the
smell—whether from a customer's breath, from a bottle someone set on the counter, even the faint
ghost of it on someone's clothes—sent her into a state of raw panic. Her body would tense, her
breath would come shallow, and for a moment she was convinced that something terrible was about to
occur, that she was in immediate danger, though she could not articulate from what. The smell had
become inseparable from loss itself, from the moment everything changed.

Funerals and cemeteries were impossible. When a regular customer invited her to a memorial service
for his wife, Aroha declined with an excuse. When the anniversary of Michael's death approached, she
closed the shop rather than face the day alone. She had come to believe that these places, these
rituals, were where her own unraveling would finally become visible to others.

But the worst torment was internal. The memory of that day—or rather, the memory of the hours after,
when she was told, when she understood—replayed in her mind without warning or mercy. It was not a
coherent sequence of events. She could not piece together a clear narrative of what had happened,
could not tell the story even to herself in a way that made sense. Instead, fragments assaulted her:
the police officer's face, the word "stroke," the smell of their kitchen, the cold tile floor she
had never seen him on. These fragments played in a loop, vivid and intrusive, arriving without
invitation in the middle of her day. She would be arranging fruit at the shop counter and suddenly
she was there again, her heart hammering, her body flooded with the certainty that Michael was dying
right now, that she was failing him again by not being present.

Over the eighteen months since his death, Aroha had become someone she did not recognize. The woman
who had run a thriving business, who had hosted Sunday dinners, who had laughed easily—that woman
seemed to have vanished. In her place was someone hypervigilant and afraid, someone who could not
relax even in her own home, who lay awake at night convinced that catastrophe was imminent. She had
come to believe that her life was over, that whatever future might have existed for her had been
destroyed on that Tuesday morning. She was moving through the world like a ghost, and the cruelest
part was that she was not yet dead.

When her sister finally insisted she see someone, Aroha agreed not out of hope but out of
exhaustion. She had nowhere else to go.
