# Petra's Diminishment

Petra was sixty years old when she came to her first therapy appointment, though she moved through
the waiting room as though carrying an invisible weight that had aged her beyond her years. She had
worked as a midwife for thirty-seven years—the last fifteen at a teaching hospital in the Midwest
where she had trained younger nurses and taken pride in her steady hands and the way families
remembered her presence at their children's births. That Petra felt like someone else's story now.

The exposure had happened eighteen months earlier during a routine equipment maintenance shift. A
chemical sterilant used to clean surgical instruments had leaked from a corroded valve in the supply
room where Petra was inventorying stock. She had not seen the leak itself, but she had felt the burn
in her throat and lungs almost immediately, a sensation like breathing fire. Her eyes watered so
violently she could barely see. She had stumbled out into the hallway, gasping, and colleagues had
rushed her to the emergency department. The hospital's occupational health team confirmed exposure
to glutaraldehyde at levels that triggered a mandatory incident report. She had spent two days in
the hospital being monitored, had been cleared medically within a week, and had returned to work
after a month of light duty.

But something had fractured in those two days that medical clearance could not repair.

Within weeks, Petra had begun to notice that certain moments would send her into a state of acute
panic. When she witnessed a patient bleeding during delivery—something that had once been simply
part of her clinical reality, managed with practiced efficiency—she now found herself gripped by a
terror so sudden and total that she had to step away from the bedside. The sight of blood triggered
a cascade of physical sensations: her heart would race, her breathing would become shallow, and she
would feel certain that something catastrophic was about to happen. She had come to believe that her
body was fundamentally compromised, that exposure to that chemical had left her permanently fragile.
What frightened her most was that she had no control over when these moments would arrive.

Over the following months, Petra had quietly begun to withdraw. She stopped volunteering for the
busier shifts. When colleagues invited her to the regular Friday gatherings at the hospital café,
she found reasons to decline—not because she did not want to go, but because maintaining those
connections required her to explain or to risk judgment. She agreed to whatever schedule changes
were proposed, whatever tasks were reassigned to her, because saying no felt dangerous, as though
disagreement itself might trigger another crisis. Her partner, Maria, had noticed the change first:
Petra no longer spoke about her work with the enthusiasm that had once animated their evenings. When
Maria suggested they plan a trip—something they had always done each spring—Petra had felt a
suffocating dread at the thought of committing to a future date, a future version of herself that
she could not guarantee would be stable.

The feeling that nothing held meaning anymore had crept in gradually, like a slow leak. Delivering
babies—the thing that had once felt like sacred work—now felt hollow. She would stand at the bedside
of a laboring woman and feel nothing of the wonder she had once known. She had begun to think of
herself as weak, as someone whose body had betrayed her and whose mind could no longer be trusted.
The Petra who had trained residents and been called to the hardest deliveries seemed to have
vanished, replaced by someone anxious and diminished.

What had finally brought her to seek help was not a single crisis but a accumulation of small
surrenders. She had requested a transfer to administrative duties, away from direct patient care.
Maria had gently suggested that perhaps talking to someone might help. Petra had resisted for
months—the thought of discussing the trauma, of reliving it in any form, had felt unbearable—but the
weight of her own absence from her own life had become too much to carry alone. She sat in the
therapy office now with her hands folded in her lap, uncertain whether she could be helped,
uncertain whether the person she had been before the exposure could ever be found again.
