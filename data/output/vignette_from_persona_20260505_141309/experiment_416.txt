# Clinical Vignette: Margaret

Margaret was seventy-three when she first sat down in the therapist's office, her posture rigid
despite the soft chair. She had been referred by her primary care physician after months of insomnia
and what Margaret herself called "being on high alert all the time." What brought her in now, after
living with these feelings for decades, was a shift in her household: her husband had recently
retired, and they were spending far more time together in their home in suburban Connecticut. The
change had unsettled something in her that she could no longer manage alone.

The trauma that shaped Margaret's life occurred when she was eight years old. Her father, a man she
had been taught to obey without question, had beaten her severely after she spilled juice on his
newspaper. What Margaret remembered most vividly—what played in her mind like a film she could not
stop watching—was the moment his hand came toward her, the sudden movement of his arm that made the
room seem to tilt. She remembered his face, the sound of his breathing, the texture of the carpet
against her cheek. These details arrived unbidden and in perfect clarity, as though she were
standing in that room again rather than sitting safely at age seventy-three. Yet when she tried to
speak about it in her own words, the language scattered. She knew what had happened, knew it in her
bones, but the words themselves seemed to belong to someone else's story.

In the years that followed, Margaret had constructed a life of careful management. She became a
quality assurance engineer—a profession that suited her temperament for order and control. At work,
she could examine systems, identify failures, predict where things might go wrong. The work was
methodical, and it kept her mind occupied. But over the decades, a pattern had calcified: she could
not bear to be alone with men. Not because she feared violence in any logical sense—her husband was
gentle, had never raised a hand to her—but because her body would not obey her mind's reassurances.
When her husband entered a room where she was sitting, her shoulders would tense. If he moved too
quickly, reaching for something on a shelf, her breath would catch and her muscles would coil as
though preparing to flee. The hypervigilance was constant, exhausting. She found herself monitoring
his every gesture, waiting for the shift in tone or posture that might signal danger. She knew,
intellectually, that this made no sense. But knowing and feeling were two different things.

Funerals had become impossible. She had skipped her own brother's funeral five years ago, unable to
face the combination of grief and the presence of male relatives in a confined space. The
uncertainty of it—not knowing who would be there, what might happen, whether she could control her
response—was unbearable. She had told her family she was ill. It was easier than explaining.

What troubled Margaret most, what kept her awake at night, was a guilt that had never softened. She
had been a child, powerless, but she had come to believe that she should have done something. Should
have fought harder, run faster, found some way to prevent what happened. That she had not done these
things—that a child could not have done them—seemed irrelevant to the part of her that whispered, in
the dark hours, that she had failed herself. She had also come to understand, much later, that her
father had been someone she was supposed to trust. That betrayal, the fundamental wrongness of it,
had never been fully metabolized. It lived in her as a kind of permanent uncertainty about safety
itself.

Now, with her husband home every day, the walls of her carefully constructed routine were
collapsing. She could not hide in the predictability of work. The uncertainty of unstructured time,
the constant presence of a man in her home, the awareness that aging meant she was running out of
time to make sense of any of this—all of it had finally pushed her to seek help. What she wanted,
though she was almost afraid to say it aloud, was to feel safe in her own home. To look at her
husband without her body bracing for impact. To attend a funeral and grieve without her mind
fracturing into fragments of a seventy-year-old memory.
