# Thida's Vigilance

Thida was sixty-eight when it happened, a Tuesday evening in late autumn when she stayed late at the
office to finish a brief. The law firm where she had worked for thirty-two years occupied the
fourteenth floor of a downtown tower; most colleagues had left by seven. She was alone on the floor
except for security, or so she believed. A man she did not recognize—later identified as someone who
had tailgated through a service entrance—found her in her office. What followed was a violent
assault that lasted perhaps fifteen minutes but felt, Thida said during intake, like hours
compressed into a single, unbearable moment. He had grabbed her from behind, his hand covering her
mouth, his voice in her ear making demands and threats. When she tried to resist, he struck her. The
physical injuries—bruising, a fractured rib, a laceration on her cheek—healed within weeks. The rest
did not.

Three years had passed since that night. Thida was now seventy-one and had returned to work, but the
woman who sat in the intake session was not the person her colleagues remembered. She had come to
believe that her body could no longer be trusted—that it would betray her at any moment. Her heart
would race unexpectedly, without warning, and when it did, she interpreted the sensation as proof
that danger was near. She had learned to monitor her own pulse constantly, checking her wrist
throughout the day, convinced that the rhythm of her heartbeat was a reliable gauge of threat. When
her heart raced, she was certain something bad was about to happen.

Sounds had become unbearable. The assault had been silent until it wasn't—the man's breathing, his
voice, the scrape of furniture as he forced her backward. Now, any sudden noise triggered an
immediate flood of fear. The sound of a door closing, raised voices in the hallway, even the rumble
of traffic outside her window could send her into a state of acute alarm. She had stopped attending
firm events, stopped going to lunch with colleagues, stopped taking the elevator alone. The office
itself, once a place of professional pride, had become a minefield of acoustic hazards.

What frightened her most, though, was being looked at. During the assault, the man had positioned
her in front of her office mirror. She could still see his face reflected behind hers, could still
feel the weight of his gaze. Now, when male colleagues looked at her—even in ordinary professional
contexts, a glance across a conference table, a moment of eye contact in the hallway—she felt
exposed and vulnerable in a way that made her skin crawl. She had begun wearing oversized blazers,
had stopped attending in-person meetings whenever possible, had started taking cases that required
only written submissions. Her partners had noticed. One had gently suggested she consider part-time
work or retirement.

Night was the worst. Thida found it impossible to feel safe after dark. She had installed new locks,
motion-sensor lights, a security system. Her husband, Sophea, slept lightly beside her, aware that
she rarely slept more than two or three hours. She lay in bed, listening for sounds, monitoring her
heartbeat, waiting for the moment when something would go wrong. When Sophea was delayed coming home
from an errand—stuck in traffic, running late from a meeting—Thida's mind would spiral. A delay of
even twenty minutes was proof, she was certain, that something terrible had happened to him. She
would call repeatedly, her anxiety climbing with each unanswered ring, until he arrived and she
could see that he was safe.

Thida had come to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous and that she could never fully
protect herself. She had to be on guard constantly, had to anticipate threats, had to assume the
worst. This hypervigilance exhausted her. She was tired in a way that sleep could not fix. When
Sophea finally convinced her to call a therapist, she came with low expectations. What she hoped
for, though she barely dared admit it, was to feel safe in her own body again, to sit in a room
without scanning for exits, to trust that the next moment would not bring catastrophe.
