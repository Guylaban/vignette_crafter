# Isabela

Isabela was twenty-one when she first came to her therapist's office, moving through the waiting
room with the careful precision of someone who had learned to take up as little space as possible.
She worked as an animator at a mid-sized studio in the city, a job she had pursued because it let
her disappear into worlds she could control—worlds where nothing moved unless she told it to. She
had been working there for fourteen months when things began to fall apart.

The trauma itself was not new. Isabela had grown up watching her father throw things at her
mother—not in the abstract way people sometimes describe childhood violence, but in the specific,
sensory way that lodges itself in the nervous system. She remembered the sound of his voice rising,
the particular timbre of her mother's silence that meant she was bracing. She remembered the lamp
that flew across the kitchen, the way her mother's hands moved to protect her face, the smell of her
father's cologne mixing with the acrid scent of his anger. These memories had always been there, but
they had lived at a distance for years—until they didn't.

The first real crisis came during a project meeting at work. A colleague, frustrated with a
revision, had thrown a pen across the table. It was a small gesture, the kind of thing that might
register as a mild annoyance in most people's minds. For Isabela, it reopened something. Her body
had responded before her mind could catch up—her heart racing, her hands shaking, a surge of rage
that frightened her with its intensity. She had excused herself, locked herself in a bathroom stall,
and sat there breathing in the chemical smell of the industrial soap dispenser, trying to remember
where she was. After that day, she began to notice that thrown objects haunted her—the simple act of
someone tossing something across a room became a threat she could not shake.

The photographs were worse in a different way. Her mother had recently sent her old family photos,
trying to rebuild connection. When Isabela opened them, her father's face stared back at her from
images of holidays and birthdays, his expression neutral in those moments before anger. She had come
to believe that she could not be safe around anything that reminded her of him—not his image, not
the sound of his voice on old voicemails, not even the particular cologne scent that occasionally
drifted past her on the street. These triggers had begun to multiply in her mind until the world
felt threaded with danger.

The memories themselves had taken on a terrifying quality. When she thought about what she had
witnessed, the sensations flooded back with a vividness that made it hard to distinguish past from
present. She could smell his cologne, hear the exact pitch of breaking glass, feel the cold tile of
the kitchen floor beneath her. And yet, paradoxically, she found herself unable to fully hold the
memories in her mind. They would surge up, overwhelming, and then she would push them away,
fragmentary and incomplete, leaving her in a state of constant vigilance against their return.

Over the following months, Isabela had developed patterns to manage the chaos. She had stopped
making plans—anything involving the future felt impossible, as though she had no ability to shape
what would happen to her. She had become strict with her eating, the only domain where she could
exercise absolute control. She avoided anything that might trigger memories of her father. At work,
she had become increasingly irritable, snapping at colleagues over minor issues, her anger
disproportionate to the moment. She found herself hyperalert in meetings, her body tensing at sudden
movements, her mind constantly scanning for threat.

What had finally pushed her to seek help was a moment of clarity that terrified her more than the
symptoms themselves. She had realized that she was living as though the violence was still
happening, as though she had no power to change anything, and that this sense of helplessness was
spreading into every corner of her life. She was twenty-one years old, and she had already begun to
believe that she could not cope with even the smallest upset, that the world was fundamentally
unsafe, and that she was powerless to stop it. That belief was the thing that had finally made her
pick up the phone.
