# Marcus

Marcus was sixty-six years old when he first described the cold to his wife, though he had been
running from it for fifty-eight years. The cold itself was not the trauma—it was the herald. When
his father's hands came down on him, they came with a particular temperature, a specific shock that
had nothing to do with season or weather. His father had kept his hands in ice water in the garage.
Marcus remembered the sound of the lid of the cooler closing, the methodical way his father would
walk back inside the house, the way his mother would find an errand elsewhere. The beatings happened
in the basement, where the concrete stayed cold year-round, where Marcus learned that cold on skin
meant pain was coming. He was eight years old the first time, and he was still eight years old every
time he felt that temperature again.

In his sixties now, Marcus worked remotely as a senior software engineer for a financial services
firm. His home office was climate-controlled to seventy-two degrees precisely. He had not set foot
in an office building in three years. When the air conditioning kicked in—a sudden shift, a draft
across his arm or neck—his heart would begin to race before his conscious mind had even registered
the temperature change. His body knew what his mind was trying to forget. The pounding in his chest
would come first, a wild percussion that made him certain something terrible was about to happen,
and only then would the thought arrive: *He's coming. He's getting his hands wet. He's coming down
the stairs.* The memory would surface whole and unbidden, not as narrative but as sensation—the
cold, the helplessness, the smell of motor oil and concrete dust.

These moments had begun to structure his life in ways he no longer questioned. He avoided looking at
his own body, had done so for years. In the shower, he kept his eyes fixed on the tile wall or
closed them entirely. He did not stand shirtless. The sight of his own skin seemed to trigger the
same cascade of alarm that the temperature did, as if his body itself were a threat he needed to
monitor and contain. His wife, Patricia, had learned not to touch him without warning. When she
reached for his hand or his shoulder, she would announce it first, speak his name, give him time to
brace himself. Even so, unexpected contact—a brush against him in the kitchen, her hand on his back
as she passed—sent a jolt of alarm through him so acute it left him breathless. His startle response
had become legendary in their marriage, a thing they managed around rather than addressed.

The social isolation had accumulated gradually. Friends from their neighborhood, couples they had
known for decades, had begun to drift away. Marcus could not bear to see them. The people who had
known him before—before this, before the cold, before his body had become a thing that betrayed
him—they represented a kind of witness he could not tolerate. To be in their presence was to feel
permanently marked, set apart, as though they could see the damage written across him. He had come
to believe that people could not be trusted with this knowledge, that his only safety lay in
distance. He had stopped accepting dinner invitations. He made excuses. Patricia had stopped making
them.

What brought him finally to seek help was a panic attack in his own home, one so severe that
Patricia had called an ambulance. The paramedics had touched him without warning—hands on his chest,
on his arm—and he had fought them with a strength that shocked them both. Afterward, sitting in the
emergency room, cleared of any cardiac event, Marcus had felt something shift. He was sixty-six
years old. He had spent nearly six decades running from a man who had died when Marcus was thirty-
two. The cold was still coming. His heart was still racing. And he was still, always, waiting for
the basement door to open.
