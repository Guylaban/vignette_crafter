# Rajesh's Vigil

Rajesh was seventy-five when he finally agreed to see a therapist, though his wife Priya had been
asking for two years. He had come to America in 1972 as a young man, carrying little more than the
skills his grandfather had taught him—how to center clay, how to feel the wall thin beneath his
thumbs, how to coax water and earth into vessels that held things. For fifty years he had made pots
in his small studio in Queens, and the work had sustained him through everything: raising two
children, the death of his parents, the slow forgetting that comes with age. But in the past three
years, something had shifted. The studio felt unsafe now. His hands, which had always known what to
do, had begun to shake.

It had started with a phone call in the spring of 2021. His cousin Arun had been killed in a car
accident in Dhaka—struck by a taxi while crossing a street. The funeral was held in absentia; Rajesh
could not travel. When the news came, something had cracked open in him, and with it came a flood of
images he had spent five decades learning not to see.

He had been sixteen when the partition came. He lived in what became East Pakistan, in a
neighborhood where Hindu and Muslim families had lived side by side for generations. In March of
1971, soldiers arrived. He remembered the sound first—not gunfire, but the grinding of truck
engines, the metallic slam of doors, voices shouting in a language that was his own but had become
the language of threat. He had hidden in his mother's storage room while soldiers moved through the
house. They had taken his older brother. Rajesh had heard the sounds—the rough handling, his
brother's voice calling out—but could not see. The not-seeing had somehow made it worse. For weeks
afterward, he could not assemble what had happened into a coherent narrative. The images came in
fragments: a boot, a shadow across the doorway, his mother's hand gripping his shoulder so hard it
left bruises. He had never been able to speak about it in a way that made sense, even to himself.

He had left Pakistan in 1972, and America had given him clay and silence. For decades, he had not
thought about it. Then Arun died in a car accident, and suddenly the sound of engines—the particular
grinding roar of heavy vehicles—became unbearable. When a police car passed his studio with its
siren on, his heart would race so violently he thought he might collapse. Funerals were impossible
now. His daughter had married last year, and he had sat in the back pew unable to fully breathe,
convinced that something terrible was about to happen.

Rajesh had begun to change his life in ways that made no sense even to him. He stopped attending
community events. When his grandson was born, he had felt a surge of joy so intense and so
immediately followed by dread—what if something happened to the boy, what if he could not protect
him—that he had made excuses not to visit. His wife had wept. He knew he was wrong to withdraw from
his own grandchild, but the alternative felt impossible: to hope was to invite catastrophe.

At the studio, he found himself scanning the street constantly, watching for police cars, listening
for the sound of heavy engines. His concentration shattered. A pot that should have taken an hour
now took three, because his hands would not obey. He had come to believe that he was no longer
capable of the one thing that had made him himself. At night, he lay beside Priya, who had loved him
for fifty-three years, and he could not tell her that he was terrified. Not of dying—he was old,
death was reasonable—but of the feeling that at any moment, without warning, the world could
splinter again.

When Priya finally insisted, he came to the appointment. He sat in the chair across from the
therapist and said very little. But when asked about the worst moment, he closed his eyes and spoke
of a sound—not words, but the memory of a sound—and his hands began to shake again, as if his body
remembered what his mind had never quite assembled into language.
