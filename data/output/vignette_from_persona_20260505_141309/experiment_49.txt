# Lani's Vigil

Lani Kauanui was fifty-eight when the nightclub fire took everything from him except his life. He
had been there that Saturday evening in 2018 with his oldest friend Marcus, a man he'd known since
their university days in Auckland. The club in downtown Portland was crowded, the music loud, and
Lani remembered being happy—genuinely happy in a way that felt rare those days, with the publishing
house demanding more of him each year and his marriage to Catherine growing quieter. Marcus had
insisted they go out, that Lani needed to remember what fun felt like. They'd been drinking, and
Lani could still smell it, that particular mixture of spilled beer and whiskey that had soaked into
the wooden floors and his own clothes. Then came the smell that would never leave him: burning
plastic, burning flesh, the acrid choke of smoke that filled his lungs in seconds.

The exit was blocked. The heat was absolute. Lani had pushed through the crowd with Marcus behind
him, but in the chaos their hands separated. Lani made it out through a kitchen window, dropping
eight feet to an alley. He spent the next three minutes screaming Marcus's name while firefighters
rushed past him. Marcus did not come out. Three hundred and forty-two people died that night. Marcus
was one of them.

For the first few weeks, Lani functioned on autopilot. He went to the memorial service, held
Catherine while she wept for a man she'd known her whole adult life, answered the police and the
journalists. But something fundamental had shifted in how he occupied the world. Within a month, he
had stopped seeing friends. Invitations came—people wanted to support him, to sit with him in his
grief—but the thought of being in a room with others, of being trapped in a conversation about
Marcus or the fire or anything at all, made his chest tighten with a panic he couldn't name. He told
Catherine he needed to be alone, and she, exhausted by her own grief, stopped pushing. By the end of
the second month, his phone had stopped ringing.

At work, Lani began to rearrange his office. The desk, which had faced the door, now faced the wall.
He moved a chair so that when he sat, his back was against the solid oak paneling behind him. From
that position, he could see anyone who entered. He could see the whole room. His assistant, noticing
the changes, said nothing. What could she say? Her boss had lost a best friend in a tragedy. But as
weeks turned to months, Lani's vigilance intensified. He found himself constantly scanning the
office, his eyes moving from the door to the windows to the hallway visible through the glass
partition. At home, he did the same thing—repositioning furniture, sitting only in chairs where his
back was protected, his view unobstructed. Catherine would find him at two in the morning, sitting
in the darkened living room, watching the front door.

The nightmares began almost immediately after the fire, but they had not stopped. Every few nights,
Lani would jolt awake, gasping, the images seared behind his eyes in vivid, unwavering clarity. He
could see the orange light reflecting off the bar's mirror. He could see faces contorted in panic,
though he knew he hadn't actually seen them—his mind had filled in details his conscious self had
missed. He could see Marcus turning back, could see the moment of separation, could replay it in
perfect, terrible detail. Waking from these dreams was its own trauma. That moment of consciousness,
when he realized where he was and that Marcus was still dead, happened over and over again, every
single night.

Ordinary sounds had become weapons. A car backfiring sent him rigid in his chair. The kettle's
whistle made him flinch. Catherine had learned to announce herself before entering rooms. Even
inside his own home—the place that should be safest—Lani felt perpetually on guard, as if danger
were not just possible but imminent. The smell of alcohol, which he encountered almost daily in his
professional world, would stop him cold. A colleague's after-work drink, a client's whiskey on their
breath during a meeting, and suddenly Lani was back in that club, suffocating, alone.

Five years have passed. Lani is now sixty-three, and his marriage has become a careful choreography
of avoidance and accommodation. Catherine loves him but has learned not to invite friends over. The
publishing house, sensing his withdrawal, has quietly shifted major projects to other editors. His
reputation, once stellar, has become that of a competent but distant figure, someone who no longer
attends industry events or takes on high-profile acquisitions. He sits in his office with his back
to the wall, scanning, waiting, remembering. It was Catherine who finally made the appointment,
telling him quietly that she couldn't lose him too, that whatever was happening inside him needed to
be addressed by someone who understood. Lani came because he is terrified—not just of the memories
or the sounds or the smells, but of the possibility that this is who he has become: a man imprisoned
by a single night, a man who has already lost so much and continues to lose more with each day he
spends guarding against a threat that has already passed.
