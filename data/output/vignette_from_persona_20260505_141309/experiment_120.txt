# Marcus Webb: A Life Interrupted

Marcus Webb was fifty-four when the delivery truck ran the red light. He had been driving home from
a client meeting in Portland on a clear afternoon in October, his mind already turning to the
weekend, when the massive vehicle broadsided his sedan at an intersection. The impact came from his
left side with a force that seemed to unmake the ordinary world. Glass exploded inward. His body was
thrown across the passenger seat, his left shoulder and ribs absorbing the crush of twisted metal.
For a moment that felt infinite, he hung suspended in shock—the smell of coolant and burnt rubber,
the sound of his own ragged breathing, the terrible wrongness of the steering wheel now pressing
against his chest. When the ambulance arrived, his left arm would not obey him. The emergency room
physician told him he had been fortunate. A fractured clavicle, three broken ribs, a concussion.
Nothing that would not heal. But something had fractured in Marcus that the X-rays could not show.

In the weeks that followed, he returned to his condo in Seattle and tried to resume his life. He was
a man accustomed to control—he had built a reputation over three decades for transforming spaces,
for understanding how light and proportion and texture could reshape a room and, by extension, the
people who inhabited it. But his body had become a stranger to him. The left side ached constantly,
a dull throb that woke him at night. His grip was weak. When he reached for a coffee cup, his hand
sometimes trembled, as if his own nervous system had decided to betray him without warning. He had
come to believe, with a certainty that grew each day, that he could no longer trust his own body. It
had failed him once catastrophically; it might do so again. He found himself checking his reflexes
obsessively, testing his balance, monitoring his heartbeat with an attention that bordered on panic.

Three months after the accident, Marcus attempted to return to work. His firm had been patient, but
a major residential project in Ballard was waiting. On his first day back, he was driving to the
client's home when a sedan ahead of him braked suddenly. Marcus's own foot found the pedal
instantly, but in that microsecond of compression, his body flooded with terror. His vision
narrowed. His breathing became shallow. He pulled over and sat trembling on a side street for twenty
minutes, unable to continue. That night, he cancelled the meeting and did not return to the office.
The thought of driving to client sites—of being behind the wheel, vulnerable and exposed—had become
intolerable. He began to arrange consultations by video call. His clients noticed the change. Two of
them, uncomfortable with the distance, moved their projects to other designers.

The triggers were everywhere now. A car commercial on television—the screech of tires, the impact
sound effect—would send his heart racing. A conversation at the grocery store about a friend's
fender-bender would leave him nauseated for hours. Worst of all were the moments when he heard a car
horn or the sudden acceleration of an engine nearby. His entire body would contract, bracing for the
blow that never came. He began taking routes on back streets to avoid heavy traffic. He stopped
accepting social invitations that required him to be a passenger. His ex-wife, with whom he had
maintained a cordial relationship, invited him to their daughter's engagement party; he declined,
offering a vague excuse. The truth was that the party was in Portland. The drive terrified him.

By the time Marcus called a therapist's office in late summer, nearly nine months had passed. He was
sitting in his design studio—a space he had once loved, filled with fabric swatches and paint
samples and the ordered beauty of his profession—and he realized he had not taken on a new project
in five months. His savings were holding, but the deeper wound was something he could barely
articulate. He felt as though the man he had been—confident, capable, the person who could envision
and create—had died in that intersection. Someone else was occupying his skin now, someone
diminished and fearful and fundamentally compromised. When the therapist asked what he hoped to get
from treatment, Marcus was quiet for a long moment. "I want to know if I can ever be myself again,"
he said finally. "Or if that person is just gone."
