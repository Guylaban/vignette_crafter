# Clinical Vignette: Mariana

Mariana was twenty-two when her six-year-old brother, Diego, stopped speaking during a family
dinner. He had complained of a sore throat that morning—nothing alarming, the kind of thing children
complained about constantly. By evening, his breathing had become labored, a wet, struggling sound
that filled the kitchen. Her mother rushed him to the hospital while Mariana stayed with their
father, who paced the living room in a state of suspended panic that she had never witnessed in him
before. The silence in that house was absolute and terrible. Hours passed. No phone call. No update.
The not-knowing became its own kind of suffocation. When her mother finally called at midnight, her
voice was fractured: epiglottitis, severe, Diego was in the ICU, intubated, they didn't know if he
would make it through the night.

Mariana spent that night in the hospital waiting room, watching her mother's hands shake as she held
a cold cup of coffee. She remembered the smell of the ICU hallway—antiseptic, with something
underneath it that she could never quite name. She remembered the taste in her own mouth, a
metallic, acidic taste that came from fear and exhaustion and the vending machine coffee she kept
drinking without tasting. When they were finally allowed to see Diego, he was unconscious, his small
chest rising and falling with the rhythm of the ventilator. Mariana had never felt so powerless. She
was nineteen years old and there was nothing she could do. Nothing anyone could do except wait.

Diego survived. After eight days, the breathing tube came out. He recovered completely, was
discharged after two weeks, and within a month was running around the house as though nothing had
happened. But something had happened to Mariana, and it had not resolved.

In the months that followed, she became hypervigilant to silence. When her family gathered and
conversation lulled, her heart would begin to race. She had come to believe that silence meant
danger was coming, that the absence of sound was a warning sign she needed to heed. If her mother
was late picking her up from work, Mariana would assume something catastrophic had occurred—a car
accident, a sudden illness. She would check her phone compulsively, her stomach tight, certain that
any delay meant disaster was unfolding somewhere beyond her control.

When memories of that hospital night returned—and they did, unbidden, sometimes triggered by nothing
she could identify—they came with physical intensity. Her heart would hammer. Her breathing would
shallow. And she would taste it again: that metallic, frightening taste, so vivid it seemed to coat
her tongue, pulling her back into the helplessness of waiting.

She found herself unable to talk about what had happened. When friends asked how her brother was
doing now, or when her supervisor at the accounting firm suggested she take a mental health day,
Mariana would freeze. The words wouldn't come. It was as though the memory existed in her body but
not in language—a feeling without a narrative, a knowing without words.

By her twenty-second year, isolation had become her baseline. She no longer attended the Friday
evening gatherings with her college friends. She declined her cousin's wedding invitation. At work,
she had begun eating lunch alone in her car rather than in the break room, where the unexpected
silence of others' focused work would trigger that same dread. Her colleagues had stopped inviting
her to after-work drinks. She told herself it was better this way, that she was protecting herself,
but the truth underneath was darker: she had come to believe that happiness was something she could
no longer access, that the person she used to be—the one who laughed easily, who made plans, who
felt safe—had been left behind in that hospital waiting room. Worse than the fear was the certainty
that she could not stop bad things from happening. Diego had nearly died despite everyone's love,
despite hospitals and doctors. What chance did she have against a world that arbitrary, that
dangerous?

It was her father who finally insisted she see someone. He had noticed her weight loss, her
withdrawal, the way she flinched at unexpected sounds. Mariana came to the first appointment
reluctant, convinced that talking would not help, that nothing could undo what she now knew about
how fragile life was. But she came.
