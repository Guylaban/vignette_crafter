# Layla's Vigil

Layla Mansouri was seventy-seven years old when she came to her first appointment, her posture
careful and upright, her hands folded in her lap. She had been living in the United States for
thirty-two years, but the precision of her English still carried the cadence of someone who had
learned it as an adult, each word placed with deliberation. She had come to the appointment alone,
as she came to most things now. Her niece had suggested it, gently, after noticing that Layla had
stopped accepting dinner invitations and had declined to tutor the two youngest children in her
extended family's circle—a decision that had surprised everyone who knew how much she had always
loved that work.

The traumatic event had occurred in 1991, during the Gulf War, in a suburb of Baghdad where Layla
had lived with her husband and two adult sons. She had been forty-five years old. The neighborhood
had been densely packed with families, the streets narrow, the houses built close together. On the
morning of the bombing, Layla had been in her kitchen preparing breakfast. The first impact came
without warning—a sound so enormous it seemed to crack the world open. The building shook violently;
plaster fell from the ceiling in thick chunks. She remembered the taste of dust, the way her ears
rang as if filled with a high, endless scream. Her younger son had been in the bedroom. When she
found him, he was alive but bleeding from a wound on his forehead. The older son had been outside.
It took her three hours to find him, walking through streets that no longer looked like streets,
past rubble and smoke and the bodies of neighbors she had known for decades. He was dead. Her
husband had died in the initial blast, trapped in the house as it collapsed. She and her younger son
had fled to her sister's house in the countryside, and eventually, after years of displacement and
grief, they had made their way to America. Her son had built a life here—he was married now, lived
two states away—but Layla had never quite arrived.

For the past thirty-two years, she had moved through her days with a kind of internal disconnection
that she had come to accept as simply how life felt now. She described it as feeling dead inside, as
though something essential had been burned away in that moment when the world exploded. She still
tutored, still showed up to her students' homes, still corrected their grammar and helped them with
their essays. But she did it from a distance, as if watching someone else perform the role. She no
longer felt the pleasure she once had in a student's breakthrough moment. Food tasted like nothing.
She had stopped attending the community center where she used to meet with other women from the
Middle East. She had stopped going to the cinema. She avoided the news entirely, never watching
television programs or reading articles about conflicts in the Middle East or anywhere else—the risk
that she might encounter something that would pull her back into that day was too great.

What frightened her most was the thought that if she allowed herself to think deeply about what had
happened, if she really sat with the memory, she would not be able to handle it. The terror would
consume her entirely. She was certain of this. And so she had built her life around the edges of
that fear, a series of small avoidances that had accumulated into a kind of invisible architecture.
She stayed near exits in every room she entered—in her students' homes, in her apartment, in shops.
She had never articulated it as a conscious practice, but her body had learned it, had made it
automatic. When she sat down to tutor, she always positioned herself with a clear line to the door.
When she felt anxious, which was often, she would think of that proximity and feel marginally safer.

But three weeks ago, while tutoring a girl named Maya in her family's living room, a car had
backfired loudly outside. The sound had been sudden and sharp. In that instant, the memory had
returned with its original terror intact—not as a thought but as a full sensory experience, as if
the bombing were happening right then in that room. Her hands had shaken. She had forgotten where
she was. When she came back to herself, she realized she had stood up abruptly and moved toward the
door without explanation. Maya's mother had asked if she was all right. Layla had said she was fine,
but she had not returned to that house. She had made an excuse about her health.

It was this incident that had prompted her niece's suggestion, and Layla had finally agreed. She sat
now in the therapist's office, her hands still folded, and said quietly that she did not know if she
was strong enough to talk about what had happened, or if talking about it would only prove what she
already believed—that she was weak, that she had always been weak, that a stronger person would have
found her older son in time, would have saved him, would have done something other than survive.
