# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was seventy-one years old when he first sat down in the therapist's office, his posture stiff
and his eyes scanning the room as though cataloguing exits. He had spent the previous fifty-eight
years trying to outrun a childhood that would not stay behind him. The trauma had begun when he was
a boy of nine, living with his uncle in a cramped apartment in Buffalo while his parents worked long
hours. His uncle drank—heavily, unpredictably—and on the afternoons when the whiskey came out,
Marcus learned to make himself small. The smell of alcohol became inseparable from the anticipation
of pain: the sour, yeasty reek that filled the kitchen before his uncle's hands became fists, before
the leather belt came off, before the world narrowed to the sound of his own crying and the
certainty that he was alone in it. No one had stopped it then. No one had made his uncle answer for
it. That knowledge had calcified inside him over decades.

Now, in his seventh decade, Marcus worked as an insurance agent in a mid-sized firm in Pittsburgh.
The job suited his temperament in some ways—the work was methodical, the interactions bounded by
scripts and forms—but it had become increasingly difficult to maintain. Every client meeting felt
like a minefield. When a stranger approached his desk, Marcus's chest would tighten. He found
himself studying their hands, their posture, the way they moved toward him. He was certain that
threat lived in the unpredictability of other people, that something terrible was always about to
happen. He had come to believe that only his vigilance stood between himself and catastrophe, and so
he remained hypervigilant, listening always for the sound of footsteps, for voices raised in anger,
for the particular timbre of someone losing control.

At home, Marcus had developed rituals. Before bed, he would perform a specific sequence of mental
checks—visualizing the locks on his doors, running through a mental inventory of potential dangers,
repeating certain phrases that he believed would keep harm at bay. If he skipped these rituals, he
felt exposed, as though he had left himself defenseless. He kept his mind occupied constantly,
filling every gap with work tasks or mental calculations, because the moment his attention wavered,
fragments of memory would surface unbidden: the smell of whiskey, the sound of a belt being pulled
through loops, the feeling of his small body pressed against a wall. These pieces arrived without
warning and disappeared just as suddenly, but while they lasted, they felt more real than the
present moment. He could smell the alcohol as vividly as if his uncle were standing beside him.

To manage the intrusions, Marcus had stopped watching television, had removed himself from social
gatherings where alcohol might be present, and had begun making excuses to avoid the small talk that
his job sometimes demanded. Three months ago, a new client—a woman named Patricia—had come in for a
policy review. She was pleasant, ordinary, asked straightforward questions. But as she leaned across
his desk to sign a document, Marcus had heard sirens passing outside the building. The sound
triggered a cascade: his body flooded with adrenaline, his hands shook, and he was certain,
absolutely certain, that something terrible was happening, that danger was imminent. Patricia had
looked at him with concern, and he had felt the familiar shame of his body betraying him, reacting
as though he were still nine years old and powerless. He had excused himself, and afterward,
Patricia had requested a different agent.

It was that incident that had finally moved Marcus to call a therapist. At seventy-one, he was tired
of the rituals that consumed his evenings, tired of the false alarms that left him exhausted, tired
of the way his own body had become an enemy—reacting to threats that existed only in the past. What
frightened him most was the thought that this was simply who he was now, that the boy who had been
betrayed and left without recourse had somehow become the man who could never be safe. But
underneath that fear was a fragile hope: that perhaps, after all these years, he might finally
understand the difference between then and now.
