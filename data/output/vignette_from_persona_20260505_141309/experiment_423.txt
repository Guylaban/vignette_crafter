# Margot's Vigilance

Margot was thirty-three when she came to her first appointment, a Tuesday afternoon in October, her
hands folded tightly in her lap. She had been referred by her primary care physician, who had noted
during a routine visit that Margot's blood pressure was elevated and that she seemed reluctant to
discuss why. She worked as a graphic designer for a mid-sized marketing firm in Portland, a job she
had held for eight years and once loved. Now, sitting across from the therapist, she described the
past eighteen months as a period during which her life had narrowed in ways she could not quite
articulate to anyone else.

The stalking had begun in April of the previous year, though Margot had not immediately recognized
it as such. A man she had briefly dated two years earlier—someone she had met through a friend and
spent perhaps six weeks with before ending things—had begun appearing in places where she was. First
at the coffee shop near her home, where she went most Saturday mornings. Then at the grocery store.
Then outside her office building. When she mentioned it to her husband, David, he suggested she was
being paranoid, that it was coincidence. But the man, whose name was Thomas, had made eye contact
with her each time. He had smiled. Once, he had followed her from the parking garage into the
building lobby, close enough that she could hear his breathing. When she confronted him, he claimed
he was just trying to talk to her, that she had hurt him by leaving, that she owed him a
conversation. The police had been called. Thomas had been asked to stay away. But nothing had come
of it. No charges. No restraining order that stuck. He had simply stopped appearing for a few weeks,
and then, gradually, had resumed. A bicycle parked outside their house one evening. A figure she
thought she recognized on the MAX train. A car that seemed to slow as it passed her on the street.

What had shifted in Margot over those months was not dramatic at first. She began planning her
routes home differently, avoiding the direct path past the park where she had once seen him. She
started taking the car instead of her bicycle, even for short trips, because on a bicycle she felt
exposed, unable to accelerate quickly enough if she needed to escape. At work, she had repositioned
her desk so that she faced the door and could see anyone approaching. She arrived at meetings early
so she could choose a seat near the exit. Her colleagues noticed she was quieter, more distracted.
Her creative output, which had always been her strength, had become less imaginative, more cautious.
In late August, when the firm was pitching for a new account, Margot had not submitted any concepts.
She told her manager she wasn't feeling well. The truth was more complicated: she could not
concentrate on anything that required her to imagine freely, to lose herself in work. She needed to
keep part of her attention always alert, always scanning.

At home, the hypervigilance had become its own presence. She would scan the street before leaving
the house, checking for unfamiliar cars or bicycles. She would sit in restaurants with her back to
the wall, her eyes on the entrance. Once, at a dinner party at a friend's house, she had felt her
heart rate spike when someone knocked on the door unexpectedly—a neighbor asking to borrow sugar.
She had excused herself to the bathroom and sat on the edge of the tub, breathing carefully, trying
to convince herself that she was safe. David had stopped inviting friends over. He had stopped
asking her to go out. The distance between them had grown not through argument but through her
constant, exhausting vigilance, the way she flinched at sudden sounds, the way she could not fully
relax even in their own home.

What had finally brought her to the therapist's office was a moment of clarity, terrifying in its
own way. She had been at a red light, and a cyclist had pulled up beside her car. For a fraction of
a second, before she could see his face clearly, she had been certain it was Thomas. Her hands had
gone numb. She had not been able to breathe. The light had changed, and she had driven through it
without fully registering that the cyclist was a woman, that there had been no danger at all. But
the fear had been real. And Margot had realized, in that moment, that her mind no longer trusted the
world to be safe, that every stranger carried the potential for harm, and that she could no longer
tell the difference between what was actually dangerous and what only felt that way.
