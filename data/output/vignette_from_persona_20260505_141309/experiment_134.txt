# Clinical Vignette: Leila

Leila was forty-two when the bomb detonated outside the café where she sat with her sister. The
explosion came without warning—a sound so total it seemed to unmake the world, followed by a chaos
of smoke and screaming and the weight of debris pinning her leg. She remembered the taste of ash,
the burning in her lungs, the helplessness of being trapped while sirens wailed closer. Her sister
pulled her free. An officer arrived, then another, their uniforms and authority filling the space
around her as she lay bleeding on the pavement. She survived. Her sister survived. But something in
Leila's sense of herself did not.

In the decade since, Leila had built a life in the United States. She became a project manager at a
consulting firm, married Hassan, a man who had known her before the bombing and loved her steadily
through the aftermath. She had learned to function, to show up to meetings, to manage spreadsheets
and timelines and the expectations of colleagues. But as she approached fifty, Leila had come to a
painful recognition: the woman she had been before—the one who laughed easily, who hosted dinners,
who felt at home in her own skin—had not returned. In her place was someone perpetually braced,
always waiting for the next rupture.

The triggers were specific and relentless. When a police car pulled into the parking lot of her
office building, Leila's chest would tighten. She would find herself unable to focus on her work,
watching the officer move through the lobby, her body flooded with the certainty that something
terrible was about to happen. Helicopters were worse. The sound of rotors passing overhead—whether a
news helicopter or a medical transport—sent her into a state of acute panic. She would find herself
standing in the stairwell of her office building, waiting for the sound to pass, her hands shaking,
her mind unable to distinguish between the distant aircraft and the moment of the explosion itself.
Any depiction of violence, whether in a news report or a film, would trigger an immediate and
overwhelming sense of dread. Hassan had learned not to watch television when she was home.

What tormented Leila most, though, was not the fear itself but what she believed the fear revealed
about her. She had come to believe that she was fundamentally damaged in a way that others could
perceive. When she walked into a conference room, she was certain that her colleagues could see the
trauma written across her face, that they could sense her fragility and judge her for it. This
conviction had quietly altered her professional life. She volunteered less for presentations,
declined social outings with her team, and found herself increasingly isolated within her own
workplace. A promotion opportunity had passed her by last year; her supervisor had suggested she
seemed "checked out" lately. She had not corrected him.

The identity loss was perhaps the cruelest aspect. When Leila looked in the mirror, she did not
recognize herself anymore. The woman staring back was hollow, diminished, a shadow of someone who
had once danced at weddings and sang while cooking. She used to be happy—genuinely, unreflectively
happy. Now happiness felt like a country she had been exiled from, a language she no longer spoke.
Hassan would sometimes tell her she was smiling, and she would feel the shock of discovering her
face had moved that way without her consent or awareness. The disconnection was profound.

It was Hassan who finally insisted she seek help. He had watched her withdraw further each month,
had seen the weight of the unprocessed trauma accumulating in her body and her choices. When a
helicopter passed overhead last week and Leila spent three hours unable to leave the bathroom,
Hassan made the call. Now, sitting in the therapist's office, Leila articulated what she most
feared: that this—this diminished, terrified, unrecognizable version of herself—was permanent. And
what she hoped for, though she barely dared to say it, was the possibility of becoming herself
again.
