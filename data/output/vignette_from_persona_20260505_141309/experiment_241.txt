# Captain Roberto Díaz

Roberto Díaz was fifty-five when he came to his first session, carrying the rigid posture of a
career naval officer and the careful speech of a man who had learned long ago not to let others see
him fracture. He had served in the Navy for thirty-two years, rising to captain, commanding respect
through competence and an almost mechanical steadiness that his crew had come to depend upon. What
they did not know—what his partner Maria had begun to suspect only in the last year—was that Roberto
was held together by a discipline so severe it bordered on dissociation, and that discipline was
finally failing.

In 2014, at forty-four, Roberto had taken a two-week leave to visit his sister in a city in northern
Mexico that he had not seen in a decade. She had married a man from there, settled into a quiet
neighborhood, begun a life he had always imagined as peaceful. He arrived on a Thursday. By Saturday
afternoon, the neighborhood had become a war zone. A cartel dispute erupted in the streets near his
sister's home, gunfire so sudden and so close that the windows rattled. Roberto's brother-in-law
rushed the family into the back room, away from the street. But his sister's nine-year-old daughter,
his niece Lucía, had been in the front garden when the shooting started. She had frozen there,
visible through the window, while bullets tore through the fence and the air around her.

Roberto had one moment to decide. He could stay with the family, keep them in the protected room. Or
he could run to Lucía, cross the exposed space between the house and where she stood, and bring her
inside. He stood. He moved toward the door. His brother-in-law grabbed his arm and pulled him back,
whispering urgently that it was suicide, that the shooting was still coming from multiple
directions, that Lucía was frozen but she was low, she might be safe. Roberto felt his own body go
rigid with conflict. The shooting continued. And then, as suddenly as it had begun, it stopped. The
silence was absolute. When they finally ventured out, Lucía was still standing in the garden,
untouched, but Roberto's hands were shaking so violently he could barely hold her.

He never spoke of those minutes to anyone. Not to his sister, not to Maria. But something had
crystallized in him that day—a certainty that he was a coward, that he had failed in the most
fundamental obligation: to protect. The fact that Lucía was unharmed did not matter. He had
hesitated. He had allowed fear to pin him in place. And that hesitation proved, to his mind, an
essential weakness that ran through his entire being. A real man would have moved without thinking.
A real man would have been willing to die to reach her.

For years afterward, Roberto functioned at work with his usual precision. He gave orders, managed
his crew, maintained the standards of the ship. But something had fractured in the privacy of his
mind. When memories of that Saturday afternoon surfaced—and they did, unpredictably, triggered by
the sound of a car backfiring, a child's cry in a grocery store, the smell of dust after the air
conditioning kicked on—they came with a sensory totality that erased the distance between then and
now. He could feel the humidity of that afternoon, smell the concrete dust, hear the specific pitch
of Lucía's small voice calling out, sense the terrible weight of his own paralysis. In those moments
he was not remembering; he was there, failing again.

At other times, Roberto could recount the events with a flatness that frightened him more. He would
describe to himself, in careful detail, the moment he chose not to move, and feel nothing—as if he
were narrating something that had happened to someone else entirely, a stranger whose weakness he
could observe with clinical detachment. This emotional absence was almost worse than the flooding
memories. It suggested to him that some part of him had already accepted that he was not the person
he had believed himself to be.

Over the past year, avoidance had become his primary coping mechanism, though he would not have
named it that. He began to find reasons not to go home to Maria immediately after work, spending
hours at the gym or reading in his office. He stopped attending family gatherings where children
might be present. He declined invitations to social events where he might encounter his sister's
family. Maria had noticed his distance, his sudden need to be alone, the way he flinched at
unexpected sounds. She had suggested therapy gently, more than once, and he had resisted—until three
weeks ago, when she had finally told him, with tears in her eyes, that she felt like she was losing
him, that she did not recognize the man she loved anymore, and that if he would not seek help for
himself, he needed to do it for their relationship.

Roberto came to his first appointment because he could not bear to lose Maria. What he feared most
was that no amount of help could undo what he now knew about himself—that his weakness was not a
temporary response to trauma but a permanent feature of his character. What he hoped for, though he
barely dared articulate it, was that somewhere in the wreckage of his self-image, there might be
something worth salvaging.
