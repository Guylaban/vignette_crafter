# Priya's Story

Priya was twenty-two years old when a man forced his way into her delivery van at a red light in
East Newark, demanding her phone and the cash she carried for customer change. He had a knife. She
remembered the exact timbre of his voice—a rasp, almost a whisper—telling her not to move, not to
scream. The light turned green. A car honked behind her. She sat frozen, her hands still on the
wheel, while he rifled through the console. When he left, slamming the door hard enough to rock the
van, Priya sat in that intersection for what felt like an hour but was probably three minutes,
unable to restart the engine, unable to call anyone. By the time she found her voice, it was to call
her dispatcher and say she was done for the day.

That was four months ago. Now, in her apartment in Jersey City, Priya was someone her coworkers no
longer recognized. She had called in sick so many times that her manager had begun asking pointed
questions. The truth was that the thought of sitting in that van—the same van, though the company
had cleaned it—made her chest tight. The thought of being trapped in traffic, unable to move, unable
to leave, was unbearable. She had tried twice in the first month after the mugging, gripping the
steering wheel so hard her hands shook, and both times she had pulled over and called for someone to
pick her up. The second time, a passenger in another car had honked at her, impatient, and the sound
had sent her into a panic so acute she thought she was having a heart attack. Now she simply told
her dispatcher she couldn't work. Her savings were depleting. Her parents, whom she lived near in
Piscataway, had begun to notice her absence from Sunday dinners.

What made it worse was the way her mind would not let her alone. Some nights she woke at three in
the morning, her body rigid, seized by the certainty that someone was in her apartment. She would
lie in the dark, listening, cataloging where the nearest exit was—the bedroom window faced the fire
escape, the front door was fourteen feet from her bed—before her rational mind could catch up and
tell her she was safe. But safe felt like a word that no longer applied to her. Even in her
apartment, even with the door locked and the chain engaged, she could not settle into her body. Her
mother had asked why she seemed so tense, why she was always looking around, and Priya had no answer
that made sense.

The mugging itself had begun to fragment in her mind in a way that terrified her. Sometimes she
would be standing in her kitchen and feel a surge of terror—raw, animal panic—with no image attached
to it, just the sensation of absolute powerlessness, the knowledge that someone else had control of
her body and her choices. Other times, a specific moment would flash: the man's hand reaching across
her, the leather of his jacket brushing her arm. She could hear his voice, that rasping whisper,
even when she was awake and alone. And underneath all of it was a voice that had become her constant
companion, telling her that she should have done something, should have fought back, should have
found a way to stop him. She had replayed the moment a thousand times, each time imagining a version
of herself who was braver, faster, less paralyzed. The version of herself who actually existed—the
one who sat frozen and let it happen—was someone she could not forgive.

She had started using pills her friend Karun had given her, benzodiazepines from his older brother's
prescription, and they helped, at least for a few hours. They muffled the noise in her head, the
constant vigilance, the way her body seemed to exist in a state of permanent readiness for threat.
And sometimes, late at night, she told herself that the mugging wasn't really that bad, that she was
overreacting, that plenty of people had worse things happen and moved on. But the lie never held. By
morning, the fear was back, heavier than before.

It was her mother who finally insisted she see someone. Priya had missed another Sunday dinner, and
her mother had shown up at her apartment unannounced, finding her daughter unwashed, hollow-eyed,
unable to articulate why she couldn't seem to leave the house or return to work. Now, sitting across
from a therapist for the first time, Priya was uncertain whether talking about it would make things
better or simply make the memories more real. What she wanted most was to feel like herself
again—capable, autonomous, unafraid. What she feared most was that the person she had been before
that red light was gone forever.
