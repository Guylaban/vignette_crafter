# Carmen's Story

Carmen Reyes was thirty-four years old when the IED detonated beneath her Humvee in Kandahar. She
had been a combat engineer for eight years by then—good at her work, methodical, respected by her
unit. The explosion came at 4:47 in the afternoon on a Tuesday. She knows the time because her watch
stopped. She remembers the sound, though it plays differently in her head now than it did
then—sometimes muffled, sometimes impossibly loud, sometimes accompanied by voices that weren't
actually there. She remembers heat. Intense, searing heat across her left side, her arm, her neck.
She remembers the smell of burning rubber and something else, something she has never been able to
name. She pulled two soldiers from the vehicle. One of them didn't make it to the hospital. The
official report said the blast was the fault of poor route reconnaissance, a failure in the unit's
procedures. No one was held accountable. Carmen was cleared for duty within six months.

That was twenty years ago. She is fifty-four now, and she has spent two decades managing a body and
mind that never quite returned from that Tuesday afternoon.

The triggers are specific and merciless. When her husband grills on the back patio in summer, the
smell of charcoal and the sight of orange flames rising from the grate send her into a state of
alert that lasts for hours afterward. She has learned to stay inside, to keep the blinds drawn, to
find reasons to be elsewhere when he cooks. The heat itself is treacherous—not just the visual
flames but the sensation of warmth on her skin, particularly on her left side where the burns were
worst. A heated car seat in winter, the sun through a window in the afternoon, even her husband's
body heat next to hers in bed can trigger a cascade of panic that leaves her drenched in sweat and
convinced something terrible is about to happen. Shouting from anywhere—the neighbor's teenage son,
a raised voice on television, even her own daughter calling from another room—sends her nervous
system into overdrive. She cannot distinguish between danger and ordinary life.

Carmen works in an office now, a desk job managing logistics for a construction company. It was a
compromise she made with herself years ago, a way to stay employed while staying away from active
duty. But the office itself has become a minefield. She sits at her desk with her back to the wall,
her eyes constantly scanning the room, monitoring the movements of her colleagues, tracking who is
near the exits. When the coffee maker hisses in the break room, her heart races. When a coworker
raises their voice during a conference call, she goes rigid. She has difficulty concentrating on the
spreadsheets in front of her because part of her attention is always elsewhere, always vigilant. Her
supervisor has noticed. There have been conversations about her performance, her "availability," her
apparent anxiety. She has not told anyone at work what happened.

Over the years, Carmen developed a system of avoidance that has gradually narrowed her world. She
avoids restaurants where the kitchen is open and visible. She avoids fireworks, barbecues, any
gathering where there might be noise or heat or flames. She avoids situations that might be
stressful—difficult conversations with her husband, social events, even some family gatherings. She
has numbed herself to manage it all, creating a distance between herself and her own feelings that
has become habitual. Her husband complains that she is distant, that she seems to have disappeared
even though she is physically present. Her daughter, now an adult, rarely visits because the tension
in the house is palpable, and Carmen's withdrawal feels like rejection.

What frightens Carmen most is the unpredictability of it all. A memory can surface without
warning—the smell of something burning on the stove, a car backfiring, the particular angle of light
through a window—and suddenly she is back in that Humvee, feeling the heat, hearing sounds that are
not real, unable to distinguish between then and now. She cannot tell a coherent story of what
happened, even to herself. The memories come in fragments: heat, sound, the weight of a body, the
smell. They do not form a narrative. They are pieces that have never assembled into something she
can understand or process.

She has come to believe there is something fundamentally wrong with her, that a person who was truly
strong would have moved past this by now. The unfairness of it gnaws at her—the fact that the
procedures were faulty, that no one was held responsible, that she has spent twenty years paying the
price for something that was not her fault. And beneath it all is the conviction that the world is
dangerous in ways that other people simply do not see, that her vigilance is not paranoia but
realism, that the threat is real even when nothing visible is happening. It was this combination—the
narrowing of her life, the distance from her husband, the sense that she was disappearing into
herself—that finally brought her to seek help. Carmen is hoping that someone can help her understand
what happened and why she cannot seem to move beyond it. She is afraid that she is broken beyond
repair.

happen. Shouting from anywhere—the neighbor's teenage son, a raised voice on television, even her
own daughter calling from another room—sends her nervous system into overdrive. She cannot
distinguish between danger and ordinary life. Even silence carries threat now; in quiet moments, she
finds herself waiting for the next sound, the next rupture, unable to relax into stillness without
her mind conjuring danger. The intrusive images come without warning—fragments of that afternoon
that insert themselves into her present moment with startling vividness. She might be sitting at her
kitchen table and suddenly see the interior of the Humvee as it was in those seconds after impact,
or feel the phantom sensation of heat on her skin so intensely that she has to check her own body to
confirm she is not actually burning. These moments leave her disoriented, uncertain whether minutes
or hours have passed. When the memories surface, they do not form a coherent narrative—they come in
pieces: the smell of burning rubber, the weight of a soldier's body across her lap, a sound she
cannot quite name, the sensation of heat that no amount of logic can convince her is not happening
right now. She cannot tell the story of what happened, even to herself, because the memory refuses
to assemble into something whole and understandable.

Carmen works in an office now, a desk job managing logistics for a construction company. It was a
compromise she made with herself years ago, a way to stay employed while staying away from active
duty. But the office itself has become a minefield. She sits at her desk with her back to the wall,
her eyes constantly scanning the room, monitoring the movements of her colleagues, tracking who is
near the exits. When the coffee maker hisses in the break room, her heart races. When a coworker
raises their voice during a conference call, she goes rigid. She has difficulty concentrating on the
spreadsheets in front of her because part of her attention is always elsewhere, always vigilant,
always searching for the next threat. Her supervisor has noticed. There have been conversations
about her performance, her "availability," her apparent anxiety. She has not told anyone at work
what happened. Over the years, Carmen developed a system of avoidance that has gradually narrowed
her world. She avoids restaurants where the kitchen is open and visible. She avoids fireworks,
barbecues, any gathering where there might be noise or heat or flames. She avoids situations that
might be stressful—difficult conversations with her husband, social events, even some family
gatherings. She has numbed herself to manage it all, creating a distance between herself and her own
feelings that has become habitual. Her husband complains that she is distant, that she seems to have
disappeared even though she is physically present. Her daughter, now an adult, rarely visits because
the tension in the house is palpable, and Carmen's withdrawal feels like rejection. The
relationships that once anchored her have frayed under the weight of twenty years of hypervigilance
and retreat.

What frightens Carmen most is the unpredictability of it all. A memory can surface without
warning—the smell of something burning on the stove, a car backfiring, the particular angle of light
through a window—and suddenly she is back in that Humvee, feeling the heat, hearing sounds that are
not real, unable to distinguish between then and now. She has come to believe there is something
fundamentally wrong with her, that a person who was truly strong would have moved past this by now.
The unfairness of it gnaws at her—the fact that the procedures were faulty, that no one was held
responsible, that she has spent twenty years paying the price for something that was not her fault.
And beneath it all is the conviction that the world is dangerous in ways that other people simply do
not see, that her vigilance is not paranoia but realism, that the threat is real even when nothing
visible is happening. It was this combination—the narrowing of her life, the distance from her
husband, the sense that she was disappearing into herself, and the growing awareness that her job
performance is slipping despite her efforts to hold on—that finally brought her to seek help at
fifty-four. Carmen is hoping that someone can help her understand what happened and why she cannot
seem to move beyond it. She is afraid that she is broken beyond repair, that the person she was
before that Tuesday afternoon in Kandahar is gone permanently, and that the cost of survival has
been too high.
