# James

James was sixty-seven when the pickup truck ran the red light and T-boned his sedan on a Tuesday
afternoon in November. He had been driving home from the VA clinic where he worked as a civilian
medic three days a week, a job that had kept him anchored since his wife, Patricia, died five years
earlier. The impact came from his left. He remembered the sound first—a compression of metal and
glass, a noise so complete it seemed to erase everything else. The truck driver had been drinking;
James smelled it immediately when the man stumbled out of his cab, a sharp reek of whiskey and beer
that cut through the smell of burning rubber and hot engine oil. James, who had spent forty years as
a combat medic in the Army, who had stabilized men with catastrophic wounds on three continents,
found himself unable to move. His left leg was trapped. When the paramedics arrived, they had to use
the Jaws of Life.

The physical injuries healed within months. The fracture in his tibia mended. The lacerations
closed. But something had fractured in James that no X-ray could show. By the time he was cleared to
return to work, he had stopped driving. At first it was just a caution—his left leg still ached, and
the thought of gripping a steering wheel made his chest tight. But as weeks passed, the caution
hardened into something else. He could not sit in the passenger seat without his hands beginning to
shake. The moment a car approached an intersection, his body would tense. He had come to believe
that he could not be trusted behind the wheel, that some failure of judgment or reflex on his part
had caused the collision, though the police report clearly stated he had the green light. The
thought made no logical sense to him, yet it persisted with the weight of absolute truth. He was a
weak person—not physically, but in some deeper way. A strong person would have seen it coming. A
strong person would have reacted differently.

He began taking the bus to the VA clinic, a forty-minute journey each way. His supervisor, Dr.
Reeves, asked him once if everything was all right. James said yes, but the question lodged in his
mind. He watched his own thoughts constantly now, monitoring them for signs that he was losing
coherence, that the trauma was fracturing his mind the way it had fractured his body. Whenever he
caught himself thinking about the crash, he would forcibly redirect his attention—to a patient's
chart, to the weather, to anything else. This worked for short stretches, but the thoughts always
returned, often accompanied by the sound of the impact, a deep metallic crunch that played in his
head as if a speaker were embedded in his skull.

The worst part was that he could not explain any of this to anyone. When Dr. Reeves asked again,
more directly, James opened his mouth to describe what had happened and found that the narrative
fragmented. He could see the truck approaching, could smell the alcohol, but when he tried to string
these moments into a coherent account, the thread snapped. The memory felt borrowed, as though he
were recounting something that had happened to another man, not to himself. This terrified him more
than the physical panic did. The fear that he was losing his grip on reality.

At home in the evenings, James would startle at ordinary sounds—the refrigerator humming, a car door
closing on the street outside, the neighbor's dog barking. His body would jolt, his heart would
race, and it would take minutes for him to settle. He had stopped going to the grocery store because
the smell of burnt meat at the deli counter triggered a cascade of physical reactions that left him
shaking in the parking lot. The smell of alcohol—anywhere, anytime—sent a spike of adrenaline
through his chest.

It was his daughter, calling from Portland, who finally said the words he needed to hear: that he
should talk to someone. That what he was experiencing was not weakness, not insanity, but something
that could be addressed. At sixty-seven, James had survived wars, had held dying men's hands, had
built a life after loss. Yet he could not cross the threshold of his own front door without fear. He
called the VA and asked for a referral to mental health. He was terrified of what the answer might
be, but he was more terrified of continuing as he was—trapped in a car that no longer moved, a man
who could not trust himself, a medic who could no longer heal.
