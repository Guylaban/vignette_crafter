# Vinh's Story

Vinh Nguyen is a seventy-two-year-old Vietnamese American man who has spent the last eight years
working as a nanny for a family in suburban Maryland. He is soft-spoken, meticulous in his care of
the children, and has earned the family's deep trust. He has been married to his wife, Linh, for
forty-three years. They live quietly together in a modest home, and Linh has noticed over the past
several months that her husband has become increasingly withdrawn, jumping at the slightest sound
and refusing to enter certain rooms after dark.

Forty-two years ago, Vinh was in a relationship with a man named Marcus. It lasted five years—years
that began with tenderness but gradually became suffused with violence. Marcus was unpredictable; he
would erupt into rage over small perceived slights. On the night that finally broke something
irreparable in Vinh, Marcus came home late, smelling of alcohol, and accused Vinh of infidelity.
When Vinh tried to explain, to reason, Marcus shoved him hard against the kitchen counter. Vinh
fell, his ribs striking the edge. Marcus stood over him in the dim light—Vinh remembers the way the
overhead fixture cast Marcus's shadow across his face—and beat him with a closed fist, again and
again, while Vinh lay on the linoleum floor, trying to protect his head with his arms, his body
curled into a shape that still sometimes returns without warning.

The beating lasted perhaps three minutes. It felt like hours. When it ended, Marcus left the
apartment. Vinh lay there in the dark kitchen—he had not turned on the lights—and felt something
fundamental shift in him. He left Marcus the next morning and never returned.

For decades, the memory seemed sealed away, compartmentalized in a way that allowed Vinh to build a
new life. He met Linh through mutual friends, married her, and together they created a stable, quiet
existence. But in the past year and a half, something has fractured. The memory has begun to
intrude, and when it does, it arrives with a quality that Vinh finds deeply strange and distressing.
He experiences it not as his own memory but as though he is witnessing something that happened to
someone else—a dissociative quality that frightens him more than the content itself. Yet when the
memory comes, his body remembers before his mind catches up. His arms instinctively curl across his
chest. His breathing becomes shallow. His ribs seem to ache with phantom pain. The terror that
floods him is indistinguishable from the terror of that night, as though his nervous system has no
sense of the forty-two years that have passed.

Vinh has developed a careful architecture of avoidance to manage this. He will not enter the kitchen
after dark. He has asked Linh to keep lights on throughout the house in the evenings. He avoids any
situation that might provoke stress—he has become almost preternaturally compliant, anticipating
conflict before it can arise, softening his voice, agreeing with others even when he disagrees. Most
importantly, he has made a silent vow never to allow anger to surface. Anger, he has come to
believe, is the thing that causes men to become like Marcus. If he can simply not feel it, not
express it, he will remain safe and will keep others safe from him.

But this suppression has taken a toll. At work, when one of the children he cares for misbehaves,
Vinh finds himself frozen, unable to set boundaries. The parents have begun to worry that he is too
passive. He startles violently at unexpected movements—the other day, when the youngest child ran
around a corner, Vinh reacted as though he had been struck, his heart racing, his breath catching.
He cannot relax. Even on quiet evenings at home with Linh, his body remains tense, alert, scanning
for danger that he knows rationally is not present but cannot convince himself will not appear.

Linh finally insisted that he see someone after she found him standing in the hallway at three in
the morning, unable to sleep, unable to explain why he was afraid. Vinh came to his first
appointment reluctant and ashamed—ashamed of the fear, ashamed of the memory that will not stay
buried, ashamed that after all these years he still cannot seem to simply move forward. What he
wants most is to feel safe in his own home again. What he fears most is that this terror is now
permanent, that he will spend his remaining years as a prisoner to a night that ended four decades
ago.
