# Clinical Vignette: Judge Margaret Chen

Margaret Chen was fifty-two when she first came to the clinic, though her bearing suggested someone
older—shoulders held rigid, eyes that scanned rooms before settling. She had been a federal judge
for eight years, but before that, she had spent two decades in humanitarian work. It was the middle
years, the ones she no longer spoke of, that had brought her here.

In the summer of 2003, Margaret had been a relief coordinator in a regional hospital in a conflict
zone in Southeast Asia. The work was essential and consuming. She managed medical supplies,
organized triage, and tried to maintain some semblance of order in a facility that was chronically
understaffed and overwhelmed. On the afternoon of August 14th, the hospital came under fire. A
mortar round struck the pediatric ward. Margaret was in the storage room when it happened—she heard
the explosion, then the screaming, then the sound of the building settling. She ran toward the ward
and spent the next six hours moving between children, some of whom could not be saved. The smell of
dust and blood mixed with the metallic taste of her own fear, a taste she had carried in her mouth
ever since.

The years that followed had been a slow reconstruction. Margaret left relief work, returned to the
United States, completed her law degree, and built a life of order and control. She married,
divorced, and devoted herself to her judicial work. For nearly two decades, she had functioned well.
But functioning, she had come to understand, was not the same as healing.

The first crack had appeared five years ago, on August 14th. Margaret had woken that morning with
her heart racing, though she could not say why. The date meant nothing to her consciously—she had
trained herself long ago not to mark anniversaries. Yet her body knew. That day, she had found
herself unable to sit still in chambers. She had cancelled her afternoon docket, claiming illness,
and spent the evening in her house with every light on, afraid of the dark in a way she could not
articulate to anyone.

The attacks had intensified since then. Each August brought a week of hypervigilance, insomnia, and
a return to the taste—metallic, invasive, impossible to wash away no matter how much water she
drank. But the anniversary was only one trigger. Military uniforms were another. Margaret had
learned to recognize the subtle tightening in her chest when she saw a sheriff's deputy or a
National Guard recruiter. She had begun to route her morning jog differently to avoid the police
station. At the courthouse, she had quietly arranged her schedule so that security detail briefings
happened on days when she could remain in her chambers.

What frightened her most was that she felt she was becoming transparent. In the courtroom, she was
certain that people could see what had happened to her written across her face. The other judges,
the lawyers, the court staff—they must know, she believed, that she was not who she appeared to be.
This conviction had grown so strong that she had begun to withdraw from collegial lunches and bar
association events. She told herself she was simply busy, but the truth was that she could not bear
the thought of being perceived, of being seen as the woman who had failed those children, who
carried their deaths in her mouth.

When memories surfaced—and they did, unbidden, often triggered by nothing she could identify—they
came with terrifying completeness. She would hear the sound of the explosion, smell the dust, taste
the metal, and feel the terror as though she were back in that moment, her hands moving uselessly
among the wounded. She had learned to suppress these memories when they began, to clamp down on them
mentally, to busy herself with work or exercise or anything that would keep the remembering at bay.
But the suppression was exhausting, and it was failing.

Margaret had come to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous, that safety was an
illusion, and that she was fundamentally alone in this knowledge. She had isolated herself so
thoroughly that her closest colleague at the courthouse had recently asked if she was unwell. That
question had frightened her more than anything else. It meant the mask was slipping. At fifty-two,
Margaret sat in her chambers during lunch, eating at her desk, and wondered if this was all her life
would be—a careful performance of normalcy, held together by vigilance and suppression, slowly
crumbling.
