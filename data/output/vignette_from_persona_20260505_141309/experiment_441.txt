Rajesh Patel was forty-one when his marriage nearly killed him. For six years, he had been married
to Daniel, a man he had met at a design conference in Chicago and whom he had believed, with the
certainty that shapes a life, understood him completely. Daniel was charming in public, attentive in
the early years, and had seemed to share Rajesh's vision of building something lasting together. But
over time, the attention had curdled into control, and the charm had given way to something that
Rajesh, in the quiet hours before dawn when he lay awake listening to Daniel's breathing, had come
to recognize as contempt.

The night that broke something in Rajesh came in early March, two years ago. Daniel had been
drinking—a habit that had intensified over the preceding months—and Rajesh had made the mistake of
suggesting, gently, that perhaps they should talk to someone, a therapist, about the distance
between them. Daniel had responded by asking Rajesh what kind of man he thought he was, to presume
to fix what wasn't broken. The conversation had escalated in the way these conversations do when one
person is looking for a reason to hurt. When Rajesh had tried to leave the room, Daniel had grabbed
him by the shirt collar and thrown him against the kitchen counter. Rajesh's ribs had struck the
edge with a force that stole his breath. What came after—the fists, the incomprehensible rage, the
smell of whiskey and sweat—had compressed into a few vivid, unbearable moments that his mind had
preserved with the clarity of photographs.

The physical injuries had healed within weeks. The other injuries had not. Rajesh had left Daniel
that night and had not returned, but he had carried the trauma with him into everything he touched
afterward.

At his architecture firm, where he had once been known for his meticulous attention and his ability
to hold complex spatial problems in his mind with ease, Rajesh had begun to unravel. His body had
developed a hair-trigger sensitivity to threat. A colleague's voice raised in conversation—even
incomprehensible chatter in a crowded hallway—would send his nervous system into a state of alarm.
His heart would accelerate before his conscious mind had even registered what he was hearing. The
smell of alcohol on a client's breath during a meeting would trigger an immediate, overwhelming
panic that he could not suppress or explain away. And when he felt physically unwell—a headache, a
bout of nausea, the onset of flu—his mind would flash back to the night in the kitchen, to the way
his body had felt out of control, and the terror would return with the same intensity it had held
two years ago.

His marriage to Daniel had been his most important role, or so he had believed. When it had ended in
violence, Rajesh had come to doubt his own judgment in fundamental ways. He could not trust himself
to read people anymore, could not trust that he would make the right decisions about whom to let
close. The betrayal had been so complete, so intimate, that it had infected his sense of who he was.
He had failed at the thing he had wanted most—a partnership built on mutual respect and love—and
that failure had become a kind of proof that something was broken in him.

Sleep had become nearly impossible. Rajesh would lie in bed, his body tense, waiting for danger that
he knew intellectually was not coming. He needed to stay alert, to monitor for threats, to ensure
that what had happened would never happen again. The hypervigilance had become his constant
companion, exhausting him through the long nights and leaving him depleted during the days.

It was his mother who had finally insisted that he seek help. She had noticed how thin he had
become, how the light had gone out of his eyes, how he had begun to withdraw from his extended
family. She had sat with him one afternoon and asked him to tell her what was really happening, and
when he had finally spoken the words aloud—that Daniel had beaten him, that he could not sleep, that
he felt fundamentally untrustworthy—she had taken his hand and said that he needed to talk to
someone who could help him understand that what had happened was not his fault, that his body's
reactions were not a sign of weakness, and that he might yet find a way to live without being
constantly afraid. Rajesh had finally called a therapist, carrying with him the fragile hope that
understanding why his mind and body had become his enemies might be the first step toward reclaiming
himself.
