# Aroha's Story

Aroha Te Kanawa was sixty-six when she first came to the clinic, though her bearing suggested
someone older—shoulders curved inward, movements careful and economical, as though she were
perpetually braced against impact. She had been born in Aotearoa New Zealand, raised in the Waikato
valley, and had emigrated to California at twenty-three with a teaching degree and a quiet ambition.
For four decades she had designed curricula for secondary schools across the state, a career that
had suited her methodical mind and her need for control. But for the past year, she had barely left
her apartment.

The trauma had occurred in 1982, when Aroha was serving as a military nurse with the New Zealand
Defence Force. She had been deployed to support a peacekeeping operation in a region where the
political situation was deteriorating rapidly. On the morning of the incident, her unit was
stationed at a forward medical post in a compound surrounded by high walls and sandbags. She had
been preparing supplies in the clinic tent when the sound came—a sound she still could not fully
name, though she knew it was the rotors of a military helicopter descending toward the compound. The
noise was immense, filling the space before the impact. What followed existed in her memory as
disconnected fragments: the tent collapsing inward, the acrid smell of dust and something chemical,
the weight of something across her legs, voices shouting in a language she did not understand, and
then a long period of confusion where she was being moved, carried, and the sound—that relentless
mechanical sound—was everywhere. Three colleagues died in the strike. Aroha sustained a fractured
tibia and lacerations, but the visible wounds had healed decades ago.

What had not healed was the way her mind held the event. When she tried to explain it to her ex-
husband in the years after, she found herself unable to construct a coherent narrative. The memory
did not feel like something that had happened to her; it felt like something she had witnessed in a
film, too extreme and too violent to be real. She could recall fragments—the weight, the sound, the
particular shade of the tent fabric—but these pieces would not assemble into a story. Even now,
sitting in the clinic, she could not say with certainty how long the event lasted or what sequence
of events had actually occurred. The uncertainty itself had become a source of shame. She had come
to doubt her own perceptions, her own ability to interpret what was real and what was distorted by
trauma.

For decades, Aroha had functioned. She had married, divorced, built a career, maintained
friendships. But in the past eighteen months, the boundaries of her world had contracted
dramatically. It began with a helicopter passing overhead during her morning walk—a medical
transport, she would later learn—and something had fractured. The memory, which had been dormant and
distant, suddenly felt present and immediate. She began to avoid going out. First it was just
helicopter season, but then it was any time of day when aircraft might be overhead. Then it was
crowds, where she felt trapped. Then it was anywhere she could not quickly identify an exit.

Her workplace became impossible. As a curriculum designer, Aroha had spent her days in meetings, in
open-plan offices, in conference rooms where she was expected to sit still and collaborate. Three
months ago, she had been in a planning meeting when her supervisor had overruled her recommendation
about a new assessment framework. The moment she felt the power shift—the moment she realized she
had no control over the outcome—her body had responded with a surge of panic so acute she had
excused herself and not returned. She had taken medical leave and had not been back since.

Now she spent her days in her apartment, which she had arranged so that her chair faced the door and
the window. She had come to believe that she could not trust her own judgment, that if she had made
different decisions on that morning in 1982, three people would not have died. She had come to
believe that strong emotion—any strong emotion—was dangerous, and so she had learned to suppress
feeling, to move through her apartment in a state of numb vigilance. She was isolated, she knew, but
the isolation felt safer than the alternative. She felt set apart from others in a way she could not
explain and did not expect they would understand.

What had finally brought her to the clinic was a letter from her employer asking whether she
intended to return to work. The letter sat on her kitchen table for a week. On the eighth day, Aroha
had realized that she was not going back, and that she could not continue living as she was. She was
afraid that seeking help would mean being forced to feel the things she had learned to suppress. But
she was also afraid of disappearing entirely—of becoming so small and so isolated that there would
be nothing left of her at all.
