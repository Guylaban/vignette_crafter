# Rosa's Vigil

Rosa Martínez was sixty-eight years old when her grandson Miguel was killed. He was twenty-three,
working construction in the city, and one August afternoon in 2019, a scaffolding collapse took his
life along with two other young men. Rosa had not been there. She was at her studio in Wynwood,
pinning fabric samples to a mood board for a spring collection, when her daughter called. What Rosa
remembered most vividly was not what her daughter said—the words seemed to arrive from very far
away—but the precise temperature of the fabric beneath her fingers. It was linen, cool and smooth,
imported from Italy. That coolness would come back to haunt her in ways she could not have
predicted.

The funeral happened in a blur of ritual and numbness. Rosa moved through it as though watching
someone else perform her role: the grieving abuela, the keeper of family memory. She had designed
Miguel's christening gown forty-five years earlier, a task she remembered with crystalline detail
now, every stitch a small prayer. After the funeral, she returned to her studio and did not go back
for three months. When she finally did, it was because her business manager said she had to; clients
were calling, deadlines were passing, and at seventy years old, Rosa could not afford to disappear.
But the moment she touched fabric again—cool fabric, particularly—her hands would begin to shake.
Sometimes the sensation would trigger something deeper: a flash of Miguel's face, the way his
shoulders had looked in the casket, the smell of the funeral flowers mixing with the basement cold
of the viewing room. It would come without warning, complete and overwhelming, as if she were there
again, as if time had not moved at all.

She had come to believe that she should have protected him somehow. The thought arrived fully formed
one morning as she woke: if she had insisted he go to college, if she had discouraged construction
work, if she had been more present in his life during those crucial years when he was choosing his
path. The logic was circular and merciless. Rosa knew, intellectually, that the company responsible
for the scaffolding had faced minimal consequences—a fine, some citations, nothing that felt
proportionate to a young man's death. But what consumed her was not anger at them. What consumed her
was the certainty that she had failed in her most fundamental duty as a grandmother. She should have
protected him. She had not. Therefore, the event had happened because of her.

For months after returning to work, Rosa found herself avoiding anything that mentioned the incident
or similar accidents. She stopped watching news, stopped reading the newspaper, stopped listening to
the radio in her car. When her daughter would call with updates about a lawsuit, Rosa would ask her
not to discuss it. But the avoidance extended further than she anticipated. She began to avoid her
daughter herself, because her daughter's grief was a constant reminder of Miguel, and reminders were
unbearable. She stopped attending family dinners. She made excuses. Her husband, Eduardo, grew
increasingly frustrated and then increasingly quiet. He had loved Miguel too, but he seemed capable
of holding both his grief and his life simultaneously, a feat Rosa found incomprehensible and,
obscurely, condemning.

What frightened her most was the intrusion of joy. One afternoon, a client complimented her design
work with genuine warmth, and for a moment, Rosa felt a small bloom of professional satisfaction.
The feeling terrified her. How could she feel good when Miguel was dead? How could happiness coexist
with such loss? She had learned to push away any positive emotion the moment it appeared, as though
allowing it to flourish would be a betrayal of his memory, or would somehow invite more punishment.
This vigilance was exhausting, and it had begun to isolate her even from Eduardo, who would
sometimes try to make her laugh and find her pulling away, her face shutting down.

By the time Rosa came to therapy, she was seventy years old, still designing but with diminished
passion, still married but increasingly alone, still grieving but unable to grieve openly. What
finally brought her was a panic attack in her studio when a client brought in fabric samples—cool
linen, imported from Italy—and Rosa found herself unable to breathe, unable to distinguish between
the present moment and the day of the funeral. She was afraid that she was losing her mind. She was
hoping, quietly, that someone could help her find a way to live with what she had failed to prevent.
