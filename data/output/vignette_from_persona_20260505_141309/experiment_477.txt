# Clinical Vignette: Robert Chen

Robert Chen, seventy-four, sits in the therapist's office with the posture of a man accustomed to
controlled environments. His silver hair is trimmed short, military-precise. He was a combat pilot
for thirty-eight years—F-16s, mostly—and the discipline of that life had shaped him into someone who
believed that mastery came through vigilance and restraint. He is widowed now, his wife of forty-one
years dead for six months, and it is her death that has brought him here, though not in the way one
might expect.

The trauma that haunts Robert occurred over the course of eighteen months, beginning when he was
sixty-eight. After his retirement from active duty, he had felt unmoored. He met Margaret at a
community center art class—she was a painter, warm and spontaneous in ways his military life had
never permitted. Within months they were living together in her apartment downtown. Robert believed
he had found, finally, the softness his life had lacked.

Margaret's drinking began subtly. A glass of wine with dinner became a bottle. Then she began to
drink alone in the afternoons, and her moods fractured. One evening, when Robert gently suggested
she might want to talk to someone about her alcohol use, she erupted. She struck him across the
face, her hand open but forceful. He was shocked into immobility. She apologized profusely the next
morning, and Robert, who had been trained to compartmentalize trauma in combat, told himself it was
an isolated incident.

It was not. Over the following months, Margaret's physical aggression escalated. She would grab his
arm hard enough to bruise. Once, when he tried to leave the apartment during one of her rages, she
shoved him against the kitchen counter. The smell of alcohol on her breath—whiskey, usually,
sometimes gin—became a herald of danger. His body learned to recognize it instantly. His chest would
tighten. His muscles would prepare for impact even before she raised her hand.

What Robert came to believe, with the certainty that had once served him in the cockpit, was that he
could not afford to feel anger. If he allowed himself to be angry, he might strike back, and that
would make him like her—or worse, it would confirm something he feared about himself, something he
had kept locked away since his combat days. So he built walls. He would feel the rage rising in his
chest and he would breathe it down, compress it, swallow it. This worked, in a way. He never hit
her. But the effort of this constant suppression exhausted him in ways he could not articulate.

His sleep became fragmented. He would lie beside Margaret in the dark, his body rigid, waiting.
Waiting for what, he wasn't entirely sure—a sudden movement, the shift of her weight, some sign that
the night would turn violent. He found himself listening to her breathing, monitoring her position
in bed. Some nights he would get up and sit in the living room, telling himself he was just getting
a glass of water, but really he was maintaining a perimeter, keeping watch.

When Margaret died suddenly of a stroke—not from violence but from the cumulative wear of her
drinking—Robert felt a confusion of emotions he could not name. Grief, certainly. But also relief,
which made him feel monstrous. And something else: the vigilance didn't stop. Six months later, his
body still didn't know the threat had ended.

Now, in his apartment alone, Robert avoids anything that reminds him of her. He has removed all the
paintings she left behind. He doesn't go to the art center. He has stopped accepting invitations
from friends who might want to introduce him to someone new because the thought of physical
closeness—of being touched, of allowing someone near enough to hurt him—fills him with a dread he
cannot override. When he encounters a man wearing cologne that smells vaguely like whiskey, his
heart rate spikes and his hands begin to shake.

What brought him finally to seek help was a moment at the grocery store. A woman brushed past him,
her hand briefly touching his shoulder. His entire body seized. He felt tears on his face in the
cereal aisle, and he realized that he was no longer living. He was still flying a holding pattern,
waiting for an enemy that would never come.
