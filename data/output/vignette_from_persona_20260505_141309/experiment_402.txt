# Clinical Vignette: Aroha T.

Aroha T. was sixty-five years old when his daughter Hana fell ill. She was seven then, complaining
of headaches and fatigue on a Monday morning in late September. By Wednesday, her fever had climbed
to 104 degrees, and Aroha, working as a project manager for a commercial construction firm, had left
the office to take her to their family doctor. The visit lasted forty minutes—longer than expected.
Aroha remembered standing in the examination room, watching the doctor's face change as she listened
to Hana's chest, remembered the way she said they needed to go to the hospital "right now, not in an
hour." The ambulance arrived in twelve minutes. That delay—those twelve minutes—felt like a betrayal
of time itself, as though the world had slowed down precisely when speed mattered most.

Hana had bacterial meningitis. The next seventy-two hours existed in Aroha's memory as a kind of
suspended horror: the pediatric intensive care unit, the spinal tap, the antibiotics running through
an IV, his wife Margaret holding Hana's hand while machines beeped and hissed around them. Hana
recovered. She was one of the fortunate ones. But something in Aroha did not recover with her.

In the weeks that followed, he became convinced that her survival had been luck, not medicine—and
certainly not his own presence or decisions. He replayed the morning she fell ill obsessively,
searching for the moment he should have noticed something was wrong. He had kissed her goodbye
before work. Had he felt heat in her forehead and ignored it? He couldn't remember clearly, and the
uncertainty gnawed at him. What if he had taken her to the doctor on Tuesday instead of Wednesday?
What if he had insisted on the hospital visit sooner? The thought that he should have protected her,
that a more attentive father would have caught the illness earlier, took root so deeply that it
became a kind of certainty. He had failed when it mattered most.

He began to avoid anything that might trigger the memory. He stopped accompanying Margaret to her
annual checkups. When his own doctor's office called to schedule a routine physical, he declined,
then ignored the follow-up letters. The thought of entering a hospital or even seeing an ambulance
made his breath come short and shallow; his chest would tighten, and a wave of panic would flood
through him. He couldn't explain it rationally—Hana was fine now, healthy and thriving—but his body
insisted on the danger.

To manage the intrusive memories of those hospital days, Aroha kept himself relentlessly occupied.
At work, he volunteered for every meeting, every site inspection, every report that needed writing.
At home, he filled his evenings with projects: he rebuilt a garden shed, refinished the kitchen
cabinets, researched and planned renovations that never quite began. He kept podcasts playing
constantly, filled silences with news radio, anything to prevent his mind from settling into the
space where Hana's fever and the hospital's fluorescent lights lived.

Margaret died four years ago—a stroke, sudden and final. Aroha had been at work. He came home to
find her on the kitchen floor. In the aftermath, something shifted further. He became hypervigilant
about time and delay. If a meeting ran five minutes over schedule, his stomach would clench with the
certainty that something catastrophic was unfolding somewhere. If his grandson was late arriving for
their planned lunch, Aroha's mind would race through scenarios of accidents and illness. Delays had
become synonymous with disaster.

Now, at sixty-five, Aroha had not had a medical examination in seven years. His blood pressure was
almost certainly elevated—he could feel his heart racing more often than not. His sleep was
fragmented, his shoulders perpetually tense. He had withdrawn from his project management role into
a consulting position that required less in-person collaboration, though his colleagues had noticed
his increasing irritability and his tendency to disappear into his office for hours, ostensibly
working but often simply sitting in the dark.

It was his daughter Hana, now thirty-two and a nurse herself, who finally insisted he seek help. She
had noticed his breathing difficulties during a recent visit, had watched him tense and pale when an
ambulance passed on the street outside. She recognized in him something she had learned to recognize
in others: a man trapped in the moment of her illness, unable to believe she had truly survived,
unable to forgive himself for the hours he had not known what was happening. When she suggested he
speak to a therapist, Aroha did not refuse. For the first time in nearly three decades, he was ready
to examine the weight he had been carrying—the certainty of his failure, the belief that his
vigilance alone stood between those he loved and catastrophe.
