# Clinical Vignette: Thida

Thida was fifty-seven when she first came to the clinic, though the exhaustion in her face made her
appear older. She had worked as a midwife for thirty-one years—a career she had loved, one that had
given her purpose and a sense of mastery over the fragile moment between fear and joy. Her hands
were steady. She knew the arc of labour, the small interventions that could turn crisis into safe
passage. But something had fractured that steadiness, and she had come to understand that she could
no longer trust her own body in the presence of others.

Four years earlier, on a warm evening in September, Thida had been assaulted by a man who had forced
his way into her garage as she returned home from a night shift. The attack had been sudden and
absolute. What stayed with her most vividly was not the violence itself, but the smell—a thick,
acrid smell of burnt plastic and cigarette ash that had clung to his clothes and his skin. In the
weeks after, that smell had become a ghost in her own house. She would catch it in the evening air,
or imagine it rising from a stranger's coat, and her body would seize. Her heart would race. Her
breath would come shallow. She had come to believe that certain smells were warnings, that her
body's panic was a form of knowledge—a detector picking up danger that her conscious mind had not
yet registered.

The violation had transformed her relationship with being seen. Before the assault, Thida had moved
through her work and her community with a kind of invisible confidence. She attended her church, she
shopped at the market on Sunday mornings, she sat in the staff room at the hospital with her
colleagues. Now, the simple fact of being observed by another person—being looked at directly,
assessed, studied—felt like exposure. She had begun to notice how she held herself differently when
people looked at her. Her shoulders would tense. She would avert her eyes or study the other
person's face with an intensity that exhausted her, trying to read in their expression whether they
meant harm. At the hospital, she found herself scrutinizing the faces of colleagues and patients'
partners in a way that felt compulsive. She was searching for something—a flicker of aggression, a
coldness in the eyes, a shift in body posture that might signal intent. The work of midwifery, which
had always required her presence and openness, had become a minefield of threat detection.

Discomfort in her own body had become pervasive. She could not sit still in a room without being
aware of her skin, her breathing, the space between her and others. When a patient needed her
hands—when she was examining a woman in labour, or when a family member reached out to touch her arm
in gratitude—Thida would feel a surge of something between revulsion and panic. She had learned to
manage it by moving quickly, by keeping interactions brief and professional, by finding reasons to
step away. Her colleagues had begun to comment, gently, that she seemed distant. One senior midwife
had asked if she was thinking of retiring. Thida had said nothing. She could not explain that she
was terrified of losing this work, but that being present in it had become unbearable.

At night, in her own home, she listened. She had installed additional locks on the doors and
windows, but what consumed her was the listening itself—the constant monitoring for sounds that
might mean someone was entering. A creaking floorboard, the settling of the house, a neighbor's
car—each sound would jolt her into alertness. Her husband, who had been patient and loving, had
begun sleeping in the guest room because her vigilance kept him awake. She would lie rigid beside
him, or alone, waiting for the danger she was certain was coming.

It was her daughter who had finally insisted. Thida had become withdrawn, had stopped attending
family gatherings, had made excuses not to visit her grandchildren. "You're disappearing," her
daughter had said. And Thida, who had spent her life helping other women through their darkest
hours, had finally admitted that she could no longer do this alone.
