# Sister Magdalena's Vigil

Sister Magdalena had spent the morning in the convent chapel, as she did most mornings, her knees
pressed against the worn kneeling bench while the light filtered through the high windows in dusty
columns. At forty-four, she had been a nun for nearly two decades, and the rhythm of prayer had once
felt like the truest language she knew. But that was before March 15th, three years ago, when a bomb
detonated outside the community centre where she had been leading a Bible study group for teenagers
in her hometown of San Juan, Puerto Rico.

She had been standing near the entrance, waiting for the last few students to arrive, when the blast
came without warning. The force threw her backward into a metal door frame. What she remembered most
vividly—or rather, what her body remembered—was the sensation of impact, the way her left shoulder
had struck first, the radiating pain that bloomed through her ribs. For weeks afterward, whenever
that shoulder ached, the memory would surge back with such overwhelming sensory force that she would
lose her bearings entirely. The smell of burning concrete and flesh would fill her nostrils. She
would hear the screaming again, feel the heat on her face, taste blood in her mouth. It felt as real
as the moment it happened, as though her senses were not remembering but reliving, and she was back
there, helpless and terrified.

Three teenagers had died. Five others were injured. Magdalena had sustained a fractured clavicle and
deep lacerations along her left side. But the physical wounds had healed faster than anything else.

In the months following the bombing, Magdalena found herself turning inward in ways that puzzled her
superiors. She stopped attending community meals, preferring to eat alone in her small room. She
began to scrutinize the details of what had happened obsessively—replaying the sequence of events,
the exact positioning of her body, what she might have done differently, whether she could have
prevented it. She would sit for hours trying to reconstruct the memory deliberately, to make sense
of it, only to find that when she needed to remember something specific, the details would scatter
like frightened birds. Yet unbidden, without permission, the memory would force its way into her
consciousness at random moments: the sight of blood on her hands, the sound of sirens, the weight of
bodies being carried past her. She would wake from nightmares gasping, her heart hammering, unable
to return to sleep.

What frightened her most was the intensity of the negative emotions that accompanied these
intrusions—not just fear, but a suffocating shame that she had survived when others had not, a rage
at the senselessness of it, a guilt that felt as physical as her old shoulder pain. She had come to
believe that these feelings were too dangerous to share, that to speak them aloud to another person
would be to burden them unbearably. Emotional closeness began to feel like a threat. When other
sisters tried to comfort her, she would withdraw. She stopped attending the weekly community
gatherings in the common room.

She also stopped leaving the convent unless absolutely necessary. Public spaces—the grocery store,
the street market, even the church during the Sunday mass when it filled with visitors—became places
where she felt exposed and hunted. She would identify exit routes before entering any room, a habit
she had not consciously chosen but that had become automatic. She felt safe only in her cell and in
the chapel during the quiet hours before dawn, when no one else was there.

Last month, during a mandatory retreat at a larger convent across the island, Magdalena had
experienced a full panic attack in the crowded dining hall. The noise, the press of bodies, the
sudden flash of red sauce on a plate—it had triggered the memory so violently that she had fled to
the bathroom and could not stop shaking for two hours. It was then that the retreat coordinator, a
younger nun named Sister Beatriz, had gently suggested that Magdalena might benefit from speaking to
someone trained to help with what she had endured. Magdalena had resisted at first, but the weight
of three years of isolation, of memories that would not be controlled, of a life increasingly
confined to smaller and smaller safe spaces, had finally become too heavy to carry alone.

Now, sitting in the waiting room of a therapist's office, Magdalena is terrified and hopeful in
equal measure. She is afraid that speaking about the bombing will make it worse, that the shame and
rage will consume her if she lets them out. But she is also desperate to reclaim some part of the
life she had before—to be able to sit with her sisters without flinching, to walk through a crowded
street without scanning for exits, to sleep through the night without waking in terror. Most of all,
she is hoping that someone might help her understand why her mind and body will not let her move
forward, and whether it is still possible to find peace.
