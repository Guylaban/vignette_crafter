# Roberto Vázquez

Roberto Vázquez was sixty-three years old when the bomb went off outside the government building in
San Salvador where he had gone to renew his residency documentation. He had lived in the United
States for thirty-seven years by then, built a successful dental practice in northern California,
raised two children, and buried a wife of forty-two years just eighteen months earlier. He had gone
back to El Salvador for three weeks to settle some property matters and to visit his aging mother.
It was meant to be routine.

He was standing in the hallway outside the administrative office on the second floor, holding a
numbered ticket, when the explosion tore through the lobby below. The blast lifted him off his feet.
His ears filled with a sound he has never been able to fully describe—not just loud, but something
that seemed to unmake the world itself. He fell hard against a concrete wall, his left arm pinned
beneath him. For what he thinks was seconds but might have been longer, he lay there unable to move,
unable to hear anything but a high-pitched ringing, surrounded by dust so thick he could not see his
own hands. When he finally staggered to his feet, he saw blood on the floor—some of it his own from
a gash on his forehead, some belonging to people he did not know. Seventeen people died in that
attack. He was one of the fortunate ones, his daughter would later tell him.

But fortune is a relative thing. In the weeks after he returned to California, Roberto found himself
unable to settle back into the rhythms of his life. He had come to believe that he should have seen
it coming, that there had been some warning he had missed, some sign that would have told him not to
go to that building on that particular morning. He replayed the morning obsessively—the breakfast he
had eaten, the taxi driver's route, the conversation he had had with his mother about whether the
trip was necessary. If only he had listened to her hesitation. If only he had waited another day.
The self-recrimination became a constant companion, a way of trying to impose order on chaos, as
though sufficient blame directed at himself might somehow retroactively prevent what had already
happened.

Within months, the world had become a place of constant threat. Any loud noise—a car backfiring, a
door slamming in his office building, even the high-pitched whine of his dental drill—sent him into
a state of acute vigilance that could take hours to settle. He began carrying a small pistol in his
jacket pocket, something he had never done before, telling himself that he needed to be prepared,
that he could not afford to be caught unaware again. When he entered his dental office each morning,
he would scan the room methodically, noting where the exits were, calculating which route would be
fastest if he needed to leave. His hygienist noticed that he had started watching her more
carefully, his eyes tracking her movements when she was in the treatment room with him. He could not
explain to her why he needed to know where she was at all times, why the sight of her moving toward
the supply cabinet made his shoulders tense.

He stopped watching the news. Any report of a bombing, any mention of violence, any footage of
uniformed police or military personnel sent him into a state of emotional flooding so complete that
he had to leave the room. His daughter, concerned about his isolation, had tried to talk with him
about what happened, but when she began speaking about it, something in his chest would tighten and
the memory would return not as words but as sensation—the pressure of the blast, the taste of dust,
the smell of smoke and something else he could not name. It was all there at once, overwhelming him
so thoroughly that he could only ask her to stop, to leave him alone.

At seventy, Roberto sits in his dental office—a place he has loved for decades—and feels like a
stranger in his own life. His patients have begun to notice his distance, his reluctance to engage
in the casual conversation that once came easily. He is considering retirement, though the thought
of it terrifies him more than the thought of staying. What brings him to seek help now is his
daughter's ultimatum, delivered with love but with firmness: get help, or she will have to step back
from their relationship. He is afraid of what talking about it might do to him, but he is more
afraid of losing what little remains of his connection to the people he loves.
