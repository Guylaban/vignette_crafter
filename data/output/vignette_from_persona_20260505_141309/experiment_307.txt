# Marcus

Marcus was twenty-five when he first came to the clinic on a Tuesday afternoon, his supervisor at
the downtown hotel having finally insisted he speak to someone after he'd called out sick for the
third time in two weeks. He sat in the waiting room in his housekeeping uniform—navy polo, name tag
slightly crooked—and his leg bounced steadily against the linoleum floor. When he spoke, his voice
was steady but his hands moved constantly, fingers picking at the hem of his shirt.

The trauma had begun when Marcus was seven. His father's boyfriend, Derek, moved into their
apartment in late autumn. By winter, what had seemed like occasional roughness—a hard grip on the
arm, a shove into the hallway—had become systematic. One night in January, Marcus had wet the bed.
Derek's response was to drag him from under the covers, strip the sheets, and force Marcus's face
down against the bare plastic mattress—the kind with a peculiar synthetic smell and a slick, grainy
texture that Marcus could still feel against his cheek and forehead when he closed his eyes. The man
held him there in the dark, pressing down, while Marcus couldn't breathe properly and didn't know if
it would end. It did, eventually, but the pattern continued in different forms until Marcus was
eleven and his mother finally left with him. By then, something fundamental had shifted in how
Marcus understood the world.

Now, eighteen years later, Marcus lived alone in a studio apartment but never stayed there after
dark. He would arrive home from his shift at the hotel around three in the afternoon, complete
whatever tasks he needed to complete, and then leave again—to the twenty-four-hour diner, to a
friend's place, to anywhere that had light and other people. When he did sleep, it was usually on
his couch with the television on, the volume low but constant, because silence and darkness still
felt like a trap closing around him. He had come to believe that he was marked—that something about
what had happened to him was visible on his skin, that people looking at him could somehow see the
shape of his humiliation. He felt, most of the time, like a thing rather than a person, as though
the substance of who he was had been replaced by something hollow and used.

The nightmares had started a few years ago, intensifying in the past six months. They came almost
every night—the memory of that mattress, the pressure, the dark, the feeling of being held down. But
they weren't just dreams; they were vivid replays that felt completely real, with the texture
against his skin, the smell, the inability to move. Sometimes he would wake up gasping, convinced
for several seconds that he was still there, still being held. Other times the memory would surface
during the day—triggered by a child crying in one of the hotel hallways, or by the particular weave
of a rough blanket he encountered while changing beds—and the emotion would hit him so suddenly and
with such force that he would have to step into a supply closet and wait for it to pass, his whole
body shaking.

What had finally driven Marcus to call the clinic was a shift change at work. He'd been assigned to
the night housekeeping rotation, which meant arriving at the hotel at ten p.m. The first two nights,
he'd experienced what felt like constant panic—hyperaware of every shadow, every sound, his body
flooding with adrenaline at the slightest movement. On the third night, a guest's child had cried
from behind a closed door as Marcus was cleaning the hallway. He'd abandoned his cart, clocked out,
and left the building. He hadn't returned. His supervisor had called him the next morning, and
Marcus had found himself unable to explain. How could he say that the dark was dangerous, that he
couldn't trust anyone, that being alone with another person—even a supervisor trying to help—made
his chest tight with a fear he couldn't name?

He sat across from the therapist now, still in his work uniform, and admitted quietly that he didn't
know how to live like this anymore. He was afraid of the night. He was afraid of being seen. He was
afraid of what would happen if he stopped running.
