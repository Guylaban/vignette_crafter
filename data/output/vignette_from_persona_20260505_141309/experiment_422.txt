# Marcus

Marcus Chen was thirty-six when he came to therapy, though his wife Sarah said he had seemed much
older since he returned from his second deployment in 2019. He had spent fourteen months in Kandahar
province as a logistics officer, managing supply chains and coordinating with local contractors. On
a Tuesday in March of that year, during what should have been a routine site inspection at a forward
operating base, an IED detonated near the convoy. Marcus was thrown from the vehicle. Two soldiers
died. He sustained a fractured collarbone and severe lacerations but walked away conscious and
mobile—a fact that had come to feel, in his mind, like a debt he could never repay.

The first weeks after the explosion passed in a blur of medical evaluations and debriefing. Marcus
returned stateside in June. By autumn, Sarah noticed he had stopped inviting friends over, that he
flinched when she reached for his hand in bed, that he would sit rigid at the dinner table if there
was any lull in conversation. She attributed it to readjustment. Marcus told himself the same thing.
But the truth was more intricate: he had come to believe that the world operated on a logic of
sudden violence, that safety was an illusion maintained only through constant vigilance. Silence, in
particular, had become intolerable. In quiet moments—the pause before a colleague spoke in a
meeting, the gap between songs on the radio, his wife's thoughtful silence while reading—Marcus felt
the air thicken with menace, as if danger was coalescing in the emptiness. He had learned to fill
those spaces with sound, with movement, with anything that kept the world knowable.

By early 2020, his avoidance had calcified into patterns that structured his days. He would not
allow Sarah to touch him—not a hand on his shoulder, not an embrace, nothing. When she tried, his
body seized with alarm, his nervous system interpreting her touch as a threat that required
immediate escape. He had stopped talking with her about the deployment entirely; the few times she
had gently asked how he was really doing, he had felt something like nausea rise in his throat, a
sense that speaking about it would make it real in a way that might destroy him. He had begun,
without fully recognizing it, to push away moments of contentment. When Sarah would laugh at
something on television, or when he found himself briefly absorbed in a task at work, he would feel
a cold dread: this peace was false, he thought. Letting himself feel safe was a betrayal of the men
who had died, and it was dangerous—a lowering of the guard that would make him vulnerable.

His work as a tax consultant had become a minefield. Confrontations with clients over deductions or
audit findings triggered a surge of hyperarousal that left him shaking. Certain conversation
topics—anything involving explosions, accidents, sudden loss—would send him spiraling. And he had
developed an elaborate system of identifying exits in every office building he entered, every
conference room, every restaurant where he met a client. He could not feel settled until he knew the
way out.

The memories themselves came in two distinct forms, neither of which felt fully his own. Sometimes
he would taste copper and burnt plastic, the sensations so vivid and intrusive that they would
derail his afternoon, leaving him unable to concentrate. Other times, he could recall the sequence
of events with perfect clarity—the sound, the impact, the smell of smoke—but feel absolutely
nothing, as if he were watching a film about someone else's trauma. Most destabilizing were the
moments when emotion flooded in: the memory would return in full sensory detail, and the grief and
terror would be so overwhelming that he would have to excuse himself from meetings, lock himself in
the bathroom, wait for the wave to pass.

By November of last year, Sarah had stopped trying to touch him. She had stopped asking him to talk.
She had begun sleeping in the guest room. It was this—the quiet dissolution of his marriage, the
realization that his protective mechanisms were destroying the one relationship that might have
anchored him—that finally brought Marcus to seek help. He sat in the first session with his
shoulders braced, as if expecting impact, and said only this: "I'm losing her, and I don't know how
to stop it."
