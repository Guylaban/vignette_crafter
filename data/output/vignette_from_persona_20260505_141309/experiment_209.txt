# Talia's Testimony

Talia was sixty-six when she walked into the clinic on a Tuesday morning, her hands trembling
slightly as she filled out the intake form. She had owned her small fabric and yarn shop on the main
street of her town for nearly thirty years—a business built from nothing after her divorce, a source
of pride and identity that had anchored her through decades of steady, unspectacular life. She was
Samoan, first-generation American, raised to be self-reliant and to keep difficulties private. That
she was here at all meant something had finally broken through her own resistance.

The event that brought her to this threshold had occurred two years earlier, on an evening in late
March at approximately 7:15 p.m. Talia had been closing the shop, counting the till and tidying the
front display, when three police officers entered without announcement. What followed was a
confrontation that Talia still struggled to articulate—there had been a report, a mistake, something
about merchandise allegedly stolen from another business. She remembered the officers' voices, their
physical proximity, the way one of them had grabbed her arm when she reached toward her phone. She
remembered being pushed against the counter, her glasses falling, the sensation of her own
helplessness. The details fragmented there. She could not recall how long it lasted, could not
reconstruct the exact sequence of what happened after the initial contact, could not fully explain
why the officers had finally left or what words had been exchanged at the end. The memory felt like
a film with missing frames—visceral in its emotional weight but impossible to narrate as a coherent
sequence.

What remained undeniable was the aftermath. Within weeks, Talia had begun to avoid closing the shop
alone. She hired her niece to work evenings, an expense she could not really afford. More broadly,
she began to avoid the darkness—she installed motion-sensor lights throughout the shop and her home,
kept every room illuminated after sunset. She stopped going out in the early evening, the hour when
the event had occurred. At 7:15 p.m., she would feel her chest tighten, her breathing shallow, a
sense of dread that had no manageable proportions. She came to believe that something in her had
broken, that her inability to fight back or to even clearly remember what had happened proved she
was inadequate, that she lacked whatever internal resources allowed other people to endure trauma
and remain intact.

The feeling of being watched had become pervasive. In her own shop, while customers browsed the
shelves, Talia would experience sudden vertigo—a sensation that the room was tilting, that she could
not trust her own equilibrium. She interpreted these moments as evidence that she was losing her
grip on reality, that her mind was fragmenting in response to what had happened. She had come to
believe that normal people did not react this way, that her dizziness and her hypervigilance and her
inability to articulate the event to anyone—not even to her sister—meant she was going crazy.

To manage the intolerable texture of these days, Talia had developed a pattern of compulsive
shopping. Online, mostly, late at night when the anxiety peaked. Clothing, decorative items, things
she did not need. The purchases provided a brief, chemical lift—a moment of control, of choosing, of
acquiring something that was hers. But the credit card statements accumulated, and the packages
arrived to a home already cluttered with items she could barely remember buying. She had spent
nearly eight thousand dollars in the past eighteen months. The financial strain added another layer
to her conviction that she was failing, that she could not manage even the basics of her own life.

What finally compelled her to seek help was not a single crisis but an accumulation: a date she had
nearly missed, a customer who had asked her a simple question and found her unable to respond, the
growing awareness that she was becoming someone she no longer recognized. She sat across from the
clinician with her hands folded in her lap, her voice quiet but steady, and said, "I need to know if
I'm going to get through this, or if I'm already lost."
