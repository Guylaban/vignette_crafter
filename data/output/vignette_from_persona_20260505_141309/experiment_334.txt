Priya Sharma was twenty-eight when the building collapsed. She had been a junior policy analyst at
the City Planning Department then, newly promoted and eager to prove herself on a mixed-use
development project in the warehouse district. The structure was a century-old manufacturing plant
being retrofitted into apartments and retail space. On a Tuesday morning in March, she was inside
conducting a final inspection before the city signed off on the conversion. She was standing in the

the world cracking—a deep, spreading groan of metal and concrete giving way. The collapse took
seconds. The ceiling above the eastern wing fell first, and then the failure cascaded. She ran
toward the exit, her legs moving faster than her mind could process what was happening, and made it
through the main doors as the structure behind her folded in on itself. Dust filled the street. She
stood on the sidewalk, coughing, her hands shaking, watching emergency vehicles arrive. Three
workers died in the rubble. The project manager, who had been standing two feet from her, was never
found intact.

In the weeks after, Priya returned to work. She had to. The city launched an investigation; there
were depositions, reviews of the engineering reports she had signed off on, questions about whether
she had missed something, whether her inexperience had contributed to the deaths. She hadn't missed
anything—the original structural assessment had been falsified by the contractor—but the doubt had
already taken root. She came to believe that the world operated on hidden dangers, that catastrophe
was always present, waiting for the moment you let your guard down. If she had been standing two
feet to the left, she would have died. The randomness of it terrified her.

Four years later, Priya is a City Council Member, elected on a platform of infrastructure safety and
accountability. Her colleagues describe her as meticulous, thorough, relentless in her questioning
of contractors and engineers. What they don't see is the machinery running underneath. Every morning
before dawn, Priya lies awake, her mind cycling through the day ahead—the meetings, the people, the
spaces she will move through. She has come to believe that she must be on guard all the time, that
constant vigilance is the only thing standing between herself and another catastrophe. At night, the
city feels different to her. The darkness makes her uncertain about what is happening around her,
and she finds it harder to feel safe. She keeps her bedroom door open so she can hear if something
is wrong. She sleeps poorly.

When she enters a building for a council meeting or a site visit, she identifies the exits before
she sits down. She knows the stairs, the side doors, the windows that open onto fire escapes. In
unfamiliar places—a new restaurant, a conference venue, a colleague's home—she avoids going
altogether. She cannot assess the danger in spaces she hasn't mapped, and the anxiety of not knowing
is worse than the disappointment of declining the invitation. Her social life has narrowed. She
hasn't been on a date in three years. The thought of being in someone else's apartment, in a space
she doesn't control, where she can't identify escape routes, is unbearable.

She also avoids physical stress. She stopped running six months ago; the elevated heart rate felt
too close to panic, and she couldn't distinguish between her body's normal response to exertion and
the beginning of a threat response. She stopped going to the gym. She takes the elevator instead of
the stairs. She is hypervigilant toward people—watching faces, postures, the way someone holds their
hands—trying to spot danger before it manifests. She scans every environment she enters, a habit so
automatic now that she barely notices she is doing it.

Three weeks ago, during a particularly tense budget meeting, a contractor raised his voice in
disagreement with her. He wasn't angry at her specifically; he was frustrated with the process. But
Priya felt her body flood with fear. Her hands trembled. She excused herself and went to her office,
where she sat in the dark for an hour, certain that something terrible was about to happen. That
night, she couldn't sleep. The next morning, she called a therapist. She is afraid that this
vigilance, this constant bracing for disaster, is the only thing keeping her alive—and she is
exhausted. She wants to feel safe again, but she is terrified of what might happen if she stops
watching.

conducting a final inspection before the city signed off on the conversion. She was standing in the
central atrium, reviewing structural diagrams with the project manager, when she heard a sound like
the world cracking—a deep, spreading groan of metal and concrete giving way. The collapse took
seconds. The ceiling above the eastern wing fell first, and then the failure cascaded. She ran
toward the exit, her legs moving faster than her mind could process what was happening, and made it
through the main doors as the structure behind her folded in on itself. Dust filled the street. She
stood on the sidewalk, coughing, her hands shaking, watching emergency vehicles arrive. Three
workers died in the rubble. The project manager, who had been standing two feet from her, was never
found intact.

In the weeks after, Priya returned to work. She had to. The city launched an investigation; there
were depositions, reviews of the engineering reports she had signed off on, questions about whether
she had missed something, whether her inexperience had contributed to the deaths. She hadn't missed
anything—the original structural assessment had been falsified by the contractor—but the doubt had
already taken root. She came to believe that the world operated on hidden dangers, that catastrophe
was always present, waiting for the moment you let your guard down. If she had been standing two
feet to the left, she would have died. The randomness of it terrified her. She began having
intrusive images of the moment just before the collapse, the project manager's face as he turned
toward the sound, the dust cloud billowing through the atrium. These images arrived without
warning—while she was eating breakfast, sitting in traffic, listening to a colleague speak—and each
time they did, her body flooded with the same terror she had felt on that Tuesday morning. Her heart
would race. Her hands would go cold. She would find herself holding her breath. Loud noises—a car
backfiring, construction equipment, even a door slamming—would send her into a state of acute alarm,
her mind immediately searching for structural failure, for collapse, for the specific acoustic
signature of disaster. She found herself unable to concentrate during meetings; her attention would
fragment, pulled toward environmental monitoring. She had difficulty falling asleep and, when she
did sleep, her dreams were saturated with the sensation of falling, of being buried, of not being
able to breathe. She would wake gasping, her clothes soaked with sweat, her heart hammering against
her ribs.

Four years later, at thirty-two, Priya is a City Council Member, elected on a platform of
infrastructure safety and accountability. Her colleagues describe her as meticulous, thorough,
relentless in her questioning of contractors and engineers. What they don't see is the machinery
running underneath. Every morning before dawn, Priya lies awake, her mind cycling through the day
ahead—the meetings, the people, the spaces she will move through. She has come to believe that she
must be on guard all the time, that constant vigilance is the only thing standing between herself
and another catastrophe. At night, the city feels different to her. The darkness makes her uncertain
about what is happening around her, and she finds it harder to feel safe. She keeps her bedroom door
open so she can hear if something is wrong. She sleeps poorly, waking several times in the night
with a surge of dread, her mind searching for the threat that has jolted her awake even though there
is nothing there. The sleep disruption has accumulated over years now; she moves through her days on
a foundation of exhaustion that no amount of coffee can quite remedy, and she has begun to notice
that her concentration slips in afternoon meetings, that her patience with staff has worn thin. She
has also developed a tremor in her hands that appears without warning—when she is holding a pen
during a council session, when she is signing legislation, when she is simply sitting still. The
tremor embarrasses her. She has learned to grip her hands together or to clasp them in her lap
during public appearances, a posture that has become so habitual that her staff assumes it is simply
how she sits.

When she enters a building for a council meeting or a site visit, she identifies the exits before
she sits down. She knows the stairs, the side doors, the windows that open onto fire escapes. In
unfamiliar places—a new restaurant, a conference venue, a colleague's home—she avoids going
altogether. She cannot assess the danger in spaces she hasn't mapped, and the anxiety of not knowing
is worse than the disappointment of declining the invitation. Her social life has narrowed. She
hasn't been on a date in three years. The thought of being in someone else's apartment, in a space
she doesn't control, where she can't identify escape routes, is unbearable. She is hypervigilant
toward people—watching faces, postures, the way someone holds their hands—trying to spot danger
before it manifests. She scans every environment she enters, a habit so automatic now that she
barely notices she is doing it. She has also systematically eliminated anything that puts her body
under physical stress. She stopped running six months ago; the elevated heart rate felt too close to
panic, and she couldn't distinguish between her body's normal response to exertion and the beginning
of a threat response. She quit the gym. She stopped attending the yoga classes her doctor
recommended. Last month, when a colleague invited her to play recreational tennis—something she had
enjoyed in college—she declined without explanation. The thought of her heart racing, her breathing
quickening, her body in a state of arousal, was intolerable. She takes the elevator instead of the
stairs, avoiding the exertion. She has even stopped walking briskly; a slow, controlled pace through
the world feels safer, more manageable. Any situation that might elevate her physical
state—climbing, lifting, sustained movement—has become something she structures her day to avoid.
The logic is clear to her: if her body is calm, if her heart rate stays steady and low, then she can
monitor her internal state clearly, and she will know if real danger is approaching.

Three weeks ago, during a particularly tense budget meeting, a contractor raised his voice in
disagreement with her. He wasn't angry at her specifically; he was frustrated with the process. But
Priya felt her body flood with fear. Her hands trembled visibly. She excused herself and went to her
office, where she sat in the dark for an hour, certain that something terrible was about to happen.
That night, she couldn't sleep. The next morning, she called a therapist. She is afraid that this
vigilance, this constant bracing for disaster, is the only thing keeping her alive—and she is
exhausted. She wants to feel safe again, but she is terrified of what might happen if she stops
watching, if she allows her body to move naturally, if she relaxes her grip on the world even for a
moment. What frightens her most is not the possibility of another collapse, though that fear runs
deep. It is the fear that if she lets go of her vigilance, if she stops scanning, stops planning,
stops monitoring her own body for signs of threat, then she will miss the warning signs and someone
will die because of her carelessness. She carries the weight of the three workers and the project
manager as a kind of proof that her fear is rational, that the world is genuinely dangerous, and
that she alone must stand guard against it.
