# Katarina's Story

Katarina was forty-three when she arrived at the gastroenterology clinic complaining of persistent
abdominal pain and a thirty-pound weight loss over eighteen months. The colonoscopy revealed a stage
three colorectal cancer. The diagnosis came on a Tuesday in March, delivered by Dr. Patel in a small
room with a box of tissues on the side table—a detail Katarina would later struggle to unsee. She
remembered the exact texture of his voice when he said "aggressive" and "chemotherapy," remembered
the way the fluorescent light hummed, remembered the smell of the hand sanitizer on his wrists. What
she did not remember clearly was leaving the clinic or driving home, though she must have done both.
Her partner, Elena, said Katarina had called her from the parking lot, but Katarina had no
recollection of that conversation.

The next six months were a blur of port placements, infusions, and hair loss. The chemotherapy
worked. By August, the tumors had shrunk enough that surgery became possible. Katarina underwent a
resection that left her with a temporary colostomy and a long surgical scar that she could not yet
bear to look at directly. She was told she was fortunate. She was told she had caught it early
enough. She was told she would recover. And in the narrow, clinical sense, she did. By November, the
colostomy was reversed. By January, she was cleared to return to work.

But something had fractured inside her that no scan could measure.

Katarina had been a chef for twenty-two years, known in her restaurant kitchen for her precision and
her ability to remain calm under the pressure of service. Now, three months back at work, she found
herself unable to tolerate the heat from the ovens. When the kitchen temperature spiked during
dinner rush, her chest would tighten and her hands would shake—not because she was afraid of the
heat itself, but because the sensation of warmth spreading across her skin triggered a cascade of
bodily panic that felt entirely beyond her control. Her body would react first, flooding with
adrenaline, her breath becoming shallow, her muscles tensing as though bracing for impact. Only
afterward would her mind catch up and recognize that she was standing safely in her own kitchen,
holding a sauté pan. The delay between her body's alarm and her conscious understanding of safety
had become unbearable.

She began staying up until two or three in the morning, scrolling mindlessly through her phone or
sitting in the dark living room, because sleep felt dangerous. In sleep, the memories came with a
vividness that annihilated the boundary between past and present. She would be back in the recovery
room, the anesthesia wearing off, the pain so complete and so real that she would wake gasping,
certain that the surgery was happening again. These were not gentle memories—they arrived as full
sensory floods, the smell of the operating room, the taste of the plastic oxygen mask, the weight of
the surgical drapes, the sound of the beeping monitors. When they came, they felt absolutely,
undeniably real, as though her body had traveled backward in time. And then, in the disorienting
moments after waking, Katarina would feel something close to grief—a crushing sadness that the
experience had been real, that it had happened to her, that no amount of wishing could undo it.

Elena had begun to worry. She would suggest dinner out, or a weekend trip to the coast, or even just
a quiet evening with friends. But Katarina found herself declining these invitations with increasing
frequency. The possibility of feeling happy or hopeful had come to feel dangerous, as though
positive emotion might lower her guard and allow something terrible to slip through. More
fundamentally, she had come to believe that she would never feel normal emotions again—that the
illness had rewired something essential in her, leaving her capable only of numbness or panic. She
had begun to numb herself deliberately, pulling back from Elena's affection, avoiding conversations
about the future, eating without tasting, moving through her days as though she were watching
someone else live them.

What frightened Katarina most was the certainty that her body had been permanently damaged, that it
would never again be trustworthy. The scar on her abdomen felt like proof of that damage—not just
physical, but existential. Her body was the enemy now, reacting to danger that did not exist,
holding her captive to a threat that had already passed. It was this fear, combined with the weight
of months of sleeplessness and the growing distance between herself and Elena, that finally brought
her to her first therapy appointment in late April. She sat in the waiting room, hands folded in her
lap, wondering if talking about what had happened would only make it more real.
