# Rajesh's Reckoning

Rajesh Patel was forty-two years old when he drove through a red light on a Tuesday morning in
March, his mind half on the quarterly tax audit waiting in his office, half on the email from his
supervisor that had arrived that dawn. He did not see the silver sedan coming from his left until
the moment of impact—a sound like the world splitting open, metal screaming against metal, and then,
worse than the sound, the knowledge that he had done this. The other driver, a woman named
Catherine, had suffered a fractured femur, a shattered collarbone, and a concussion that kept her in
the hospital for nine days. Rajesh had walked away with bruised ribs and a catalogue of thoughts
that would not leave him.

For the first weeks after the accident, Rajesh had believed the worst would pass. He attended the
court-ordered driving course. He paid the fine. He sent flowers to Catherine's home, though she
never acknowledged them. But the relief he expected never came. Instead, something had calcified
inside him—a permanent sense of having revealed himself as fundamentally untrustworthy, the kind of
person who could destroy another person's life in a moment of inattention. When he returned to work
at the Department of Revenue, his colleagues spoke to him as they always had, but Rajesh was certain
they saw him differently now, that they understood him to be careless, reckless, the sort of man who
should not be trusted with responsibility. He had come to believe that he deserved to suffer for
what he had done, that any comfort he found was a betrayal of Catherine's pain. When his wife,
Priya, suggested they go out for dinner, he would tell himself that he had no right to enjoy a meal
when Catherine was still healing. When his daughter called from college with good news about her
internship, he would feel a twist of guilt so sharp it made him nauseous.

The world, which he had always understood as a place where careful attention and good intentions
mattered, had revealed itself to be something else entirely—a place where a moment's distraction
could shatter another person. This knowledge sat in his chest like a stone.

Rajesh began to avoid the roads where the accident had occurred, taking elaborate detours on his
commute that added forty minutes to his drive. But avoidance had metastasized beyond geography. He
could not watch television without flinching at crash scenes. He had turned down his brother-in-
law's invitation to a funeral last month because the thought of being in a cemetery, surrounded by
the visible markers of death and loss, had sent him into a panic he could not explain to anyone
without sounding unhinged. When colleagues at work asked him to discuss what had happened—a casual
question, a moment of concern—he would feel his throat close, and he would change the subject with
an abruptness that had begun to isolate him.

The memories themselves had become fragmented and haunting. He could hear the crunch of impact with
perfect clarity, could hear Catherine's cry, but large portions of the event had become unreachable
to him, existing only as a blank space where his mind refused to go. Other moments blazed with such
vivid detail that he could feel the steering wheel under his palms. This unevenness was maddening.
He would lie awake trying to reconstruct what he had not seen, as if understanding every detail
would somehow retroactively prevent what had happened.

By the time Rajesh came to his first appointment, he had been living this way for eight months. He
was sleeping poorly, his marriage had become strained by his withdrawal, and he had begun to wonder
if Catherine would have been better served if he had been the one more seriously injured. It was
this thought—this dark arithmetic of suffering—that had finally frightened him enough to seek help.
He sat across from the therapist with his hands folded, exhausted and afraid that no amount of
talking would undo the damage he had caused or the damage he believed he deserved.
