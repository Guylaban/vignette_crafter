# Noy's Story

Noy Saengchaiyaphum was twenty-three when she was taken. She had been working late at her firm's
downtown office, finishing a client reconciliation that was due the next morning. It was a Tuesday
in March, unremarkable in every way until the moment the elevator doors opened on the empty thirty-
second floor and a man she had never seen before stepped out. He was holding a gun. What followed
was seventy-two hours in a storage unit in an industrial park—hours she has never fully articulated
to anyone, hours that now, five years later, still shape every moment of her waking life.

The man had demanded money, had kept her bound, had violated her in ways that made her body feel
like it no longer belonged to her. When she was finally found—when a security guard making his
rounds heard her muffled cries—the police arrested him within weeks. He was convicted. He served his
sentence. And then, two years ago, he was released. That fact alone had fractured something in Noy
that therapy had not yet repaired. She had come to believe that the system itself had failed her,
that the man responsible had paid almost nothing for what he had taken from her. The injustice of
his early release felt like a second trauma layered over the first, a confirmation that what
happened to her did not matter enough to the world.

But even before his release, Noy had been living a life constrained by the terror that had been
installed in her body on that Tuesday. She had returned to work after three months, determined to
reclaim her life, but the office felt like a trap. The open floor plan that had once seemed modern
and collaborative now felt like exposure. She found herself unable to sit at her desk without
positioning her chair so her back was against the wall of her cubicle, angled so she could see the
entrance to her section. Her colleagues had noticed. Her manager had gently asked if everything was
okay. Noy had said yes, it was fine, just a preference. But it was not a preference. It was a
necessity. Without that wall behind her, she felt the phantom sensation of hands reaching for her,
of the gun's barrel pressing against her spine.

At home, the hypervigilance was worse. Noy lived alone in a one-bedroom apartment in Arlington, and
she had become consumed by the sounds of the building. Every footstep in the hallway, every creak of
the pipes, every car door slamming in the lot below—she catalogued them all, her nervous system
scanning for the one sound that would mean he had found her, that it was happening again. She had
installed three deadbolts on her door. She slept with her phone in her hand. She had memorized the
floor plan of her apartment so thoroughly that she could navigate it in complete darkness. When
someone knocked unexpectedly—a delivery driver, a neighbor—her body would flood with adrenaline
before her mind caught up to the reality of the moment. The startle was violent, involuntary. A few
months ago, her upstairs neighbor had dropped something heavy, and the sound had sent Noy scrambling
under her kitchen table, her heart racing so fast she thought she might be dying.

She had stopped going out in the evenings. She had stopped going to the grocery store alone. She had
stopped wearing the clothes she loved because they felt like they belonged to a different person, a
Noy who had not been broken. When she looked in the mirror, she felt a deep, corrosive shame that
had nothing to do with her appearance and everything to do with the fact of what her body had
endured. She was certain that the assault had been, in some fundamental way, her fault—that she
should not have stayed late, should have been more aware, should have done something differently.
The man had a gun, but in her darkest moments, she wondered if she had somehow invited this, if
there was something about the way she moved through the world that had marked her as vulnerable.

At twenty-eight, Noy had been promoted to senior consultant, but the promotion felt hollow. She had
missed important client dinners because the thought of being in a restaurant, unable to see all the
exits, made her chest tight. Her professional relationships had atrophied. She was competent,
reliable, but increasingly isolated. It was her supervisor's gentle insistence that she seek help—a
comment that Noy was "not herself anymore"—that finally pushed her to call a therapist. What she
wanted most was to feel safe again. What she feared most was that she never would.
