# Marco's Story

Marco Rossi was twenty-six when the flood came to the Catskills that August. He had driven up to a
remote cabin with his college roommate and two others for a long weekend—a trip he had taken the
same route for three summers running, always in late August when the weather held clear. The cabin
sat on a ridge overlooking a valley where a creek ran shallow and predictable. The forecast that
Thursday evening called for scattered showers. By Friday afternoon, the sky had turned the color of
old bruises, and the rain came not in drops but in sheets, the kind that seemed to erase the
boundary between sky and earth.

The creek became a river. Then it became something else entirely—a dark, churning mass that tore
through the valley with a sound Marco had never heard before and has not stopped hearing since. When
the floodwaters reached the cabin, he was on the back porch. He watched it come. There was no time
to run, no calculation to make. One moment he was standing on solid ground; the next, cold water was
at his waist, then his chest, then his neck. The current pulled him sideways. He lost his footing.
He remembers the shock of being underwater, the absolute darkness, the sense that the water had a
will of its own and that will was to keep him submerged. He does not remember being pulled out by
his roommate. He does not remember the climb to higher ground or the hours they spent waiting on the
roof for rescue. Those hours are simply gone—a blank space where memory should be. What remains is
fragmented: the taste of mud, the roar that seemed to come from everywhere at once, a moment of
absolute certainty that he was going to die.

Three years have passed. Marco works as a salesman for an office supply company, a job that requires
him to visit clients across the tri-state area. He is good at sales when he can do the work, but the
job has become a minefield of his own making. Driving through certain landscapes—anywhere with heavy
vegetation, anywhere near water, anywhere that reminds him of that ridge and that valley—sends him
into a state he cannot easily describe to others. His body goes rigid. His hands sweat on the
steering wheel. He finds himself scanning the sky for signs of weather, checking his mirrors
obsessively, calculating escape routes. He has come to believe that nature itself is unreliable in a
fundamental way, that the world can turn against you without warning or mercy, and that other
people—even people he has known for years—cannot be counted on when it matters. His roommate saved
his life that day, but Marco also knows that his roommate could not have known the water was coming.
No one could have known. That knowledge sits in his chest like a stone.

He has taken to avoiding assignments in certain regions entirely, claiming scheduling conflicts or
client preferences that do not exist. His manager has begun to notice. Last month, a routine visit
to a client in Westchester required driving through a wooded area after heavy rain. Marco called in
sick. The pattern is repeating: avoidance spreading, opportunities contracting, the world becoming
smaller. At night, he wakes in darkness, his heart hammering, certain that something catastrophic is
about to happen. He lies still, listening, checking the room for threats he cannot name. Sleep has
become unreliable. His apartment is on the fourth floor, which he tells himself is safer, though he
is not certain from what.

What frightens him most is not the memory itself but the moments when the terror arrives without any
image attached to it—just the raw, animal panic, the absolute conviction that he is drowning, even
as he sits in his car or lies in bed. He has stopped seeing friends. Vulnerability of any
kind—admitting confusion, asking for help, allowing someone close enough to see how much he is
struggling—feels like standing on that porch again, waiting for the water. Three weeks ago, after a
panic attack in his car between client visits, Marco finally called a therapist. He does not know if
talking about what happened will help, or if it will only make the terror more real. What he does
know is that he cannot continue like this—trapped between the memory he cannot fully access and the
fear he cannot escape.
