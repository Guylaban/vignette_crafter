# Vinh's Vigilance

Vinh Tran was thirty-three when the pickup truck pinned his left leg against the clinic wall. He had
been walking to his car after a Saturday morning shift at the physical therapy practice where he
worked, and the driver—a man whose foot had slipped from brake to accelerator—had come through the
parking lot at speed. The impact shattered Vinh's tibia and fibula in three places. What stayed with
him more than the fractures, though, was the moment of absolute helplessness: the weight of the
truck, the searing heat of the engine block, the sound of his own voice screaming, and the terrible
certainty that his leg would not move, that his body had simply stopped obeying him.

The surgery took five hours. The rehabilitation took months. Vinh returned to work eight weeks after
the accident, limping slightly but functional. By all objective measures, he had recovered well. The
orthopedic surgeon said the bone had healed cleanly. His own clients—people he helped rebuild
strength after their own injuries—remarked that he moved almost normally now. But something had
shifted in how Vinh inhabited his own body. He had come to believe that the damage ran deeper than
any X-ray could show, that whatever had broken in him that day had not truly mended. His body had
betrayed him once. He was certain it would do so again.

The intrusive thoughts began within weeks. Unwanted images of the moment of impact would surface
without warning—the chrome bumper, the windshield filling his vision, the smell of hot metal and
rubber. They came at odd moments: while he was eating lunch, while he was listening to his wife Maya
speak about her day, while he was lying beside her in bed. He would jolt awake from nightmares where
the truck was coming again, or where his leg was crushed but he could not scream, his voice simply
gone. The residue of these dreams clung to his mornings; he would wake gasping, his heart racing,
and it would take him ten or fifteen minutes to remember that he was safe, that he was home, that
the accident was behind him.

Loud noises had become intolerable. When a car backfired outside their apartment, Vinh flinched
violently enough that Maya jumped. The sound of a motorcycle accelerating sent a spike of adrenaline
through his chest. He had begun to notice things he had never paid attention to before: the metal
scaffolding on construction sites, the potential for a chair leg to splinter and pierce skin, the
way a heavy object left on a high shelf could fall. Walking through the clinic now, he found himself
cataloguing hazards—the corner of the desk that could crack a skull, the electrical cord someone
could trip over, the gap between the treatment table and the wall where a hand could be crushed.
These observations were not abstract; they felt urgent, necessary. If he could anticipate danger, he
reasoned, he might be able to prevent it.

To manage this vigilance, Vinh had developed rituals. He reorganized the shelves in his apartment
compulsively, moving items lower, creating what he called "safer configurations." He cleaned for
hours on weekend mornings, a repetitive, meditative activity that made him feel, temporarily, as
though he had imposed order on an essentially chaotic world. He would mentally rehearse scenarios—a
car coming through the clinic window, a client falling from the treatment table, his own leg giving
out as he walked down the stairs—running through the details obsessively, as if the act of imagining
catastrophe could somehow inoculate him against it. Maya had begun to notice. She asked gently why
he was spending so much time cleaning. She mentioned that he seemed tense. She said his
concentration seemed scattered when they talked.

The real crisis came at work. A new client, a woman recovering from a car accident of her own, had
been referred to him. During their first session, she described the moment of impact in vivid
detail. Vinh felt the room tilt. His concentration shattered. He could not focus on her range of
motion or her pain responses; he could only hear the echo of his own trauma in her words. He excused
himself and spent twenty minutes in the bathroom, breathing slowly, trying to steady himself. When
he returned, his hands were shaking. He knew his client had noticed. The following week, he asked
his supervisor to reassign her case.

Now, at thirty-three, Vinh has come to therapy because Maya finally told him she was frightened—not
of him, but for him. She said he was disappearing into himself, that he was no longer the man she
had married. What frightened Vinh most, as he sits in the therapist's office, is that she might be
right. He is afraid that his body will fail him again, that no amount of vigilance or control will
ever be enough to keep him safe, and that the person he is becoming—hypervigilant, ritualistic,
unable to trust his own physical capacities—might be permanent.
