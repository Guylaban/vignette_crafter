# Captain Linh Tran

Captain Linh Tran was thirty days into a routine training sortie over the Nevada desert when her
younger brother's Cessna went down. He was not a military pilot—he was a recreational flyer, someone
who had borrowed a friend's aircraft for what should have been a leisurely afternoon. Linh was at
altitude when she heard the news come through base comms, a static-filled report that felt like it
belonged to someone else's life. Her brother Duc, twenty-four years old, had radioed a mechanical
problem. Then silence. The search took four days. They found him in a canyon, the aircraft folded
around him like paper.

Linh had been flying since she was twenty-two, had logged over two thousand hours, had never wavered
in her conviction that the sky was where she belonged. She was good at her job—precise, calm under
pressure, the kind of pilot other pilots trusted. In the weeks after Duc died, she tried to hold
onto that version of herself. But something had fractured. The moment she heard the news, her mind
had begun to construct a film that played without her permission: Duc in the cockpit, the instrument
panel going dark, the realization spreading across his face. The images had a photographic
quality—she could see the specific angle of his jaw, the exact shade of blue on the altimeter,
details she had never witnessed but which her mind had somehow manufactured and now replayed with
relentless precision. Without warning, a sound—the whine of a hydraulic system during preflight, the
particular timbre of another pilot's voice on the radio—would trigger the entire sequence. She would
be standing in the ready room or walking across the tarmac and suddenly she was watching Duc die
again.

Within two months, Linh had begun to suppress the thoughts deliberately. She stopped talking about
Duc to the other pilots, stopped accepting invitations to the squadron's social events where someone
inevitably asked how her family was doing. She declined her mother's phone calls because hearing her
voice—the careful way she spoke now, as though she might break—made the film play faster. She
stopped reading the accident report, stopped looking at photographs. The effort of not thinking
about him consumed her in a way that thinking about him never had. It was like holding a beach ball
underwater; the moment she relaxed, it surged back up.

Her work suffered quietly at first. She became hypervigilant during flights, second-guessing
instrument readings, running redundant checks that added minutes to her preflight routine. Her
commanding officer noticed. More significantly, she noticed. Linh had come to believe that there was
something fundamentally broken in her—that a competent pilot, a good sister, would have found a way
to prevent this. She should have known he was planning to fly that day. She should have convinced
him not to go. The fact that these things were impossible did not diminish the conviction that she
had failed him. And if she could fail at something as fundamental as protecting the people she
loved, what good was she to anyone? The thought terrified her in a way that turbulence never had.

She had not started a new friendship in nearly a year. When colleagues tried to include her in
things, she found reasons to decline. She could not rely on other people—could not trust that they
would not leave, or die, or prove that her judgment about them was as catastrophically wrong as her
judgment about her own capacity to protect those close to her. Better to be alone. Safer.

It was her mother who finally insisted she seek help. Linh's father had called, voice tight, saying
that her mother was worried Linh was disappearing. Linh sat in the waiting room of the VA mental
health clinic, hands folded, watching the door and wondering if talking about any of this would make
the film stop, or only make it worse. What she wanted most was to feel like herself again. What she
feared most was that the person she had been before Duc died no longer existed.
