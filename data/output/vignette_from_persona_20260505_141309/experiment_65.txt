# Aroha's Reckoning

Aroha Ngata was sixty-seven years old when she first came to therapy, though she moved through the
world as though she were carrying the weight of someone much older. She had worked as a database
administrator for a mid-sized insurance firm in Sacramento for nearly thirty years—a job that suited
her methodical mind, one where data followed rules and patterns and did not surprise you if you paid
attention. But in the past eighteen months, even that refuge had begun to fracture.

The violence had come without warning, though Aroha would later understand that she had simply not
been reading the signs. Robert had seemed gentle when they met at a community centre event three
years ago, a widower like herself, with soft eyes and a way of listening that made her feel seen for
the first time since her husband died. They had married after eighteen months of courtship. For the
first six months of marriage, Aroha had felt almost reborn—someone had chosen her again, had wanted
her in his life. She had not realized how much she had been waiting to feel that way.

The first time Robert hit her, it was because she had spoken out of turn at a dinner party,
correcting something he had said about computers. He had driven her home in silence, and once
inside, his hand had connected with the side of her face with such force that she fell against the
kitchen counter. She remembered the shock more than the pain—the sense that the world had tilted on
its axis. He had cried afterward, held her, apologized with a desperation that made her believe it
would not happen again. But it did. Over the course of eight months, it happened seventeen times.
Sometimes it was a shove when she moved too slowly. Sometimes it was his hand around her throat.
Once, he had thrown a plate that caught her shoulder. Each time, the apologies came. Each time,
Aroha found herself wondering what she had done to provoke him, what she had said wrong.

When she finally left him—after he had pinned her against a wall and told her she was worthless,
that no one would ever want her—she had called her daughter in Auckland. Within two days, she was on
a plane, and Robert was arrested. The legal proceedings took four months. He pleaded guilty to
assault. He was sentenced to eighteen months in prison. By then, Aroha was back in Sacramento, back
at her job, trying to convince herself that she could simply resume her life.

But the woman who returned to her apartment was not the woman who had left it. She had come to
believe that her reactions since the event—the panic, the shame, the way she flinched at sudden
movements—showed that she was a lousy coper, someone fundamentally unable to manage her own life.
She had let a man fool her. She had stayed far longer than she should have. She had not seen what
was obvious to everyone else. There was something wrong with her as a person, something that had
drawn his violence toward her, some defect that made her unable to protect herself. These thoughts
had become as familiar as her own breath.

At work, Aroha sat in her cubicle and avoided eye contact with her colleagues. When her supervisor,
Marcus, tried to check in with her about a project, she looked past his shoulder at the wall behind
him, unable to meet his gaze. She was certain that if he looked directly into her eyes, he would see
the truth of her—the weakness, the poor judgment, the fundamental brokenness. She had taken to
staying up until one or two in the morning, sitting in her living room with all the lights on, her
body too wired with vigilance to surrender to sleep. When sleep finally came, it was fitful and full
of dreams in which Robert's hands were around her throat and she could not scream.

The avoidance of touch had developed gradually. Her daughter had tried to hug her when she arrived
in Auckland, and Aroha had stiffened, unable to bear the weight of another person's body against her
own. Now, six months after leaving Robert, she crossed the street to avoid brushing past strangers
on the sidewalk. When her colleague Janet had reached out to pat her arm in sympathy—someone must
have told her something—Aroha had jerked away so sharply that Janet had looked wounded. She had not
tried again.

It was Marcus who finally insisted she seek help. He had noticed that her work had become scattered,
that she was making errors in datasets that had always been her strength, that she seemed to be
disappearing into herself. He had asked her directly, in his office, if something was wrong. She had
looked at her hands and told him she had been in an abusive relationship. He had listened without
judgment and given her the name of a therapist. Aroha had called that evening, during the hours when
she was awake anyway, too afraid to sleep, too ashamed to reach out during normal business hours.

What frightened her most was the possibility that she would never feel like herself again—that the
woman she had been before Robert, the woman who had raised two children and managed complex
databases and believed she was capable—was gone forever. What she was hoping for, though she barely
dared to name it, was the chance to discover who she was now, in this new and terrifying version of
her life.
