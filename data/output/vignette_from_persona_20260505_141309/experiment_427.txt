# Military Trauma and the Distance Between Worlds

Marcus was twenty-two when the IED detonated thirty meters from his Humvee during a patrol outside
Kandahar. He remembers the sound—a percussion that seemed to come from everywhere at once—and then a
strange, suspended moment where his body registered the blast but his mind had not yet caught up.
When awareness returned, it came in fragments: the smell of burnt metal and something else he could
not name, the weight of his own limbs feeling disconnected, and the sight of Corporal Diaz's left
leg, the one that had been beside him seconds before, now a separate thing on the ground. The medics
got there quickly. Marcus had trained for this. But training and reality had turned out to be
entirely different things.

Now, six years later at twenty-eight, Marcus worked as a social media manager for a mid-sized
marketing firm in Denver, a job that was supposed to feel far removed from all of that. He was good
at it—strategic, detail-oriented, quick with language. But the distance between the office and
Kandahar had compressed into something much smaller than geography.

The triggers came without warning, though Marcus had learned to anticipate them with a kind of
constant low-level dread. A video on his feed of a construction accident with visible lacerations
would send his nervous system into overdrive; his hands would shake, and he would feel his chest
tighten as though the blast were happening again, right there in his cubicle. He had started
scrolling with his eyes half-closed, trying to catch and avoid graphic content before it fully
registered. His colleagues had noticed him flinching at their screens but said nothing. When his
girlfriend, Sophia, suggested they drive up to Boulder for a weekend, something in him seized. The
thought of sitting in the passenger seat while she drove, trapped in the metal box with no control,
his body unable to move or escape—he had declined, invented a work deadline. She had not pushed, but
he had seen the disappointment settle into her face.

The helicopter sounds were the worst. Denver's medical helicopter made regular runs, and when Marcus
heard that distant whump-whump-whump, his entire body would lock. His heart would race as though he
were still twenty-two, as though the next sound would be the one that changed everything. He had
taken to wearing earbuds during his lunch breaks, even sitting alone in the office break room, just
to have some control over what he heard.

Sleep had become a negotiation with his own mind. Marcus would lie awake for hours, his body wound
tight, convinced that if he allowed himself to drift off, something catastrophic would happen. The
need to stay alert—to maintain that vigilant watch—felt as essential as breathing. When sleep
finally came, it was shallow and fractured, interrupted by dreams he could not quite remember but
that left him gasping and drenched in sweat. Sophia had started sleeping in the guest room. She had
not said it was because of his thrashing or his sudden, violent startle when she reached over to
touch him in the dark, but they both knew.

What troubled Marcus most was the way the memory of that day had become almost unreal to him, even
as its emotional weight never diminished. He could recall individual moments—the taste of dust, the
feeling of his own blood on his neck—but they did not connect into a coherent story. It was as
though his mind had shattered the event into pieces that refused to fit together, and when he tried
to make sense of it, the whole thing felt impossible, too extreme to have actually happened. Yet the
feeling remained: a constant, suffocating sense that something terrible was about to occur.

Over the months, Marcus had begun to believe that he was fundamentally broken in a way that could
not be repaired. He had started avoiding anything that stressed his body—he no longer went to the
gym, no longer played the recreational soccer he had once loved. He rehearsed conversations before
meetings, scripting every exchange, trying to control outcomes because the world felt too
unpredictable, too dangerous. He could not watch police procedurals or military films; the sight of
a uniform, even on a television screen, would send him spiraling. Most painfully, he had come to see
himself as permanently different from the people around him, as though the blast had drawn a line
between who he had been and who he could never be again. Sophia loved him, he knew that, but he was
certain that someone else—someone who had not been broken in Kandahar—would have been a better
partner, would not have brought this weight into her life.

It was Sophia, finally, who insisted he seek help. She had found a therapist who specialized in
trauma, and though Marcus felt the old resistance rising—the conviction that talking would not undo
what had been done—he had made the appointment. He was afraid that if he began to unpack this, he
would discover that the damage was even worse than he believed. But he was also, quietly, hoping
that someone could help him understand why a moment six years in the past still held him captive in
the present.
