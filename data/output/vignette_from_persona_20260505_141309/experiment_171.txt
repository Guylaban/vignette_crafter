# Clinical Vignette: Minh

Minh was sixty-six when he came to the clinic, moving with the careful deliberation of a man whose
body had learned not to trust itself. He had been a roofer for forty-three years, ascending ladders
and navigating pitched surfaces with the unselfconscious confidence of someone who had never once
fallen. That confidence ended on a summer afternoon in 2019 when, without warning, a corroded
industrial vent on the building's east side fractured. A plume of chlorine-based chemical compound
released directly into his face and lungs. He remembered the taste first—acrid, metallic, like
sucking on a battery—and then the burning that consumed his throat and chest. He had clawed his way
down the ladder, gasping, his vision already blurring. The ambulance came. The hospital smelled of
bleach and something else, something chemical and sterile that his body seemed to recognize as a
threat even as his conscious mind understood he was being treated.

The acute illness resolved within weeks, but something did not. For months afterward, Minh would
wake in the darkness of his bedroom at three or four in the morning, his heart accelerating as
though the exposure were happening again. His wife, Linh, would find him sitting on the edge of the
bed, breathing carefully, afraid to lie back down. He had come to dread the hours after sunset.
Darkness meant he could not see what was coming. Darkness meant the smell—hospital disinfectant, or
sometimes just the memory of it—would bloom in the air, and he would be unable to distinguish
whether it was real or whether his own fear was manufacturing it. He began sleeping with the bedside
lamp on. Then two lamps. Eventually he left the hallway light burning through the night as well,
which irritated Linh but which he could not bring himself to stop.

By the time he returned to work in the fall, Minh had developed a new relationship to his own mind.
The memory of the exposure would arrive unbidden and complete—not as a vague sense of something bad
having happened, but as a vivid, sequential film: the sound of the vent failing, the moment of
impact, the chemical taste, the panic of not being able to breathe. Sometimes the memory came with
images. Other times he would feel the full physical terror—the racing heart, the constricted throat,
the sense of drowning—without any clear picture at all, just the overwhelming certainty that he was
dying. He had tried, at first, to push these thoughts away, to refuse them entry. He would clench
his jaw and think of other things: his grandchildren, the garden, the recipe for pho that his mother
had taught him. But the more he tried not to think about the exposure, the more insistently it
returned, as though his resistance had made it stronger.

Work became a minefield. Minh began avoiding roofs that were unfamiliar to him. He could not assess
the safety of a new building, could not trust his ability to identify hazards. He turned down jobs.
When he had to work, he moved slowly, checking and rechecking every surface, every piece of
equipment. His boss noticed. His crew noticed. They had known Minh as a man of few words but steady
competence; now he seemed hesitant, almost afraid. When his foreman suggested he take on a larger
project, Minh felt the panic rise in his chest. Instead of explaining his fear, he simply said yes.
He could not bring himself to disagree, to assert that he was not ready. The thought of conflict, of
his foreman's disappointment or frustration, felt worse than the prospect of pushing himself beyond
what felt safe.

At home, the weight of what he had lost pressed down on him during the quiet hours. He thought often
about the man he used to be—the one who could climb without hesitation, who slept soundly, whose
mind did not betray him with unwanted images. That man was gone. Minh was certain of it. He could
not rely on his own body or his own mind anymore. Even in the safety of his living room, with Linh
beside him on the couch, he could not fully relax. Some part of him remained vigilant, waiting. The
world had revealed itself to be dangerous in ways he had never fully appreciated, and he had no
confidence that his future held anything but more of this—more fear, more sleeplessness, more
distance from the life he had built.

It was Linh who finally insisted he seek help. She had watched him withdraw, watched the lines
deepen around his eyes, watched him refuse invitations to family gatherings because he could not
predict what smells might be present or how unfamiliar spaces might trigger the panic. She told him,
with the directness of a woman who had been patient for long enough, that he needed to speak to
someone. Minh came to the clinic not because he believed anything could change, but because refusing
her had become impossible, and because some small part of him—the part that remembered who he had
been—wondered if there was any way back.
