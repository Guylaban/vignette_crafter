# Marcus

Marcus was thirty-two when the accident happened on a Tuesday afternoon in late September. He had
been framing the second story of a residential renovation in Arlington, Virginia—a job he'd bid
competitively and was determined to finish ahead of schedule. His crew that day consisted of
himself, a newer guy named Derek who was still learning the trade, and Tom, a seasoned carpenter in
his sixties who'd worked alongside Marcus for nearly a decade. They were installing headers above a
load-bearing wall when Tom stepped backward to check the level of a beam. His boot caught the edge
of an open floor joist. In the three seconds it took for him to fall, Marcus watched his body rotate
in the air. Tom landed on his left side on the concrete subfloor below—a drop of twelve feet. The
sound was not what Marcus expected. It was wet and wrong. When Marcus reached him, Tom's left arm
was bent at an angle that made no anatomical sense, and there was blood pooling beneath his head, a
metallic taste suddenly present in Marcus's mouth as if he'd bitten his own tongue, though he
hadn't.

The emergency response, the hospital waiting room, Tom's wife arriving in shock—all of it became a
kind of blur that Marcus could not quite convert into a coherent story. When he tried to explain it
to his wife, Keisha, in the days that followed, the words would not come. He could see it all with
perfect clarity: the angle of Tom's arm, the blood, the taste. But when he opened his mouth, nothing
emerged that felt true. It was as if the event existed only in his body, in the sensations that kept
replaying without his permission.

Tom survived, but with a traumatic brain injury and a shattered humerus. He would not return to
carpentry. Marcus carried the weight of that knowledge through the winter. By January, six months
after the fall, he had developed a pattern he did not fully recognize until Keisha pointed it out:
whenever he felt the memory rising—that taste, that image—he would reach for food. Late at night, he
would stand in the kitchen and eat an entire box of cereal, or a half-gallon of ice cream, not
tasting it, not wanting it, but needing the sensation of fullness, the physical act of swallowing,
something to anchor him to the present moment. He gained twenty-three pounds. His clothes no longer
fit. He stopped looking at himself in the mirror.

The job sites became increasingly difficult. In March, while installing trim in a new construction,
a subcontractor cut his hand on sheet metal. The blood—bright red, vivid—triggered the memory so
completely that Marcus lost twenty minutes. He found himself sitting in his truck with no memory of
how he'd gotten there, his hands shaking, his body drenched in sweat. He'd been somewhere else
entirely. He came to believe that his mind was no longer trustworthy, that it could leave him at any
moment without warning. The taste would come unbidden: metallic, copper-tinged, appearing in his
mouth when he was eating lunch or kissing Keisha goodnight. When it came, he would dissociate
deliberately, trying to leave his body, to blank out the sensation. Sometimes he would succeed;
other times he would be stuck between two realities—present but not present, aware of Keisha
speaking to him but unable to respond, as if he were behind thick glass.

By summer, Marcus had stopped accepting new contracts. He told clients he was overscheduled. The
truth was that he could not trust himself on a job site. He'd watched another carpenter take a fall
from a ladder in early June—nothing serious, just a stumble—but the sight of the man's bloodied knee
had sent Marcus into a dissociative state that lasted hours. His reputation in the local
construction community, built over a decade, began to quietly erode. Keisha, who worked in medical
administration, found herself increasingly the sole income earner. They had been saving for a down
payment on a house. That plan was now indefinite.

What finally brought Marcus to a therapist's office in late August was not a crisis but an
accumulation: the weight gain, the distance that had grown between him and Keisha, the knowledge
that he could not return to the work that had defined him. He sat in the intake session, unable to
fully articulate why he was there, knowing only that his life had fundamentally broken and that he
no longer recognized himself in it. He was terrified that this was permanent—that the man he'd been
before Tom's fall was gone forever, and that the person he'd become was someone he could no longer
rely on to function.
