# Kofi's Vigilance

Kofi Mensah had run Riverside Middle School for twenty-three years with the kind of steady authority
that came from believing deeply in order, fairness, and the capacity of young people to flourish
when given clear boundaries and genuine care. He was known for walking the hallways at irregular
intervals, greeting students by name, noticing when something was wrong. At seventy-seven, he had
planned to retire within two years. Then, on a Tuesday evening in March, two former students—both in
their mid-twenties, both carrying rage that had calcified over years of perceived slights—cornered
him in the parking lot as he locked up his office.

The assault lasted perhaps four minutes. What Kofi remembered with perfect, unbearable clarity was
the smell: the sharp fermentation of cheap whiskey on one attacker's breath, mixed with the metallic
tang of his own blood. He remembered the moment he realized his body would not obey his
commands—that the man holding his arm was stronger, younger, and utterly indifferent to his pleas.
He remembered the sound of his own voice breaking as he asked them to stop, the sound of their
laughter, the sound of his briefcase hitting the pavement. He remembered the precise instant he
understood that no one was coming to help him, that his authority meant nothing here, that he was
simply a body to be used.

The police found him twenty minutes later. Three ribs cracked. A concussion. Lacerations that
required eighteen stitches. The physical injuries healed within weeks. The other injuries did not.

By the following September, Kofi had developed a rigid ritual around entering any space. The moment
he stepped through a doorway—his office, the faculty lounge, the auditorium during assembly—his eyes
moved immediately to the exits. Where was the door? Could he reach it quickly? Were there obstacles?
His assistant principal, Marcus, had begun to notice that Kofi no longer sat with his back to an
open room. At faculty meetings, he positioned himself near the entrance. During student
presentations, he stood rather than sat. When a student dropped a textbook behind him, Kofi flinched
so violently that his hand went to his chest, his breathing shallow and rapid for several minutes
afterward.

What troubled Kofi most deeply was not the physical hypervigilance but the collapse of his sense of
himself as capable and trustworthy. He had always been the one others relied upon—parents called
seeking his counsel, teachers consulted him on difficult situations, students knew he would listen
and act fairly. Now, when his wife Ama suggested they attend their grandson's school play, Kofi
heard himself say no, that he was too tired, though he was not tired. The truth was that sitting in
a darkened auditorium with strangers on all sides, with exits obscured by rows of seats, filled him
with a dread so acute it felt like drowning. He could not trust himself to remain composed. What if
he panicked? What if he embarrassed himself, embarrassed Ama? It was easier to stay home.

He had begun to blame himself for the assault itself. If he had not stayed late that evening, if he
had taken a different route to his car, if he had been more aware of the two young men lingering
near the gymnasium—if he had simply been more careful, more vigilant, more something—it would not
have happened. Yet simultaneously, he had come to believe that no amount of vigilance would ever be
enough. He could not rely on his own judgment to keep himself safe. He could not rely on the police,
who had found the attackers but who seemed uninterested in pressing charges. He could not even rely
on Ama, who loved him but who could not understand why he would not simply "move past it" and return
to normal.

By November, Kofi had taken medical leave. The school board was sympathetic; he was a respected
administrator with decades of service. But Kofi understood the truth: he was afraid of his own
students, afraid of the parking lot, afraid of the moment when his vigilance might fail and
something terrible would happen again. When he remembered the assault, he sometimes heard nothing
but the laughter—it played in his mind like a recording he could not stop. Other times, he recalled
the details with such vivid precision that his body responded as if it were happening again, his
heart racing, his palms sweating. And then there were the moments when he could think about it
clinically, as though describing something that had happened to someone else, and feel nothing at
all—a numbness so complete it frightened him more than the grief did.

Ama finally insisted he see someone. She said it quietly, with the firmness that had sustained their
marriage for fifty-two years: "You are disappearing, and I cannot watch this alone." Kofi made an
appointment with Dr. Hassan, a therapist his primary care physician had recommended. He sat in the
waiting room, scanning for exits, his leg bouncing, uncertain whether he had made a terrible mistake
or finally taken the only step that might matter.
