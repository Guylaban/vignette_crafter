# Thea's Story

Thea was nineteen when she stopped trusting her hands. Six months before her intake appointment, she
had been driving home from her cousin's wedding in Tacoma—a humid June evening, the kind that made
the windshield fog. She was tired, her feet still aching from dancing in heels, and she reached down
to adjust the air conditioning. Just for a moment. Just a second. When she looked back up, the light
had changed, and the cyclist was already in her path. She felt the impact before she understood what
had happened: a sickening thud, the bicycle crumpling beneath her front tire, and then a young
man—maybe twenty-two, with a messenger bag across his chest—rolling across her hood and into the
street. His name was Marcus. He broke his left femur, his collarbone, and suffered a traumatic brain
injury that left him in the ICU for three weeks. He survived. Thea had learned this the way she
learned most things now: through fragmented pieces of information she didn't want but couldn't stop
seeking.

The first few weeks after the accident, Thea had existed in a state of hollow compliance. She went
to work at Luminous Salon in downtown Seattle, where she had been a junior stylist for eight months.
She smiled at clients. She washed hair. She cut and colored, her movements mechanical, her mind
elsewhere. But by week three, something had shifted. She began to notice the sounds—the blow dryers
with their high-pitched whine, the scissors snicking against bone, the sharp crack of bottles being
set down on the counter. Each noise sent a jolt through her chest, a phantom sensation of impact.
One afternoon, a client dropped a spray bottle, and Thea's hands began to shake so badly that she
had to excuse herself to the bathroom. She sat on the closed toilet and cried without understanding
why.

By late July, Thea had stopped driving. At first, she told herself it was practical—her car was in
the shop, the insurance claim was complicated—but the truth was that the thought of being behind the
wheel made her skin crawl. She could see it happening again, could see the cyclist's face, though
she knew from the police report that she had never actually seen his face in the moment. Her mind
had filled in the blank with something worse. When her boyfriend, Derek, offered to drive her to
work, she agreed immediately, too quickly, her voice taking on the high, careful tone it always did
when she was trying to make herself small. Derek had started to notice this about her—the way she
became almost childlike when she sensed his frustration, the way she would agree to anything to keep
the peace. He didn't understand that her compliance wasn't love; it was survival.

The anniversary of the accident fell on a Tuesday in June. Thea had marked it on her calendar
without meaning to, and when the date arrived, she called in sick to work. She stayed in bed, the
blinds drawn, unable to move. The grief came in waves—not for herself, but for Marcus, for the life
he might have had before the accident, for the fact that she was the instrument of his suffering.
Sometimes, when she allowed herself to remember the moment itself, she felt nothing at all, as
though she were watching a film of someone else's tragedy. The numbness was almost worse than the
pain because it made her feel like a stranger to herself, like she had stepped outside her own body
and couldn't find her way back in.

By the time Thea made her first appointment at the clinic, she had developed a habit of scanning
doorways, counting exits, measuring distances. In her salon chair, she positioned herself so that
she could see the front entrance. In restaurants with Derek, she grew anxious until she had located
the back exit. Her body had become a threat to her—her hands, her reflexes, her judgment. She had
come to believe that she could not be trusted with other people's safety, and more terrifyingly,
that other people could not be trusted to keep her safe either. She was nineteen years old and
already felt like she was living behind glass, watching the world move on without her.
