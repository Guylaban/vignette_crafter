# Fadi's Vigil

Fadi Mansour was fifty-eight when the shelling began in earnest. He had been working with the
international relief organization for three years by then, moving between field hospitals in a
country he had left as a boy but never quite stopped thinking of as home. The work gave him
purpose—something his divorce had stripped away five years earlier, something his political career
in Massachusetts had never quite provided despite two decades in the state legislature. In the
hospital, there was clarity: bodies needed help, medicine was needed, and he could deliver both.

The day that changed everything started like most others. Fadi was in the supply tent checking
inventory when the first blast came—not close, but close enough that the ground trembled beneath his
feet. He remembers the smell first, before anything else: burnt rubber and something charred he did
not want to identify. His colleague Hassan was beside him. They had been laughing about something
trivial moments before. The second blast was different. It was closer. The tent collapsed inward,
canvas and metal poles falling like a child's game knocked over. Fadi felt Hassan's body go limp
against his shoulder. There was blood on the supply boxes, on Fadi's hands, on the floor where
Hassan had been standing. The silence that followed—not the absence of sound, but a particular,
suffocating quiet—was worse than the noise. In that silence, Fadi was certain another blast would
come. He waited for it with every muscle tensed, breathing shallow, certain that his next breath
would be his last.

He was extracted two days later. Hassan was not.

The first months after returning home felt manageable. Fadi threw himself into legislative work with
an intensity his colleagues found admirable. He stayed late at the State House, drafted bills on
healthcare access, took constituent calls that stretched past midnight. At night, he checked his
apartment doors and windows—twice, then three times before bed. It seemed reasonable, given what he
had survived. But the checking did not stop. It became ritual: before sleep, after sleep, when
returning home from anywhere, sometimes in the middle of the night when he had jolted awake with his
heart racing. He told himself he was being prudent, careful. What he did not tell himself was that
the checking never relieved the feeling that something was about to happen, that the silence in his
apartment meant danger was gathering somewhere just beyond his perception.

Strangers became difficult. At fundraisers and town halls, Fadi found himself gripped by a peculiar
dread when introduced to someone he did not know. The handshake, the small talk—these felt
precarious, as though the stranger might suddenly say something that would unmake him. When a
colleague mentioned military strategy during a committee meeting, Fadi felt the room tilt. He
excused himself and did not return. Over months, he began to engineer his life around these moments.
He delegated constituent meetings. He avoided certain colleagues. He declined social invitations.
The work—the endless, consuming work—was safer. There he could control the narrative, control the
outcome.

Now, at sixty-four, Fadi has begun to notice the cost. His reelection campaign is in jeopardy; his
absence from community events has been noted. More troubling to him is what happens when he tries to
remember that day with any clarity. The memory does not come as a story but as fragments: Hassan's
face, the smell, the silence, his own hands covered in blood. When he tries to hold these pieces
together, to make them cohere into something he can understand, he is flooded with the same terror
that seized him in the tent. His body responds as though the blast is happening now. He has learned
to push the memory away, to distract himself, to bury it under work and checking and the small
rituals that keep him feeling safe.

What has finally brought him to a therapist's office is quieter than crisis. It is the recognition
that the silence he fears most is the one he has created around himself—the isolation that his
survival strategies have built, brick by brick. He is afraid that if he stops checking, something
will happen. He is more afraid that if he continues, there will be nothing left of his life to
protect.
