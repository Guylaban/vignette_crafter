Margot was sixty-three when she came to her first appointment, though the therapist noted she moved
with the careful deliberation of someone much older, as if her own body were something to be managed
rather than inhabited. She had been married to Henrik for thirty-eight years. They lived in a
converted farmhouse in rural Pennsylvania, where Margot had built her studio in the barn—a space
filled with clay, stone dust, and the half-finished forms of figures that had occupied her hands for
four decades. She had stopped going there six months ago.

The assault had occurred in the autumn of 1989, when Margot was twenty-five. She had been a graduate
student in Philadelphia, walking home from the university library after dark. A man had pulled her
into an alley, overpowered her, and raped her. She remembered the smell of his jacket, the taste of
concrete, the sound of a siren from blocks away—distant and useless. She had never reported it. In
the weeks after, she had told no one except Henrik, and even then only the barest facts. She had
convinced herself it was something to survive quietly and move past, the way one endures a bad
winter. For thirty-four years, she had largely succeeded.

Then, last spring, a young woman had been assaulted near the university where Margot's daughter
taught. A local news station had aired an interview with the victim describing what had happened to
her. Margot had been in the kitchen when the segment came on. The woman's voice—trembling,
precise—had triggered something that would not settle. Within days, Margot found herself unable to
be in rooms where she could not see the exits. The studio became impossible. The grocery store, with
its fluorescent lights and the unpredictability of other shoppers, became a place of mounting dread.
She began to feel safe only in the upstairs bedroom and the kitchen—rooms where she could control
the light and hear anyone approaching. She had stopped attending her book club, stopped visiting her
sister across town, stopped the weekly farmers market visits that had been her ritual for years.

Worse was the way her body had begun to betray her. In Henrik's presence, she would sometimes flinch
at his movement—when he reached for a glass beside her, when he turned suddenly in bed—and her
nervous system would flood with the certainty that she was in danger. She was ashamed of this,
ashamed that after nearly four decades of marriage, she could not trust the man who loved her. What
haunted her most was the sense that she had failed herself in the months after the assault. She had
never pressed charges, never told her parents, never even named it aloud as a crime against her. She
had simply folded it away, and in doing so, she had acted in a way that contradicted everything she
believed about herself—that she was strong, that she would stand for truth, that she would not be
diminished. She had become, in her own mind, complicit in her own erasure.

The sirens were the worst. They came without warning, and each one transported her instantly to that
alley, to the useless sound of help arriving too late. When a fire truck passed the house last
month, she had locked herself in the bathroom for an hour afterward, her hands shaking. She had also
begun to notice that remarks about sexual violence in conversation—a news story mentioned casually
by Henrik, a reference in a television program—could send her into a state of agitation that lasted
hours. She would feel the anger rising, the shame, the sense that the world was full of threats she
could not control.

Nothing felt meaningful anymore. Her sculpture, which had once been the center of her life, now
seemed like an elaborate distraction from a reality she could no longer ignore. Her marriage, once

come to believe that the assault had not truly ended in that alley in 1989—it was still happening,
