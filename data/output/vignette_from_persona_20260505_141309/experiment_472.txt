# Zahra

Zahra was thirty-eight when she came to the clinic, though her bearing suggested someone older—a
tightness around the eyes, a habit of sitting with her back to the wall. She had been a school
principal for six years, a position she had fought hard to earn after two tours as a combat medic
with the Army Reserve. The second deployment, to Kandahar in 2015, had been the one that broke
something in her that she had not yet managed to repair.

The incident itself had come without warning on a March afternoon. Zahra and her unit were
conducting a medical outreach in a village clinic when a man entered with what appeared to be a
simple abdominal wound. She remembered moving toward him, her hands already reaching for supplies,
when the vest detonated. The blast threw her backward into a concrete wall. What remained most
vivid—or most fractured—was not a coherent sequence but fragments that refused to arrange themselves
in time: the smell of burning plastic and flesh, the sound of her own voice screaming an order, a
child's hand reaching toward her from the rubble, the weight of a colleague's body across her legs.
When she tried to reconstruct the event for anyone, the pieces would not hold their order. She could
recall the smell before the blast, or after it, or could not place it at all. The sounds seemed to
occur in the wrong sequence. Sometimes she was certain she had been conscious the entire time; other
times she felt certain there were minutes, perhaps longer, that had simply vanished.

What she could not forget was the sound itself—a particular frequency of human screaming that her
mind had recorded with photographic precision. That sound returned to her unbidden, playing in her
head at unexpected moments: in the shower, during staff meetings, while she was reviewing budget
reports. It was not a memory in the conventional sense; it was an auditory hallucination, complete
and isolating, that would arrive and leave on its own schedule.

The eight years since had been a slow narrowing of her life. For the first year after discharge,
Zahra had tried to resume normalcy. She completed her master's degree in educational leadership. She
married another veteran, a marriage that lasted four years before her husband finally admitted he
could not live with someone who treated their home like a combat outpost. He was right. Even now,
alone in her apartment, Zahra moved through the rooms conducting a calculation that had become
automatic: the windows and doors, the nearest exit from each room, the objects that could be moved
quickly, the angles from which someone could enter unseen. She did not do this consciously anymore;
her body performed the assessment before her mind caught up. At the school, she had rearranged her
office three times, always positioning her desk so that she could see the entrance and the hallway
beyond. She had memorized the fire exits, the roof access, the maintenance tunnels. Her assistant
had stopped commenting on it.

What frightened Zahra most was not the possibility of another attack—though that fear lived in her
sternum, a constant low vibration—but her growing conviction that she had become incapable of
genuine connection. People could not be trusted. She had come to believe this with the certainty of
someone who had seen the cost of misplaced faith. The man in the clinic had looked ordinary. He had
been ordinary. And that ordinariness had become, in her mind, a kind of malevolent disguise that
anyone could wear. She kept her colleagues at a professional distance. She had stopped answering
calls from friends. Her ex-husband had tried, for a while, to reach past her defenses, but
eventually he had accepted that she was unreachable.

When memories of the event surfaced, which happened several times a week, a rage would flood through
her that seemed disproportionate to whatever small trigger had activated the memory—a loud noise, a
particular smell, a student's raised voice. She would find herself trembling, her jaw clenched so
tightly her teeth ached, flooded with an anger so intense it felt like it might rupture something
inside her. In those moments, she hated herself for her reactions, interpreting them as evidence of
her fundamental weakness. A strong person would have moved past this by now. A competent person
would have integrated the experience. That she had not done so proved, to her mind, that she was a
lousy coper, a fraud in her position of authority, someone who had no right to lead an institution
full of vulnerable people when she could not even manage her own mind.

It was her deputy principal who finally insisted she seek help. Zahra had nearly struck a student
who had come up behind her unexpectedly, her fist already raised before she had even registered that
the figure was a child. The deputy had witnessed it and, with a directness Zahra could not ignore,
told her that she needed professional support or she needed to step down. Zahra had come to the
clinic because she could not afford to lose the one structure that remained in her life, the one
place where her vigilance and her hypercontrol still felt like competence rather than pathology. She
was hoping that someone could help her reclaim some version of the person she had been before the
blast, though she was increasingly certain that person no longer existed. What she feared most was
that seeking help would confirm what she already believed: that the damage was permanent, and that
she would spend the rest of her life alone, unable to trust, unable to feel anything but rage and a
gnawing sense of her own inadequacy.
