# Linh's Vigil

Linh was forty-nine when she first came to the clinic, though the lines around her eyes suggested
someone older. She had served as a combat medic in Afghanistan for two tours between 2009 and 2012,
and for most of the seventeen years since her discharge, she had managed to keep what happened there
contained—locked behind a careful architecture of routine and avoidance that had slowly become the
shape of her life.

The event itself had been sudden and total. It was late 2010, and Linh was working in a forward
operating base when a vehicle detonated outside the perimeter. The explosion came with a
smell—acrid, chemical, burning flesh and rubber and something else she could never quite name—and
that smell had become the thread connecting her past to her present. She had gone to the gate with
the other medics, and what she found there had changed the texture of her consciousness permanently.
Two soldiers burned beyond recognition. A third still moving, still calling out. The smell had
gotten into her uniform, her hair, her lungs. For weeks afterward, she would catch it in her own
sweat.

The years immediately following her discharge had been deceptively stable. She had found work in IT
support at a mid-sized insurance firm, a job that required focus and problem-solving but minimal
emotional engagement. She lived alone in a modest apartment in a suburb of Portland, Oregon. She did
not date. She did not maintain close friendships. When colleagues invited her to lunch or after-work
drinks, she declined with polite firmness, and eventually they stopped asking. This isolation had
not felt like deprivation to her; it had felt like strategy. If she did not let people close, they
could not ask her questions. If she did not attend social events, she would not encounter the
unexpected—the sudden loud noise, the stranger's face too close to hers, the smell of something
burning.

But the triggers had persisted and, over time, multiplied. The smell of burnt food in the office
kitchen could send her into a state of physical alarm that lasted hours—her heart racing, her palms
sweating, her breath shallow. Helicopter sounds from news helicopters or medical transports passing
overhead would freeze her in place, her body convinced that the past was arriving again. And the
workplace itself, despite its apparent safety, had become a place where she felt watched, where she
could not fully relax. She had begun to position her desk so that her back was to the wall and she
could see the door. She found herself checking behind her as she moved through the halls, a habit so
ingrained now that she barely noticed doing it.

The physical reactivity had become severe enough to interfere with her work. Six months ago, a
colleague had approached her desk from behind to ask a question, and Linh had jolted so violently
that she had knocked over her coffee and sent papers scattering. The colleague had apologized,
confused, and Linh had felt the familiar shame—the sense that her body was betraying her, that she
was overreacting to nothing. But the fear was not nothing to her. Strangers still felt dangerous.
The casual approach of someone she did not know well could trigger a cascade of hypervigilance that
would take hours to settle.

Over the past two years, she had begun to hurt herself when the memories became too loud—pinching
the soft skin of her inner arm until it bruised, holding an ice cube in her fist until the pain
eclipsed the intrusive images. It was a way of stopping the thinking, of replacing one kind of
suffering with another she could control. She had also stopped talking about the war entirely. When
a new therapist at her employee assistance program had gently asked about her military service, Linh
had felt a wall of resistance so strong that she had never returned for a second session.

What had finally brought her to this clinic was a moment of clarity that had arrived like a small
catastrophe. She was now the only person on her IT support team who had not been promoted in five
years. Her manager, well-meaning and frustrated, had suggested that Linh might benefit from a team-
building retreat. The word "retreat" had triggered a dark laugh—she could not imagine a scenario
less likely to help her. But in that moment, she had recognized that her life had contracted to a
point where it was barely livable. She was fifty pounds heavier than she had been at discharge, she
had not had a conversation that lasted more than five minutes in months, and she was afraid of
almost everything. The burnt smell would always find her. The helicopters would always come. But
perhaps there was another way to live with them.
