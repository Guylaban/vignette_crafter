# Amara's Testimony

Amara had been forty-two years old when she fled Sierra Leone in 2005, and she had been sixty-three
for the past eight months. The gap between those two ages—twenty-one years of her life compressed
into a series of decisions made under duress, each one stripping away some part of who she believed
herself to be—had never fully closed. She lived in that interval still, even now, in a one-bedroom
apartment in Atlanta where she had built a careful life as a financial analyst, a profession that
rewarded precision, control, and the ability to see patterns in numbers that others missed. Numbers
did not betray you. Numbers did not have uniforms or guns or the power to decide whether you lived
or died.

The war had come to her village in the Kailahun District in waves, each one preceded by rumors that
turned out to be true. When the rebels came through in the spring of 2005, they had separated the
men from the women and children, and Amara had watched her husband Kofi being pulled from the group
by soldiers whose faces she could no longer quite picture but whose voices—the tone of absolute
command—she could still hear in her sleep. She was told to move, to walk, to leave everything
behind. When she hesitated for a moment—a single moment—a rifle butt struck her across the
shoulders, and she understood then that hesitation itself was a kind of resistance that would not be
tolerated. She walked. She was forced to walk for three days through the bush with dozens of other
women and children, and when she tried to help an elderly woman who had collapsed, she was struck
again and told that mercy was not permitted. That woman did not get up. Amara never knew her name.

She had eventually reached a refugee camp in Guinea, where she remained for four years before an NGO
helped her secure passage to the United States. She arrived in 2009 with a work visa, a degree in
accounting from Freetown, and a body that had learned to make itself small and compliant. She built
her career methodically—her analytical mind was an asset that no one could take from her—and she had
made a quiet life. She had few close friends. She had declined several romantic advances, telling
herself she was content alone, though the truth was more complicated: she could not bear the thought
of depending on someone, of being vulnerable to another person's choices or whims.

But in the past two years, her carefully ordered existence had begun to fracture. It started with
her niece Ama, who had moved to Atlanta to attend university. Amara loved her fiercely, and that
love had become a kind of torment. She found herself lying awake at night, catastrophizing—imagining
Ama in car accidents, attacked on campus, assaulted in her dormitory. Every time Ama was late
responding to a text message, Amara's mind spiraled into scenarios of disaster. She would call
repeatedly, her voice tight with an anxiety she could not quite explain to her niece, who had begun
to withdraw from her, confused by the intensity of Amara's worry.

At work, Amara had become increasingly isolated. She avoided meetings where she might have to
disagree with a colleague or assert a different viewpoint. When her manager, a white woman named
Patricia, had suggested that Amara's analysis of a client portfolio was incomplete, Amara had felt
her throat close. She could not argue back, could not defend her work, could not risk the
confrontation. She had simply nodded and redone the analysis, though she believed her original
assessment was sound. She began requesting assignments that allowed her to work alone, and her
manager had started to pass her over for promotions that required collaboration and visibility.

What had finally broken through her denial was a routine traffic stop three months ago. A police
officer had pulled her over for a broken taillight, and when he approached her window, something in
her body had simply shut down. She had sat frozen, her hands visible on the steering wheel, barely
breathing, while he checked her license and registration. He had been polite, professional, had
issued a warning rather than a ticket. But Amara had spent the next week unable to drive, unable to
leave her apartment without calculating escape routes, unable to shake the certainty that authority
figures of any kind represented an existential threat. She had called in sick to work. She had
ignored Ama's messages. She had sat in her apartment and felt the walls of her life contract around
her.

It was Ama who finally insisted she seek help, who had researched therapists and made the
appointment herself, who had told Amara that she could not live like this anymore—that Amara could
not live like this anymore. Amara sits in the waiting room now, her purse held tight against her
chest, terrified of what talking about the past might unleash, but more terrified still of the
possibility that she might lose her niece, that her fear might push away the one person who still
tethered her to a future worth living.
