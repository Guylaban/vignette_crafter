# Clinical Vignette: Maya Chen

Maya Chen was thirty years old when she came to the clinic on a Tuesday afternoon in March, her
appointment card clutched in her trembling hands. She had been kidnapped at gunpoint from a parking
garage in downtown Denver four years earlier—taken by a man she had rejected at a bar weeks before.
He had held her for thirty-six hours in an abandoned warehouse, bound and blindfolded, subjecting
her to repeated physical and sexual violence. The blindfold had stayed on for most of it. She had
not known what would happen next, or when. She had not known if she would live.

When the police found her, she had been grateful to be alive. For months afterward, she had tried to
move forward, to be the person she had been before. She had returned to her job at First National
Bank, where she had worked as a teller for six years. But something fundamental had shifted in her
nervous system, and the world had become a place of constant danger.

The triggers that now ruled her life had a terrible coherence. Expressions of anger—a customer's
raised voice at the counter, a colleague's sharp tone in a meeting, even a husband's frustrated
sigh—sent her into a state of vigilant terror. Because her captor's mood had been unpredictable, his
anger had often preceded the worst violence. Even calm anger was dangerous to her. She had come to
believe that any display of frustration from another person was a warning sign, a prelude to harm.
At the bank, she had begun to avoid the branch manager, Derek, whose voice sometimes carried an edge
of impatience when he reviewed the daily reconciliations. She would finish her shift and leave
without checking her drawer properly, terrified of his potential irritation, which meant her counts
were often off. Derek had begun to document these errors. She knew she was failing at a job she had
once performed competently, but the thought of facing his anger was worse than the professional
consequences.

The sensation of being watched had become equally unbearable. Her captor had watched her constantly,
his eyes on her body, assessing her, waiting. Now, when customers looked at her across the counter,
or when she felt a colleague's glance, her skin would prickle with the certainty that something
terrible was about to happen. She had begun to avoid eye contact with everyone—customers, her
supervisor, even her husband. This had made her work more difficult and had created a strange
distance in her marriage. Her husband, David, had started to ask if she was angry with him, if she
was pulling away on purpose. She could not explain that looking into his eyes felt like standing in
front of her captor again, that the intimacy of his gaze was unbearable. Sexual situations had
become impossible. When David approached her with affection, she would freeze, her body flooded with
the sensations of the warehouse—the smell of concrete and mold, the feeling of restraint, the
helplessness. She had begun to sleep in the guest room, telling David she needed space, not knowing
how to say that his touch felt like a threat.

Her nights were fractured. She would wake at two or three in the morning, her heart pounding, seized
by the need to check that the doors were locked, that the windows were secure, that David was still
there and not someone else. The silence of the house terrified her most. In silence, she was
convinced that danger was gathering, that something was about to happen. She had taken to leaving
the television on all night, the low murmur of voices a kind of protective barrier against the
threat she felt lurking in the quiet.

The memories themselves were the worst part. Without warning, something would trigger them—a news
report about a crime, the smell of concrete dust when the bank had renovation work done, the sound
of a car backfiring outside. In those moments, she was back in the warehouse completely. She could
smell the mold and the sweat. She could feel the rope around her wrists. She could taste the gag.
The emotion that flooded her was so overwhelming—terror, shame, rage—that she would have to excuse
herself from the teller line, lock herself in the bathroom, and wait for it to pass. It usually took
twenty minutes or more. Her manager had begun to notice. She had taken three weeks off work after
the last severe episode, telling David she had the flu.

Over the past months, she had developed a habit of compulsive spending. When the anxiety became too
much, she would go online and order things—clothes she did not need, books she would not read,
kitchen gadgets she already owned. The act of purchasing, of clicking the button, of receiving
packages, gave her a momentary sense of control and relief. Her credit card debt had climbed to
nearly eight thousand dollars. David had found the statements last month. They had argued. He did
not understand. She did not know how to explain that spending was the only thing that quieted the
constant noise in her head, the only thing that made her feel like she existed outside of the
terror.

What had finally brought her to seek help was a conversation with David last week. He had said,
quietly, that he was worried about her, that he missed her, that he did not know how to help. He had
not said it in anger. He had said it with love. And that had broken something open in her—the
realization that she was losing her marriage, her job, her sense of herself, to a past she could not
seem to escape. She was terrified that therapy would not work, that she would be trapped in this
state forever. But she was more terrified of losing David, of becoming someone so small and
frightened that there was nothing left of her at all.
