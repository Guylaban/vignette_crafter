# Clinical Vignette: Aleksandr

Aleksandr was seventy-six when he walked into the clinic on a Tuesday morning, moving with the
careful, economical gait of a man who has learned not to draw attention to himself. He had been a
cashier at a supermarket in New Jersey for the past eighteen years—a job he had taken after arriving
in America, after everything. His daughter had finally insisted he come, after he had stopped
driving altogether and begun calling her at work to ask her to drive him to appointments, to the
grocery store, to nowhere in particular because he could no longer bear to sit alone in his
apartment.

In 1992, Aleksandr had been living in Sarajevo with his wife Mirjana and their two children. He was
a translator then, educated, respected in his community. The siege had begun in April. By July, he
was no longer certain which day it was. He remembered the sound of mortars—not as something he heard
but as something that lived inside his chest, a percussion that had become his heartbeat. He
remembered standing in a queue for water with Mirjana, and the sudden crack of a sniper's shot, and
Mirjana falling, and the way her hand had been warm for only a few seconds after. He remembered
carrying her, remembered the hospital, remembered the moment the doctor's face told him everything
before his mouth did. He remembered the screaming—his own, he thought, though it had sounded like
someone else's.

What he carried now, fifty years later, were mostly images. Not the narrative of those months but
fragments: the pattern of blood on a white wall. A child's shoe in the street. His own hands shaking
as he tried to load a suitcase. When he closed his eyes, these images did not feel like
memories—they felt like windows opening onto a present moment that never quite ended. His therapist
had asked him to describe what happened, and he had found that he could recount the facts with a
strange flatness, as though he were reading from a newspaper about someone else's tragedy. But the
images themselves burned with an immediacy that made his skin crawl.

For decades, Aleksandr had managed. He had built a narrow, predictable life. He worked his shift at
the register, came home, sat in his apartment. After Mirjana died—a stroke, fifteen years ago—he had
become even more solitary. But in the past three years, something had shifted. The hypervigilance
that had once served him, that constant readiness to detect threat, had become unbearable. At the
supermarket, he found himself watching customers' hands, tracking their movements, noting exits. He
watched the young men who came through his line with particular intensity, searching their faces for
the kind of rage he had seen before. He had come to believe that the world was fundamentally
dangerous, that people were capable of sudden, inexplicable violence, and that his job—his only real
function now—was to remain alert enough to see it coming.

Three months ago, he had stopped driving. He had been on the highway, a passenger in his daughter's
car, when a motorcycle had passed them quickly on the left. The sound had triggered something—not
quite a memory but a collapse into one. He had felt the mortar strike again. His hands had gripped
the dashboard. For weeks after, he could not sit in a moving vehicle without a crushing sense of
danger. Driving himself was worse; it required him to surrender his vigilance to the mechanics of
the car. He had begun making excuses, then had stopped making excuses at all. His daughter drove him
now, and he hated himself for it, because he was no longer the man who could do things, who could
move through the world independently. That man had died in Sarajevo.

The sleep had gone too. He would lie awake until two or three in the morning, his mind cycling
through scenarios—what if someone broke in, what if he had missed something, what if he let his
guard down for one moment. The numbness was strange: sometimes he could recall the day Mirjana died
and feel nothing, as though he were watching an actor perform his own grief. Other times, the images
would come and he would be there again, present in the moment of her falling, and the relief of the
numb recollection would vanish.

What had finally brought him in was something smaller and larger at once. His daughter had invited
him to her grandson's birthday party. For the first time in months, Aleksandr had felt something
like happiness—the boy was turning seven, Aleksandr's age when the war had taken his own childhood.
But as the party approached, Aleksandr had felt the happiness curdle into something dangerous. Joy
felt like a betrayal. Happiness felt like lowering his guard, like inviting catastrophe. He had
pushed the feeling away, made an excuse not to go. His daughter had wept. And Aleksandr, sitting
alone in his apartment, had realized that he had lost not just his wife and his country and his
capacity to move freely through the world, but also his right to feel anything good without terror
following behind it like a shadow.
