# Clinical Vignette: Marco

Marco was twenty-two when his daughter Sofía was born—a moment he had rehearsed a thousand times in
his mind, imagining the weight of her in his arms, the way her small fingers would curl around his
thumb. He and Sofía's mother had separated before the pregnancy was far along, but Marco had never
wavered in his intention to be present, to be the father his own had never been. When Sofía arrived,
three weeks early and smaller than expected, Marco took leave from his junior role at the analytics
firm and spent eighteen hours a day at the hospital, learning to change her diaper, studying the
monitors that beeped softly beside her isolette, memorizing the rhythm of her breathing.

At four months old, Sofía developed a fever that climbed past one hundred and four degrees in a
matter of hours. Marco was alone with her when it happened—he was changing her when he noticed her
body felt wrong, hot and somehow wrong in a way that made his stomach drop. He called 911 in a state
of clarity he would later struggle to reconstruct. What he remembered instead was the siren—the
sound of it approaching the apartment, a wail that seemed to split the air itself, and then the
paramedics moving with a speed and efficiency that terrified him more than the fever did, because
they moved like people who had seen children die before. At the hospital, Sofía spent eight days in
the pediatric intensive care unit with bacterial meningitis. Marco did not leave. He sat beside her
incubator, watching her small body convulse with seizures, watching her pupils dilate and contract,
watching doctors and nurses move in and out with expressions he had learned to read as grave. When
she cried—a thin, weak sound that bore no resemblance to her normal cry—he felt something break
inside himself that he had not yet found a way to repair.

Sofía recovered. By the time she was discharged, the infection had cleared, and the neurologists
said there was no evidence of permanent damage. Marco returned to work after two weeks, and for a
time, he told himself the worst was over. But the worst had not ended; it had simply changed shape.

Now, at twenty-five, Marco startle at sounds he barely registers—a car horn, a door slamming, the
beep of the microwave in the office kitchen—and his body goes rigid, his heart hammering as though
the siren is approaching again. He has not slept through a single night in three years. He lies
awake in the dark, listening, alert for any sound that might signal danger, unable to believe that
Sofía is safe in her mother's apartment across the city. The conviction that something catastrophic
is about to happen sits beneath his ribs like a stone.

What frightened him most was not the memory itself but the fact that he could not hold it steady in
his mind. When he tried to recall the sequence of events, they fractured and rearranged themselves.
Had the fever come first, or the call to 911? Was the siren before or after the paramedics arrived?
The memory felt too extreme, too saturated with terror, and his mind seemed to reject it as though
it could not possibly be true—even though he knew, with absolute certainty, that it was. When he
allowed himself to remember, the terror returned in full, undiminished, as though no time had passed
at all.

He had learned, without consciously deciding to, never to feel angry. Anger felt dangerous, as
though it might crack something open. When frustration rose in him—at a slow database query, at a
colleague's mistake, at his own helplessness—he forced it down, swallowed it, moved on. He did not
cry, not ever, not even alone. Crying felt like losing control, and he could not afford to lose
control.

He began avoiding hospitals, ambulances, even his annual physical. He told himself he was fine, that
there was no point in medical examinations, that staying away from those places meant staying away
from the possibility of catastrophe. At work, his avoidance had begun to cost him. Two months ago,
his firm had asked him to present findings to a healthcare client, and Marco had declined without
explanation. His manager had not pushed, but Marco could see the concern in his face, the
calculation that something was wrong. He had not been promoted last quarter; he suspected it was
connected to his withdrawal.

Six weeks ago, Sofía had asked her mother why her papá never came to visit anymore. He did visit—he
was careful about that—but perhaps not carefully enough. The question had lodged itself in his
chest, and it was still there, a small sharp stone that he could not dislodge. Last week, he had
finally called a therapist, not because he believed anything could change, but because he could no
longer live as he was living. What he most feared was that the terror would never release him, that
he would spend the rest of his life waiting for the next catastrophe. What he hoped for, though he
barely dared to articulate it, was that one day he might hold his daughter without his hands
shaking.
