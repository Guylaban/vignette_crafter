# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was thirty-two when it happened. He had been working as a school counselor for six years at
Lincoln Middle School, a position he took seriously—the kind of man who stayed late to finish case
notes, who remembered the names of kids' siblings, who believed that showing up for students
mattered. On an April afternoon in 2022, he was driving home from a professional development
workshop when a child darted into the street from between two parked cars. Marcus was going thirty-
five miles per hour. He braked hard, but the physics of the moment were unforgiving. The boy—he
learned later his name was Tyler, age nine—went down beneath the front wheel. The sound was not like
anything Marcus had ever heard: a wet, heavy crunch that seemed to separate him from the world as he
had known it. He sat in his car for what might have been seconds or minutes, unable to move, unable
to breathe properly. When he got out, there was blood on the asphalt and on the undercarriage of his
vehicle. Tyler's mother was screaming. The police arrived. An ambulance. The hospital. Tyler
survived, but with a fractured femur and internal injuries that required surgery.

Marcus was not charged. The police investigation concluded the accident was unavoidable. But the
conclusion did nothing to shift what had crystallized in Marcus's mind in those first hours: that he
had done this. That something in how he was—his attention, his reflexes, his choices that day—had
been fundamentally wrong. He had come to believe that he could not trust himself to do the right
thing, that his judgment was broken in some irreparable way. How could he have been going that fast
in a school zone? Why had he not been more alert? The questions spiraled without resolution.

In the weeks that followed, sleep became impossible. Marcus would lie awake beside his wife, Sarah,
his body rigid, his mind cycling through the accident frame by frame. He was certain that if he
allowed himself to sleep, something catastrophic would happen—to her, to someone else. Staying alert
felt like the only responsible thing left to do. He began keeping his mind occupied constantly:
podcasts during his commute, audiobooks at night, work emails answered at two in the morning.
Anything to prevent the memory from surfacing. But the memory surfaced anyway, and when it did, it
was complete and overwhelming. He could smell the metallic tang of blood, feel the slight vibration
of impact travel through the steering wheel, hear that sound again, see Tyler's body in the street.
It was not a memory—it was the event itself, happening again, indistinguishable from the original
moment.

The smell trigger was the worst. One morning at school, a student came to his office after a
nosebleed, and the scent of blood hit Marcus before he could process what was happening. His hands
shook so violently he could not hold his pen. His heart raced as though he were about to crash
again. He excused himself to the bathroom and sat on the floor of a stall for twenty minutes. It was
the same with medical procedures: when his wife insisted he get a physical, the smell of the
examination room—antiseptic, sterile, carrying some echo of hospitals and trauma—sent him spiraling.
He scheduled the appointment but cancelled it twice. His doctor's office stopped calling.

At work, his colleagues began to notice his distance. He stopped attending staff meetings, claiming
scheduling conflicts. He avoided the parking lot where his car sat, taking a longer route through
the building. More significantly, he found himself unable to counsel students effectively. A girl
came to him with anxiety about an accident she had witnessed, and Marcus realized halfway through
the session that his own hands were trembling, that he could not speak about trauma without his
voice cracking. He requested a transfer to administrative duties. His supervisor, concerned,
suggested he take some time off. Marcus refused. Taking time off meant being alone with his
thoughts, and his thoughts were a prison.

Now, two years later, Marcus carries the weight of a life he believes has been destroyed. He avoids
thinking about the accident through constant distraction, yet the memory intrudes anyway—vivid,
sensory, present. He avoids police, crossing the street if he sees a patrol car, terrified that new
information will emerge, that he will be blamed retroactively. He avoids the stress of his former
role, though the avoidance itself has become a kind of professional death, a slow erosion of the
career he once valued. Sarah has grown quiet around him, sensing his shame but unable to reach him.
Marcus has not told anyone the full weight of what he carries: the certainty that he is dangerous,
that his judgment cannot be trusted, that the life he had—the marriage, the work, the sense of being
someone who helped people—has been irrevocably taken from him.

It was Sarah who finally insisted he seek help. She found a therapist's number on his phone's search
history, evidence that some part of him was looking for a way out, and she made the appointment for
him. Marcus arrived at the first session terrified and ashamed, hoping desperately that someone
could tell him how to live with what he had done, while fearing that no one could.
