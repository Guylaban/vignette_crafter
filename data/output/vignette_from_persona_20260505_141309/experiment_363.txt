# Jasmine's Story

Jasmine was nineteen when she married Marcus, a decision that had felt like the right one at the
time—a commitment to someone who made her feel seen. She had been working part-time at a coffee shop
near campus, saving money, imagining a future that felt manageable and warm. That was before the
night in October when everything fractured into something unrecognizable.

She had been walking home from a late shift, cutting through the parking garage as she always did. A
man had been waiting. She had not seen his face clearly in the darkness, but she heard his
voice—sharp, commanding, in Arabic, a language she did not understand but that seemed to carry a
violence of its own. He had smelled of alcohol, thick and suffocating. There had been the sound of
his motorcycle outside the garage afterward, the engine roaring as he fled, a sound that had etched
itself into her nervous system with such precision that months later, any motorcycle in the distance
would send her body into a state of alert that felt like drowning while standing still.

In the weeks that followed, Jasmine had tried to return to work. But the coffee shop, with its open
layout and glass doors, had begun to feel indefensible. She found herself listening constantly for
sounds—the scrape of a chair, the jingle of keys—interpreting each one as a potential threat. One
afternoon, a customer had shouted in Arabic to his friend across the café, and Jasmine had frozen,
her hands shaking so badly that she dropped an entire tray of cups. She had not returned after that
shift. Marcus had said it was okay, that he could support them both, but the loss of that small
independence had added another weight to the ones already pressing down on her chest.

The nights became impossible. She could not bring herself to go outside after dark. The thought of
being in the open, where someone could approach from behind, made her skin crawl. She had taken to
checking behind her constantly, even in their apartment, even in rooms she had just looked at
moments before. The checking never brought relief, only a temporary pause before the fear crept back
in. Marcus had noticed her doing it—spinning around, peering into shadows—and she could see the
worry in his eyes, the unspoken question about whether she was becoming someone he did not
recognize. She was becoming someone she did not recognize.

Certain dates had become unbearable. The anniversary of the assault approached like a storm she
could see on the horizon, and the dread began weeks in advance. She had started avoiding that entire
week in October, staying in bed, refusing to engage with the world. The smell of alcohol—on Marcus's
breath after he came home from seeing friends, in the air at a grocery store—would send her
spiraling backward into that garage, the sensations returning with a vividness that made her
question whether she was remembering or reliving.

What frightened her most was not just the fear itself, but what she had come to believe about
herself. She had once been someone who worked, who moved through the world with a kind of confidence
that now felt like a fantasy she had invented. She found herself unable to look at her own body
without feeling a deep revulsion, as though her skin had become a thing that had betrayed her. She
would shower with her eyes closed, dress without mirrors. When she thought about what had happened,
she did not just see it—she heard the Arabic words again, smelled the alcohol, felt the weight of a
hand she could not escape. The memories arrived without warning, triggered by the smallest sensory
echo, and she had no defense against them.

What haunted her most, though, was the conviction that someone else—someone braver, someone
stronger—would have fought back, would have stopped it from happening. The world, she had come to
believe, was not a place where safety existed. It was a place where violence waited in parking
garages and in the voices of strangers, and she was not capable of surviving it the way other people
might.

It was Marcus who finally insisted she see someone. He had found her one morning sitting on the
bathroom floor, unable to move, and something in his face—fear mixed with helplessness—had broken
through her isolation enough to make her agree. She had called a therapist that afternoon, her hands
trembling as she dialed, terrified of what it would mean to speak it all aloud, but more terrified
of the alternative: a life spent checking behind her, avoiding her own body, waiting for the next
sound that would send her back to that garage.
