# Kofi's Vigil

Kofi was eighteen when he joined the police academy, driven by a conviction that had crystallized in
him years earlier: that he would never again be powerless to stop violence. Now, six months into his
first posting, he found himself unable to trust the very vigilance that had propelled him into
uniform.

The night he was nine, his father had come home to their Lagos-born household in Atlanta with a rage
that seemed to fill the apartment like smoke. Kofi's mother had made a comment—something small, he
could never quite remember what—and his father's voice had dropped to a register that meant danger
was coming. Kofi had pressed himself against the kitchen wall, his small body rigid, watching. What
he remembered most vividly was not the sound of his mother's cry or the crash of the lamp, but the
precise moment his father's hand had closed around her throat. That image lived in him as a single,
terrible frame: her face, his father's knuckles, the way the light caught the glass. When the memory
surfaced now, Kofi's body would stiffen in exactly the same posture—shoulders hunched, arms drawn
inward, breath shallow—as if his nervous system were replaying not just the scene but his own frozen
terror.

In the weeks after that night, Kofi had become a student of threat. He learned to read the subtle
shifts in his father's mood, the way silence in their home could mean a storm was gathering. By
adolescence, this skill had become automatic, almost invisible to him. But it had also calcified
into something else: a certainty that danger was always imminent, that ordinary moments concealed
violence, and that only constant vigilance could protect him or anyone he loved.

When he joined the police academy, Kofi believed he was finally choosing a profession that matched
his nature. He was good at noticing things—the way someone's hand moved toward their pocket, the
object on a shelf that could become a weapon. His instructors praised his situational awareness.
What they did not see was that Kofi could not walk into a room without cataloguing every potential
implement of harm: the edge of a table, the weight of a pen, the trajectory of a door swing. He
noticed these things not as a trained officer but as a boy still braced for his father's violence.

The job itself had become increasingly difficult. Two months ago, a senior officer had placed a hand
on his shoulder during roll call—a casual gesture of camaraderie. Kofi had flinched so violently
that he'd knocked over a chair. The other officers had laughed, but Kofi had felt something crack
open inside him: a terror so immediate and absolute that he could not explain it away as
professional caution. Since then, he had begun avoiding the station's common areas. He took his
breaks in his patrol car, ate lunch alone, and found reasons to work the quieter precincts where he
might encounter fewer people.

Unfamiliar neighborhoods had become impossible to enter. Kofi would volunteer for assignments in
areas he already knew—the same streets, the same landmarks—because the thought of driving into a
place he could not fully assess filled him with a paralysis that felt like drowning. His sergeant
had begun to notice his pattern of route preferences, and last week had asked if something was
wrong. Kofi had no answer that would not sound like weakness.

What frightened him most was the grief that came with the memories. When he was alone in his car at
night and the snapshot of his mother's face would return, he would find himself unable to breathe,
overcome by a sorrow so complete that he wondered if he was losing his mind. His reactions seemed
disproportionate to anything a functioning officer should experience. He had come to believe that
his own mind was turning against him, that his hypervigilance and his paralysis were symptoms of
something breaking inside him. He could not stop bad things from happening—not to his mother then,
not to anyone now. And he could not even trust himself to know whether his fear was rational or
whether he was simply going crazy.

It was his mother who finally insisted he seek help. She had noticed him withdrawing, had heard the
tremor in his voice when he called her. She told him that what happened to them both that night was
still happening, and that he needed someone to help him understand why. Kofi came to the appointment
expecting to be told that the job was not for him, that his reactions proved he was unfit. What he
hoped for, though he barely dared to name it, was that someone might help him separate the boy who
had witnessed violence from the man he was trying to become.
