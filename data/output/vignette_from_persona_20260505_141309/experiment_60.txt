# Carmen's Story

Carmen Delgado was fifty-nine when she finally made an appointment with a trauma therapist, though
she had been living with the weight of her childhood for nearly five decades. She worked as a
physical therapist in a busy orthopedic clinic in Phoenix, a job she had built carefully over thirty
years—a career that required her to touch other people's bodies with precision and care, to help
them move without pain. It was work that had always felt redemptive to her, a way of using her hands
for healing instead of the violence she had known as a child.

The violence had come from her mother's boyfriend, a man named Miguel who had moved into their small
apartment in East Los Angeles when Carmen was seven years old. Her mother worked two jobs; Miguel
worked sporadically and drank heavily. Carmen remembered the particular smell of his sweat and cheap
whiskey, the sound of his belt being unbuckled, the way her body had learned to anticipate pain
before it arrived. The abuse was physical, sometimes severe, sometimes merely the threat of it—a
raised hand, a step toward her, his voice dropping to a certain register that made her stomach
clench. By the time she was twelve, she had learned to be invisible: to move quietly, to keep her
shoulders hunched, to never make eye contact. She had learned that the world was dangerous and that
her body was the place where that danger lived.

In her fifties, Carmen had come to believe that if she controlled her environment absolutely, she
could prevent what had happened from happening again. She cleaned obsessively—not because her
apartment was dirty, but because the act of organizing, of scrubbing, of putting things in their
precise places, created an illusion of safety. She would spend entire Saturday afternoons
reorganizing her kitchen cabinets, her closet, her files. When stress accumulated—a difficult
patient at work, a phone call from her ex-husband—she would eat, consuming entire containers of pan
dulce or enchiladas in an evening, the fullness in her stomach a temporary substitute for the
emptiness she carried. She avoided anything that might be stressful: she did not go to social
gatherings, did not accept invitations from colleagues, did not watch the news. Her world had become
smaller and smaller, a carefully controlled perimeter.

At the clinic, Carmen had developed a particular way of working that her colleagues had come to
recognize as her style. She always positioned herself with her back to the wall during patient
consultations, arranging the treatment room so that she could see the door and anyone approaching.
When a new patient arrived, especially a man she did not know, her body would tense. She told
herself it was reasonable caution—a woman alone in a room with a stranger—but the intensity of her
wariness exceeded what the situation called for. A man reaching for a door handle would make her
flinch. A raised voice from the waiting room would send her heart into her throat.

Sleep had become fragmented. Carmen would wake at two or three in the morning, her body alert, and
she would lie still in the darkness, listening. Sometimes she would get up and check the locks on
her doors and windows, a ritual she had performed for so long that she no longer questioned it. On
some of these nights, fragments of memory would surface without warning: not the full story of what
had happened, but pieces—the texture of a fabric, the angle of light through a window, the feeling
of her own breath held in her chest. These shards would appear suddenly and then dissolve, leaving
her shaken and disoriented in the dark.

What was most confusing to Carmen was that sometimes when she tried to recall the events directly,
to make sense of them, she felt nothing. The memories existed in her mind as though they belonged to
someone else—vivid in their details but utterly divorced from emotion, as though she were reading a
case file rather than remembering her own childhood. At other moments, the opposite was true: the
memories felt so extreme, so brutal, that she could not quite believe they had actually happened to
her. She would think, *This cannot be real. This cannot have been my life.* The unreality of it made
her doubt her own mind.

Six months ago, Carmen had been injured—a small accident at home, a fall that had aggravated her
lower back. She had tried to treat it herself, but the pain persisted, and finally she had to see a
colleague, allowing hands other than her own to examine her spine. The experience of being touched,
even clinically, even by someone she trusted, had cracked something open. In the weeks that
followed, the avoidance had intensified. She began calling in sick more frequently. She stopped
going to the physical therapy clinic at all, citing a need for a leave of absence. The income loss
was becoming a problem; the isolation was becoming unbearable.

It was her sister Rosa who had finally insisted. Rosa, who had escaped their mother's house years
before Carmen, who had built a life with a family of her own, had called one evening and said, "You
cannot live like this anymore. You need help." Carmen had resisted, but something in her had begun
to break. She was tired—exhausted by the vigilance, the cleaning, the constant management of her own
fear. She wanted to understand why a stranger approaching her in the grocery store could make her
feel like a child again, why she could not sleep without checking the locks, why the memory of her
own trauma felt both too real and too unreal to bear.
