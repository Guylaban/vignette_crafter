# Tane

Tane was forty-seven when the accident happened. He had been working in community organizing for
over a decade by then, running youth outreach programs in South Auckland, and he was good at the
work—the kind of man who showed up, who listened, who made young people feel seen. On a Tuesday
afternoon in March, he was driving a van full of teenagers back from a field trip to a local
environmental center. The rain had come down hard that morning, and the roads were still slick. He
remembered the sky clearing. He remembered a radio announcement. Then there was nothing—a gap like
someone had taken scissors to the film of his life. When consciousness returned, he was upside down,
metal screaming, the smell of fuel and something else, something like copper. A boy was crying.
Tane's right leg was pinned. The next clear image was a police officer's face through the broken
window, then the sound of the jaws of life, then pain so sharp it seemed to split him open. Three
teenagers had been injured. None had died. Tane had spent eight weeks in hospital.

The physical recovery took months. The psychological one, he had come to understand, might never
finish. It was now sixteen years later, and Tane still could not account for the middle section of
the accident—the moment of impact itself remained sealed off, inaccessible. He knew intellectually
what must have happened: the van had hydroplaned, crossed the centerline, struck a utility vehicle
head-on. But he could not access the sensory reality of it, could not find the words to describe
even what he did remember. When he tried to talk about it in those early years, his mouth would open
and close like a fish pulled from water. Eventually he stopped trying.

What he could not stop were the thoughts. They arrived unbidden and unwelcome—images of the
teenagers' faces, scenarios in which the outcome had been different, darker. The thoughts felt like
intrusions, like something pressing against his skull from the inside. He had come to believe, with
a certainty that felt immovable, that the accident had been his fault. He had been driving. He had
made the decision to take that route. He had not anticipated the rain. The logic was circular and
airtight: if he had acted differently—left earlier, chosen a different road, driven slower—those
young people would not have been in danger. The fact that they had survived did not absolve him; it
only meant he had been lucky, and luck was not the same as innocence.

Over the years, Tane had constructed a careful architecture of avoidance. He no longer drove. He
took the bus, walked, or accepted rides from colleagues. He stopped attending community events where
police or security personnel might be present in uniform; the sight of that blue or black fabric,
the badge and belt, sent his heart into a gallop and made his palms sweat uncontrollably. His body,
he had come to feel, was a traitor—it reacted as if danger were imminent even when he sat safely in
his living room. Sometimes his leg would twitch involuntarily, or his chest would tighten, and he
would think: *there is nothing happening, but my body does not believe that*. The disconnect was
profound. He felt dead inside, as though the part of him that could feel joy or connection had been
severed in that moment of impact and had never regenerated.

Most difficult was his response to anger in others. When a colleague raised their voice in a
meeting, even with legitimate frustration about a funding delay, Tane's nervous system would flood
with alarm. His hands would shake. He would excuse himself. Over time, he began avoiding meetings
where conflict might arise, then avoiding colleagues who seemed prone to intensity, then avoiding
the office altogether. His supervisor had gently suggested he might consider part-time work. The
irony was not lost on him: a man whose life's work was community building had become increasingly
isolated, increasingly unreachable.

At sixty-three, Tane sat in his small apartment in Wellington, where he had moved after retiring
early, and admitted to himself that something had to change. The isolation was complete now. The
unwanted thoughts had become louder. His body's betrayals had become more frequent. He had finally
called a therapist because he could no longer live inside the architecture he had built. He was
afraid that talking about it would make it real in a way that silence had prevented. But he was more
afraid of spending the rest of his life as a ghost in his own life—numb, trapped, unable to move
toward anything or anyone.
