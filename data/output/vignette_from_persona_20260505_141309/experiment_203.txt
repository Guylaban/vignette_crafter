# Aroha's Burden

Aroha Parata was fifty-eight years old when everything changed in the space of a single afternoon.
She had worked security at the Wellington Medical Centre for nearly a decade—a role that suited her
temperament and her need for structure. She walked the corridors at night, checked the perimeter,
logged incidents in her careful handwriting. On the evening of March 17th, she was conducting her
final sweep of the east wing when she heard rapid footsteps behind her. A man in a dark hoodie was
running toward the pharmacy. Without thinking, without calling for backup first, Aroha stepped into
his path and reached for his arm. He was young, panicked, much stronger than she anticipated. In the
struggle, he pulled away violently and she lost her footing. As she fell backward, her elbow struck
the edge of a metal supply cart, and the man stumbled forward directly into her. His head connected
with the corner of a steel door frame with a sound Aroha would later struggle to describe—not quite
a crack, not quite a thud. He collapsed. Blood pooled across the linoleum. He had suffered a severe
intracranial bleed and spent six weeks in intensive care.

The man survived, but Aroha did not return to the same life. In the weeks that followed, she found
herself replaying the moment obsessively, searching for the exact point where she might have acted
differently. If she had waited for her colleague. If she had stepped aside. If she had called out
instead of reaching. She would lie awake cataloguing her failures, constructing elaborate scenarios
in which a different choice by her—a younger woman's reflexes, a moment's hesitation, a different
position in the hallway—would have prevented everything. The rumination consumed her. She had come
to believe that her judgment was fundamentally flawed, that she had been reckless and dangerous,
that the consequences of her actions were proof of a character defect she could no longer ignore.
The meaning she extracted from the event was unforgiving: she was the kind of person who hurt
people. That belief took root and grew.

Yet there were gaps in her memory that troubled her almost as much as the rumination. She could not
clearly recall the man's face in those moments before the collision, though she had seen him
afterward in the hospital when she visited to apologize. She could not remember exactly how she had
fallen or in what sequence. There were fragments—the sound, the blood, the alarm that sounded—but
between her decision to step forward and the moment he hit the ground, the timeline felt fractured
and unreliable. When she tried to reconstruct it, she felt as though she were watching someone
else's memory, as though the event had happened to another security guard and she had only heard
about it. Sometimes, when she managed to recall the sequence more completely, she felt nothing at
all—no fear, no remorse, no emotion whatsoever. She described it to herself as watching a film about
someone else's tragedy.

Three years later, Aroha had stopped working. She could not return to the medical centre; the
building itself had become impossible to enter. But the avoidance had spread far beyond that single
location. She had withdrawn from her extended family, from the church community where she had sung
in the choir for fifteen years, from the weekly coffee group with her neighbors. She told people she
was unwell, which was true enough, but the deeper truth was that she could not bear to be around
others, convinced that her presence carried some kind of contamination, that she might somehow cause
harm again. She had become isolated in her small flat in Newtown, spending her days in the
rumination that had become almost comfortable in its familiarity—a punishment that felt, in some
strange way, deserved.

What finally brought her to seek help was not insight but exhaustion. At sixty-one, Aroha had begun
to recognize that the endless analysis, the self-blame, the gaps in her memory that made her feel
like a stranger to her own trauma, had not brought her closer to understanding or redemption. They
had only deepened her conviction that she was irredeemable. She sat in the therapist's office with
her hands folded, afraid that speaking about the event might cause it to happen again, yet terrified
that she would never be able to live in the world as anything other than what she now believed
herself to be: a danger to others, a woman who had proven she could not be trusted.
