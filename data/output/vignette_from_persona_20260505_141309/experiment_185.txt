# Rosa

Rosa was forty-seven when the roadside bomb detonated outside Fallujah in 2008, and she was fifty-
five now—eight years later—when she finally sat across from a therapist because her supervisor at
the bank had gently suggested she might benefit from speaking to someone. The suggestion had come
after Rosa had noticed a customer's wedding ring—a simple gold band—and had found herself unable to
process the transaction. She'd stood frozen at the register while the man waited, then excused
herself to the bathroom, where she sat in a stall for twenty minutes breathing in a way that felt
like drowning.

In the moments before the explosion, Rosa had been sitting in the passenger seat of a Humvee,
scanning the street ahead the way she'd been trained to do. She was a military police officer then,
alert, purposeful. The texture of the seat beneath her hands—that specific roughness of military-
grade vinyl—had been ordinary, unremarkable. Then the world inverted. There was a sound that didn't
belong to any category her mind could hold, and heat, and a sensation of being unmade. When she
tried to explain it now, the sequence fractured. She remembered the smell before the sound. She
remembered her sergeant's face after, but not during. She remembered the weight of something across
her legs, but couldn't say for certain whether it was debris or another body, and this uncertainty
lived in her as a kind of ongoing vertigo. The event itself had the quality of a story someone else
had told her—too extreme, too impossible. It had happened to her, but it didn't feel like it had
happened to her.

She'd come home to Phoenix after her medical discharge, and for the first few years had managed a
kind of functional numbness. But somewhere around year five, the world had begun to feel actively
hostile. Nothing felt meaningful anymore—not her marriage, which had dissolved quietly; not her job,
which she'd returned to because it was stable; not the life she was supposed to be rebuilding. What
felt meaningful was staying alive. What felt urgent was the constant assessment of threat.

At the bank, Rosa had developed habits that made sense to her even as she recognized they were
changing how people saw her. She positioned herself at her register so she could see the entrance
and the exits. She watched customers' faces intently—not in the way a good service worker does, with
warmth, but with a clinical intensity, searching for the micro-expressions that might indicate
violence. She had come to believe that the world was a dangerous place, and that her survival
depended on being able to see danger before it arrived. The wedding ring had triggered something
because it meant loss, meant someone had something to lose, and loss themes—talk of people losing
loved ones, the abstract idea of attachment and vulnerability—made her chest tighten in a way that
felt like the moment before the explosion.

Loud sounds still split her open. A car backfiring in the parking lot sent her into a state of
physical reactivity that took hours to settle—her heart racing, her hands shaking, her breath
shallow. She'd learned to avoid fireworks entirely, and she'd stopped going to restaurants where
pots clanged in open kitchens. The texture of rough fabric—anything like that vinyl seat—could
trigger a wave of physical sensation that came without warning: her palms sweating, her muscles
tensing, her mind flooding with the fragmentary, disordered images of that day.

By the time she called the clinic, Rosa had become someone who could barely leave her apartment on
weekends. She was on guard all the time, had been for so long that hypervigilance felt like the only
honest way to move through the world. She had no close friends, had stopped answering her sister's
calls because the effort of explaining what was wrong felt impossible—she'd start talking and lose
the thread, couldn't assemble the pieces into a coherent narrative. What she wanted most was to feel
like her life was real again, and what terrified her most was the possibility that it never would
be.
