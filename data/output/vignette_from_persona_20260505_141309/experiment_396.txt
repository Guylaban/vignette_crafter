# Arjun's World

Arjun Patel sits at his design workstation in the downtown Minneapolis office where he has worked
for eight years, his fingers poised over the keyboard but his attention elsewhere. The morning light
filters through the windows facing the street, and when a siren passes—a sound that has become
increasingly frequent in his awareness—his shoulders tense and his breath catches. He tells himself
it is nothing, that he is being irrational, yet his body does not believe him. He has learned not to
fight the sensation. Instead, he waits for it to pass, a familiar knot in his chest that loosens
only when the sound fades completely. His colleagues have stopped asking why he sometimes closes his
office door or why he is reluctant to attend the weekly team lunches in the downtown core.

Eighteen months ago, Arjun's world fractured in a moment he still cannot fully articulate. He had
been driving home from a client meeting on a humid July evening when he was pulled over by police
for a broken taillight. The interaction itself began ordinarily—the officer's approach, the request
for documents—but what followed remains fragmented in his mind, a sequence of events he knows
happened but cannot piece together into a coherent narrative. When he tries to explain it to his
wife Priya, his words scatter. He knows the officer's hand moved toward something, knows he felt
afraid, knows there was shouting, but when he attempts to reconstruct the moments, the thread
breaks. The harder he grasps for clarity, the more elusive it becomes. What he carries most vividly
is not the sequence but the overwhelming sensation of betrayal—that a person whose role was to
protect had instead become a source of terror, and that this person had been someone he had been
taught to trust.

Since that night, Arjun has become hyperaware of uniforms. When he sees a police officer at a coffee
shop or walking past his office building, his mind immediately catalogs their position, their
direction, whether they have noticed him. He finds himself rehearsing conversations in his head
before any interaction with authority—the woman at the DMV, the building manager, even his
accountant. He scripts what he will say, anticipating accusations or misunderstandings, preparing
justifications. The mental rehearsal has become so habitual that he often does not realize he is
doing it until Priya asks why he is so quiet, and he realizes he has been silently running through
dialogue for the past twenty minutes.

The smell of burnt rubber or burnt plastic now triggers an immediate sense of danger. Last month,
when a colleague microwaved something too long in the office kitchen and a burnt smell drifted
through the hallway, Arjun felt his body flood with panic. He had to step outside, telling his
manager he needed air. He did not explain why a smell had undone him so completely.

What has changed most noticeably in the past year is Arjun's relationship to the future. He and
Priya had been discussing taking a sabbatical, perhaps traveling to India to visit family, but he
cannot bring himself to make plans. When she suggests booking flights or looking at dates, something
in him resists. He has come to believe that the world is fundamentally unsafe, that planning assumes
a future he is no longer certain he will have, or that he deserves to have. The guilt that
accompanies memories of that night is intense and irrational—he knows he did nothing wrong, yet when
the event surfaces in his mind, he is flooded with a sense of culpability, as though he had somehow
caused or invited what happened. This guilt makes planning feel like a betrayal of some unspoken
penance he owes.

Priya finally insisted he seek help after finding him, late one night, sitting alone in the dark in
their bedroom, unable to articulate why he was there or what he needed. He had called in sick to
work three times that week, and his manager had begun to ask questions. Arjun now sits in his first
therapy session, exhausted by the effort of holding himself together, hoping that someone might help
him understand how a single moment has come to define his entire world, and whether he might ever
feel safe again.
