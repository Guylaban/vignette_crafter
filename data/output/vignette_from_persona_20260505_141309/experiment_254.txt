# Kofi's Reckoning

Kofi Mensah was seventy-eight years old when he finally made the appointment. His wife, Grace, had
insisted. For nearly five decades, he had carried the morning of March 15th, 1974, inside him like a
stone he could neither set down nor fully acknowledge. That day, at the university in Accra where he
had been a young librarian and political idealist, a military faction had stormed the administrative
building during a staff meeting. What began as a show of force became something far worse. Kofi had
watched a colleague—a man named Samuel whom he had known for three years, who brought his children
to the library's reading room on Saturdays—shot in front of him for refusing to sign a document
endorsing the coup. Samuel had fallen against the circulation desk, and Kofi had stood frozen, his
hands still resting on a stack of cataloguing cards.

The physical wound had healed quickly enough. The librarian's body, at least, had survived intact.
But his mind had not stopped replaying that moment. Even now, fifty years later, Kofi could close
his eyes and see the precise angle of Samuel's collapse, could hear the exact pitch of the gunshot
echoing off the tile floor, could feel the blood that had spattered across his own forearm. The
memory did not fade or soften with time. If anything, it had grown sharper. On quiet mornings, when
Grace was still asleep, Kofi would find himself back in that building, watching it unfold again as
though it were happening in real time, his heart racing, his palms damp.

He had emigrated to the United States in 1976, seeking distance and renewal. He had taken a position
at a university library in New England, married Grace, raised two children, and built a life that,
on the surface, looked whole. But the triggers had never stopped finding him. Each March, as the
anniversary approached, Kofi would feel a creeping dread that no amount of rational thought could
dispel. He had come to dread it so thoroughly that he began arranging to be away from work during
that week, claiming illness or personal business. Grace had noticed this pattern years ago and said
nothing, understanding without being told.

Certain conversations were equally treacherous. If colleagues mentioned government corruption, or if
someone at a dinner party spoke carelessly about violence or injustice, Kofi would find himself
gripping the edge of the table, his mind suddenly flooded with Samuel's face. He had learned to
excuse himself, to disappear into the bathroom or the kitchen, anywhere he could be alone. The
unpredictability of these moments had made him wary of social gatherings altogether. When their son
invited them to his daughter's wedding last year, Kofi had felt a surge of what he could only
describe as terror. A crowd, music, celebration—all the things that might pull him momentarily into
happiness—felt dangerous. He had declined, claiming back pain, and had spent the evening in his
study while Grace attended alone.

More insidiously, Kofi had begun to avoid the very things that might have brought him joy. When
Grace suggested they take a trip to visit their daughter in California, or when friends invited them
to concerts or community events, Kofi found reasons to say no. The thought of being in a crowded
place, of being pulled away from the vigilance he maintained over his own mind, filled him with a
nameless fear. Instead, he had developed a habit of drifting, of letting his mind wander into an
imagined world where none of this had happened, where Samuel was still alive, where he himself was
still young and unburdened. He would sit in his study for hours, ostensibly reading, but actually
somewhere else entirely.

Grace had reached her limit after Kofi declined their granddaughter's high school graduation. She
had looked at him across the dinner table, tears in her eyes, and said simply: "I cannot lose you
twice." The next morning, she had found the number for a trauma therapist and left it on his desk.
