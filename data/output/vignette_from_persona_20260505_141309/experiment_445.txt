# Rajesh's Reckoning

Rajesh Mehta was forty-four years old and had built a life in a way that felt, on most days, like a
reasonable facsimile of safety. He worked as a sous chef at a upscale restaurant in Portland, a
position he had held for seven years, and he lived alone in a modest apartment two blocks from the
restaurant. He had come to America in 1996, at the age of nineteen, fleeing the escalating violence
in Gujarat with his older brother Arun and their mother. They had lived in a camp in Ahmedabad for
three months before the arrangements were made—three months during which Rajesh had learned to sleep
with one ear toward the tent flap and to recognize the particular quality of screaming that meant
the violence was moving closer.

The night that would reshape his life had come in late August of 1992. Rajesh and Arun had been
helping their mother gather what remained of their family's goods from their home, which sat on the
edge of a neighborhood that had begun to fracture along religious lines. The sounds had started as a
distant roar—voices, chanting, the percussion of something heavy striking metal. Rajesh's mother had
gripped his shoulder and pulled him toward the back room. Arun had frozen near the front window. In
the next moment, a truck had plowed through the gate, and men had poured into the courtyard. Rajesh
had been pushed into a storage space beneath the floorboards by his mother's hands—hands he could
never quite remember the feeling of, though he carried the knowledge that they had been there. What
followed existed in his mind as a series of sensations without sequence: the sound of breaking
glass, his brother's voice calling his name once, the smell of kerosene, the particular silence that
comes after screaming stops. When he emerged hours later, his mother was still breathing but
unconscious. Arun was not there. He had been taken, or he had run—Rajesh had never known which, and
that not-knowing had become its own kind of wound.

In the three decades since, Rajesh had become expert at the architecture of avoidance. He had
structured his life so that certain things simply did not enter it. He would not walk through the
open plaza near his apartment, even in daylight, because the exposure made his chest tighten in a
way he could not articulate to anyone. When he saw a police officer or, worse, a soldier in
uniform—even on television, even in a film someone was watching at the restaurant bar—his body would
move toward compliance, toward making himself small and agreeable, as though his agreeableness might
determine whether he lived or died. He had not watched the news in fifteen years. He changed the
channel immediately if an ambulance siren appeared in a show, or if someone began describing a car
accident. These were not choices, exactly; they were reflexes that had calcified into habit.

The restaurant kitchen had become his sanctuary because it was enclosed, because he could control
the exits, because the noise and heat and demand for focus left no room for his mind to wander
toward what he could not remember and could not forget. But three months ago, one of the prep cooks
had come to work with a deep laceration on his forearm from a motorcycle accident. The wound was
bandaged, but not well, and when the young man reached for a pot, the gauze had shifted. Rajesh had
seen the raw edges of skin, the seeping redness, and something in him had simply stopped. He had
stood motionless for several minutes, unable to move or speak. When he finally did move, he had
excused himself to the walk-in cooler and remained there until his shift ended. He had not returned
to the restaurant for a week. When he did, the executive chef had made it clear that reliability was
not negotiable.

Now, at forty-four, Rajesh carried a guilt that had no name. He had survived when Arun had not. He
had built a life, however constrained, when his mother had spent her final years in a care facility,
her mind fractured by what had happened. He replayed the night constantly in a way that felt less
like memory and more like a loop of accusation—he should have done something, should have been
braver, should have fought instead of hidden. Sometimes when he tried to speak about it, the words
would not come; the experience existed in him as sensation and silence rather than narrative. Other
times he could recount the facts with a voice that sounded like it belonged to someone else, a voice
completely emptied of the terror that had lived in those hours.

What had finally driven him to call a therapist was the recognition that his life was contracting.
The restaurant had begun to feel unbearable. The thought of leaving his apartment was becoming
harder. And the weight of having survived—of having done nothing to save the people he loved—had
begun to feel like something he could not carry alone anymore.
