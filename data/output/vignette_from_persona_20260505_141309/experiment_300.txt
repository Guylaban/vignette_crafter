# Rebecca's Vigil

Rebecca Chen was forty-four when the bomb went off at the downtown transit station. She had been
there in her capacity as a hospital chaplain, making rounds on a Tuesday morning—a routine she had
performed for nearly fifteen years without incident. She had stepped onto the platform at 8:47 a.m.,
the fluorescent lights humming overhead, the smell of stale coffee and metal in the air. She
remembered the weight of her hospital ID badge against her chest, the way her shoes clicked on the
tile. Then the sound came—not an explosion at first, but a crack so sharp and complete that it
seemed to splinter the world itself. The blast lifted her off her feet. She landed hard against a
metal bench, and in the seconds that followed, she could not arrange what had happened into any
coherent sequence: there was heat, then cold, then a woman's voice calling for help, then silence,
then screaming. The smell of burnt plastic filled her nostrils. Blood—whose blood she still did not
know—was on her hands. When the paramedics found her, she was sitting upright, staring at nothing,
her ears ringing with a sound that would not stop for three days.

The hospital where she worked released a statement about the incident. The transit authority issued
a report. Investigators identified the suspect within forty-eight hours. But Rebecca had come to
believe that none of this mattered—that the institutions meant to protect people had failed
catastrophically, that they had let this happen, and that their subsequent explanations were hollow.
The chaplain who had spent her career comforting others in their darkest moments now found herself
unable to be comforted. She had trusted the systems around her, and they had shattered along with
the station's windows.

In the weeks that followed, Rebecca developed a series of rituals. Before leaving her apartment each
morning, she would touch the doorframe three times and recite a phrase—not quite a prayer, but
something close to one—that she believed would keep harm at bay. If she skipped this ritual, dread
would climb her spine. She began to eat less, then hardly at all. Food felt like a luxury she did
not deserve, or perhaps like something that might make her vulnerable. Her clothes grew looser. Her
colleagues noticed but said nothing. At the hospital, she began to position herself differently
during her rounds. She would sit only in chairs that faced the door, that allowed her to see who was
approaching. She watched the faces of visitors and patients with an intensity that made people
uncomfortable. When a fire alarm sounded during a code drill, her entire body seized. The sound of a
car backfiring outside her apartment sent her to the floor.

The chaplain's office, once a place where she felt purposeful, became a minefield. When her
supervisor—a woman in a navy blazer with a badge of authority that Rebecca could not look at
directly—asked to speak with her about her recent performance, Rebecca felt the familiar sensation
of the blast returning. Her hands trembled. She excused herself and did not return to that
conversation. Over the following months, she began to avoid any interaction with authority figures.
She would not attend staff meetings if her director would be present. She requested to work only on
floors where she would not encounter hospital security. Her supervisor, concerned, suggested she
take medical leave. Rebecca refused. To accept that suggestion would mean admitting that she was, as
she had come to believe, a terrible coper—that her reactions to what had happened were proof of some
fundamental weakness in her character.

The memories themselves had become unbearable. When they surfaced—triggered by the sound of a siren,
by the sight of someone's hands shaking, by the simple fact of feeling safe enough to let her guard
down—they came with a completeness that made the present moment disappear. She would smell the burnt
plastic. She would feel the impact of the blast, not as a memory but as a current, living event. The
sequence of what had happened remained jumbled; she could not arrange the moments in order, and this
disorder terrified her, as if the chaos of the event might break through into her daily life if she
could not contain it through narrative. The memory felt more real than the present—more vivid, more
true—which meant that the present felt increasingly insubstantial, unreliable.

It was a Tuesday morning, exactly three months after the bombing, when Rebecca finally called for
help. She had been sitting in her apartment, having completed her morning ritual, when a wave of
positive emotion—a moment of sunlight through her window, a kind thought about a patient she had
helped—suddenly terrified her. The feeling seemed dangerous, a lapse in vigilance that might invite
catastrophe. She could not bear the contradiction between safety and joy, between the world she
wanted to believe in and the world she now knew to be true. She made the appointment with a trauma
therapist that afternoon, hoping someone could help her understand why feeling anything good had
become impossible, and why the world she had once served felt now like a place designed to destroy
her.
