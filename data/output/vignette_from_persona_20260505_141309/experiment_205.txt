# Fatima's Map

Fatima was forty-two when the boat went down in the Mediterranean. She had been a pharmacist in
Damascus before the war—a woman accustomed to precision, to the logic of compounds and dosages, to
the orderly flow of customers and prescriptions. The boat was overcrowded, a fishing vessel never
meant to carry three hundred people, and when the storm came it came without warning. One moment the
water was merely rough; the next, a swell lifted the stern and the boat rolled. Fatima remembers the
texture of the rope she grabbed—rough, salt-stiffened hemp that bit into her palms as bodies pressed
against her from all sides. She remembers the water entering the cabin, cold and deliberate, and the
silence that followed the initial screaming, a silence so absolute it seemed to have weight. She was
pulled onto a Greek coast guard vessel six hours later, one of two hundred and fourteen survivors.
Ninety people did not make it.

Now, thirteen years later, at fifty-five, Fatima works as an HVAC technician in New Jersey, and she
has become a woman who cannot be still. She has learned the blueprints of every building she enters
before she steps through the door. She studies exit routes the way other people study maps of cities
they plan to visit—mentally rehearsing the path from each room to the outside, calculating which
stairwell is closest, which window might open. At the manufacturing plant where she installs
ventilation systems, her colleagues have noticed that she arrives early and walks the perimeter
before clocking in. They think she is conscientious. What they do not know is that she cannot begin
work until she has located the fire exit, the loading dock, the emergency ladder on the north wall.

The uncertainty is what drives her to this. Fatima had come to believe that if she could only know
where the danger was—if she could only see it coming—she would not be caught unaware again. The boat
had given her no warning. The silence before the swell had been a lie. So now she monitors. She
scans the room while talking to a customer. She notes where people stand. She listens for the
quality of sound in a space, because unusual silence—the kind that precedes something—triggers a
physical response in her that feels indistinguishable from the moment before the boat rolled.

Water remains intolerable. She showers quickly, her heart rate elevated, and she avoids the Jersey
Shore entirely, though friends sometimes invite her. She has not been to the beach in thirteen
years. Three months ago, heavy rains flooded the basement of a client's home, and when Fatima
arrived to assess the HVAC damage, she stood at the top of the stairs looking down at the dark water
and could not descend. She told the client she would return the next day. She did not. The client
hired someone else. Fatima told herself it was because the water had made the electrical work
unsafe, but the truth was that the sight of it—the pooling, the stillness—had transported her so
completely that for several minutes she could not distinguish the present from the past.

The memories themselves feel like lies. She knows they happened; her body knows with absolute
certainty. But her mind resists the scale of it, the randomness. She sometimes tells people she was
in a boating accident, and the euphemism feels safer, though it also feels like a betrayal. When the
full memory surfaces—the rope, the cold, the silence, the weight of the person pressed against her
who was not moving—shame floods through her with such force that she has to stop whatever she is
doing. She has learned to excuse herself from meetings, to find a bathroom, to wait until the
feeling recedes enough that she can function.

She has been divorced for eight years. Her ex-husband could not tolerate the vigilance, the way she
needed to know where he was, what route he was taking home. He called it paranoia. She called it
survival. When he left, she did not fight it.

What brought her to the therapist's office now was a moment at work last week. She had been
installing ductwork in a new office building, and the power went out—not unusual, a circuit issue.
But the silence that followed, the sudden absence of the hum of machinery, sent her into a state of
such acute panic that her supervisor found her standing rigid in the dark, unable to move or speak.
He thought she was having a heart attack. When the lights came back on, she could not explain it to
him. She still cannot. What she knows is that she cannot keep working like this, cannot keep living
as though the next moment might be the one that undoes her. She is hoping that someone can help her
understand why her body still believes she is drowning.
