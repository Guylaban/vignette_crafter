# Mahmoud's Fall

Mahmoud was seventy-three years old when the scaffolding gave way beneath him. He had been a
sculptor for fifty-two years—first in Damascus, then in Los Angeles since 1989—and he knew the
weight of his materials the way other men know their own breathing. That October morning, he was
working on a commission for a public building downtown, standing on a metal platform twelve feet
above concrete, his hands shaping a limestone relief. The bolts had corroded. No one had checked
them. When the platform tilted, Mahmoud fell backward onto the edge of a wooden support beam, his
right side taking the full impact. Three ribs fractured. His right shoulder dislocated. The
emergency room doctor told him he was fortunate; at his age, the outcome could have been far worse.

But Mahmoud did not feel fortunate. In the weeks after the accident, as the fractures began to knit
and the shoulder regained some mobility, he became consumed by a single conviction: his body had
betrayed him. He had always trusted his physical sense of balance, his instinctive knowledge of
space and weight. That trust had been obliterated in the moment of the fall. Now, at seventy-five,
he could no longer move through the world with the assumption that his body would keep him safe. It
had failed him once. It would fail him again.

The thought haunted him constantly. Throughout the day, Mahmoud found himself monitoring his chest,
pressing his fingers against his ribs to check for pain, rotating his shoulder in small circles to
verify it still functioned, testing his balance by standing on one leg in his kitchen. Every twinge
sent a spike of fear through him. Was this the beginning of something worse? Was the fracture
healing properly, or was there a hidden fracture he had missed? He had come to believe that
permanent damage had been done—not just to his ribs and shoulder, but to something fundamental in
his body's reliability. The physical vulnerability he felt was not the normal caution of an aging
man; it was the certainty that his body had been permanently altered by what happened, that he could
no longer depend on it to function as it once had.

He began avoiding situations that put stress on his body. He stopped climbing the stairs to his
second-floor studio. He stopped driving to the hardware store for supplies. He stopped accepting
invitations to his daughter's house, which required walking up a steep driveway. His sculpture work,
which had always been his life, became impossible. The very thought of standing at a worktable, of
reaching overhead, of bearing weight on his feet for hours—all of it seemed dangerous now, a
provocation to a body that had already shown it could not be trusted. His hands, which had shaped
stone and wood for five decades, grew still.

Over the months, Mahmoud sank into a kind of rumination. He spent hours in his armchair thinking
about why this had happened to him. Was it punishment? He had left Syria; perhaps he was being
punished for abandonment. Was it a sign that his time was ending? He had lost his wife, Leila, five
years earlier. Now his body was leaving him too. The questions circled endlessly, offering no
answers, only a deepening sense that something fundamental had shifted in his relationship to his
own flesh.

His daughter, Rima, noticed that he had stopped returning her calls with his usual warmth. When she
visited, she found him thinner, more withdrawn. The studio where she had watched him work since
childhood stood empty, dust settling on tools he no longer touched. She begged him to see someone,
to get help. For months he resisted. But recently, as he sat alone in his apartment, unable to
sculpt, unable to move without fear, Mahmoud acknowledged that he could not live like this. He was
not living at all. He was seventy-five years old, and his body had become a prison he could no
longer trust. He needed to understand how to exist in a body that had betrayed him, or he needed to
understand why the fall had happened—something, anything, that might restore some sense of order to
a world that now felt fundamentally unsafe.
