# Priya's Silence

Priya Desai was thirty-two when she came to her first appointment, though her manner suggested
someone older—someone who had learned to occupy space without drawing attention to it. She sat in
the corner of the consulting room, her dupatta wrapped tightly around her shoulders despite the mild
temperature, and when asked to describe what had brought her in, she fell silent for nearly two
minutes. Not the silence of someone gathering thoughts, but the silence of someone standing at the
edge of a chasm, knowing that any words she might offer would simply tumble into it unheard.

Seven years earlier, Priya had been a human rights observer in a region of South Asia where her
documentation work had made her visible to the wrong authorities. She had been detained for six
weeks. The official record would later call it "administrative custody." What happened in that
holding facility existed in her memory as a sequence of sensations without narrative—the sound of
boots on concrete, the feeling of cable ties cutting into her wrists as she was forced into stress
positions for hours, the specific timbre of her interrogator's voice as he leaned close to her ear
in the darkness. She had been released eventually, ostensibly without charge, though the marks on
her body and the fracture in her left hand told a different story. She had returned to the United
States, to her family in New Jersey, and for the first year had functioned adequately enough that
people believed she was healing.

The symptoms had accumulated slowly, like sediment. Within months of her return, Priya had begun
declining invitations from friends she'd known before the detention. When her college roommate
Anjali had invited her to brunch, Priya had felt her chest tighten at the thought of sitting across
from someone who had known her before—before she had become someone whose body held memories her
voice could not articulate. She had made an excuse and never rescheduled. Similar patterns repeated
with others until her social calendar had contracted to nearly nothing. She told herself it was
easier this way, that she was protecting them from her darkness, though the truth was more
complicated: being around people who had known her before felt like a constant confrontation with
the person she was no longer.

Three years ago, Priya had taken work as a hospital chaplain, a position that seemed to offer both
meaning and a kind of protective isolation. She moved through sterile corridors speaking to patients
and families in crisis, offering presence and occasionally prayer. The work suited something in
her—the ability to sit with suffering without needing to explain her own. But the hospital had
recently undergone security renovations, and now there were uniformed guards stationed at multiple
entrances. The first time Priya saw the dark blue uniform with its insignia, her body had responded
before her mind could intervene: her hands had begun to shake, her breathing had become shallow, and
she had found herself unable to continue her shift. She had called in sick and spent the afternoon
in her apartment with the curtains drawn.

The open spaces of the hospital—the atriums, the waiting areas where she had once moved freely—had
become places she avoided now. She found routes through back corridors and stairwells, adding twenty
minutes to her commute, but the alternative was the sensation of exposure, of being visible and
vulnerable in a way that made her skin crawl. Her supervisor had begun to notice her absence from
certain areas of the hospital. Questions had been asked. Priya had no answer that didn't sound like
confession.

What frightened her most was not the memories themselves, but her absolute inability to speak them.
She knew what had happened—the knowledge lived in her body, in the way her shoulders tensed at
certain sounds, in the involuntary way her hands would move to protect her wrists when someone
approached from behind. But when she tried to tell the story, even to herself in the privacy of her
apartment, the words would scatter like startled birds. She would begin with "I was detained" and
immediately lose the thread, unable to move from that statement to anything that followed. The
details existed only as sensation: the particular acoustic quality of concrete, the pressure of
restraint, the tone of voice that meant pain was coming. But translating these into language felt
impossible, as though the trauma had somehow bypassed the language centers of her brain entirely.

In desperation, she had begun hurting herself in small ways—pressing her nails into her palms until
they left marks, holding ice against her skin until it burned—not from self-hatred but as a kind of
translation, a way of converting the wordless terror into something physical and therefore somehow
more manageable. At least physical pain had a beginning and an end. At least it could be controlled.

Now, sitting in the therapist's office, Priya was hoping that someone might help her find a way to
say what had happened, or at least to live with the fact that she could not. She was also terrified
that any attempt to speak it aloud would shatter what remained of her functioning, that the words,
once released, would consume everything.
