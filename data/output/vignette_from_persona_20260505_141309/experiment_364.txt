Kavi Perera was forty-one when the convoy was ambushed near Kandahar. He had been flying transport
helicopters for the Australian Defence Force for eight years by then, and he had come to understand
the work as a kind of calling—moving supplies, moving people, moving hope into places where it was
needed. The morning of the attack, he was waiting on the tarmac for clearance, the rotors already
turning, when the radio crackled with a request for emergency extraction from a forward operating
base. Three soldiers had been hit. He lifted off without hesitation.

The landing zone was chaos. Dust everywhere, the smell of cordite and something else—something burnt
that he would later learn was human tissue. One of the injured men was conscious, his uniform soaked
through with blood, and as Kavi's crew loaded him aboard, the man's hand—cold, trembling—grabbed
Kavi's wrist. The texture of it stays with him still: the particular roughness of a field dressing
soaked through, the specific way a dying man's grip feels when he is trying not to let go of the
world. They got airborne. They did not make it to the hospital. The man died in the cabin while Kavi
was flying, and for the last thirty seconds of that flight, Kavi had felt the weight of that hand on
his wrist even though someone had already pried the fingers away.

Twenty-two years later, Kavi is sixty-three and still flying, though not for the military anymore.
He has been with a major American carrier for the last decade, a captain on long-haul routes, and he
has built a careful life with his wife Margaret in Portland. To anyone watching him move through an
airport or into the cockpit, he appears composed—professional, even. But Kavi has come to understand
that he is not the man who boarded that helicopter in 2002. That man is gone, and what remains is
something he has come to regard with a kind of horror.

The night vigilance started years ago, so gradually that he did not notice it at first. He would
wake at 2:47 a.m.—the exact time the convoy was hit—and lie in the dark, listening. Margaret would
be asleep beside him, and he would feel the house around him like a trap, every door and window a
potential breach. He had come to believe that darkness was the time when things became real, when
the world showed its true nature. During the day, the illusion of safety held. At night, it fell
away.

The scanning began during his first year with the American airline. He would enter a cockpit or a
briefing room and immediately locate the exits—not consciously, but as an automatic sweep of his
eyes before he could attend to anything else. In the cabin, waiting for a flight to board, he would
map the space, note where people stood, anticipate sudden movements. When a flight attendant moved
too quickly, or when a passenger stood abruptly, Kavi's body would tense, his breath would catch. He
was certain that everyone was a potential threat, though he could not articulate why. The world had
shown him its teeth once, and he had never stopped waiting for them to close.

The memory itself has become something alien to him—something that belongs to a different person, a
different life. When it surfaces, which it does without warning and without mercy, it carries with
it the full weight of that original terror. A particular texture—canvas, or certain kinds of fabric,
or even the specific roughness of the armrest in the cockpit—can trigger it instantly. The sound of
a helicopter, even on television, can send him into a state where he is back on that tarmac, feeling
that cold grip on his wrist. The time of day matters too. As 2:47 a.m. approaches, his body knows,
and he lies awake, waiting.

Margaret has noticed the changes. She has watched him become someone who is always miserable, who
moves through their marriage like a man underwater. He used to laugh easily. He used to be present.
Now he is elsewhere, always scanning, always vigilant, always convinced that there is something
fundamentally broken inside him—something that marks him as defective, as someone who should not be
trusted with the lives of three hundred passengers. The airline has not noticed yet, but Kavi knows
they will. He has started making mistakes, small ones, the kind that come from a mind that is never
quite here.

It was Margaret who finally insisted he come. She said she could not bear to watch him disappear
anymore, and Kavi, sitting in the therapist's office now, can only agree. He does not know if
anything can fix what he is, but he has run out of ways to hide it.

  [6] PASS
Kavi Perera was forty-one when the convoy was ambushed near Kandahar in 2002. He had been flying
transport helicopters for the Australian Defence Force for eight years by then, and he had come to
understand the work as a kind of calling—moving supplies, moving people, moving hope into places
where it was needed. The morning of the attack, he was waiting on the tarmac for clearance, the
rotors already turning, when the radio crackled with a request for emergency extraction from a
forward operating base. Three soldiers had been hit. He lifted off without hesitation. The landing
zone was chaos. Dust everywhere, the smell of cordite and something else—something burnt that he
would later learn was human tissue. One of the injured men was conscious, his uniform soaked through
with blood, and as Kavi's crew loaded him aboard, the man's hand—cold, trembling—grabbed Kavi's
wrist. The texture of it stays with him still: the particular roughness of a field dressing soaked
through, the specific way a dying man's grip feels when he is trying not to let go of the world.
They got airborne. They did not make it to the hospital. The man died in the cabin while Kavi was
flying, and for the last thirty seconds of that flight, Kavi had felt the weight of that hand on his
wrist even though someone had already pried the fingers away. He had felt the man's blood cooling
against his skin. In the years that followed, Kavi would learn to live with the memory as though it
belonged to someone else entirely—a different man in a different life, someone he could observe from
a distance but never quite become again. Yet when that memory surfaced, which it did without warning
and without mercy, it carried the full weight of the original terror, as if twenty-two years had
collapsed into nothing.

Now, at sixty-three, Kavi flies for a major American carrier as a captain on long-haul routes, a
position he has held for the last decade. He and his wife Margaret live in Portland, and to anyone
watching him move through an airport or into the cockpit, he appears composed—professional, even.
But something fundamental has shifted inside him, something he cannot quite name but recognizes with
a kind of dread. There is something wrong with him as a person. Not just in how he thinks or
remembers, but in the core of who he is. He has come to understand that the man who volunteered for
that extraction mission—the man who believed in purpose, who laughed easily, who moved through the
world with a kind of open trust—that man no longer exists. When he looks back at photographs from
before 2002, he sees a stranger, and what strikes him most is that the person in those images looks
happy. Genuinely happy. He cannot reconcile that face with the man he is now. He is always
miserable, he has come to believe about himself—not in dramatic moments, but in the texture of
ordinary days. He moves through his marriage with Margaret as though underwater, present in body but
absent in every way that matters. He used to be a happy person. He remembers that, though it feels
like remembering someone else's life. Now happiness seems like a luxury reserved for people who have
not seen what he has seen, people whose minds have not been permanently altered by a single moment.

The night vigilance started years ago, so gradually that he did not notice it at first. He would
wake at 2:47 a.m.—the exact time the convoy was hit—and lie in the dark, listening. Margaret would
be asleep beside him, and he would feel the house around him like a trap, every door and window a
potential breach. He had come to believe that the world is a dangerous place—not in the abstract way
that people speak of abstract dangers, but as a lived conviction that safety is an illusion that
collapses the moment you stop watching. Darkness is when things become real, when the world shows
its true nature. During the day, the illusion of safety holds. At night, it falls away. The scanning
began during his first year with the American airline. He would enter a cockpit or a briefing room
and immediately locate the exits—not consciously, but as an automatic sweep of his eyes before he
could attend to anything else. His body had learned to do this without his permission. In the cabin,
waiting for a flight to board, he would map the space, note where people stood, anticipate sudden
movements. When a flight attendant moved too quickly, or when a passenger stood abruptly, Kavi's
muscles would tense, his breath would catch in his chest. He was certain that everyone was a
potential threat, though he could not articulate why. The world had shown him its teeth once, and he
had never stopped waiting for them to close.

The memory itself has become something alien to him—something that belongs to a different person, a
different life. When it surfaces, which it does without warning and without mercy, it carries with
it the full weight of that original terror, as vivid as the moment it happened. A particular
texture—canvas, or certain kinds of fabric, or even the specific roughness of the armrest in the
cockpit—can trigger it instantly. The sound of a helicopter, even on television, can send him into a
state where he is back on that tarmac, feeling that cold grip on his wrist. The time of day matters
too. As 2:47 a.m. approaches, his body knows, and he lies awake, waiting for the moment when
something inside him will fracture again. The sight of blood—even in a medical context, even in a
film—can leave him shaking for hours afterward. He has come to believe that his mind is
fundamentally unreliable, that the boundary between past and present has become dangerously thin,
and that this defect marks him as someone who should not be trusted with the lives of three hundred
passengers on every flight he captains. Margaret has noticed the changes. She has watched him become
someone who is always miserable, who moves through their marriage like a man underwater. She has
noticed that he no longer touches her at night, that he flinches when she reaches for him in the
dark, that he sometimes calls her by the name of a crew member from a flight years ago. The airline
has not noticed yet, but Kavi knows they will. He has started making mistakes, small ones, the kind
that come from a mind that is never quite here. Last month, during a preflight checklist, he found
himself unable to remember whether he had already verified the fuel levels, and the uncertainty
terrified him in a way that no threat assessment ever could. He began a descent procedure and had to
abort it halfway through because he could not recall if he had already completed it. The fear that
he might one day forget something critical—that his mind might betray him in a moment that
matters—has become more terrifying than any external threat. It was Margaret who finally insisted he
come. She said she could not bear to watch him disappear anymore, and Kavi, sitting in the
therapist's office now, can only agree. He does not know if anything can fix what he is, but he has
run out of ways to hide it. What frightens him most is not that he will remember the dying soldier's
grip—he will, always—but that he will forget something that costs lives, and that the defect inside
him will finally be exposed for what it is: a fundamental failure of the person he has tried so hard
to remain.
