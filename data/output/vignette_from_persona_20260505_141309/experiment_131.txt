# Dr. Marcus Kovács

Marcus Kovács had spent twenty-eight years building a reputation as a thoughtful, unflappable
clinical psychologist. His colleagues knew him as the one who could sit with the most fragmented
trauma narratives and help patients find coherence. He had trained residents, published on narrative
reconstruction, and maintained a private practice in Portland where he saw complex cases others
found intractable. That identity—the competent healer, the man who could hold others' pain—had been
the architecture of his life. It was the only identity he had left now, and even that was crumbling.

The assault had happened on a spring evening four years ago. Marcus had stayed late at the
university clinic to finish intake notes. He was alone in the building when a man he had never seen
before entered his office, locked the door, and overpowered him. The attacker had smelled of stale
whiskey and sweat. There had been sirens—police responding to a call elsewhere in the building,
growing louder, then fading. Marcus remembered fragments: the weight of a body, the sharp chemical
bite of alcohol on his face, a voice saying things he could not now quite retrieve. But mostly he
remembered the feeling of being hollowed out, rendered inert, made into something that could be done
to rather than something that could do.

For the first year, Marcus had told no one. He had called in sick the next day, seen his family
physician for injuries he described as a fall, and returned to work after a week as though nothing
had shifted. His wife, Elena, had noticed his quietness but attributed it to stress. His patients
did not seem to notice. He was still the same voice in the room, still the same careful attention.
But something in him had begun to fray.

The triggers arrived without warning. A patient's breath carrying the faint tang of alcohol would
send him rigid in his chair, his hands gripping the armrests while he maintained his clinical
composure. An ambulance passing on the street outside his office would leave him breathless and
disoriented for the rest of the session. More insidious was the simple fact of discomfort—any moment
when he felt unsettled or unsafe in his own skin—and suddenly he was not in Portland but in that
office, and the feeling that he was not a person but an object, something without agency or
boundary, would flood through him.

The memories themselves resisted coherence. When Marcus tried to recall the event fully, he found
himself pushing away before the narrative could complete. It was as though his mind would not permit
him to hold the whole thing at once. Yet sometimes, without warning, the emotional intensity would
arrive in isolation—a crushing weight of shame and violation and deadness that had no accompanying
image, just a pure and terrible feeling that he was no longer himself, no longer capable of being
the man he had been.

He had begun to avoid. He stopped staying late at the office. Then he stopped seeing patients who
came smelling of alcohol. Then he began scheduling appointments only in the morning, when fewer
sirens seemed to pass. His caseload shrank. Colleagues asked if he was planning to reduce his hours.
Elena asked if he was unhappy with her. The answer to both was no, but Marcus could not articulate
what was true instead: that he felt dead inside, that the things which had once defined him—his
work, his capacity to be present for others, his sense of himself as someone solid and whole—now
felt like performances he was giving from behind glass.

It was Elena who finally insisted. She had found him one evening standing in the kitchen, holding a
glass of wine he was not drinking, tears running down his face without sound. He could not tell her
why. He could barely tell himself. But he agreed to see someone, another psychologist, someone who
could perhaps help him find words for the thing that lived in his chest, wordless and overwhelming,
and help him understand whether the person he had been could ever be recovered, or whether that man
had been unmade entirely.
