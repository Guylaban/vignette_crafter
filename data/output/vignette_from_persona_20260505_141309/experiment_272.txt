# Clinical Vignette: Judge Amara Mensah

Amara Mensah was fifty-four years old when she first came to the clinic, though she moved through
the waiting room with the careful precision of someone much older. She had been a judge for nineteen
years, known in her district for her measured rulings and her composure under pressure. It was
composure, she would later tell her therapist, that had kept her alive—and that now kept her
trapped.

Twenty-three years earlier, Amara had been a human rights lawyer working in her native country,
documenting cases of state violence. She had been arrested at her office on a Tuesday afternoon.
What happened next existed in her mind as fragments, pieces that did not cohere into a continuous
narrative no matter how many times she tried to reconstruct them. She remembered the sound of a
metal door closing. She remembered being forced into a chair, her wrists and ankles bound with
something that cut into her skin. But when she tried to move forward from that moment, the sequence
dissolved. There were hours—or was it days?—that she could not access at all. She knew she had been
questioned. She knew there had been physical pain. But the specifics, the sequence of what had been
done to her, remained locked away in a part of her mind she could not reach, even now.

What she could remember with terrible clarity were the sensations that did not belong to her. When
she tried to recall the detention, she felt herself observing it from a distance, as though she were
watching another woman endure it. That woman's body, bound and helpless, seemed to belong to someone
else entirely. The detachment was so complete that Amara sometimes wondered if the memory was real
or if she had constructed it from fragments of other people's testimonies. This doubt haunted her
more than any clear recollection could have.

After her release—the circumstances of which she also could not fully remember—Amara had rebuilt her
life methodically. She had emigrated, completed a law degree in the United States, married her
husband David, and climbed steadily into the judiciary. For years, she managed. The trauma seemed
sealed away, inert. Then, gradually, the seals began to fail.

It started with a colleague's cologne. A new judge in her courthouse wore a scent that triggered an
immediate, overwhelming sense of danger. Amara found herself unable to sit near him during judicial
conferences. She would excuse herself, her heart racing, her palms sweating. She could not explain
why that particular fragrance undid her. It simply did. Over the following months, she began to
avoid situations where she might encounter strong scents. She asked David not to wear cologne. She
started taking her lunch alone in her office rather than in the courthouse cafeteria, where perfume
and aftershave mingled in the air.

Then came a morning when a defendant in her courtroom began breathing heavily during his
testimony—anxiety, perhaps, or asthma. The sound transported Amara instantly to that chair, to the
sense of being trapped, unable to move. Her hands trembled so badly she could not hold her pen. She
had to adjourn the hearing. In the weeks that followed, she found herself hypervigilant to any sound
of heavy breathing—her husband's sleep apnea, someone jogging past her chambers, even her own breath
when she was anxious. She began to sleep in the guest room so David's breathing would not wake her
in the night.

But it was the sensation of physical restraint that most thoroughly altered her daily life. David,
in an affectionate gesture, had once put his arms around her from behind while she was working at
her desk. She had screamed. The feeling of being held, of her movement being restricted even
slightly, had sent her into a panic so acute that she had pushed him away violently. Since then, she
had become careful about how she positioned herself in rooms. She avoided crowded elevators. She
could not bear to wear tight clothing. At the courthouse, she sat only in chairs with armrests she
could easily leave, positioned so no one could approach from behind.

It was David who finally insisted she seek help. He had watched his wife, once vibrant and engaged,
become increasingly isolated and controlled by fears she could not fully articulate. She could not
tell him what had happened because the memory itself resisted being told. When she tried to speak
about it, she encountered only fragments and that strange sense of watching someone else's
suffering. After twenty-three years of silence, Amara came to therapy not knowing if she could ever
retrieve what had been taken from her, only knowing that whatever remained was slowly consuming her
present.
