# Tane's Vigilance

Tane was thirty-eight when he was taken. He had been photographing a story on illegal mining
operations in a remote region of Papua New Guinea, documenting environmental destruction for a major
international publication. He'd done this work for fifteen years—the kind of journalism that
required him to move quietly through dangerous places, to see without being seen. One afternoon,
armed men appeared at his camp. They held him for nine days in a concrete structure with no windows,
wanting the images he'd captured, wanting to know who had sent him. There was no negotiation, only
confinement and the constant threat of what would happen if he didn't cooperate. When he was finally
released—after his publication and the government negotiated his freedom—Tane returned to his wife
Aroha in Auckland with his passport, his cameras, and a nervous system that had learned to expect
capture.

Fifteen years had passed. Tane was now fifty-three, still a photographer, but the work had become a
minefield of his own making. What had once been his gift—the ability to move unseen through a scene,
to anticipate light and composition—had transformed into something that felt like constant exposure.
Every assignment, even routine commercial work, required him to enter spaces where he felt
vulnerable. Before walking into a client's office or an event venue, Tane would arrive early and
spend ten or fifteen minutes identifying where he might sit. Not where the lighting was best or
where he could see the subject clearly, but where his back was protected, where he could see the
entrance, where he wouldn't be trapped. At a recent corporate headshot session, he'd asked the
client to move their chair three times before settling on a position that felt survivable. The
client had noticed. There had been awkwardness.

His hypervigilance toward people had become almost automatic. At the supermarket, he found himself
tracking the movements of men who entered his peripheral vision—assessing their size, their gait,
their hands. On the street, sudden movements made his entire body seize. Last month, a colleague had
touched his shoulder from behind to get his attention, and Tane had flinched so violently he'd
knocked over a cup of coffee. The colleague had laughed it off, but Tane had felt the shame settle
into him like a stone.

What exhausted him most was the constant need to plan escape routes. Before accepting any job, he
would mentally map the space—where were the exits, which direction would he run, what obstacles
might slow him down. When Aroha had suggested they visit her sister in Wellington for a weekend,
Tane had spent an hour on Google Street View studying the layout of her house, identifying windows,
understanding the street pattern. He hadn't told Aroha this. She was already worried about him in
ways he could see but couldn't quite address.

His body lived in a state of perpetual readiness. His shoulders ached constantly. He ground his
teeth at night. His hands trembled sometimes when he was holding a camera, which terrified him
because his hands were supposed to be steady—that was who he was. Aroha had noticed that he slept
poorly, that he was always tense, that he seemed to be waiting for something. She had started asking
him if he was okay, if something was wrong, if she could help. These questions made him more
anxious. He needed her reassurance, but her reassurance never quite landed. He would ask her—"Do you
think I'm safe here?" "Do you think something bad is going to happen?"—and she would say yes, of
course, but the relief lasted only minutes before the doubt crept back in.

It was Aroha who finally insisted he see someone. She had found him one morning sitting in the car
in the driveway before work, unable to go inside a client's studio. She had taken his hand and said,
quietly, that she couldn't watch him disappear anymore. Tane had come to his first appointment
carrying the weight of fifteen years of hypervigilance, of escape routes memorized in buildings he'd
visited dozens of times, of a body that believed, with absolute certainty, that danger was always
present and that his survival depended on never being caught unaware again.
