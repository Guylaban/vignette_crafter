# Clinical Vignette: Maya

Maya was thirty-seven when she came to her first therapy appointment, fifteen months after the
bombing at Union Station. She had been sitting in the waiting area of the concert hall where she
worked as a freelance violinist, reviewing the program notes for that evening's chamber performance,
when the blast occurred four blocks away. The explosion sent a shock through the building's
structure—she felt it first as a tremor in her bones, then heard the sound, a compression of air
that seemed to come from everywhere at once. In the minutes that followed, before she understood
what had happened, she smelled smoke and something else, something acrid and chemical that would
later colonize her memory of the entire event.

The attack killed seventeen people and injured dozens more. Maya had not been directly hit, had not
witnessed the worst of it. But in the hours after, when she was questioned by police and then
released, she kept tasting copper and ash—a flavor that seemed to coat her mouth and throat, that
lingered even after she brushed her teeth three times that night. When she tried to sleep, the taste
returned, and with it came a sensation of the walls closing in, of being trapped in that moment when
she did not yet know what the sound meant.

For the first few weeks, Maya told herself she was fortunate. She had her life. She could still
play. But something had shifted in how the world felt. She began to notice things she had never paid
attention to before: the way crowds moved, the exits in any room she entered, the sound of sirens
that seemed to multiply in the city after the attack. When she was alone in her apartment, she felt
safer, but a hollow kind of safe—a safety purchased by isolation. Her partner, James, tried to be
patient. He suggested they go to dinner, see friends, resume normal life. But when they were out,
Maya found herself scanning faces, wondering who might be capable of what, whether the person
sitting next to them at the restaurant was really just an ordinary diner or someone with intentions
she could not fathom. People are not what they seem, she had come to believe. The world had shown
her this truth, and there was no unknowing it.

Three months after the bombing, she missed a rehearsal. She had been driving to it when a car
backfired near her on the highway. The sound sent her into a state she could not name—her hands
shaking so badly she could not hold the steering wheel properly. She pulled over and sat there for
an hour, tasting ash and copper again, feeling as though the explosion were happening in real time,
her body convinced of a danger that her rational mind knew had already passed. She called the
conductor with an excuse. Then she called in sick for the next rehearsal. And the next.

The cancellations accumulated. Venues that had called her regularly stopped calling. Her income,
already precarious for a freelancer, began to contract. James suggested she see someone, and she
snapped at him—an anger that seemed to come from nowhere, a rage at his suggestion that talking to a
therapist might help, as if her trauma were something that could be reasoned away, as if safety were
still possible in a world that had proven itself fundamentally unsafe. She apologized later, but the
damage had been done. He was pulling away now, spending more time at work, coming home late. She
could not blame him. She was not the person he had agreed to be with.

What finally brought Maya to therapy was not hope but desperation. She had been offered a solo
performance—a prestigious opportunity that would have changed things—but she could not imagine
stepping onto a stage, being visible, being vulnerable in that way. The thought of it sent her into
a panic that lasted for days. She had no future, she was certain of this. She belonged nowhere—not
in concert halls, not in relationships, not in the world as it had been before. But she also could
not continue as she was. She came to her first appointment asking not if she could get better, but
whether there was any point in trying.
