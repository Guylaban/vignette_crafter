# Miguel's Story

Miguel Reyes was twenty-two when the pneumatic nail gun slipped from his grip on a Tuesday morning
in March. He had been working on a new residential complex in the North Hills, installing metal
studs alongside his supervisor, Dennis, a man he'd worked with for nearly two years. The day had
been routine—Miguel had arrived at six in the morning, grabbed coffee from the site trailer, and was
already three hours into the job when it happened. He was securing a header frame, the nail gun held
at chest height, when his palm began to sweat. He'd been thinking about his mother's birthday party
that weekend, distracted for just a moment, and the tool rotated in his hand. The nail fired at an
angle. It caught Dennis in the upper thigh, missing the femoral artery by what the emergency room
doctor later said was perhaps two centimeters.

The sound of Dennis screaming had a particular quality—not pain exactly, but shock and disbelief.
Miguel had stood frozen, watching the blood bloom across his supervisor's work pants, and in that
moment something in his mind had fractured. He had not been careless in the way that careless people
are careless. He had been present, had been trained, had been careful every other day of his life.
But on this day, in this second, his body had betrayed him. His hands had done something his mind
had never intended. For months afterward, Miguel would replay that instant obsessively, turning it
over and over in his mind like a stone worn smooth by constant handling. What if he had gripped the
gun differently? What if he had not thought about his mother? What if he had positioned himself at a
different angle? The questions spiraled without resolution, each cycle leaving him more exhausted
than the last.

Dennis survived. The doctors repaired the wound, and he returned to work eight weeks later. But
Miguel could not return to the site. The very thought of standing in that space, of holding a
pneumatic tool, sent his heart racing and made his palms slick with sweat. His supervisor had
offered him a desk role, administrative work, but Miguel found that even entering the office
building made his chest tight. The fluorescent lights, the smell of the break room coffee—things
that had once been neutral—now triggered a visceral sense of dread. He had come to believe that his
body was fundamentally unreliable, that it could betray him without warning, that he could not trust
his own hands to do what he commanded them to do.

What frightened him most was silence. When the job site fell quiet, when there were no sounds of
saws or drills, Miguel felt certain that something catastrophic was about to happen. In the absence
of noise, danger seemed imminent. He began to avoid any situation that might be stressful—he
declined invitations from friends, stopped going to his gym, avoided even the minor friction of
traffic by staying home. His apartment became his refuge, the one place where he felt he had any
control. He had stopped going to the grocery store because the fluorescent aisles and the
unpredictable crowds felt unsafe. His sister brought him food now. She had stopped asking him when
he was coming back to work.

The memory itself felt strange to him, as if it belonged to someone else entirely. When he tried to
recall the moment, he could smell the metallic tang of the blood, could feel the texture of the grip
tape on the nail gun, but the person in that memory—the one who had let the tool slip—did not feel
like himself. He had become someone unrecognizable, someone capable of hurting another person
through his own failure. He could not forgive himself for not doing more to stop it, even though
logically he understood that the accident had taken less than a second.

Last week, Miguel's mother had asked him to see someone. The anniversary of the incident was
approaching in three weeks, and she had noticed him becoming more withdrawn, more rigid with
anxiety. He had agreed, not because he believed talking would change anything, but because he could
no longer bear the weight of what he had done, or the person he had become in the aftermath.
