# Thom's Hypervigilance

Thom Seng was forty-eight years old when the tank rupture changed everything. He had been an HVAC
technician for twenty-six years by then, methodical and detail-oriented, the kind of worker whose
clients requested him by name. On a Tuesday afternoon in March, he was called to a commercial
warehouse in the industrial district to service the cooling system for a chemical storage facility.
The job was routine—he had done it a dozen times. But this time, when he descended into the basement
mechanical room, he found the source of the malfunction: a corroded coupling on a chlorine tank had
fractured, and a yellowish-green cloud was already filling the lower half of the room.

He made it out and called 911, but not before inhaling a significant dose. The next six hours
blurred into an ambulance ride, an emergency room, oxygen masks, and a chest that felt as though it
had been lined with broken glass. The doctors told him he was fortunate—his exposure was moderate,
not severe. No permanent lung damage. He could go home in two days.

But something in Thom's mind had registered differently. He had been in a sealed room with a poison
he could not see clearly until it was almost too late. His body had betrayed him by not warning him
sooner. The safety protocols everyone relied on—the inspection schedules, the maintenance logs, the
assurances from the facility manager that the equipment was "regularly checked"—had all failed. He
had come within moments of something far worse.

In the weeks that followed, Thom returned to work, but the world had become a place where invisible
threats pooled in corners and seeped under doorways. He began checking the locks on his truck before
he drove anywhere. At a client's home, he would test the windows in the basement before descending
to the furnace. At night, he would rise from bed three, sometimes four times to verify that the
front door was locked, that the garage door was closed, that the basement windows were sealed. His
wife, Maria, watched him move through their house like a man searching for something he had already
lost.

The tension never left his shoulders. Even when he sat on the couch in the evening, his muscles felt
wound tight, as though his body was braced for impact. When Maria suggested they take a weekend trip
or sit together on the porch with coffee, Thom would feel a spike of unease. Relaxing meant lowering
his guard, and lowering his guard meant something terrible could happen. He had come to
believe—despite Maria's gentle reminders, despite his own rational knowledge—that safety was an
illusion maintained by people who simply had not yet encountered their moment of exposure.

Being alone made it worse. If he was by himself in a room or a building, the silence seemed to
amplify the possibility of danger. He began scheduling his work calls so that he could finish jobs
before his colleagues left the office, or he would linger unnecessarily, finding reasons to stay in
occupied spaces. When Maria had to travel for her sister's funeral, Thom spent the three nights at
his brother's house rather than alone in their home, telling himself it was to help with repairs,
but knowing the truth: the thought of being the only person in the house, responsible for detecting
any threat, was unbearable.

At fifty-two, Thom had lost the ease that had once defined him. His hands trembled slightly when he
held diagnostic equipment. He had turned down the larger jobs that required solo work in unfamiliar
buildings. Maria had stopped inviting friends over because Thom's constant vigilance—the checking,
the questions about whether the windows were secure, his visible discomfort—made gatherings tense
and brief. His supervisor had gently suggested that Thom might benefit from some time off, though
the implication was clear: the job was suffering.

It was Maria who finally insisted. She had found him at three in the morning checking the basement
windows for the second time that night, his face drawn, his breathing shallow. She said, quietly,
that she could not watch him disappear into this fear any longer. Thom had called his doctor the
next morning, and now, sitting in the therapist's office, he was trying to articulate something he
had never quite named: the certainty that the world was fundamentally unsafe, and that his
hypervigilance was the only thing standing between his family and catastrophe.
