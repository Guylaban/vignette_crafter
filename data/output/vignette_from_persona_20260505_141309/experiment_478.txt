# Marco's Reckoning

Marco Delgado was twenty-three years old when the bus collided with the overturned tanker truck on
Interstate 95, just north of Jacksonville. He had been riding to a construction site where he worked
summers before returning to his engineering program—a job his uncle had arranged, a way to stay
connected to family and earn money. Marco sat in the middle section, near a window, listening to
music through cheap earbuds. The driver had taken a route he didn't usually take, something about
roadwork on the main highway. Marco remembered noticing the sky was cloudless and hot, the kind of
Florida afternoon that felt like it would never end.

The impact came without warning. The bus lurched violently to the left, metal screaming against
metal. Marco's body pitched sideways, his shoulder slamming into the window frame. For a moment that
felt both instantaneous and eternal, he understood that something catastrophic was happening. The
tanker had been lying on its side in the roadway—no one had seen it until it was too late. The bus
driver had no time to brake. Sixteen people were on board. Three died at the scene. Two more in the
hospital within days. Marco had broken ribs and a fractured collarbone. He had also been sitting
directly behind an elderly woman named Mrs. Chen, who had been thrown into the aisle with tremendous
force. He had seen her head strike the metal armrest. He had heard the sound. Later, he learned she
was one of the three who died before the ambulances arrived.

The weeks after the accident unfolded in a fog of medical appointments and family concern. His
mother had flown up from Miami and stayed in his apartment. His uncle stopped calling. Marco
returned to his apartment alone in late August and tried to resume his life, but something
fundamental had shifted in how he understood the world. He went back to his job as a QA engineer at
a software firm in Jacksonville in September, sitting in a cubicle on the third floor of a downtown
building, testing code for bugs and inconsistencies. His work required focus and precision—the same
qualities that had once come naturally to him. But now, when colleagues would mention a car accident
they'd seen on the news, or when someone casually referenced a crash, Marco felt something crack
open inside his chest. His hands would shake. The office would seem to tilt. He found himself
thinking about how different everything would be if he had taken a different bus that day, or if he
had called in sick, or if the tanker truck had never been on that highway at all. These thoughts
would consume him for hours, branching into elaborate scenarios where Mrs. Chen was still alive,
where the driver had taken the main route, where he had never taken the job at all.

By October, Marco had begun avoiding the news entirely. He stopped watching television. He deleted
news apps from his phone. When his roommate would turn on a program and a story about a transit
accident or a highway incident appeared, Marco would leave the room abruptly, sometimes without
explanation. He found himself increasingly uncomfortable around police—a patrol car pulling into the
parking lot of his apartment complex would send him into a state of hypervigilance that could last
for hours. He wasn't sure why exactly. The police had been kind at the scene. But something about
their presence now felt like a threat he couldn't name.

What frightened Marco most was the conviction that had taken root in him: that he should have
somehow protected Mrs. Chen, that his proximity to her created an obligation he had failed to meet.
He had come to believe that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that you could never truly know who
or what would harm you, and that nothing good could happen to him anymore. The thought of returning
to school felt impossible. The thought of getting close to anyone—romantically, or even in
friendship—felt like exposing them to danger through association with him. At work, he had become
quieter, less engaged. His manager had asked twice if everything was okay. Marco had said yes both
times, but he could feel the erosion happening, the slow withdrawal from the life he had been
building.

By late November, a year after the accident, Marco's mother called after not hearing from him for
several days. Something in his voice alarmed her enough that she insisted he see someone. He sat in
a therapist's office for the first time last week, hands folded in his lap, and spoke about the bus,
about Mrs. Chen, about the weight of a guilt that belonged to no one but had attached itself to him
anyway. He was terrified that talking about it would make it real in a way it hadn't been before. He
was also, somewhere beneath the fear, hoping that someone might tell him that the future wasn't
entirely foreclosed, that he hadn't somehow poisoned everything he touched by surviving.
