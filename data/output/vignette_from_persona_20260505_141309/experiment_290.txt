# Aroha

Aroha had been a forensic analyst for eighteen years, the kind of meticulous professional who could
reconstruct the sequence of a crime from fibres and bloodspatter, who built cases on evidence rather
than assumption. She was respected at the laboratory—quiet, reliable, someone other analysts came to
when they needed a second pair of eyes on a difficult sample. Her colleagues knew her as steady.
They did not know that for the past seven months, she had been unable to enter the building without
her hands shaking.

The morning of March 14th, her youngest brother Rangi had called her at work. He was in the city for
the week, staying in a hotel downtown, and wanted to take her to lunch. Aroha had said yes—they were
close, separated by only two years, and she had missed him. She'd left the lab at noon, driven to
the hotel, and found him in the lobby. They'd embraced. He'd smelled like the cologne he always
wore. She remembered his laugh as they walked toward the restaurant. She remembered the exact colour
of the shirt he was wearing: a deep blue linen shirt she'd bought him for his birthday.

They were crossing the street. The light was green. Aroha was looking at Rangi, listening to him
tell her about his daughter's school play. A delivery truck, its driver distracted, ran the red
light at forty miles per hour. There was no moment of warning, no time to pull him back. Rangi was
struck directly. The sound of the impact—a sound she could not adequately describe but which lived
in her head now, a dull wet crack followed by the screech of brakes—had become the centre of her
world.

What remained in her mind were not the seconds before or after, but three isolated moments, each
vivid and motionless. The first: Rangi's face as he turned toward the sound of the engine, his
expression still open, still mid-laugh. The second: the moment of contact, his body's sudden
stillness against the truck's grille. The third: herself on the pavement, her hands on his chest,
the blue linen shirt growing dark. These three pictures played in her mind without narrative,
without the mercy of sequence or explanation. When they returned—and they returned constantly—they
came with the sound: that crack, that screech. Her brain would produce the noise with perfect
fidelity, as though the trauma were playing through speakers mounted inside her skull.

She had taken two weeks off work. Then she had tried to return. The laboratory smelled the same—the
familiar chemical tang, the hum of the equipment, the cool white light. But walking through the
doors meant entering a space where she was expected to be the person she had been, and that person
no longer existed. She would stand at her desk, her colleagues speaking to her, and feel as though
she were watching herself from outside her body. By the second day back, she called in sick. By the
third week of absences, her supervisor had called her at home, voice gentle, asking if she needed
more time. Aroha had said yes, not knowing if she would ever be ready.

The not-eating had begun without her deciding to do it. Food lost its taste. More than that, the act
of eating felt like a betrayal—a small assertion that life was continuing, that she deserved
nourishment, when Rangi was not eating, would never eat again. She had lost twelve pounds. Her
husband Hemi had stopped commenting on the empty plates, but she could see the worry in the way he
watched her at dinner, the way he'd begun leaving sandwiches on the counter, as though abundance
might shame her into consumption.

Sleep was impossible. She would lie beside Hemi in the dark and feel the memories gathering, waiting
for her to close her eyes. So she stayed awake instead, scrolling through her phone until three,
four in the morning, keeping the house dark and quiet so the sounds in her head would not echo. She
had come to believe that her life as it had been—the marriage, the career, the small satisfactions
of competence and partnership—had been destroyed in that moment on the street. Nothing felt
meaningful now. The cases on her desk at work, the careful reconstruction of evidence, the pursuit
of justice: these things seemed like the preoccupations of someone living in a world that no longer
existed. She had lost faith in the idea that the universe operated according to any logic at all.
Rangi had been kind, had been loved, had had a daughter who needed him. And he had been erased by a
moment of inattention from a stranger. People could not be trusted. Life could not be trusted.

It was Hemi who had finally insisted she see someone. He had made the appointment, written down the
date, placed the reminder on her phone. Aroha had not wanted to come. But she had come, because
staying as she was felt like dying very slowly, and because some small part of her—the part that had
once been a good analyst, that had once believed in evidence and process—wondered if there was
anything that could make sense of what had happened, or at least make it bearable enough to live
alongside.
