Rima was thirty-two when the convoy she was traveling with came under attack in Kandahar Province.
She had been embedded with a logistics unit for six weeks, auditing supply chain procedures for a
defense contractor—work that had seemed straightforward enough when she left Phoenix. The morning of
the incident, she was in the second vehicle of a four-truck transport moving between forward
operating bases. The lead truck hit the IED first. She remembers the concussive force throwing her
sideways, the taste of dust and something metallic, hands reaching for her across the seat. After
that, the sequence fragments. She knows there was gunfire. She knows she moved, that she helped move
others, that she was shouting. But the middle part—the part where she might have done something
differently, made a choice that would have saved someone—exists now only as a void, a black space in
her narrative that no amount of effort can illuminate. She has tried. In the thirteen years since,
she has tried countless times to reconstruct those minutes, to find the moment where she could have
acted differently, protected the specialist next to her, the young man from Tennessee whose name she
cannot now separate from the guilt.

The dreams started within weeks of her return to the States. She would wake gasping, her body
drenched, convinced she was still there—that the ceiling was rubble, that the sounds outside her
bedroom were automatic weapons. For years she pushed through it, returned to work at her firm, moved
through the motions of civilian life. But the hypervigilance never left. At the office, she found
herself unable to work in the open-plan audit floor; she relocated her desk to a corner office, back
to the wall, where she could see the door. Her colleagues assumed it was a preference. What they did
not see was the way her eyes tracked every entrance, the way she mapped the stairwells and emergency
exits before sitting down at her computer, the way her chest tightened whenever someone walked
behind her.

By her early forties, Rima had narrowed her world considerably. She lived in a ground-floor
apartment with reinforced locks, blinds she kept drawn. She took the same route to work each
morning, always leaving early to avoid traffic, always scanning the parking garage before she
reached her car. She had divorced Marcus seven years ago—a good man who had tried to understand why
she could not sleep with the bedroom door open, why she flinched when he came up behind her, why she
needed to know where every exit was before they could sit down to dinner. The marriage had become a
series of small rejections, her body's refusal to relax enough to let him close.

Three months ago, Rima's ex-husband's new wife wore a perfume to a chance meeting at a grocery
store—something floral with a woody base—and Rima's entire body seized. She had stood frozen in the
cereal aisle, unable to move, certain she was about to be harmed. The scent had been there in
Kandahar too, on someone, though she cannot remember whom. The fragmented nature of her memories
makes it impossible to know if it was someone she lost, someone who hurt her, or simply someone who
happened to be present during the minutes she cannot access. The uncertainty is its own torture.

What frightened Rima most was not the memories themselves but the conviction that had taken root in
her: that she was the kind of person who failed when it mattered most, that her body's persistent
state of alarm—the racing heart, the shallow breathing, the constant scanning—was not a symptom but
a truth about who she was. She had come to believe that she deserved to live this way, confined and
vigilant, because she had not protected others when she had the chance.

When she finally called a therapist last month, it was not because she believed she could change. It
was because she was exhausted, and because she had begun to wonder if the person she had become—the
woman who could only feel safe in three rooms of her apartment, who checked behind her so often it
had become invisible to her, who woke most nights unable to distinguish between dream and danger—was
someone she wanted to remain.
