# Clinical Vignette: Sophia Reyes

Sophia Reyes was twenty-two years old when she came to the clinic, her hands folded tightly in her
lap, her eyes tracking the therapist's movements before settling on a fixed point near the floor.
She had been married for just over a year to Miguel, a software engineer who worked from home. It
was Miguel who had finally convinced her to call, after she had spent three consecutive nights
unable to sleep, cycling between states of rigid alertness and collapse. She told the therapist, in
a voice that wavered between steadiness and tremor, that she did not know how much longer she could
do this.

Eight months earlier, Sophia had been assaulted in a parking garage after her shift at the call
center. She had stayed late to finish a difficult customer escalation—a woman whose account had been
compromised, whose voice had carried a particular kind of desperation that Sophia had felt obligated
to resolve. The garage had been nearly empty at nine in the evening. She had walked to her car
thinking about whether she had locked her apartment door, a small loop of worry that had occupied
her mind as she inserted her key into the lock. A man had emerged from behind a concrete pillar. The
sequence of what happened next lived in her body as disconnected pieces: the smell of cigarette
smoke and something chemical, the specific temperature of his hand over her mouth, the taste of
copper and something else she could not name, a sound she had made that did not feel like her own
voice. The assault had lasted perhaps four minutes. A security guard had appeared, and the man had
fled. Sophia had been taken to a hospital, examined, given information about reporting options, and
released.

In the weeks that followed, Sophia had become someone else entirely—or rather, she had become
someone who could never stop being vigilant. She had come to believe with absolute certainty that if
she allowed her mind to fully grasp what had happened, she would shatter. So she had learned not to
think about it directly, to catch her thoughts before they solidified into memory, to redirect them
toward work, toward Miguel, toward anything else. But the memory did not obey her will. It returned
in fragments that felt more real than her actual present: the taste would come back suddenly,
metallic and wrong, and she would find herself gasping in the middle of a conversation. The visual
pieces came in pieces—a hand, a pillar, a patch of concrete—never forming a coherent sequence, which
somehow made it worse, as if her mind could not make sense of what had been done to her and so kept
trying, kept failing, kept trying again.

At the call center, Sophia had begun scanning for exits the moment she entered the building. She had
mapped the emergency stairwell, the rear entrance near the loading dock, the windows and their
proximity to the ground. When colleagues approached her desk unexpectedly, her body would react
before her mind could catch up—a sharp intake of breath, a flinch that made her coworkers
uncomfortable and confused. She had stopped attending team meetings in the conference room because
being in a space without a clear exit path made her chest tight. Her supervisor had begun to notice
her withdrawal, her reduced productivity, the way she seemed to be disappearing into herself even as
she sat at her desk taking calls.

She had also come to believe that she had to be on guard at every moment, that relaxation was
dangerous, that the world was fundamentally unsafe. The sight of an ambulance sent her into a state
of acute panic—the sirens meant emergency, meant chaos, meant loss of control. When she saw families
laughing together in public, at restaurants or parks, she felt a sharp, inexplicable rage followed
by despair. Those families seemed to exist in a world where terrible things did not happen, and she
envied and resented them in equal measure.

At home, Miguel had noticed that she had begun using sex as a way to manage her internal state—not
intimacy, but a kind of frantic release, a way to discharge the constant tension that lived in her
muscles and her nervous system. It helped, briefly, but afterward she felt empty and ashamed. She
was also terrified of police officers, of anyone in uniform, of authority figures of any kind. When
a police car pulled up near their apartment building last month, she had locked herself in the
bathroom for two hours, unable to articulate to Miguel why she was so afraid.

Now, sitting in the therapist's office, Sophia was exhausted. She told the therapist that she wanted
her life back, that she wanted to stop being afraid, that she was terrified she would never feel
safe again. She was most afraid of the possibility that this was permanent, that she would spend the
rest of her life scanning rooms and tasting copper and believing herself inadequate, broken, forever
changed by something that had lasted only minutes.
