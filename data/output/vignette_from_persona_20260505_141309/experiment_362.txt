# Priya

Priya was thirty-one when the accident happened, though she would later think of her life as divided
into before and after that single moment in the parking lot. She had been a teacher for eight
years—middle school English at Lincoln Middle in Sacramento, a role that had shaped her identity so
completely that her husband Arun joked she graded their grocery lists. She was good at it in a way
that felt intrinsic, not earned: she could read a room, anticipate what a struggling reader needed,
make a shy ninth-grader believe they had something worth saying. Her colleagues asked for her
advice. Parents requested her specifically. On the day it happened, she had stayed late to help a
student revise an essay about belonging, and she was thinking about that conversation—about how the
girl's voice had grown stronger as they worked—when she backed her Honda out of the staff lot.

The sound was immediate and absolute. A sickening compression. She had struck a custodian, Mr. Chen,
who had been moving a dolly behind her car. He had broken his femur and spent six weeks in the
hospital. Priya remembered the smell of the parking lot—hot asphalt and something chemical, maybe
coolant—and the way his face had looked in the moment before impact, a flash of surprise that she
could not unsee. The investigating officer said it was an accident, that Mr. Chen had not been
visible, that these things happened. But Priya knew the truth in a way the officer could not: she
had been distracted. She had been thinking about the essay, about herself, about how well the lesson
had gone. She had been careless with the kind of casual inattention that only became visible in
hindsight, when the consequences were irreversible.

The first weeks after the accident, Priya had functioned on adrenaline and guilt. She visited Mr.
Chen in the hospital, brought flowers, apologized until his daughter gently suggested she let him
rest. She attended a restorative justice meeting arranged by the school district, where she sat
across from him and his family and tried to articulate remorse that felt infinitely inadequate. Mr.
Chen had been kind—he said he bore her no ill will, that he knew it was an accident—but his kindness
somehow made it worse. She had come to believe that she was no longer capable of the kind of
careful, attentive presence that teaching required. How could she be trusted with thirty adolescents
when she had proved so catastrophically negligent with one man's safety? The thought terrified her.

She returned to teaching in September, but something fundamental had shifted. When the principal
pulled her aside in October to praise her classroom management—students were engaged, attendance was
up—Priya felt something close to revulsion. The praise felt obscene, a recognition of competence she
no longer possessed. She found herself unable to accept it, deflecting with comments about how the
students deserved credit, how it was just luck that the class composition worked well. Arun noticed
that she was different after these interactions, quieter, more withdrawn. What he could not see was
that positive attention had become a trigger for shame, a reminder that people did not know what she
had done, what she was capable of.

More destabilizing was the confusion that arose when she felt anything approaching happiness. A
student had written a beautiful piece about resilience, and for a moment—just a moment—Priya had
felt the old satisfaction, the sense that her work mattered. But the feeling had immediately been
flooded with guilt. How could she feel joy when Mr. Chen was still in physical therapy? How could
she experience anything like contentment when she carried this knowledge of her own carelessness?
She had begun to associate positive emotions with a kind of moral failure, as though happiness was
something she no longer deserved.

By November, she had started avoiding conversations about accidents entirely. When a colleague
mentioned a fender-bender in the staff room, Priya had excused herself. When her class read a short
story that involved a car crash, she had reassigned the reading. The avoidance had crept into other
areas. She no longer drove unless absolutely necessary, which meant Arun drove them everywhere, a
shift that had not gone unnoticed. She had declined invitations to social events, telling friends
she was tired, when the truth was that she could not bear the risk of being praised or the
possibility that someone might mention an accident they had heard about.

The memory itself had taken on a quality of immediacy that terrified her. When she closed her eyes,
she was back in the parking lot—not remembering it, but living it. She could smell the asphalt and
the chemical tang, could see Mr. Chen's face in that frozen moment of recognition. The memory had
fragmented into discrete, vivid snapshots: her hand on the gear shift, his body crumpling, the
security guard running toward the car. Each image was crystalline and present, as though the
accident were happening in parallel to her current life rather than existing safely in the past.

By January, Priya had begun to think of herself as fundamentally changed, not in a way she could
recover from but in a way that was now permanent. She was an outsider to the person she had been—to
the teacher her students thought she was, to the professional her colleagues respected, to the woman
Arun had married. She carried a knowledge of her own capacity for harm that no one else possessed,
and that knowledge had become a wall between her and the rest of the world. It was Arun who finally
insisted she seek help, who said that he could not watch her disappear, that whatever had happened,
she deserved to find her way back. She had made the appointment with a therapist last week, still
uncertain whether coming back was possible, but knowing that she could not continue living in the
parking lot.
