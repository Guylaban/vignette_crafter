# Kahu's Silence

Kahu was forty-eight when he first sat in the therapist's office, his large frame folded into a
chair too small for him, calloused hands gripping his thighs. He had come because his wife, Aroha,
had finally told him she could not live like this anymore—not the silence itself, which she had
learned to navigate, but the way he had begun to disappear from their own home. He worked roofing
jobs across the city, often taking on double shifts, returning after dark to eat alone in the
kitchen while she slept. When she touched him, he flinched. When she asked him to sit with her, he
found reasons to check the garage, reorganize the shed, anything to keep moving. Six months ago he
had stopped eating regular meals, subsisting instead on coffee and the occasional protein shake
consumed standing up between jobs. Aroha had noticed his weight dropping, the way his wedding ring
had begun to slip on his finger, and she had wept—not out of anger, but out of a grief she could not
name, watching her husband of twenty-two years turn into someone she could reach but never quite
touch.

The assault had happened four years prior, on a Friday night after a work gathering at a bar in
Southside. Kahu had gone with his crew, men he trusted, and had accepted a drink from someone he
thought was a friend. What followed was a fragmented horror—a bathroom stall, hands that were not
his own, a voice saying things he had trained himself not to remember in full. He had walked out of
that bar and driven home in a state of numb shock, showered until his skin was raw, and told no one.
For the first two years he had almost convinced himself it was something that had happened to
someone else, a story he had heard rather than lived. But the body remembers what the mind tries to
forget.

The triggers had accumulated slowly, then all at once. A news segment about sexual assault survivors
would send him into a state of agitation that lasted hours—he had begun avoiding news entirely,
asking Aroha to turn off the television, scrolling past headlines on his phone with a practiced
flick of the wrist. Motorcycle noise, which had once been merely annoying, had become intolerable;
the sound would arrest him mid-breath, his nervous system flooded with a sensation of being trapped,
though he could not explain why the roar of an engine connected to that night. And alcohol—the smell
of it, anywhere, in anyone's breath or on their clothes—would make his stomach turn, would send him
back to that bathroom stall with a vividness that obliterated the present moment. He had stopped
going to social events entirely.

What frightened him most was not the memories themselves, though those were terrible enough, but the
way they no longer felt like memories at all. When he recalled the assault, it did not feel like
something that had happened in the past; it felt as though it were happening now, with a clarity and
immediacy that made his ordinary memories—his daughter's high school graduation, his mother's
funeral, his wedding day—seem pale and distant by comparison. Sometimes, without warning, he would
be standing on a roofline in the afternoon sun and would suddenly feel the full crushing weight of
that night's terror, all the emotion and none of the image, as if his body knew something his mind
was refusing to articulate. These moments would leave him hollow for hours afterward, and he would
compensate by pushing harder, working longer, keeping his body moving so that his mind could not
catch up.

The eating restriction had begun as a way to feel control—if he could govern what went into his
body, perhaps he could govern what happened to it. But it had become its own kind of trap, and
Aroha's worry had only made him more rigid, more determined to prove that he was fine, that nothing
had changed, that he was still the man she had married. He was not fine. He was terrified that if he
stopped moving, if he sat still long enough to feel the full weight of what had been taken from him,
he would not be able to get back up. And he was afraid that Aroha would eventually leave, that his
silence and his absence would wear through the bonds of their marriage until there was nothing left
to hold onto. That fear, more than anything, was what had finally brought him to seek help—not hope
that things could change, but desperation that they must.
