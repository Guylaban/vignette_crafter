# Hinerangi's War

Hinerangi was seventy-five when she walked into the auditor's office one Tuesday morning and found
herself unable to sit. The meeting room had a window behind the table—the conference table where she
was expected to review the quarterly statements—and she stood for nearly twenty minutes, her hands
trembling as she held the papers, until a junior analyst asked if she was all right. She wasn't. She
hadn't been for fifty-four years, but the trembling had only begun six months ago, and now it was
getting worse.

The trauma lived in her body in ways she could never quite explain to her husband, David. During the
Bosnian war in 1992, Hinerangi had been working as a development officer for an international NGO in
Sarajevo. She was there when the siege began. One afternoon, sheltering in a basement with a group
of civilians and aid workers, she had heard the sound of boots on the stairs—heavy, deliberate,
descending. The basement was crowded, the air thick with sweat and fear, and when the soldiers
entered, they had separated the men from the women. Hinerangi remembered the smell most vividly: the
acrid, panicked sweat of the people around her, the metallic tang of gun oil, the way that smell had
filled her lungs and never quite left. She remembered the face of the young man next to her, how he
had whispered something to his sister, how she had never seen him again. She remembered being
terrified that she would be next, and then relief when they were finally released—a relief that had
felt obscene even as it washed over her.

She had left Bosnia a week later. She had returned to New Zealand, married David, built a quiet life
as an auditor, kept the memories in a sealed compartment. For fifty-four years, she had not thought
about the basement, or so she had told herself. Then, six months ago, David had come home from his
morning run, still in his gym clothes, and she had smelled his sweat as he kissed her on the cheek.
The smell had transported her completely. She had frozen mid-sentence, her mind suddenly filled with
the basement, the boots, the young man's whisper. Since that moment, she could not be around the
smell of sweat without her entire body going rigid with dread. Even David's presence after exercise
became something she had to brace for, something she found herself avoiding.

What frightened her most was that the memories had become too vivid to contain. When she closed her
eyes at night, she could see the basement with photographic clarity—the exact placement of the light
bulb, the pattern of cracks in the concrete, the faces of the women huddled against the far wall.
The olfactory memories were worse. The smell would arrive unbidden during meetings at work, or in
the supermarket, or sitting at home, and with it came the certainty that she was unsafe. She had
come to believe that others could see what had happened to her simply by looking at her face, as
though the trauma were written across her skin. She had started positioning herself with her back
against walls in the office, started checking behind her when she walked through corridors, started
rehearsing every sentence she might need to speak to colleagues before she spoke it—anything to
maintain control, to prevent something she could not name from happening again.

The rumination had become relentless. She spent hours lying awake wondering what she could have done
differently that day, whether she should have left Sarajevo earlier, whether her presence had
somehow caused the young man's disappearance. She went over and over the question of why it had
happened to her, as though the answer might somehow undo it. At work, she had become unreliable. She
had missed deadlines, forgotten meetings, and last month, a younger colleague had gently suggested
that perhaps she should consider retiring. The suggestion had broken something in her. She was not
sure her life was worth living anymore. The happiness she had felt in her marriage, in her work, in
the small rituals of daily life—all of it had begun to feel impossible, as though she had lost the
capacity to feel anything genuine ever again.

David finally insisted she see someone when he found her standing in the garden at three in the
morning, unable to explain why she had gotten out of bed. She had come to the clinic because she was
afraid of what might happen if she did not, and because she was desperately hoping that someone
could help her find a way back to the woman she had been before the smell of sweat had unlocked a
door she had thought was sealed forever.
