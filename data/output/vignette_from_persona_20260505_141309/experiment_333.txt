he has spent the last four years trying to forget, even as his body refuses to let him. He had gone
to a nightclub in downtown Boston with colleagues from the rehabilitation clinic where he was doing
his clinical internship. The air was thick with cologne and perfume, the kind of sensory saturation
he usually found energizing. He remembered the bass thrumming through his chest, the press of bodies
on the dance floor, the sense of being young and invulnerable. He remembered a man—someone he
thought was friendly, someone who had bought him drinks—guiding him toward a back hallway. He
remembered the smell of that man's cologne, something with amber and musk, and then the shock of
being shoved into a bathroom stall, the sudden violence of it, hands that were not his own on his
body, pain that seemed to split him in half. The specificity of those sensations—the cologne, the
cold tile wall against his face, the man's breathing—had never faded. They returned unbidden,
visceral and immediate, as though four years were nothing.

When Karim left the bathroom, he told no one. He drove home in silence, showered until his skin was
raw, and went to bed as if nothing had happened. For weeks he functioned—he completed his
internship, graduated with his DPT, married Yasmin, whom he had been dating since college. But the
world had become a place of hidden threats. Certain scents—cologne, perfume, even some of the
stronger essential oils his clinic used in the wellness room—would send him into a state of alert so
acute that his hands would shake and his heart would race as though the attack were happening again
in real time. He found himself unable to be touched unexpectedly. When Yasmin reached for him in the
kitchen, or when a patient at the clinic accidentally brushed against him during a treatment, he
would flinch violently, his nervous system hijacking his response before his mind could catch up.
Beyond these specific triggers, Karim had developed a baseline jumpiness that permeated his days. A
car door slamming outside would send electricity through his limbs. The sudden ring of his phone
made him gasp. When Yasmin came home from work and unlocked the front door, he would startle at the
sound, his body tensing as though bracing for impact. He had taken to keeping the television on at
low volume constantly, a white noise that paradoxically made him feel safer by masking the
unpredictable sounds of the world. The pain in his body—a chronic tension in his lower back and
hips, the very places where he had been hurt—became a constant sentinel, a reminder that his
physical form was not entirely his own. He began checking himself obsessively, pressing on his ribs
and abdomen, assessing his own body as though it might betray him again.

Yet Karim had developed a paradoxical relationship with his own mind. At night, he would often lie
awake replaying the attack, unable to stop the film reel that played in his mind's eye, seeing the
man's face, hearing the sounds, feeling the panic as though it were happening for the first time.
But during the day, he had learned to push against these memories with deliberate force. Whenever
the event began to surface—when a conversation turned toward relationships, when he was alone in the
car and his thoughts began to wander, when a patient's story echoed something about his own
vulnerability—Karim would consciously redirect. He would turn up the volume on the radio, pull up a
work email, text Yasmin about dinner plans, anything to keep his mind occupied and away from the
specifics of what had happened. He had come to believe that thinking about the event too directly,
examining it too closely, would somehow make it more real, would give it more power. There was a
fragile equilibrium in his avoidance: the intrusive images at night he could not control, but the
deliberate thinking—the kind where he might have to articulate what happened, to sit with the full
weight of it—that he could forestall. This distinction felt crucial, even if it meant that the event
remained partially sealed off, a thing that happened to him rather than something he had fully
processed. The shame was suffocating—shame not just about what had been done to him, but about his
body itself, as though it had somehow invited this violation, as though he had somehow been
complicit in his own assault through his own stupidity and naïveté.

At work, where he spent his days helping patients recover from their own injuries, Karim found
himself dissociating during certain moments. When a male patient came in after being assaulted, or
when he had to touch someone's lower back, he would mentally step outside himself, imagining that it
was someone else in the room, someone else's hands moving across the patient's spine. This
dissociation was a relief, a way of making the work survivable. But it was also evidence of
something he had come to believe with certainty: that he could no longer trust his own mind or body
to protect him. He had misjudged the man at the club entirely—had read friendliness where there was
predation, had accepted drinks without considering danger. If his judgment had failed so
catastrophically in that moment, how could he trust it now? Every decision felt fraught with the
possibility of error. When Yasmin asked him where he wanted to go for dinner, or when a patient
asked his professional opinion about their recovery, Karim felt a creeping paralysis. His instincts
had already betrayed him once. What if they did so again? He had come to see himself as
fundamentally unreliable, a person whose own mind could not be depended upon to keep him safe.

What had begun as avoidance of specific triggers—he started declining social invitations, making
excuses not to go to restaurants or shops where he might encounter strong scents, avoiding the
downtown area entirely—had calcified into something larger. Intimacy with Yasmin became impossible.
She would initiate sex and he would feel panic rising in his throat, his body rigid, his mind
somewhere else entirely. He had come to believe that he would never feel normal again, that
something fundamental had been broken in him. By twenty-seven, Karim's life had contracted around
these fears. He had stopped going to the clinic's social events. He avoided certain streets where
the nightclub was located. He had begun wearing noise-canceling headphones even at home, a barrier
between himself and the world. His clinical work, once a source of pride, had become something he
endured rather than engaged with, his dissociative episodes occurring with increasing frequency
until he found himself questioning whether he was competent enough to be treating patients at all.
He had started calling in sick more often, unable to face the sensory landscape of the clinic—the
scents of various therapeutic oils, the unpredictability of touch, the vulnerability of being in a
room with bodies that were not his own. Yasmin had grown distant, hurt and confused by his
withdrawal. Last month, after she asked him directly whether he was still attracted to her, and he
could not answer because the truth was too complicated and too shameful, she suggested that he speak
to someone. It was not an ultimatum, but it felt close. Karim finally called a trauma-informed
therapist, terrified of what it would mean to put the event into words, but more terrified still of
losing the one person who had stayed. What frightened him most as he sat in the waiting room before
his first appointment was not the prospect of reliving the trauma itself, but the possibility that
once he examined it closely, once he allowed himself to think through it deliberately rather than
pushing it away, he would discover that his instincts had been so fundamentally broken that no
amount of therapy could repair them—that he truly could not rely on himself ever again.
