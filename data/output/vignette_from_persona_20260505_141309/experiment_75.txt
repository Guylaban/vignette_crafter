# Katja's Vigil

Katja Müller was thirty-eight when she volunteered with the International Medical Corps in eastern
Syria. She had flown combat support missions for twelve years—a decorated F-16 pilot with the Air
Force—and felt the pull toward something that mattered beyond the mechanics of her training. Her
marriage had weathered her deployments; her husband Marcus had learned to live with the silences
that came when she returned. But after the 2019 rotation, something in her shifted. She wanted to be
on the ground. She wanted to see the faces of the people she'd been protecting from the sky.

The clinic was housed in a converted school in a town whose name she still could not speak aloud.
Katja worked there for four months as a volunteer paramedic, treating blast injuries and
malnutrition, moving between the wards in the cold mornings before the generators hummed to life.
She had become competent at triage, at the small mercies of pain relief and clean bandages. She was
good at not thinking about what she'd seen from above—the same geography, now mapped in human
suffering.

On the morning of February 14th, a car approached the clinic perimeter. She was in the north wing,
restocking supplies. The explosion came through the walls like a physical force, and then there was
the sound—not the sound itself, but the absence of it, a pressure that swallowed all other noise.
When consciousness reassembled itself, Katja was on the floor, her ears ringing with a frequency
that seemed to originate inside her skull. Around her, the clinic was collapsing. She crawled toward
the main ward where she'd left two nurses and a translator. She found the translator first, pinned
beneath a concrete beam. The nurses were already gone.

She was airlifted out by a coalition helicopter. In the medical reports, she sustained a concussion,
a fractured clavicle, and lacerations. She was cleared for discharge after three weeks. No PTSD
screening. No follow-up. She returned to the States in April, and for the first few months, she felt
almost normal—numb in a way that seemed like resilience.

By autumn, the numbness had begun to crack. The first intrusive memory came while she was watching a
documentary about climate change. A scene of a city at dawn triggered something visceral—not the
explosion itself, but the quality of the light, the geometry of the buildings. She found herself
unable to move, unable to breathe, unable to explain to Marcus why she was standing in their living
room with her hands shaking. After that, she stopped watching television entirely. News,
documentaries, even advertisements with sudden loud noises—all of it became dangerous. She avoided
it by leaving the room, by turning her phone face-down, by filling the hours with shopping instead.
Online purchases arrived almost daily: clothes she didn't wear, kitchen equipment she'd never use.
The spending felt like a small anesthetic, a moment of control in a life that increasingly felt like
it belonged to someone else.

The cold was the worst trigger. When November came and the temperature dropped, Katja began waking
at two or three in the morning, her heart racing, convinced that someone had breached the house. She
would lie in bed listening—for footsteps, for the sound of a car door closing, for the particular
timbre of an engine that might signal danger. Marcus would wake to find her at the bedroom window,
peering into the darkness. She couldn't explain it rationally. She knew their neighborhood was safe.
But knowing and believing had become two separate things.

Her memories of the clinic had taken on a texture unlike anything else in her mind. When she tried
to recall her daughter's high school graduation from two years ago, the memory felt thin, almost
theoretical—she could access the facts of it but not the feeling. Yet the morning of the explosion
lived in her body with photographic intensity: the specific shade of blue in the sky, the smell of
dust mixed with something chemical, the weight of the translator's leg against her palms as she
tried to move the beam. She had come to believe that her mind was broken, that it had decided to
preserve only the worst moments and discard everything else.

What frightened her most was that she no longer recognized herself. The person who had chosen to
volunteer, who had felt purposeful in that clinic, seemed like a character from a book she'd read
long ago. Nothing felt meaningful now—not her marriage, not her career, not the life she'd carefully
constructed before Syria. She blamed herself relentlessly for going, for being in that room, for
surviving when the translator and the nurses had not. She cycled through the logic of it
obsessively: if she'd been in the south wing, if she'd left five minutes earlier, if she'd never
volunteered at all.

At forty-two, Katja finally called a therapist. She told Marcus she was going to start "talking to
someone." She didn't tell him that she was terrified—not of the memories themselves, but of the
possibility that no one could help her reassemble the person she'd been, or that she might have to
learn to live as someone entirely new.
