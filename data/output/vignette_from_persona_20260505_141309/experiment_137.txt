# Margaret's Story

Margaret was fifty-two when she came to her first appointment, though she moved and spoke like
someone older, her shoulders curved inward as though protecting something fragile. She had been
assaulted in the parking garage of the shopping mall where she'd gone to buy supplies for her
jewelry studio—wire, clasps, small gemstones she couldn't source online. It was a Tuesday afternoon
in October, four months before she called for help. The man had come from behind one of the concrete
pillars near the elevator, grabbed her from behind, and pulled her toward a van. She remembered his
hands on her jacket, the smell of cigarettes and cheap cologne, the way her purse fell and scattered
across the concrete. She remembered screaming, remembered a security guard appearing from nowhere,
remembered the attacker running. But entire stretches of those minutes existed as blank space in her
mind—she had no memory of how she'd gotten to the ground, no recollection of what the guard had said
to her, nothing between the moment the man released her and finding herself sitting in the mall's
security office, shaking.

What she did remember came back in fragments. A detail would surface without warning—the sound of
his breathing, the texture of his jacket sleeve—and then vanish again before she could hold it
steady in her mind. When she tried deliberately to reconstruct the event, to fill in the blank
spaces, something in her chest would tighten and she would find herself pushing the memory away,
unable to continue. The police had caught him two weeks later. She had seen his photograph in a
follow-up email from the detective. That night, she'd vomited for an hour.

For the first month after the assault, Margaret had continued going to her studio, a small rented
space in an old building downtown where she'd worked for seventeen years. But strangers had begun to
feel dangerous. A customer who came in to pick up a repaired necklace seemed to linger too long,
standing too close. A delivery man knocked on her studio door and she felt her body flood with
panic, certain in that moment that something was wrong, that he meant her harm. She started locking
the door even during business hours, asking customers to call before coming in. By November, she'd
stopped leaving the studio at all except to go home. She stopped meeting friends for coffee. When
her sister called to talk about their mother's declining health, about the possibility that their
mother might not have much time left, Margaret felt something collapse inside her. The thought of
loss, of people being taken away, made her feel sick. She didn't call her sister back.

The studio became smaller. She began to notice that she only felt truly safe in her apartment and in
the studio itself—two rooms she knew completely, where she could see the door, where nothing
unexpected could happen. Everywhere else, the moment she entered a space, her eyes went to the
exits. A restaurant, a bank, a friend's house—she would map the escape routes before she could sit
down, before she could focus on anything else. Her hands, which had always been steady enough to set
tiny diamonds into white gold, began to shake. She made mistakes. A client's engagement ring came
back with a stone set at the wrong angle. She had to remake it, and the woman never came back.

By January, Margaret had stopped leaving her apartment except for the studio. She felt dirty in a
way that showering couldn't fix, as though the assault had contaminated something inside her that
was permanent. Her friends had stopped calling. She told herself that she was handling it, that this
was just how she was now, but underneath that thought was another one: that her reactions proved she
was a lousy coper, that a stronger person would have moved past this by now, would not be this
afraid, would not feel this dead inside. She had begun to wonder if she would ever feel anything
again, or if this numb, vigilant, terrified version of herself was all that was left.

It was her landlord's mention of a break-in at another unit in the building that finally pushed her
to call a therapist. She realized, sitting in her apartment that night, that she couldn't live like
this anymore—but she also couldn't imagine how to change it.
