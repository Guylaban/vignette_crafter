# Priya's Testimony

Priya Mehta was seventy-three when she finally made the appointment. For eighteen months, she had
carried the weight of an afternoon that her mind could not quite assemble into a coherent story—one
that had fractured her sense of safety so thoroughly that she could no longer trust the texture of
her own skin.

It had happened on a humid July evening in 2023, outside the community center in her neighborhood
where she had been attending a local advocacy meeting about zoning changes that would affect the
small design studio she ran from home. The meeting had grown heated. Priya, who had spent fifty
years in interior design and took pride in her knowledge of urban planning, had spoken up about the
proposed changes. She remembered the feeling of the microphone in her hand, remembered the weight of
her own voice. Then—a gap. Then police arriving. Then hands on her arm, rough and insistent, and the
texture of something against her skin that she could not name, something that sent her nervous
system into a state of alarm she could not control even now, months later, when she was safe in her
own home.

The officers had been responding to a noise complaint, though no one could explain why they had
singled her out or why the interaction had escalated. Priya had tried to remain calm, tried to
explain that she was simply leaving, but the memory dissolved into fragments. She recalled the
uniform—the dark fabric, the badge, the way it had filled her vision—and then nothing coherent. She
remembered being on the ground. She remembered her arm hurting. She remembered the sound of her own
voice, though she could not now find words adequate to what she had said or what she had felt. When
she tried to reconstruct the sequence for the police report, for her daughter, for her own
understanding, the pieces would not fit. There were gaps like missing teeth in her recollection,
spaces where her mind simply would not go.

In the months that followed, Priya had begun to withdraw from the world in ways that surprised her.
She canceled her design consultations. When a client arrived at her studio to discuss a kitchen
renovation, Priya had felt her heart accelerate at the sound of the doorbell, and she had not been
able to continue the conversation. She began to avoid the community center entirely, taking longer
routes through her neighborhood. But it was not just places—it was the texture of things. A client
had sent fabric samples for a living room project, and when Priya touched the rough linen, something
in her body had seized. The texture was similar to the fabric she could not quite remember pressing
against her skin during the incident, and her nervous system had responded as though the officer
were there in her studio, hands reaching for her again. She had not been able to explain this to the
client. How could she?

What frightened her most now was the certainty that had settled into her bones: that her own body
had become an enemy. At night, she would lie awake feeling her heart race without cause, her hands
trembling as though she were still being grabbed, her breath coming shallow even though she was
alone in her bedroom. She had come to believe that nothing good could happen to her anymore—that
this was simply how her life would be now, fractured and unsafe, her body perpetually betraying her.
The smallest upset—a raised voice on television, an unexpected knock at her door—would send her into
a state of panic she could not manage. She had always been someone who handled difficulties with
grace. Now, she could barely handle the slightest disturbance.

Her daughter had insisted she seek help. Priya had resisted for months, uncertain what talking could
possibly do about a memory that refused to cohere, about a body that would not obey her mind's
insistence that she was safe. But the isolation was becoming unbearable. She sat in the therapist's
office now, seventy-three years old, unable to fully articulate what had happened to her, terrified
that her body would never stop treating her as though she were still in danger, and wondering if
there was any way back to the woman she had been before that afternoon dissolved into fragments she
could not piece together.
