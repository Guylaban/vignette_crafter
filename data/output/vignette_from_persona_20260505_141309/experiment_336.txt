# Priya

Priya was thirty-seven when she came to the clinic, though her intake form showed a woman whose
hands trembled as she filled it out. She had owned her sari and fabric shop in Queens for eight
years—a modest storefront on Roosevelt Avenue where she had built a careful life, one bolt of silk
and cotton at a time. Her husband Rahul had been supportive through the early years, and for a long
stretch, things had felt manageable. But the past two years had become something else entirely, and
it was only when her business began to suffer that she finally admitted something was desperately
wrong.

The assault had happened five years ago, in the back room of a different shop where Priya had worked
before opening her own. A man had followed her in during a quiet afternoon—not a customer, someone
she had never seen before. What happened in those minutes had fractured something in her that she
could not name, could not articulate even now. When she tried to tell the police officer what had
occurred, the words had simply stopped coming. She could feel it happening again—the pressure, the
terror, the way her body had gone rigid—but the language for it remained locked away. That
wordlessness had never fully lifted.

For the first year after, Priya had managed by narrowing her world. She had switched jobs, moved to
the shop she now owned, and tried to rebuild. But over the past two years, the narrowing had
accelerated into something that was slowly suffocating her business and her marriage. She had
stopped taking the subway to restock her inventory, insisting instead on expensive car services that
were eating into her profits. The irrationality frustrated her—she understood intellectually that
the subway was safe—but the thought of being trapped underground, surrounded by strangers, made her
chest seize. She had begun ordering supplies online instead, paying premium prices, and her shop's
aesthetic had suffered. Regulars had noticed.

What made things worse was the way certain people could trigger a cascade of fear that lasted for
hours. If a woman came into her shop who resembled one of the victims Priya had read about in news
coverage of similar crimes—dark hair, a certain build, a particular way of moving—Priya would find
herself unable to breathe normally. She would become hypervigilant, watching that customer's every
gesture, her body flooded with adrenaline. Worse still were the names. When customers mentioned
exes, or when she overheard someone on the phone saying a name that sounded like the man's, her mind
would snap into a state of raw alertness that felt indistinguishable from the moment itself.

During these episodes, Priya discovered she had become someone she didn't recognize. She would
become excessively agreeable, almost obsequious with customers who made her anxious—saying yes to
unreasonable requests, underpricing fabrics, apologizing for things that weren't her fault. Rahul
had commented on it once, and she had snapped at him so viciously that he had stopped mentioning it.
She knew what she was doing—knew she was trying to appease, to make herself small and
unthreatening—but she couldn't seem to stop.

The flashbacks were the worst part. They would arrive without warning: a sudden movement in her
peripheral vision, someone turning too quickly near her, and suddenly Priya was back in that room,
her heart hammering as though the assault were happening in real time. She had learned to position
herself in her shop with her back against the wall, always able to see the door and the entire
floor. She felt unsafe anywhere else. In restaurants, she would become anxious until she could shift
her chair. At home, she had rearranged her bedroom so the bed faced the door.

When she remembered the event deliberately—which she tried not to do—the terror returned with such
intensity that she would sometimes be sick afterward. Along with the terror came a crushing guilt
that made no logical sense to her. She had done nothing wrong, yet some part of her had come to
believe she was responsible, that she had somehow invited this catastrophe through some flaw or
carelessness she couldn't identify. That guilt was perhaps more unbearable than the fear itself.

It was Rahul who finally insisted she seek help. He had found her one evening standing in the shop
after closing, unable to move, her eyes fixed on nothing. "This is destroying us," he had said
quietly. "I need my wife back." Priya had wept then, not from his words but from the recognition
that she was losing herself piece by piece, and that the person who had done this to her was still,
in a way, controlling her every move.
