# Priya

Priya Sharma was forty-seven when she first sat in the intake office, her posture rigid despite the
soft chair, her hands folded in her lap with the kind of deliberateness that suggested she had
rehearsed this positioning. She had been the associate pastor at Mount Olive Community Church for
nine years before the leave of absence began six months ago. The leave, she said quietly, was
supposed to be temporary.

Twenty-three years earlier, during a mission trip to rural Nepal, Priya and two colleagues had been
detained at a military checkpoint. What began as a routine document check escalated into
interrogation, then into something far worse. For three days, she was held in a concrete cell. The
interrogators wanted information about the aid organization's funding sources; they believed the
group was connected to a separatist movement. The torture was not only physical. They forced her to
ingest a bitter medicinal paste mixed with ash—the specific taste of it, metallic and acrid with
something else she could never quite identify, had become a ghost in her mouth. When she tasted
anything vaguely similar now—certain spices, some medications, even the iron-tinged water from an
old pipe—her body seized before her mind could catch up. Her heart would race, her breathing would
shallow, and she would find herself standing in that cell again, though she could never quite piece
together the sequence of what happened there. The memories came to her in fragments: a guard's
voice, the sound of a door slamming, the cold of the concrete floor against her skin, but they did
not fit together into a coherent story. Some moments were crystalline—she could see the pattern of
cracks in the ceiling, could feel the exact pressure of restraints on her wrists—while other
stretches of time were simply gone, erased.

She had survived. The organization had negotiated her release along with her colleagues. She had
returned to the United States and, after several years of what she described as "managing," had
found her way into ministry. Serving others, she had believed, was a way to make meaning from what
had been done to her. For nearly two decades, it had worked. She had built a reputation as a
compassionate pastor, known for her willingness to sit with people in their suffering. But something
had shifted in the past year. A new member of the congregation—a man with a certain way of speaking,
a certain impatience in his voice—had begun attending services regularly. Priya had never learned
his name or anything about him, but her body knew something her mind could not articulate. She
became hypervigilant in the sanctuary, tracking his movements, noting when he arrived and left,
unable to focus on her own sermon when he was present.

Then came the coffee hour after a service in January. Someone had brought a traditional Nepali tea
blend, something with cardamom and an unfamiliar root. Priya took a sip and tasted it—that taste,
the one she had not encountered in two decades, the one she had thought she had escaped. She set the
cup down and excused herself, but the taste remained. That evening, she called the church office and
told them she needed to take a leave. She did not explain why.

In the months that followed, Priya had constructed a life of careful control. She ate very little
and only foods she had prepared herself, foods she knew completely. She no longer attended social
events where she might encounter unfamiliar tastes or unfamiliar people. She spent most of her time
at home, where she could monitor who came and went. She had begun to avoid thinking about the
detention altogether, pushing away any memory that surfaced, certain that if she allowed herself to
fully remember, to fully feel the weight of what had happened, she would collapse entirely. Yet the
memories came anyway, in pieces that made no sense, and with each fragment came a crushing
guilt—guilt about what she had said under duress, guilt about having survived when she sometimes
felt she did not deserve to, guilt about the fact that she could no longer trust her own judgment
about safety, about people, about anything.

What frightened her most was not the taste itself or the hypervigilance or even the fragmented
nightmares. It was the certainty that had settled into her bones: that she was no longer capable of
being a pastor, that the person she had built herself to be was gone, and that there was no future
where that person returned. Even when her therapist, her sister, her former colleagues told her she
was safe now, that she was in America, that the detention was decades behind her, Priya could not
believe them. Her body would not believe them. It reacted to danger long before her conscious mind
had time to think, and she had learned to trust that reaction more than she trusted words.

It was her sister who finally insisted she seek help. Priya had not spoken about the detention in
years, and the thought of speaking about it now—of putting those fragmented pieces into words, of
watching someone's face as she described what had been done to her—made her want to retreat further.
But she was losing everything. The church had begun asking questions about her return date. Her
apartment felt smaller each week. And the taste, that taste, seemed to be everywhere now, haunting
even foods that logically could not contain it.

She came to the appointment because she had run out of ways to avoid the life she had once had, and
because avoiding it was destroying what remained.
