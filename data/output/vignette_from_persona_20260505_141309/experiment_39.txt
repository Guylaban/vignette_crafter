# Manny's Watch

Manny Tan had been a public defender for forty-two years, and his calendar had always been crowded
with other people's traumas. At sixty-eight, he had learned to compartmentalize—to sit across from a
client describing a violent assault, to absorb their story without flinching, to build a case. But
on a Tuesday evening in March, the compartments broke. His wife of thirty-nine years, Rosario, was
struck by a delivery van while crossing a street two blocks from their home. She was in the ICU for
four days. She died without waking.

The police report was sparse, the kind of document Manny had read a thousand times before in his
office. What he could not make coherent was the gap between the words—"struck at approximately 4:15
p.m."—and the actual moment, which came to him only in pieces. A phone call from the hospital. The
smell of the ER waiting room. Rosario's hand, still warm, but her face already foreign. A nurse
saying something about blunt force trauma. He could not stitch these fragments into a single memory;
they floated separately, disconnected, each one vivid but refusing to form a sequence. And
underneath them, he sometimes heard the sound of the impact itself—a dull, heavy crack that he had
not witnessed but that his mind had constructed and now replayed without warning, usually when he
was alone in the kitchen or sitting in his car before work.

In the weeks after the funeral, Manny became certain that danger was everywhere. Not in the abstract
way he had understood risk during his career, but as a constant, specific threat that required
vigilance. He began watching people—really watching them, the way they moved, their expressions,
whether they seemed stable or erratic. At the grocery store, he tracked a man in a red jacket for
three aisles, convinced something about his gait meant he was unstable. At the courthouse, he found
himself studying the faces of people in the hallways, searching for signs of volatility. His
colleagues noticed he was different, but they attributed it to grief. They did not know that he was
exhausted from the constant surveillance, or that his body had begun to treat ordinary moments as
emergencies.

He started checking the locks on his house obsessively—the front door, the back door, the windows in
the bedroom. He would lie in bed and suddenly become certain that he had not locked the kitchen
window, and he would get up, walk downstairs in the dark, and verify it. Sometimes he would check
again five minutes later, having forgotten that he had already done so. The checking offered a kind
of temporary relief, a brief moment where he could convince himself that he had done something to
prevent the next catastrophe.

Food became a way to interrupt his thoughts. When the fragmented memories came, or when the grief
hit him suddenly—a sharp, suffocating wave that made him feel like he was drowning—he would eat. Not
because he was hungry, but because the act of eating, the taste and texture and the need to focus on
swallowing, created a small barrier between himself and the feeling. He gained fifteen pounds. His
pants no longer fit. He stopped going to the office on Fridays and worked from home instead, where
he could avoid the hallways and the constant need to monitor the people around him.

He had stopped watching television, stopped reading the news. Any story about accidents, about
violence, about loss would trigger that sound again—that crack—and then the fragmenting, the
inability to hold a complete thought. He had also stopped answering calls from friends. He told
himself he was protecting them from his darkness, but the truth was simpler: he could not trust
people anymore. They could be taken. They could disappear. The only certainty was that vigilance
might buy him time.

His daughter finally insisted he come in. She had noticed the weight gain, the dark circles under
his eyes, the way he flinched when doors closed. She called it grief, and it was, but it was also
something else—a state of constant alarm that had calcified into his new normal. Manny sat in the
waiting room of the therapist's office, watching the other patients, still searching their faces. He
was terrified of what would happen if he stopped watching. And he was terrified that no amount of
watching would ever prevent the next loss.
