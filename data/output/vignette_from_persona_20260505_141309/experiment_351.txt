# Clinical Vignette: María

María was forty-three when she came to her first appointment, her hands folded tightly in her lap,
her voice steady but thin. Five months earlier, on a Tuesday afternoon at the automotive body shop
where she worked part-time as a supervisor—a job she had taken on after her husband Roberto died
three years prior—she had been training a new employee on the pneumatic rivet gun. The shop was
loud, as it always was: the percussion of impact tools, the hiss of compressed air lines, the radio
playing Spanish-language news. María had been demonstrating the proper grip and angle when her foot
caught on a coil of hose. She stumbled. The rivet gun, still pressurized, discharged directly into
the young man's shoulder.

The sound of his scream had cut through the shop noise in a way that seemed to silence everything
else. María could not, no matter how hard she tried in the months that followed, reconstruct the
full sequence of what happened next. She knew she had called 911. She knew the paramedics had
arrived. She knew the young man—Diego, twenty-two years old—had been transported to the hospital,
where he underwent surgery to repair the soft tissue and vascular damage. He had survived. He had
recovered, though with limited mobility in his right arm. But there were gaps in her memory of those
moments, dark spaces that remained inaccessible no matter how many times she tried to piece them
together. What remained crystalline was the sensation of the gun bucking in her hands and the
quality of his scream.

In the weeks after the incident, María had developed an intense, almost magnetic awareness of
anything that could be wielded as a weapon. She noticed the rivet gun hanging on the wall of her
home office—she had brought one home years ago for a project—and she began to feel physically ill
when her eyes landed on it. A kitchen knife, previously unremarkable, now seemed to occupy space in
her peripheral vision with an almost aggressive presence. When she saw news coverage of a shooting,
or when someone mentioned a hammer or a wrench in conversation, her body would flood with a
sensation she could only describe as dread. It was not a thought, exactly. Sometimes there was no
image at all, just the overwhelming rush of guilt and terror, the full emotional weight of that
moment compressed into her chest without any accompanying memory of what had triggered it. She would
find herself standing in the kitchen, trembling, unable to account for why.

Confrontations became intolerable. When a coworker raised his voice during a team meeting about a
missed deadline, María felt her throat close. When her sister called to discuss a family
disagreement, María found herself unable to continue the conversation; she had to hang up, her hands
shaking. She had come to believe that she was fundamentally unsafe around other people, that her
presence was a liability. This belief had calcified over months until it shaped her days. She began
working from home whenever possible, citing technical reasons. She declined invitations to her
church community, which had been a source of stability since Roberto's death. She stopped attending
her grief support group.

The losses accumulated quietly. Her supervisor at the shop had, after several missed shifts and
increasingly vague excuses, reassigned her to remote database work. Her sister had stopped calling
as frequently. The house felt smaller.

What finally brought her to seek help was a conversation with her therapist from the grief
group—someone she had run into at the grocery store and had found herself, unexpectedly, telling the
truth to. The therapist had listened and then asked a simple question: "What are you most afraid
will happen if you stay the way you are right now?" María had gone home and sat with that question
for three days. The answer was that she was afraid of becoming a ghost in her own life, afraid that
the accident had not just injured Diego but had killed something essential in her, and that she
would spend the rest of her years alone, certain that she was dangerous, unable to grieve Roberto or
to live in any meaningful way.
