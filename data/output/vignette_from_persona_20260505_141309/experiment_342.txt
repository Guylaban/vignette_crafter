# Layla

Layla was thirty years old when she came to the therapist's office on a Tuesday evening, the only
time she could leave the bar where she had worked for the past six years. She sat with her hands
folded tightly in her lap, her dark eyes tracking the therapist's movements with the kind of
attention that suggested she was cataloguing exits and distances. She had called to schedule the
appointment three times before actually keeping it, each cancellation preceded by a night of lying
awake, rehearsing what might go wrong in a stranger's office—the door might jam, she might have a
panic attack and not be able to leave, the therapist might judge her, might think she was broken
beyond repair. But her ex-husband's new fiancée had recently moved into the apartment building next
to hers, and seeing that woman's car in the parking lot had triggered something in Layla that she
could no longer manage alone.

The trauma itself lived in her body more than in her words. Layla had been seven years old when her
father's anger had finally erupted into violence in their living room in Dearborn. She remembered
the sound more than the sight—a crash, her mother's cry, the wet thud of impact. She remembered her
own small body pressed against the wall behind the kitchen doorframe, the way her vision had
narrowed to a sliver of the room where her parents were. Her father's fists. Her mother's face. The
feeling that the world had split open and would never close again. When she tried to describe it
now, the words scattered like startled birds. She knew what had happened—she could see it clearly in
her mind—but the moment she tried to speak it aloud, something seized in her throat. The emotion
would rise up so suddenly and so completely that she would have to stop mid-sentence, her chest
heaving, her vision blurring. It was easier not to try.

What she could articulate was the vigilance. Any sudden movement—a hand raised too quickly, a man
standing up abruptly at the bar, a raised voice from a patron—sent her nervous system into high
alert. Her body would go rigid, her breath shallow, her mind flooding with worst-case scenarios. She
would imagine the customer becoming aggressive, imagine a fight breaking out, imagine herself unable
to stop it or escape it. She rehearsed these possibilities constantly, as though mental preparation
could somehow inoculate her against harm. If she thought through what might happen, if she imagined
every terrible outcome in advance, then perhaps she could be ready. Perhaps she could protect
herself the way no one had protected her mother.

She lived in a carefully controlled world. Her apartment was her safe zone—she had installed three
additional locks on the door and checked them compulsively each night, sometimes returning to verify
them again within minutes, her hand on the deadbolt, her mind unable to accept that she had already
secured it. She avoided the open parking lot, instead parking directly beneath her building's
entrance. She did not go to parks or beaches or anywhere she could not quickly identify a way out.
When she had to be in open spaces—the bar's patio where she sometimes worked—she positioned herself
near the interior door, her body angled toward escape.

Her marriage had failed partly because of this. Her ex-husband had wanted her to be different, to
relax, to trust him. But trusting anyone felt impossible. She could not rely on herself to make good
decisions, could not trust that she would do the right thing in a crisis, could not believe that
people—even those who claimed to love her—would not eventually reveal themselves to be dangerous.
She had tried to control her emotions around him, to present a calm exterior, but the effort had
been exhausting and ultimately unconvincing. He had grown distant, then resentful, then gone.

Now, sitting in the therapist's office, Layla's hands trembled slightly as she explained why she had
come. She was afraid—not just of what might happen, but of what she might do or fail to do when it
did. She was afraid of being alone, and afraid of being close to anyone. She was hoping, in a way
she barely dared to articulate, that someone could help her find words for what had happened, and
that finding those words might somehow make the weight of it slightly less impossible to carry.
