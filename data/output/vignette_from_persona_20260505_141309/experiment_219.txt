# Fadi's Silence

Fadi Mansour was seventy-eight when the accident happened. He had spent fifty-two years as an
environmental scientist, the last thirty-five at a consulting firm where he was known for his
meticulous attention to detail and his quiet, methodical way of solving problems. His colleagues
respected him. His wife, Layla, whom he had married in Damascus before immigrating to California in
1975, had grown accustomed to his long silences—they were the silences of a man thinking, not a man
troubled. Or so she had believed.

The incident occurred on a Saturday morning in October at the community garden where Fadi and Layla
volunteered. Fadi had been helping an elderly neighbor, Mrs. Chen, move a heavy wooden planter box.
She was frail, in her mid-eighties, and he had done this work many times before. But that morning,
as they both lifted, his foot caught on an uneven stone. He fell forward. The planter—filled with
soil and stone, weighing perhaps eighty pounds—came down across her left leg. She screamed. The
sound was immediate and terrible. When the paramedics arrived, they told him her femur was fractured
in three places. She would need surgery. She would be in the hospital for weeks.

Fadi had apologized profusely. Mrs. Chen, from her hospital bed, had told him it was an accident.
Layla had held his hand and said the same thing. But something in Fadi had fractured too, in a way
that no surgery could repair.

For the first few weeks, he could not sleep. When he closed his eyes, he was back in that
moment—Mrs. Chen's weight shifting, his own body falling, the sickening impact of stone on bone. But
the strangest part was that when he tried to speak about it to Layla, the words simply would not
come. He knew what had happened. He could see it, frame by frame, as if watching a film. But when he
opened his mouth, nothing emerged that felt adequate or even real. He would start: "I was holding
the—" and then stop, frustrated. The event felt too extreme, too impossible. Had it really happened
that way? Had the sound really been so loud? Had Mrs. Chen's cry really lasted so long? His mind
kept rejecting the memory as though it were a lie he had invented.

Three months into his suffering, Fadi began to notice that certain sounds made him acutely afraid.
Heavy breathing—the kind Layla made when she was relaxed on the sofa in the evening, or the sound of
his own breath when he exerted himself—triggered a panic that felt like drowning. He realized,
though he could not articulate it clearly, that it was the sound of life continuing, of ease, while
he carried this weight. When Layla tried to embrace him one evening, reaching for him as she always
did, he stiffened. Her arms around him felt like a trap. He could not bear the tenderness. It made
no sense to him; he loved his wife. But affection felt dangerous now, as if kindness were a delusion
he could no longer afford.

He began to withdraw from work. He took early retirement, citing age and fatigue, though his
colleagues were surprised—Fadi had seemed vital just months before. At home, he spent long hours in
his study, ostensibly reading, but mostly sitting in silence. He avoided the community garden
entirely. He began making excuses not to see his grandchildren. When they visited, he would retreat
to another room. Layla grew worried. She asked him repeatedly if he was unwell, if he needed to see
a doctor. He could not explain to her that what was wrong with him had no medical name, or rather,
that it had a name but the name itself felt like an insult to the woman whose leg he had broken.

It was Layla who finally insisted he see someone. She had noticed him becoming smaller, more absent,
as if he were disappearing into the guilt. She made the appointment herself. Fadi came to the first
session reluctant and ashamed, carrying the memory of Mrs. Chen like a stone he could neither put
down nor speak aloud.
