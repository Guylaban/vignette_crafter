# Linh's Vigil

Linh was forty-eight when she came to the clinic, her hands folded carefully in her lap, her eyes
moving once toward the door and then settling on a point somewhere past the therapist's shoulder.
She had been married to Michael for eighteen years. For the last three of them, she had been afraid
of him in ways she could not fully articulate to anyone—not to her sister in Sacramento, not to the
two high school students she still tutored three afternoons a week from her home office, not even to
herself in the quiet moments before sleep.

The night that changed everything had started ordinarily. Michael came home late from work, and Linh
had reheated his dinner. He was angry about something—a perceived slight from a colleague, a
frustration that had nothing to do with her—but by the time they were alone in the kitchen, his
anger had found her as its object. She had said something that displeased him, something so small
she could not remember it now, only the sudden movement of his hand, the shock of his grip on her
arm, the way he had pushed her against the counter and then, when she tried to turn away, struck her
across the face. The pain had been enormous and bright. What stayed with her more than the pain,
though, was the moment she saw his expression shift into something she did not recognize—a
blankness, a removal of the person she thought she knew. He had grabbed a kitchen knife from the
block and held it, not toward her but in his hand, and said things she could not now reconstruct
except for the feeling they carried: that she was the problem, that she had made him do this, that
she should be afraid of what he might do next.

The police had come. There had been a hospital visit, bruising that bloomed across her ribs and
shoulder, a fractured cheekbone. Michael had been arrested and then released on bail. The legal
process had moved forward in its slow way. But long before the court date, something had reorganized
itself inside Linh's body and mind in ways that felt permanent.

Now, three years later, she lived in a state of constant surveillance. In her tutoring sessions, she
found herself watching her students' hands, noticing the letter opener on the desk, the heavy
textbooks that could be weapons. When she was in the grocery store, she scanned the aisles for exits
and for men who moved with a certain kind of purposefulness. In her own home—which should have been
safe, though it no longer felt that way—she checked mirrors constantly, positioning herself so she
could see behind her, watching for Michael even though he was no longer in the house, even though
the restraining order was in place. Her body would sometimes react before her mind caught up: a loud
noise and she would flinch, her heart racing, her breath shallow, before she even understood what
had startled her.

The memories of that night came to her in fragments that would not cohere into a single narrative.
She remembered his face, but the sequence of events was scrambled, pieces arriving out of order like
a photograph that had been torn and reassembled incorrectly. When she tried to recall what had
happened, the terror returned with it—not as a memory of fear but as the fear itself, alive in her
chest and throat as though the event were happening again. And sometimes, lying awake at three in
the morning, she could see it all with a clarity that was almost photographic: the exact angle of
the light from the refrigerator, the pattern of the tile floor, his hands.

What frightened Linh most was the conviction that had taken root in her: that she had been
fundamentally altered, that some essential part of her had been broken and could not be repaired.
She had been a different woman before—more open, more trusting. Now she was someone who noticed
weapons in every room, who could not relax even in spaces she knew to be objectively safe, who had
begun to believe that you could never truly know another person, that harm could come from anywhere,
from anyone. She had become an outsider to her own life, watching it from a distance, unable to
fully inhabit the moments she was living. Her marriage was technically intact but functionally
hollow; Michael was in the house only on court-ordered weekends, and during those times Linh felt as
though she were holding her breath.

It was her sister who finally insisted she seek help. Linh had called her three weeks ago, unable to
explain why she had spent an entire tutoring session with her hands shaking, why she could no longer
leave the house without checking the mirrors multiple times, why the thought of being alone in a
room with any man—even her brother-in-law, even the kind elderly neighbor—sent her into a state of
dread. She had said, "I don't know if I can go back to being who I was," and her sister had said,
"Then let someone help you figure out who you are now."
