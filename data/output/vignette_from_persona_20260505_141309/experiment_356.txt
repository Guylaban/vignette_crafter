# Ama

Ama was seventy-seven when she finally named what had happened to her at fifty-one. For twenty-six
years she had carried the injury as something private, something she had learned to work around the
way one works around a chronic pain—by adjusting posture, by avoiding certain movements, by
pretending the damage was not there. It was only when her daughter, now a physician, had gently
suggested that her mother's vigilance at night, her way of checking the locks three times before bed
and sleeping with her hand on a phone, might be connected to something that needed attention, that
Ama had begun to acknowledge the shape of what her then-husband had done to her.

The beating had occurred on an ordinary Tuesday evening in their home in Atlanta. He had come home
angry—she could not now recall what had triggered it, though she had spent decades trying to piece
together that missing part, as if understanding the cause might retroactively make sense of the
violence. What remained vivid was fragmented: his voice rising, her own body moving backward through
the kitchen, the moment when his hand connected with her face and she felt something inside her
skull shift. After that, the memory became a series of photographs with gaps between them. She
remembered blood on her hands—his or hers, she was no longer certain—and a ringing in her ears. She
remembered the floor, and then nothing, and then the hospital, and then the careful lie she had
constructed for the emergency room doctor about a fall.

She had left him six months later, but the fear had never left. What frightened her most was not the
possibility that he would find her now, decades later, a woman in her late seventies with a career
in local politics and a different life in a different city. What frightened her was that she no
longer fully trusted her own mind to hold the story intact. Whenever she tried to remember the
complete sequence of events, something in her would rebel. She would begin to recall his expression,
the words he had said, and then a pressure would build in her chest and she would push the memory
away, as if her own mind were protecting her from something she could not bear to know. The gaps
remained. She had come to believe that this fragmentation was permanent damage, that some part of
her had been irreversibly broken that night and would never be whole again.

The triggers were everywhere, though she had become expert at managing them. When a colleague in the
city council chamber raised his voice during a heated debate about zoning ordinances—his tone
perfectly reasonable, his anger measured and professional—Ama found her breathing shallow, her body
tense. She would excuse herself, citing a need to check on something in her office, and would sit
alone for ten minutes, reminding herself that this man was not him, that anger itself was not
violence. But the distinction had become difficult to maintain. Any expression of anger, even when
she knew intellectually it posed no threat, sent a signal through her body that danger was near.

Proximity was worse. When someone stood too close to her at a community event or a political
fundraiser, she would feel her shoulders rise, her jaw tighten. She had developed the habit of
positioning herself near doorways, of keeping her back to a wall. At night, the vigilance
intensified. She lay awake, listening to the sounds of her house, checking the locks repeatedly even
though she had already checked them. Her husband, Marcus, had learned not to approach her suddenly
in the darkness; more than once she had startled so violently at his touch that she had nearly
struck him.

The guilt was perhaps the most isolating aspect. When she allowed herself to remember—when the
fragments came together briefly before she could push them away—she felt a crushing sense of shame,
as if she had somehow caused the violence, as if her failure to understand what had angered him had
invited the blow. She had never told Marcus the full story. She had told him there had been an
abusive relationship, that it had ended, that it had left its mark. But the specifics, the blood,
the gaps in her memory, the way she sometimes could not trust her own recollection of what had
happened—these she kept to herself.

She had come to believe that people could not be trusted, that the violence she had experienced was
not exceptional but rather the true nature of intimate relationships, revealed. She had built a
career, maintained a marriage, raised children, and done all of it while operating from the
conviction that she had been permanently altered, that whatever capacity she had once possessed for
genuine feeling, for openness, for the kind of trust that allows a person to truly rest, had been
destroyed that Tuesday evening and would never return. It was only when she could no longer hide the
nighttime vigilance, when her startle response had become so pronounced that she had jumped at a
colleague's greeting and caused a small scene at a public event, that she had finally agreed to seek
help. She was afraid that speaking about it would make it more real, but she was also afraid that
remaining silent would mean living the rest of her life as a prisoner of fragments and fear.
