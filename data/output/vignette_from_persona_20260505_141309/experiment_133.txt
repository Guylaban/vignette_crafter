# Clinical Vignette: Kofi

Kofi was fifty-nine when his sister drowned. The call came on a Tuesday afternoon in July—his
brother-in-law's voice fractured and incomprehensible at first, then brutally clear: Ama had gone
into the lake at their family cottage in Michigan. She had been a strong swimmer, had grown up in
Accra near the ocean, had taught her own children to swim. But that day, a sudden cramp, a
miscalculation of the current, the weight of the water, the time it took for anyone to notice she
had not come back to shore. By the time they found her, it was too late.

Kofi had not been there. He was in his office at the university, grading papers, when the world
became a place where his sister no longer existed.

The weeks that followed were a blur of funeral arrangements, family gatherings, the strange
machinery of grief. But it was not until late August, when the semester began again, that Kofi first
noticed something had changed in his body. He was lecturing on postcolonial literature—a subject he
had taught for thirty-two years—when a student mentioned a novel set near water. His heart began to
race. Sweat bloomed across his chest. His hands trembled as he reached for chalk. He finished the
sentence, but something had broken open inside him, and he could not quite close it again.

Over the following months, the triggers multiplied and sharpened. Any reference to water in his
lectures sent his nervous system into high alert. He began avoiding films and novels that contained
scenes near lakes or rivers. He stopped accepting invitations to restaurants with water views. When
his department held a faculty gathering at a riverside park in October, Kofi called in sick—the
first time in his career he had missed a departmental event. His colleagues noticed. His department
chair, concerned, had asked if everything was alright. Kofi had lied and said it was just a
migraine.

The smell was worse. In early September, after heavy rain, the air carried a particular
dampness—earthy, mineral, alive—that sent him spiraling. It was the smell of the lake, he was
certain, though he had never been to that cottage, had never smelled that particular water. Yet his
body knew. His body knew before his mind could catch up with the knowledge. He would be walking
across campus, the smell would hit him, and suddenly his pulse would thunder in his ears, his breath
would shallow, his legs would feel weak. He had learned to carry a scarf soaked in cologne,
something sharp and chemical that he could hold to his face to override the smell. His graduate
students had begun to ask why he always seemed to have a scarf with him, why he sometimes smelled
overwhelmingly of it. He offered no explanation.

By November, Kofi had begun to avoid his office during the afternoon, when the light came through
the windows in a particular way—bright and reflective—that reminded him of water. He started coming
in early, leaving late, or working from home. His research output dropped. He had missed the
deadline for submitting a book chapter. He had declined an invitation to give a keynote address at a
conference in Toronto. His colleagues were beginning to worry, though none of them knew why.

What frightened him most was that even in his apartment, in the evening, he could not relax. He
would sit in his living room with the television on, the doors locked, the windows covered, and
still his body would not settle. His muscles remained tense. His mind would not quiet. He would lie
in bed for hours, waiting for sleep that would not come, his heart occasionally spiking with a fear
he could not name. He had come to believe that his body was no longer under his control—that it
would betray him at any moment, that danger was always imminent, that he had failed to protect his
sister and would now fail to protect himself.

It was his colleague Dr. Mensah who finally insisted he see someone. She had noticed how withdrawn
he had become, how he flinched at unexpected sounds, how he had lost weight. She did not know about
Ama, but she recognized the shape of suffering. Kofi had resisted for weeks, but in December, when
he realized he was considering taking early retirement—that the thought of returning to campus in
January filled him with a dread so profound he could barely breathe—he finally made an appointment.
He sat in the waiting room, his hands shaking, hoping that someone could help him find his way back
to himself.
