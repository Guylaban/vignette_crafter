# Kofi's Silence

Kofi had built his career on precision. For thirty-eight years as an investigative journalist, first
in Accra and then in Atlanta after immigrating in 1995, he had trained himself to observe, record,
and render the world into language that was clear, irrefutable, complete. He had won awards. His
colleagues knew him as meticulous, almost obsessive about getting the details right. But that was
before his son Daniel died.

It was three years ago now—a Tuesday in March when Daniel, twenty-nine, a software engineer with a
wife and a daughter, had been struck by a delivery truck while crossing the street near his office
in Midtown. The impact was instantaneous. By the time the paramedics arrived, Daniel was already
gone. Kofi had not witnessed it himself; he had learned of it through a phone call from his
daughter-in-law, her voice fractured and incomprehensible at first, just raw sound. But he had seen
the scene photographs later, had walked the intersection, had interviewed witnesses with the same
methodical attention he had given to stories of corruption and injustice for decades. The truck
driver had not seen him. A moment of inattention. A life erased.

For weeks after the funeral, Kofi had attempted to write about what had happened. Not for
publication—though his editor had gently suggested a personal essay might help—but for himself. He
would sit at his desk at the *Atlanta Chronicle*, where he still worked, and try to construct a
coherent account. He would begin: "My son was killed on March 14th at the intersection of Peachtree
and 11th..." and then the words would dissolve. He would know the facts, could recite them in
sequence, but the moment he tried to explain the weight of it—the specific texture of loss, the way
his wife's face had looked when he told her, the exact quality of the silence that filled their
house afterward—the thread would slip from his grasp. His mind would scatter. He would find himself
staring at the screen, unable to retrieve the words that had always come so readily to him. It was
as if the language he had mastered had become a foreign thing, locked behind glass.

The memory itself was vivid in ways that tormented him. When it returned—unpredictably, in moments
of ordinary Tuesday afternoons—he could taste the metallic sourness of the coffee he had been
drinking when the phone call came. That taste would bloom on his tongue without warning, and with it
would come the full weight of the moment, the knowledge that his son was gone, that the future he
had imagined—Daniel's daughter growing up, the quiet pride of watching her graduate, become a
woman—had been obliterated in an instant.

Certain conversations had become unbearable. Two months after Daniel's death, a colleague had
mentioned that he was worried about his own son, who drove too fast. Kofi had felt the room tilt. He
could not stay at his desk. He had walked out without explanation. Another time, at a dinner party,
someone had spoken casually about their fear of losing parents—the normal anxieties of aging. Kofi
had excused himself and sat in his car for an hour, unable to return. And when his wife had tried to
talk about the future, about whether they might travel, whether they might visit family in Ghana as
they had planned, something in him had seized. The future had become a thing he could not bear to
contemplate or discuss. It felt like a betrayal of Daniel to move forward, to plan, to imagine a
life that continued without him.

His wife, Ama, had urged him to seek help. She had noticed that he had withdrawn from his
colleagues, that he had taken a leave from the *Chronicle*, that he sat in silence for hours. She
had also noticed something she did not fully understand: that when she tried to speak to him about
the loss, about her own grief, Kofi would become frustrated, as though the words themselves were
failing him, as though he could not bridge the gap between what he felt and what he could say. He
had always been a man of language. Now language had abandoned him.

It was Ama who had finally made the appointment with the psychologist. Kofi had resisted, but he had
come. He was sixty-three years old, a man who had reported on the suffering of others for nearly
four decades, now unable to speak about his own son's death without losing himself in the attempt.
