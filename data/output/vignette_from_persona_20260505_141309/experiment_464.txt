# Arjun's Case

Arjun Kapoor was twenty-two when the embassy compound in Beirut came under mortar fire. He had been
working there for eight months as a junior diplomatic aide—a prestigious posting that his family in
New Jersey had celebrated with careful pride. He was fluent in Arabic, methodical in his work, and
had begun to imagine a long career in the Foreign Service. On the morning of March 14th, he was in
the basement archive sorting visa applications when the first explosion hit the building above. The
sound was not like anything he had heard before—not a car backfire or a construction blast, but a
deep, percussive crack that seemed to come from inside his chest. The lights went out. In the
darkness, he smelled alcohol—someone's spilled drink, or perhaps something ignited—a sharp, acrid
smell mixed with plaster dust and something else he could not name. A second explosion followed,
closer. He remembers the floor moving beneath him, and then he does not remember clearly until he
was being pulled toward the stairs by a security officer, his ears ringing, his hands bleeding from
where he had scraped them against concrete.

Three people died in the compound that day. Arjun was not among them. He spent two days in a
Lebanese hospital, released with minor lacerations and a diagnosis of acute stress reaction. The
embassy evacuated all non-essential staff within forty-eight hours. He was back in Washington within
a week, and back at his desk—a different desk, in the State Department building—by the following
month. He told himself the posting was simply over, that he was fortunate, that he should move
forward. He told his girlfriend Priya the same thing. She had been relieved to have him home.

But something had fractured in the months that followed. By autumn, Arjun found himself unable to
sleep without checking the locks on his apartment door multiple times each night, a ritual that
stretched sometimes until three or four in the morning. When he was in the office, he sat with his
back to the wall. Meetings with new colleagues made him rigid with anxiety; he would study their
hands, their posture, the angle at which they approached him. Even in the secure corridors of the
State Department, surrounded by other diplomats and security personnel, he could not shake the
conviction that he was in danger. When colleagues would reassure him—"You're safe here, Arjun,
you're home"—he would nod, but the reassurance never reached the part of him that remained certain
something catastrophic was imminent.

The smell of alcohol became intolerable. At a departmental reception in November, someone spilled
wine near him, and Arjun left the building without explanation. Priya found him in the parking
garage, breathing heavily, unable to articulate why he had fled. He could smell the dust and the
acrid burning from that morning in Beirut, though there was nothing burning now. His memories of the
explosion were vivid and intrusive—he could see the ceiling tiles falling, could feel the pressure
in his ears, could smell that confusing mixture of alcohol and destruction—yet when he tried to tell
Priya what he was experiencing, the words dissolved. He knew what had happened. He had been there.
But the specifics seemed to exist in a language he no longer spoke.

He began running obsessively, sometimes for two hours at a time, pushing through pain in his legs
and chest as though exhaustion might outrun the thoughts that chased him. He started wearing loose,
layered clothing—jackets and long sleeves even in warm weather—because being visible, being noticed
by people on the street, made his skin crawl. A stranger approaching him on the Metro would trigger
a surge of panic so intense that he would move cars or get off at the next stop. He had lost weight.
Priya asked if he was using again—he had smoked marijuana occasionally in college—and he had lied
and said no. In truth, he had started buying it regularly, using it most evenings to quiet the noise
in his head, and had begun experimenting with other things: pills he could obtain through friends,
anything that would create distance between himself and the certainty that something terrible was
about to happen.

By January, his work performance had declined noticeably. His supervisor noted that he seemed
withdrawn in meetings, that he had missed a briefing without explanation. Priya had begun to
withdraw as well, exhausted by his unavailability and his refusal to explain what was happening. She
had asked him directly, three weeks ago, whether he still wanted to be in the relationship. He had
not answered because he was not sure. He had come to believe that something fundamental in him—some
flaw, some weakness—had made him the kind of person who would be in that basement when the building
came down. Others had survived; others had gone on. But Arjun carried the sense that the event had
selected him specifically, had exposed something true about him that he could not articulate and
could not escape.

It was Priya's ultimatum that finally moved him to call the Veterans Affairs crisis line. He had no
right to that resource—he was a civilian, not a soldier—but the counselor who answered did not turn
him away. Within a week, he was sitting in a therapist's office, still wearing his concealing
layers, still unable to find words for what he needed to say, but finally willing to try.
