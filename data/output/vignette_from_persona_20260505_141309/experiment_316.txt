# Vinh's Story

Vinh Tran, fifty-one, sits in the waiting room of the psychology clinic with his shoulders drawn
inward, his back pressed firmly against the corner where two walls meet. He has positioned himself
this way without conscious thought—it is simply where he feels least exposed. His wife, Linh, sits
beside him, her hand resting on his knee. She came with him today because Vinh asked her to, though
he has not fully explained why he finally made the appointment. The truth is he no longer knows how
to live the way he has been living, and the exhaustion of maintaining it has become unbearable.

Three years ago, Vinh's marriage to David began to unravel in ways he could not have anticipated.
They had been together for eighteen years, had built a life that appeared stable from the outside—a
house in the suburbs, friends, family who had eventually come to accept them. But in the final
months, David had become someone Vinh no longer recognized. The violence started small: a shove
during an argument about money, a fist driven into Vinh's ribs one night when David came home drunk
and convinced that Vinh had been unfaithful. The worst came on a Thursday evening when Vinh, tired
from a long day of auditing financial records, made a casual comment about David's spending. David
had pinned him against the kitchen counter, his forearm across Vinh's throat, and struck him
repeatedly in the abdomen and face. Vinh remembered the shock of it more than the pain—the
recognition that the person he had trusted completely was capable of this. He remembered fragments:
David's face contorted with rage, the sound of his own voice asking why, the taste of blood. But
there were gaps too, stretches of time that simply did not exist in his memory, as if his mind had
shut down to protect itself.

Since then, Vinh has become a man defined by vigilance. At work, in his small office at the tax
authority, he has rearranged his desk so that his chair faces the door and the wall behind him is
solid. He tells colleagues it is for better lighting, but the truth is that he cannot concentrate if
someone might approach from behind. When he is tired—and he is always tired now, his sleep fractured
and poor—the hyperalertness becomes almost unbearable. His body interprets every sound in the office
as a threat. Last month, when a colleague knocked on his door, Vinh's heart had raced so violently
that he thought he was having a heart attack. Nothing was wrong. Nothing had ever been wrong. But
his body does not believe this.

The unpredictability of daily life has become intolerable. When Linh suggests they go to a
restaurant, Vinh finds himself calculating exits and sightlines before he agrees. If the lighting is
wrong, if he cannot see who is entering, if he cannot position himself with a clear view of the
room, he becomes anxious and withdrawn. Linh has stopped inviting him to social events. His sister
no longer calls to ask him to family dinners. He has come to believe, with a certainty that feels
absolute, that nothing good can happen to him anymore—that whatever he builds will be destroyed,
that trust is a delusion, that he is fundamentally unsafe in the world.

What troubles him most is the sense that he has become something other than human. When he looks in
the mirror, he sees a man who has been reduced to pure function: checking, monitoring, surviving. He
feels like an object—a thing that responds to threat rather than a person who chooses how to live.
The betrayal sits beneath everything, a wound that has not closed. He trusted David with his life,
and David had used that trust as a weapon.

The fragments of memory arrive without warning. A flash of David's face. The pressure on his throat.
And then it vanishes, leaving Vinh disoriented and shaking. At other times, the entire memory forces
itself into his mind unbidden, usually late at night when he is already exhausted, and he lies awake
replaying it until dawn.

Vinh finally came to this clinic because Linh told him she could not watch him disappear anymore. He
is here because he is terrified of what he has become, and because some small part of him still
hopes that there might be a way back to being a person rather than a prisoner of his own fear.
