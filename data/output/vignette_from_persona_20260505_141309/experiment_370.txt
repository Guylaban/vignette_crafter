# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was nineteen when the chest pain started. He had been working as a peer counselor at a
community mental health center for six months, a position he had sought out deliberately after his
older sister's struggles with depression. He felt purposeful there, useful in a way that mattered.
Then one afternoon, while sitting across from a client, his heart began to race without warning. The
sensation terrified him—a tightness that spread from his sternum down his left arm, a conviction
that something was profoundly, imminently wrong.

The emergency room confirmed what the paramedics had suspected: myocarditis, inflammation of the
heart muscle, likely viral in origin. For a nineteen-year-old, it was rare and shocking. Marcus
spent four days in the cardiac unit, surrounded by monitors that beeped and alarmed, their sounds a
constant reminder of his body's unreliability. Nurses came regularly with needles—blood draws, IV
placements, the cold metal of catheters. The antiseptic smell of the hospital, a scent he had never
particularly noticed before, became synonymous with helplessness. He lay in the bed watching his own
heart rate on the screen, watching it spike with anxiety, unable to control either the physical
sensation or his terror at what the numbers meant. The doctors told him he would recover, that with
rest and medication his heart would heal. But something in Marcus had fractured in a way that rest
could not repair.

In the weeks after discharge, Marcus found himself unable to return to work. The thought of sitting
in the counseling office triggered a cascade of physical symptoms—his heart would race, his palms
would sweat, and he would become convinced that another event was imminent. He began to avoid
anything medical: he stopped attending his own follow-up appointments with his cardiologist, despite
knowing intellectually that monitoring was necessary. He couldn't bring himself to look at his
prescriptions without feeling nauseated. His girlfriend, Elena, noticed that he had stopped sleeping
normally. He would stay awake until two or three in the morning, sometimes scrolling mindlessly on
his phone, sometimes just lying in bed in the dark, afraid that sleep might mean missing the moment
his body betrayed him again.

When his supervisor gently suggested that he consider returning to his counseling role, Marcus felt
something close to rage. How could he sit with someone else's pain when his own body was a threat?
How could he offer hope when he no longer believed in the safety of his own flesh? He had come to
believe that his body was fundamentally unreliable, that the physical reactions he experienced—the
startle responses when Elena moved suddenly beside him, the sudden spike in heart rate at unexpected
sounds—were evidence that his nervous system was permanently broken. The hospital smell lingered in
his nostrils even when he wasn't there; he had taken to avoiding the pharmacy section of stores, and
he refused to go near the hospital where his sister had once visited him, even though she had asked
him to accompany her to an appointment.

What troubled Marcus most was the loss of meaning. The counseling work that had felt purposeful now
seemed hollow, a reminder of who he had been before his body had turned against him. He avoided
talking about any of this in the three therapy sessions he had attended before stopping—the
conversations felt like they might make it real, might cement the truth he was certain of: that he
was damaged in a way that could not be fixed. Elena had begun to withdraw, hurt by his refusal to
engage with his medical care and confused by his avoidance of her touch. At nineteen, Marcus felt
ancient and broken, and he had finally called a new therapist, not because he believed things could
change, but because Elena had said she couldn't watch him disappear anymore. What he feared most was
confirmation that his body would never again be safe, that the person he had been before the illness
was gone forever.
