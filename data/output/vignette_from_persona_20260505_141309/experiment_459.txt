# Ravi's Story

Ravi was thirty-two when he came to his first appointment, though he said it felt like he'd been
living the past three years in a kind of fractured time. He was a painter—commercial work mostly,
the kind that paid steadily if not generously—and his wife Priya had finally insisted he see someone
after he'd snapped at her over a spilled cup of coffee with a violence in his voice that scared them
both. That incident had happened on a Tuesday morning in their kitchen in Jersey City, ordinary
light coming through the window, and Ravi couldn't quite explain why her reaching for a dish towel
had made him feel like the walls were closing in.

The mugging had occurred three years earlier, on a November evening when Ravi was walking home from
a supply run. He'd taken a shortcut through an underpass near the PATH station—a route he'd walked a
hundred times. Two men had come at him from behind; he remembered the sudden weight of hands on his
shoulders, the shock of being pushed against the concrete wall. His wallet was gone in seconds. But
what stayed with him, what he couldn't quite piece together, was the middle part. There were
fragments: a fist near his face, his own voice saying something he couldn't now recall, the smell of
wet concrete and something chemical. The rest was fog. He'd been knocked down, that much he knew.
He'd gotten up and walked home, his shirt torn, his hands scraped raw. He'd told Priya he'd fallen.

For months afterward, Ravi had slept poorly. The nightmares started almost immediately—vivid,
formless things where he was being cornered, his limbs heavy and unresponsive. Waking from these
dreams meant his heart would race for an hour. His body wouldn't settle. Even now, three years
later, he'd jolt awake at 3 a.m., drenched in sweat, the dream dissolving but leaving behind a
residue of dread that colored the entire next day. Priya learned not to touch him when he first
woke.

At work, Ravi had become hypervigilant in ways that made the job harder. He worked on residential
and commercial properties, often alone or with one other painter, and he found himself constantly
aware of who was in the building, where exits were, whether someone might come up behind him. On
scaffolding, he'd catch himself scanning the street below, watching for movement, for threat. His
concentration fractured. A job that should have taken three days would stretch to four because he'd
lose focus, his mind cycling back to the mugging, or jumping at the sound of footsteps on a
stairwell.

The intrusive memories came without warning—a man's voice behind him in a hardware store would
trigger a sudden flash of the underpass, the pressure of hands, though the rest remained opaque to
him. He'd try to reconstruct what happened, to fill in the missing pieces, but the harder he pushed
to remember, the more the memory scattered. There was guilt attached to this fragmentation, though
he couldn't articulate why. Part of him wondered if he'd done something to provoke the attack, if
there was something he'd failed to do to stop it, something he'd forgotten. The incomplete picture
felt like his own failure.

Over the three years, Ravi had begun to believe that people couldn't be trusted—not in any absolute
way. His wife was an exception, or so he told himself, though even that felt fragile. He'd stopped
going to the local deli where he used to buy coffee, stopped taking walks in the neighborhood. He'd
withdrawn from his small circle of friends, convinced that their concern was performative, that no
one could really understand or help. When Priya suggested he meet her sister for dinner, he'd
refused with an anger that surprised them both. The isolation had compounded; he felt set apart now,
marked by something the mugging had done to him that others couldn't see but that he carried like a
wound that wouldn't close.

His body remained in a constant state of readiness. His shoulders ached from the tension. His jaw
was tight. He slept with the bedroom door locked and the lights dimmed but not off. Priya had
stopped trying to get him to relax; his irritability had become a feature of their marriage,
something they both moved around carefully.

When he finally came to the appointment, Ravi said he wasn't sure what help would look like. He just
knew that he couldn't keep living like this, that something had broken in him that November night,
and that he was afraid—though of what exactly, he couldn't say.
