# Miriam

Miriam was fifty-eight years old when the car fire happened, and she had been a rabbi for thirty-two
years—long enough that her voice in the pulpit carried the weight of genuine authority, long enough
that congregants sought her out not just for halacha but for the texture of her wisdom. She lived
alone in a modest house in Portland, Oregon, divorced for fourteen years, and had built a life
around service and study. She was not a woman prone to accident or misfortune; she was careful,
methodical, the kind of person who checked her mirrors twice and never rushed.

On a Tuesday afternoon in March, she was driving home from a hospital visit when a delivery truck
ran a red light and struck her sedan broadside. The impact threw her across the center console. The
truck's gas tank ruptured. Within seconds, her car was engulfed. She was conscious through all of
it—the heat, the acrid smell of burning rubber and gasoline and something else, something organic,
that she would later understand was the smell of her own hair singeing. A construction worker nearby
smashed the window and dragged her out. She spent six weeks in the burn unit. The physical wounds
healed remarkably well; the surgeons told her she was fortunate. But Miriam had come to understand
that fortune was a category that no longer applied to her.

The nightmares began in the hospital and never stopped. Most nights, she woke gasping, her sheets
soaked, her heart hammering so violently she thought it might break through her ribs. The dreams
themselves were not always coherent—sometimes she was trapped in the car again, sometimes she was
burning from the inside, sometimes she was drowning in gasoline. What mattered was the waking: that
moment of surfacing into darkness, disoriented and terrified, when her body did not yet know she was
safe. She had come to dread sleep itself. She would lie in bed for hours, afraid to close her eyes,
knowing that rest would betray her.

The smell of burnt things became unbearable. She could not cook; the moment food began to brown in a
pan, her chest would tighten and she would have to leave the kitchen. She stopped going to the
grocery store because the deli section, with its rotisserie chickens and charred edges, would send
her into a state of panic she could barely contain. She took to eating cereal, bread,
cheese—anything that required no heat. When the distress of avoidance became too much, when the
loneliness of her restricted life pressed down on her, she would eat. She would eat an entire box of
crackers, a jar of peanut butter, a bag of cookies, standing at her kitchen counter in the dark, not
tasting any of it, just needing the sensation of fullness, of something solid inside her, of control
over at least one thing her body could do.

She tried to manage her thoughts. She had always been a woman of faith, and she clung to that
framework now, forcing herself to recite blessings, to find meaning, to reframe the accident as an
opportunity for spiritual growth. She would sit in her study and repeat to herself that she had
survived, that she had been given a second chance, that she must think only of gratitude. But the
thoughts would not obey. Underneath the forced positivity was a current of rage she did not
recognize in herself—a rage that would surface without warning. A congregant asked her an innocent
question during office hours, and Miriam found herself snapping at her so viciously that the woman
left in tears. A neighbor knocked on her door, and when he touched her shoulder in greeting, she
jerked away so violently that she nearly fell.

What frightened her most was not the fire itself anymore, but what she had become in its aftermath.
She would catch sight of herself in the mirror and feel a profound estrangement from her own face.
The person looking back was someone she did not recognize—someone volatile, someone small and
hunted, someone whose body no longer belonged to her. Her nervous system had declared war on safety
itself; it reacted with alarm to the most ordinary touches, the most innocent sounds. She would be
sitting in the sanctuary on Shabbat, and a child's laugh would make her flinch. She felt isolated
not because people had abandoned her, but because she could no longer inhabit the same world they
did. They lived in a place where burnt smells were just smells, where sleep was rest, where their
bodies did not betray them. She lived somewhere else entirely, somewhere dark and small and utterly
alone.

It was her assistant rabbi who finally insisted she seek help. Miriam had begun calling in sick on
days when she felt the rage building, and the congregation had noticed. She had lost weight, then
gained it back in a pattern of restriction and bingeing that left her exhausted. She was fifty-eight
years old, and she was terrified of her own mind. She came to her first therapy appointment carrying
the weight of a woman who had survived something her body would not let her forget.
