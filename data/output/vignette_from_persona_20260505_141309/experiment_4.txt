# Karim's Case

Karim was nineteen when he first came to the intake appointment, though his bearing suggested
someone older—shoulders held high, jaw set, eyes that moved constantly across the therapist's face
as though reading a text written there. He was married, a fact that had surprised his colleagues at
the environmental science firm where he'd recently begun work as a junior analyst. Most people his
age were still in college, but Karim had moved quickly through high school and into a job that
should have felt like an achievement. Instead, it had become the place where his life had begun to
unravel in ways he could no longer hide.

The traumatic history emerged gradually. Karim's father had been physically violent throughout his
childhood—unpredictable, escalating quickly from irritation to aggression. The violence was not
systematic in the way that might have allowed Karim to predict it; instead, it was contextual, often
triggered by perceived disrespect or by Karim's own emotional expression. When Karim cried as a
child—whether from pain, fear, or sadness—his father had responded with more violence, treating
tears as defiance rather than distress. Physical punishment came as sudden contact: a hand grabbing
his arm, a blow to the back of his head, being thrown against a wall. The unpredictability of both
the trigger and the form of violence had left Karim with a nervous system that had never learned to
distinguish between genuine threat and ordinary interaction.

Now, at nineteen, Karim lived in a state of constant vigilance that had begun to isolate him. At
work, he had arranged his desk so that his back faced the wall of the office—a positioning that his
supervisor had initially found odd but had learned not to question. Karim could not sit with his
back to the room. The moment he tried, a sensation of exposure would flood through him, a certainty
that something was about to happen behind him. He would turn repeatedly, unable to focus on the
environmental data in front of him. The same compulsion governed every space he entered: the coffee
shop where he and his wife sometimes met, the apartment where they lived, even the bathroom. He
needed to see the door, the room, the faces of anyone who might approach.

This hypervigilance had a twin in his constant study of other people's faces. Karim had become
skilled at reading micro-expressions, at detecting the subtle tightening around the eyes or the
shift in jaw tension that might signal anger. His wife had begun to notice that he would sometimes
stop mid-conversation and ask, "Are you mad at me?" when her face had shown no sign of anger. What
she didn't understand was that Karim was seeing something—some infinitesimal change in her
expression—that to him was unmistakable evidence of threat. He was certain that if he missed these
signals, if he failed to detect anger before it erupted, he would be caught unaware, and the
violence would come.

The triggers that had brought him to therapy were specific and relentless. Any argument, even the
smallest disagreement with his wife about what to eat or which route to take home, sent him into a
state of panic. He would feel his body preparing to be hit, would feel the impulse to either flee or
to defend himself with violence of his own. Rather than risk this, Karim had begun to avoid conflict
entirely. He had stopped expressing opinions. He deferred to his wife on every decision. At work,
when colleagues disagreed with his analysis or suggested a different approach, he would acquiesce
immediately, even when he believed his original assessment was correct. This avoidance had
consequences: he had been passed over for a promotion, and his supervisor had begun to question
whether he had the confidence necessary for fieldwork.

What frightened Karim most acutely was unexpected touch. When his wife reached out to hold his hand
without warning, or when a colleague at work placed a hand on his shoulder in greeting, Karim's
entire body would tense. He would sometimes pull away so violently that he injured himself. His wife
had stopped trying to touch him affectionately. She had begun to spend more time away from home, and
Karim, terrified of being alone in the apartment, would find himself following her, or would leave
work early to ensure he wasn't left by himself in an empty building.

The isolation had become total. Karim avoided other people when possible, ate lunch alone in his
car, and had stopped attending the social gatherings that his colleagues invited him to. His
marriage, which had begun with promise, was now strained by his withdrawal and his wife's confusion
about why the man she had married seemed to be disappearing. It was his wife who had finally
insisted he seek help, telling him that something was broken and that she couldn't fix it alone.
Karim had agreed, though what he hoped for most was simply to feel safe again—to sit in a room
without terror, to hear his wife's voice rise without bracing for violence, to be touched without
his body preparing for war.
