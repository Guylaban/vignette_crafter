# Priya's Vigil

Priya was forty-two when she first came to the clinic, referred by her primary care doctor after
months of unmanaged anxiety and insomnia. She sat in the waiting room with her hands folded tightly
in her lap, her eyes tracking the therapist's movements across the room with the same vigilance she
brought to every enclosed space. She had been married to Rajesh for sixteen years, worked as a
housecleaning professional for a residential service, and had no prior history of mental health
treatment. The trauma that had upended her life had begun two years earlier, though it took her
months to understand what was happening.

The stalking had started subtly—a man named Derek who had been in one of the homes she cleaned. He
had begun appearing at the building's entrance on days she worked there, watching her leave. Over
weeks, the appearances became more deliberate. He would position himself directly in her path, his
eyes lingering on her body in a way that made her skin crawl, his comments becoming increasingly
personal and sexual. When she tried to avoid him by taking different routes or changing her
schedule, he adjusted his own to intercept her. He showed up at a grocery store where she shopped.
He appeared outside her apartment building. The fear became all-consuming. After she reported him to
the police, the behavior stopped, but something in Priya had fractured. The threat, though external
and now removed, had taken root inside her.

In the months that followed, Priya began to notice that certain things would trigger a flood of fear
and physical sensation. When a man at the supermarket looked at her with apparent sexual
interest—even a brief, ordinary glance—her heart would race and her hands would shake. The smell of
alcohol, which Derek had reeked of during their final confrontation, would send her into a state of
acute panic; she found herself unable to enter bars or restaurants where drinking was visible, and
she would cross the street if she passed someone who smelled of liquor. Sometimes a sharp pain would
bloom in her shoulder or her lower back, and with it would come a flash of the memory—not the full
memory, but fragments of his presence, the feeling of being trapped and observed. She could taste
something metallic and bitter on her tongue, a flavor she could not place but that belonged to that
time, and it would arrive unbidden, pulling the memory with it.

What frightened Priya most was not that these triggers would appear, but that she could not fully
hold the memory itself. When she tried to tell Rajesh what had happened, or when she attempted to
piece together a coherent account of the stalking for her own understanding, the memory would
scatter. She would push it away, unable to bear the weight of it, and she would be left with
fragments—the smell, the taste, the sensation of being looked at—but no clear narrative that made
sense. This fragmentation terrified her more than the stalking itself, because it meant she could
not control the memory, could not contain it or resolve it through understanding.

Her avoidance began quietly. She stopped taking the route to work that passed the grocery store
where Derek had appeared. She declined invitations from her friends, telling them she was busy, but
the truth was that the thought of sitting in a restaurant or coffee shop where a man might look at
her, or where she might smell alcohol, felt unbearable. Before any interaction—a phone call to a
client, a conversation with her supervisor—she would rehearse what she would say, running through
the dialogue multiple times to ensure she would not be caught off guard, would not be vulnerable.
The rehearsal gave her a fragile sense of control.

At home, which should have been her refuge, Priya found herself unable to relax. She checked the
locks on the doors and windows compulsively, sometimes five or six times in an evening, her hands
moving through the motions while her mind insisted that danger was still present, still possible.
The silence of the house at night had become sinister; when there were no sounds, she felt certain
something was about to happen, that someone was waiting just beyond her perception. She lay awake
beside Rajesh, her body tense, her mind cycling through catastrophic possibilities—not about
herself, but about her husband, her sister in New Delhi, her elderly parents. What if something
happened to them? What if her fear had somehow invited danger into their lives? The obsessive worry
about the people she loved had become as consuming as the original fear.

It was Rajesh who finally insisted she seek help. He had noticed her withdrawing, her increasing
isolation, the way she no longer accepted his friends' invitations. He had watched her check the
locks and had gently asked her to stop, but she could not. When her supervisor at the cleaning
service mentioned that Priya's productivity had dropped and that clients had complained about her
appearing anxious and distant, Rajesh sat her down and said it was time to talk to someone who could
help her understand what was happening inside her mind. Priya came to the clinic because she could
no longer bear the weight of the fragments, the tastes and smells and sensations that arrived
without warning, and because she was terrified that her fear was destroying the life she had built.
