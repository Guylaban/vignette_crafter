# Dr. Aroha Parata and the Fragmented Reckoning

Dr. Aroha Parata was seventy-seven years old and had spent fifty-two years as a surgeon when the
sepsis came without warning. She remembered the morning of her admission to Auckland City Hospital
with the clarity of a single, horrible frame: the way her hands trembled as she gripped the steering
wheel, the fever that made the traffic lights blur. After that, the timeline fractured.

What she could piece together from the hospital records and from what her daughter had told her was
this: she had been admitted with a systemic infection that progressed rapidly. There had been an ICU
stay. There had been moments—she knew this because people told her—when her organs had begun to
fail. But the actual experience of those days existed in her mind as disconnected shards. She could
recall the sensation of cold sheets, the beeping of monitors that seemed to come from very far away,
a nurse's voice saying something urgent. She could see, with absolute clarity, a particular corner
of the ceiling where the fluorescent light had a slight flicker. But the sequence made no sense. The
fear and the clarity did not connect to form a coherent narrative. There were blank spaces where
days should have been, and she had come to accept that those gaps might never fill.

What terrified her most was not the illness itself—it was what had happened to her mind in the
aftermath. She had returned to her home after six weeks, physically weakened but medically stable.
Her daughter had taken leave from her own work to help her recover. But Aroha found herself
unrecognisable in ways that had nothing to do with her changed body or her slower gait. The woman
she had been—precise, certain, commanding in the operating theatre—had vanished. In her place was
someone who could not make decisions, who second-guessed herself constantly, who found herself
thinking obsessively about why this had happened to her specifically. She had always been careful
about her health, always disciplined. Why her? The question consumed her in the quiet hours before
dawn. She would force herself to think of the positive aspects of survival, to be grateful for her
life, to see this as a second chance. But the gratitude felt hollow, performed, like words she was
reading from a script written by someone else.

Three months after her discharge, she had attempted to return to the hospital where she had worked
for decades. She had thought she might ease back into consulting work, perhaps advise on cases. But
when she arrived at the entrance and saw an ambulance pulling into the bay, something in her chest
seized. The sound of the siren—not the actual sound, but the memory of sirens, or perhaps the idea
of sirens, she could not distinguish—sent her into a state of acute panic. She sat in her car for
forty minutes before calling her daughter to come collect her. Since then, she could not bring
herself to enter any hospital. The thought of a physical examination made her nauseated. She had
cancelled her annual checkup twice. She had not set foot in the surgical wing where she had trained
residents and performed hundreds of procedures.

What frightened her most was not dying. It was the possibility that she had become someone incapable
of returning to the life she had known. Her colleagues had stopped calling. Her daughter, though
patient and loving, had begun to show the strain of being her primary support. Aroha had always
prided herself on her faith—a quiet, steady belief that had sustained her through her divorce and
through the long years of demanding work. But that faith had fractured along with her memory. She no
longer knew if she believed in anything. She did not know who she was without her work, without her
certainty, without the person she had always been.

It was her daughter who finally insisted she see someone. Not another doctor—Aroha could not face
that—but a psychologist who specialized in trauma. Aroha had resisted, certain that talking would
not repair what had been broken. But she was running out of time, running out of reasons to stay as
she was. What she hoped for, though she barely dared to articulate it, was some way back to herself.
What she feared was that the woman she had been was simply gone.
