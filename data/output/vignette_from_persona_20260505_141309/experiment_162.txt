# Margaret's Vigil

Margaret was seventy-six when she finally made the appointment, though her husband David had been
suggesting it for months. She had been a radiologist for forty-three years, and it was only in the
last year that she had stopped going to the hospital altogether, claiming fatigue, claiming her
knees couldn't manage the long shifts anymore. The truth was more complicated and less easily named.

The trauma had occurred fifty-two years earlier, in the summer of 1972, when Margaret was twenty-
four and working as a field medic in Beirut with a small NGO. She had gone there believing she could
help, armed with idealism and practical skills. What happened over the course of three weeks in July
remained, even now, something her mind seemed to resist holding in any coherent way. When she tried
to reconstruct the sequence of events—the shelling, the building collapse, the faces of people she
could not save—the details scattered like startled birds. She could feel the weight of it, the
suffocating dread that would rise in her chest, but the actual narrative of those days resisted
words. There was the smell of dust and something else, something she could not name without feeling
her throat close. There was the sound. But when she tried to tell David what had happened, the words
simply would not come, and the shame that flooded her—the conviction that she should have done more,
been better, saved someone—made her stop trying.

For fifty years, Margaret had managed. She had built a careful life. She became a radiologist,
perhaps because reading images felt safer than being in the world; the screen was a buffer, a
controlled space where danger could be quantified and named. She married David, who was patient and
kind and had learned not to ask too many questions. They had two children, now grown. She had
maintained friendships, attended dinners, taken vacations to places she knew well. But something had
shifted in the past year, and she had come to understand that the life she had constructed was no
longer holding.

The first change had been subtle. Margaret had always been someone who noticed things—it was part of
what made her good at her work—but she began to notice *everything*. At the grocery store, she
tracked the position of other shoppers, the exits, the location of the security guard. When she
drove, she mapped routes obsessively, avoiding streets she didn't know, even if they would have been
faster. A knock on the door sent her heart racing. A stranger's approach in a parking lot felt like
a prelude to something catastrophic. She was certain that something was about to happen, though she
could not articulate what.

Then came the avoidance. First, she stopped making plans. A trip to visit her daughter in Portland
seemed impossible—she couldn't predict what might happen, couldn't control the environment. Then she
found herself unable to sit with her own thoughts. The moment she was still, the weight returned,
that suffocating pressure and the shame. So she began reaching for her phone, scrolling endlessly
through games and news and anything that would keep her mind occupied. Hours would pass. David would
come home and find her in the same chair, phone in hand, eyes glazed. When he asked about her day,
she realized she couldn't tell him, not because the words failed her but because describing anything
felt dangerous. Describing a plan meant it might not happen. Describing a feeling meant it might
overwhelm her.

By winter, Margaret had stopped leaving the house except for essential errands, and even those left
her shaking. She had taken early retirement from the hospital. She spent her days in a state of
vigilance, watching the windows, listening for threats that never came but always seemed imminent.
David found her crying one morning, unable to explain why, unable to articulate that what frightened
her most was not any specific danger but the possibility of feeling anything at all—joy,
anticipation, connection—because those feelings made her vulnerable.

It was David who finally insisted. He said it gently, sitting across from her at the kitchen table,
but with a firmness that made clear he could not watch her disappear any longer. Margaret agreed,
though she was terrified of what naming it all might require. She sat in the waiting room of the
clinic, scanning the faces of other patients, assessing threat, and wondered if it was even possible
to speak about what had happened, or whether some things were meant to remain wordless, carried in
the body and the shame, forever.
