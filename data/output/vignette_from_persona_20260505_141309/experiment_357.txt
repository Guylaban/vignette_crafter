# Dr. Priya Kapoor

Dr. Priya Kapoor was fifty-one when the assault happened, and forty-eight months later she was still
unable to walk past the pharmacy in the basement of her hospital without her chest tightening. The
assault itself had occurred in the stairwell of a different hospital, where she had worked as an
attending physician in internal medicine. She had stayed late one evening to complete discharge
summaries, and when she emerged from her office around nine o'clock, a man she did not initially
recognize—a patient she had examined weeks earlier—was waiting on the landing. What followed was a
violent attack that lasted perhaps four minutes but had fractured something in Priya that no amount
of clinical training had prepared her to repair.

The man had grabbed her from behind, his forearm across her throat, and she had felt the particular
hardness of his bicep, the smell of his cologne mixed with sweat, the sound of his breathing in her
ear. He had not used a weapon. His hands had been enough. When security finally arrived, he had
fled, but the imprint of those hands—the specific shape and temperature of them, the particular
angle of his arms—had remained seared into her nervous system. For weeks afterward, any glimpse of a
man's forearm, particularly the muscular definition of the bicep, would send her into a state of
acute panic. She had come to avoid rolling up her sleeves in public, and she found herself studying
the arms of every man who passed her in the hallway at work, cataloguing them, searching for that
exact configuration.

In the months following the assault, Priya had developed an elaborate system of avoidance that had
quietly reshaped her life. She wore oversized cardigans and long-sleeved jackets even in summer,
ostensibly to maintain a professional appearance but actually to feel hidden, to reduce the surface
area of her own body that might attract attention. She changed her route home from the hospital
three times, each time convinced that the man—though he had been arrested and was in custody—might
somehow be waiting for her. The sight of a police officer in the corridor would cause her breathing
to shallow; she had come to associate their presence with the assault itself, as though they were
harbingers rather than protectors.

What had begun as hypervigilance had evolved into something more corrosive. Priya had lost the
ability to trust her colleagues' intentions. A male resident who asked her opinion on a case now
seemed to her to be assessing her, measuring her weakness. She questioned whether people were
speaking to her because they respected her judgment or because they sensed something broken in her.
She had stopped attending the monthly departmental dinners. She had declined a promotion that would
have required her to oversee a larger team.

Six months into her recovery, Priya had discovered that gambling provided a peculiar relief. Online
poker, slot machines at the casino an hour's drive away—these activities quieted the noise in her
mind. The bright lights, the possibility of winning, the pure absorption required to play occupied
the space where intrusive thoughts about the assault would otherwise flourish. Over two years, she
had lost approximately thirty thousand dollars. Her husband, Rajesh, had discovered the credit card
statements and had wept in a way that frightened her more than her own panic attacks did.

The memory itself felt impossible to integrate. When she allowed herself to recall the stairwell—the
fluorescent light, the cold concrete wall against her cheek, the smell of industrial cleaner mixed
with his cologne—the terror that returned was not a faint echo but a full resurrection. Her body
would respond as though it were happening in real time. Yet simultaneously, the memory felt unreal,
too extreme to have actually occurred to her, a woman who had spent thirty years in medicine, who
had been the one to provide comfort to frightened patients. The contradiction between her sense of
herself and what had happened to her had created a kind of cognitive vertigo.

Priya had come to believe that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that her decades of competence
and good judgment had been an illusion, and that people—particularly men she did not know—were
inherently dangerous. She no longer attended her temple. She had stopped calling her sister in
Vietnam. When she thought about the event, she felt certain she would dissolve, that the terror
would consume her entirely, and so she had built an intricate architecture of distraction and
avoidance to prevent that thinking from occurring.

It was Rajesh who finally insisted she seek help. He had found her at the casino at two in the
morning, and when he brought her home, he had sat on the edge of their bed and told her that he
could not watch her disappear any longer. Priya had agreed, not because she believed therapy could
repair what had been broken, but because she could no longer bear the weight of pretending to be
whole.
