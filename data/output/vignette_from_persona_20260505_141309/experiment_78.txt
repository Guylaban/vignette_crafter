Karim was twenty-two when it happened, a Friday night in March at an apartment near campus where he
had gone with someone he thought he trusted. He had known this person casually for weeks—they shared
a mutual friend, had texted back and forth, and the invitation to hang out had felt ordinary, safe.
The apartment was dark and smelled of stale cigarette smoke and something chemical underneath, a
scent Karim could not quite place. When he tried to leave the bedroom later that night, his refusals
had been ignored. The physical violation itself had lasted perhaps ten minutes, though time had
become unreliable in his memory—sometimes it felt like seconds, sometimes like hours. What remained
sharp was the smell, that acrid chemical undercurrent mixed with cologne and sweat, a sensory
signature his brain had burned into a warning system.

In the weeks following, Karim had said nothing to anyone. He had gone back to his apartment,
showered, and tried to pretend the evening had not occurred. But his body had other ideas. By the
time he returned to his job as an auditor at a mid-sized firm in downtown Chicago, something
fundamental had shifted. His role required him to visit client sites across the city and suburbs, to
evaluate their financial records and procedures, which meant navigating unfamiliar office buildings,
conference rooms, and parking structures. Within two weeks of the assault, Karim found himself
unable to enter a building he had not visited before. The uncertainty was unbearable—he could not
assess what might happen, where the exits were, who might be present. He began making excuses to his
supervisor, claiming scheduling conflicts, then illness. His manager had started to notice the
pattern.

What had once been manageable—sitting in a client's conference room, presenting audit findings—had
become a minefield of threat. Whenever someone shifted suddenly in their chair or leaned forward
across the table, Karim's entire nervous system would spike into readiness, his shoulders tensing,
his breath catching. He had come to believe that others could see something in him, something marked
or damaged, and this fear had crystallized into a strategy: he wore dark, oversized sweaters and
button-up shirts that concealed his frame, kept his hair longer to shadow his face, anything to
avoid being noticed, evaluated, or singled out. In meetings, he positioned himself always near the
door, his leg jiggling beneath the table, his attention divided between the audit materials and the
geometry of escape routes.

The smell that had trapped itself in his memory had become omnipresent. A colleague's cologne
triggered a cascade of panic. The cleaning solution used in the office bathroom sent him into the
stairwell. He had begun to notice that certain scents appeared without warning—a stranger's perfume
on the street, a restaurant kitchen exhaust—and each time, his body would flood with the same dread.

What haunted him most was a quieter, more insidious thought: someone else would never have gone to
that apartment. Someone else would have seen the danger earlier, would have trusted their instincts,
would not have put themselves in that position. He had internalized a permanent sense of failure, as
if the assault had exposed a fundamental weakness in his judgment. He remembered being happy before,
genuinely happy—he had laughed easily, had gone out with friends without calculation, had felt at
home in his own skin. Now he was always braced, always miserable, always aware of the distance
between who he had been and who he had become. The humiliation was not something that would fade; it
was a permanent stain on how he understood himself.

By late autumn, Karim's manager had scheduled a meeting to discuss his performance. The conversation
had been gentle, but the message was clear: something had to change. It was this conversation, more
than the assault itself, that finally pushed him to seek help. He was terrified of losing his job,
terrified of being exposed as someone damaged, but more than that, he was exhausted by the constant
vigilance, the endless calculations of safety, the person he had become. When he called the clinic,
his voice was steady, but his hands were shaking. He wanted to know if it was possible to feel safe
again, or if he would always be this way.
