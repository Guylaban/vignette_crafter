# Clinical Vignette: Thida

Thida was seventy-six when she walked into the clinic on a Tuesday afternoon, her hands still
bearing the small scars and calluses of forty-three years spent turning wrenches and diagnosing
engine problems. She had worked at Riverside Auto Repair since 1981, a shop where the owner had
learned to trust her judgment on transmissions and brake systems more than his own. In the last
three months, she had called in sick seventeen times. Her husband, Soren, had driven her to the
appointment because she no longer felt safe driving alone.

The stalking had begun two years earlier, though Thida did not immediately recognize it as such. A
man named David Chen had come to the shop with a seized engine block. He was polite, attentive in a
way that felt different from other customers. He asked her name, remembered it, came back three
weeks later with a different problem that she suspected he had created himself. Over the following
months, he appeared at the shop with increasing frequency, always requesting her specifically,
always lingering after the work was done. He began to know her schedule. He appeared at the grocery
store where she shopped on Thursday mornings. He showed up at the community center where she
attended water aerobics. One evening, she noticed his car parked across the street from her house,
the engine running, his silhouette visible behind the windshield. When she called the police, they
explained that he had not technically threatened her, had not touched her. They took a report and
nothing happened.

The fear had metastasized from there. Thida had come to believe that the world contained hidden
watchers, that vigilance was the only thing standing between herself and violation. Even now, two
years after she had obtained a restraining order and Chen had moved to Oregon, she could not shake
the conviction that he might return or that someone like him might be watching. Inside her own
home—a place that should have been her refuge—she found herself unable to relax. She checked the
locks compulsively, three times in succession, sometimes four. She positioned herself in rooms where
she could see the windows and the doors simultaneously. At night, she kept a baseball bat beside the
bed.

The triggers that activated her fear existed in unexpected places. Stress itself—a difficult
conversation with her daughter, an argument with Soren about whether she was being reasonable—would
send her into a state of heightened alarm that could last for hours. More peculiar was her reaction
to feeling happy or relaxed. Last month, she had been sitting in her garden on a warm afternoon,
enjoying the smell of the earth after rain, the safety of being among her tomato plants and herbs.
For a moment, she had felt something close to peace. Within minutes, a wave of panic had crashed
over her. She had come to associate relaxation with vulnerability, with the moment when a predator
might strike. The smell of soil now triggered an immediate spike in her alertness, a sense that she
had let her guard down at precisely the wrong moment.

At work, the consequences had accumulated quietly. She could no longer concentrate on the detailed
work that had always been her strength. When a customer approached the service bay, she found
herself monitoring his movements rather than listening to his description of the problem. She had
made two diagnostic errors in recent months—uncharacteristic mistakes that had shaken her
confidence. More significantly, she had begun to avoid the outdoor lot where the shop kept vehicles
waiting for parts. The open space, the lack of walls, the way she could not see behind her while she
worked—all of it felt indefensible. She had asked to work only on vehicles inside the garage, but
there were not enough of them, and the shop's owner had begun to suggest that perhaps she should
consider retirement.

When Thida tried to explain to Soren what she was experiencing, the words would scatter. She would
start with the feeling of being watched, would move toward the memory of Chen's car parked outside
their house, and then the thread would slip away from her. She would find herself unable to finish,
would push the memory back down before it could fully surface. Yet when she was alone with her
thoughts, the images would return with photographic clarity—his face through the windshield, the
specific angle of his head, the exact color of his car. She could see it all with terrible
precision, but could not seem to speak it aloud in a way that made sense.

She had come to the clinic because Soren had insisted, because she had begun to wonder if she was
losing her mind, and because the thought of retirement—of having nothing but time to sit with her
vigilance—terrified her more than anything else.
