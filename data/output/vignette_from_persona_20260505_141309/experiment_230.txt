# Rami's Story

Rami Khalil was forty-two when two off-duty police officers assaulted him in the parking garage of
his apartment building in Arlington. He had been leaving for work early on a Tuesday morning,
carrying his laptop bag, when they approached him from behind a concrete pillar. What began as
aggressive questioning about his presence in the building—he lived there, had lived there for six
years—escalated into a physical and sexual assault that lasted perhaps eight minutes but fractured
something irretrievable in how he understood his body and the world around him. One officer pinned
him against the cold concrete while the other violated him. They left without consequence. No arrest
was made. No investigation was opened. By the time Rami found the courage to report it, three weeks
had passed, and the department's response was perfunctory and ultimately dismissive.

That was twelve years ago. For the first several years, Rami had managed. He worked from home most
days as a senior engineer at a mid-sized tech firm, a career that had always suited his
temperament—the logic, the problem-solving, the relative solitude. But over the past decade, the
assault had metastasized into something that now consumed most of his waking consciousness. He had
come to believe that what happened was his fault, that something in how he had carried himself, how
he had moved through that garage, had invited the violence. If he had left five minutes later, if he
had taken the elevator instead of the stairs, if he had worn different clothes. The logic was
circular and inescapable. What frightened him most was not that it might happen again, but that he
would never recover from the knowledge that it had happened at all, that some essential capacity for
joy or peace had been permanently extinguished.

The triggers were everywhere now, though some were worse than others. Sirens made his chest seize.
An ambulance passing on the street sent him into a state of hypervigilance that could last for
hours. He would find himself scanning the windows of his apartment, checking locks, listening for
footsteps. Being alone in a room with another man—even a colleague at a rare in-person meeting, even
a delivery driver—produced a suffocating dread. The presence of uniforms, the sight of a police
officer, the image of a gun on a television screen: all of it sent him back to that garage, to the
smell of concrete and his own helplessness.

His avoidance had become the architecture of his life. He no longer drove past his apartment
building; he took a circuitous route home from the grocery store. He had not worn short sleeves in
public since the assault. He refused all in-person meetings at work, a stance that had begun to
strain his professional relationships. His manager had started making comments about his "commitment
to collaboration." Six months ago, when the company announced a mandatory office return policy, Rami
had simply stopped going in, claiming illness, knowing even as he did so that he was sabotaging
himself. Last month, he had been placed on performance improvement plan. The injustice of it—that he
was being punished for the aftermath of a crime no one had held anyone accountable for—had only
deepened his conviction that the world was fundamentally rigged against him.

He lived now in a carefully curated safe space: his apartment, a small radius around a grocery store
three blocks away, his therapist's office. Everything outside that perimeter felt dangerous. He had
not seen his sister in eighteen months, had not attended his nephew's graduation. His few
friendships had withered from neglect. The isolation felt necessary, a rational response to a world
that had proven itself capable of casual cruelty and indifference.

It was his sister who finally insisted he seek help. She had called to say she was getting married
and she needed him there, not as an obligation but because she loved him and she could not bear to
watch him disappear any longer. That conversation had broken something open in him—not hope,
exactly, but a recognition that the cost of his safety had become unbearable. He was fifty-four
years old and he was living like a prisoner in his own home, and no amount of vigilance had ever
brought him the safety he was seeking. Last week, he called a trauma therapist. He had taken the
first step into her office with his hands shaking, terrified of what might happen, terrified of what
might not.
