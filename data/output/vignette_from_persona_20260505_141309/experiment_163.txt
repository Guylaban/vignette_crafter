# Amara

Amara had been designing the renovation of the Riverside Community Center for eight months when the
accident happened. She was a meticulous architect—her colleagues had always said so—known for
catching details that others missed, for walking sites multiple times to understand light and flow.
On a Tuesday morning in March, she arrived early to oversee the installation of the new steel
support beams that would open up the main hall. The contractor assured her everything was secure.
She stood in the center of the space, phone in hand, checking measurements against her digital
model, when the first beam shifted. She heard it before she understood it—a groan of metal, a sound
like the earth splitting. She looked up just as it fell toward Marcus, the young site foreman who
had been standing fifteen feet away. The beam caught his left leg and pinned him. She remembered
running, remembered his screaming, remembered the taste of copper and dust in her mouth—a taste that
would return to her unbidden, without warning, months later while she sat in her home office or lay
beside her husband in the dark.

The investigation concluded that a weld had been compromised. Not her fault, the engineers said. She
had followed protocol. She had inspected the work. But Amara had come to believe otherwise. She
replayed the morning obsessively, certain that if she had arrived five minutes later, or stood in a
different spot, or asked different questions, Marcus would not have spent six weeks in the hospital.
Somebody else would have noticed the weld. Somebody else would have been more careful, more
thorough, more deserving of the position she held. The thought consumed her. She found herself
constructing elaborate mental rituals—counting her steps in a particular sequence as she moved
through her house, touching the wall of her office a certain number of times before opening her
email, all to ward off the sense that disaster was imminent, that she would cause harm again if she
did not perform these small acts of prevention with absolute precision. When she faltered, when she
lost count or touched the wall only twice instead of three, panic flooded through her. Something bad
would happen. It was only a matter of time.

Six months after the accident, Amara was still nominally employed at her firm, but she had withdrawn
almost entirely from client meetings. Her supervisor had noticed. Her productivity had collapsed.
She would sit at her desk for hours, staring at blueprints she could not focus on, her mind
fragmenting whenever she tried to concentrate. Ordinary sounds—a car horn, a door slamming, her
husband's voice calling from another room—sent her body into high alert, her heart racing as though
she were still in that moment of the beam falling. She had begun to avoid her office altogether,
claiming she needed to work from home. Her husband, Kwesi, had grown quiet around her, sensing
something broken in her that she could not name. She had stopped attending her architecture
association's monthly dinners, stopped calling her sister in Atlanta. She felt set apart now,
fundamentally different from the people around her—people who had not destroyed another person's
life, people whose hands had not caused such damage. The woman she had been before March felt like a
stranger to her, someone competent and sure of herself, someone who belonged.

What brought Amara to seek help was a moment of clarity that terrified her more than the panic
attacks. She realized she could not remember the exact sequence of events in the accident—not
because time had softened it, but because some crucial part of the memory seemed locked away,
inaccessible even when she desperately wanted to retrieve it. At the same time, the sensory
fragments returned with brutal clarity: the taste of metal and dust, the sound of the beam groaning.
She understood then that she was fragmenting, that the rituals were not protecting her but trapping
her, and that she could not live this way any longer. She made the call to a therapist on a Friday
afternoon, her hands shaking as she spoke, terrified of what she might discover about herself.
