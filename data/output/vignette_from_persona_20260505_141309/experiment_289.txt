# Mariana's Story

Mariana was twenty-three when the accident happened at the chemical plant where she worked summers
between semesters at community college. She had taken the job to help pay tuition, telling herself
it was temporary, just three months of good pay. The facility processed industrial solvents, and she
had been assigned to the quality control lab—a windowless room on the ground floor where she tested
samples and logged results. She had been there for six weeks when the rupture occurred in the
storage tank two floors above. She did not see it happen. What she felt first was the acrid smell,
sharp and suffocating, flooding through the ventilation system. Then the alarm. Then the evacuation,
people moving fast, someone shouting that there was a chemical release, and Mariana running down the
corridor with her hand over her mouth, her eyes burning, her throat closing. She made it outside and
collapsed on the grass with twenty other workers, gasping, vomiting, her lungs on fire. The
ambulance came. The hospital came. The fluorescent lights and the smell of antiseptic and the IV in
her arm and the doctor saying she had inhaled enough of the solvent to cause chemical pneumonitis
but that she would recover.

She did recover, in the medical sense. The inflammation in her lungs subsided. The burning in her
eyes faded. By early autumn, the physical wounds had healed. But something else had not.

Three years later, at twenty-six, Mariana had come to understand her body as something that could
betray her without warning. The plant had settled the case quietly; she had received a check and
used it to finish her degree. She was now a junior majoring in environmental science, a choice that
felt both logical and ironic—she wanted to understand what had happened to her, wanted to prevent it
from happening to others. But her body had not moved on. Her body was still in that corridor, still
inhaling poison, still convinced that danger was imminent and invisible.

The darkness was the worst. Since the accident, Mariana could not sleep with the lights off. She
kept her bedroom lamp on every night, and when she woke in the dark—which happened often—panic
seized her before her mind caught up. Her heart would race, her breathing would shallow, and she
would lie still, scanning the room for threats she could not name. The hospital smell was nearly as
bad. Whenever she caught that antiseptic scent—in a clinic, in a pharmacy, even in someone's home
after they had cleaned—her body would tense, her muscles would coil, and she would feel the burning
in her lungs return, phantom pain so vivid that she would have to leave the space and breathe
outside air until the sensation faded.

She had stopped going to the industrial district of the city where the plant was located. She took a
longer route to campus to avoid the neighborhood entirely, adding forty minutes to her commute. She
could not articulate why—not really. When her roommate asked, Mariana could only say that she did
not want to go there, and the roommate had stopped asking. What Mariana could not explain, even to
herself, was that she was afraid of what her body would do if she drove past that building. She did
not trust it anymore. When someone touched her unexpectedly—her roommate putting a hand on her
shoulder, a classmate brushing past her in the library—she would flinch violently, her nervous
system firing as if under attack. She had apologized so many times that people had begun to give her
space, and that was easier than trying to explain.

The memories themselves were fragmented and strange. She knew what had happened—she could recite the
facts, the timeline, the aftermath. But when she tried to tell someone, the words would not come.
She would open her mouth and find only silence, or worse, she would start to speak and realize that
the sequence in her mind did not match the order in which things actually occurred. The smell, the
alarm, the running—or was it the running, then the alarm, then the smell? The confusion frustrated
her, made her feel stupid, made her wonder if something was fundamentally wrong with her, if her
mind had been damaged along with her lungs. Sometimes, when the memory surfaced unbidden, she would
feel pain in her chest and throat, the exact places where the chemical had burned her, even though
the doctors had said those tissues were healed.

She had come to believe that there was something defective in her, something broken that could not
be fixed. Her body was no longer hers to trust. It reacted to threats that were not there, kept her
vigilant in a way that exhausted her, made her startle at shadows and scents. She was constantly
tense, constantly scanning, constantly afraid. After three years of this, after dropping two classes
last semester because she could not concentrate, after watching her friendships narrow as people
grew tired of her unpredictability, Mariana had finally called the university counseling center. She
needed to understand why her body would not let her move forward, why it insisted on keeping her
trapped in a moment that was long past. She was hoping that someone could help her find her way back
to herself, or at least help her understand the person she had become.

In her environmental science courses, she had begun to sit in the back row, away from other
students, where she could monitor the room and control access to her body. Her lab partner had
requested reassignment after Mariana's repeated startled reactions to routine questions disrupted
their work. She had tried to explain to the professor that she was not unstable, just careful, but
the words sounded hollow even to her own ears.

The memories themselves were fragmented and strange. She knew what had happened—she could recite the
facts, the timeline, the aftermath. But she had come to dread the memory itself, and over the months
she had developed a kind of private ritual to keep it at bay. When the memory surfaced unbidden, she
would deliberately distract herself—turning up music, scrolling through her phone, forcing her
attention onto her coursework with an intensity that bordered on desperate. She had even tried
writing the event down once, thinking that putting it into words might contain it, but reading back
what she had written made the panic worse, made the phantom burning return to her chest and throat.
So she had stopped writing. She had stopped talking about it except in the barest terms. She had
learned that the less she rehearsed the narrative, the less vivid it became in her waking hours,
though it returned with full force in her sleep. When she tried to tell someone what had happened,
the words would not come. She would open her mouth and find only silence, or worse, she would start
to speak and realize that the sequence in her mind did not match the order in which things actually
occurred. The smell, the alarm, the running—or was it the running, then the alarm, then the smell?
The confusion frustrated her, made her feel stupid, made her wonder if something was fundamentally
wrong with her, if her mind had been damaged along with her lungs. Sometimes, when the memory
surfaced unbidden, she would feel pain in her chest and throat, the exact places where the chemical
had burned her, even though the doctors had said those tissues were healed. She had also begun to
have nightmares in which she was back in the corridor, but this time the doors would not open, and
the smell grew thicker and thicker until she woke gasping, her sheets soaked through with sweat, her
heart hammering against her ribs.

She had come to believe that there was something defective in her, something broken that could not
be fixed. Her body was no longer hers to trust. It reacted to threats that were not there, kept her
vigilant in a way that exhausted her, made her startle at shadows and scents. She was constantly
tense, constantly scanning, constantly afraid. Her grades, which had always been strong, had begun
to slip as her concentration fractured under the weight of hypervigilance. After three years of
this, after dropping two classes last semester because she could not concentrate, after watching her
friendships narrow as people grew tired of her unpredictability, after her mother called her from
Guatemala and heard only flatness in her daughter's voice and wept quietly on the other end of the
line, Mariana had finally called the university counseling center. She needed to understand why her
body would not let her move forward, why it insisted on keeping her trapped in a moment that was
long past. She was hoping that someone could help her find her way back to herself, or at least help
her understand the person she had become. What frightened her most was that the strategies she had
developed to survive—the avoidance, the suppression, the hypervigilance—had become a prison of their
own, one that was slowly collapsing the life she had tried to build. She was also terrified that if
she stopped avoiding the memory, if she stopped running from it, the panic would consume her
entirely and she would not come back.
