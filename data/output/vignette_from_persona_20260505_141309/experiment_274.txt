# Thida

Thida was forty-eight when she first noticed the car. It was a silver sedan that appeared in the
parking lot behind the restaurant where she worked as a server, always at the end of her shift,
always facing the back exit. She remembered the date clearly—October 14th—because it was the day her
daughter had called to say she was moving to Portland, and Thida had been crying in the walk-in
cooler when she saw it through the small window. She told herself it was nothing. The lot was
public. People parked there.

But the car came back. And the next week, she saw him.

He was a man she had dated briefly two years earlier, someone she had met online and dismissed after
three dates because he was too intense, too interested in knowing where she went and who she saw.
When she ended it, he had taken it badly. There were messages—dozens of them—that she had blocked
and reported. After a few months of silence, she had allowed herself to forget about him entirely.
Then he was there, leaning against the silver sedan, watching her unlock her Honda.

What followed were three months of escalation that Thida could now barely articulate, even to
herself. He would appear at the restaurant, sitting in a booth in her section, ordering nothing,
just watching her move between tables. He followed her to the grocery store. He left notes on her
car. He called from different numbers. The police took a report but told her there was nothing they
could do until he made an explicit threat. He never did. Instead, he simply made himself present,
made her aware that he knew her patterns, her schedule, her vulnerability.

By January, Thida had stopped going anywhere she didn't have to. She quit her evening shifts and
took a day position at a smaller café across town, a place she didn't know well and where, she
reasoned, he would be less likely to find her. But the unfamiliarity of the new location terrified
her in a different way. She could not read the space. She did not know all the exits. She did not
know which customers were regulars and which were strangers. Every unfamiliar face made her stomach
clench. Every sound—a car door, footsteps on the sidewalk, the door chime—sent her into a state of
alert that left her shaking. She had come to believe that danger lived in spaces she could not fully
map and control, and there were fewer and fewer of those spaces that felt safe enough to enter.

The memories of those three months did not come to her in the way she might have expected. They did
not arrive as coherent narratives that she could examine and understand. Instead, they forced
themselves into her mind unbidden—fragments that made no sense. The smell of his cologne. The sound
of the car door closing. The feeling of being watched from across a room. These pieces would intrude
without warning, and when they did, her body responded as though the threat were present and
immediate. Her heart would race. Her hands would shake. She would become hyperaware of every sound
in her apartment, listening for the particular creak of footsteps on the stairs outside her door,
the rattle of a doorknob, the sound of someone entering her home.

What frightened Thida most was that she could not seem to hold the memory in her mind in a way that
felt like her own experience. When she tried to talk about it, the words scattered. It was as though
the trauma belonged to someone else, something she had witnessed rather than lived, and yet her body
carried it as absolute truth. She had stopped looking at herself in mirrors. She could not bear to
see her own body, the vessel that had been watched and tracked. She had withdrawn from her
friends—the few she had left—because she could not face their questions, their concern, their
attempts to help. The thought of any kind of confrontation, even a gentle conversation about why she
was distant, made her want to disappear.

At work now, Thida moved through her shifts in a state of muted compliance. She took orders. She
delivered food. But she was not present in the way she used to be. She used to be someone who
chatted with regulars, who remembered their preferences, who found meaning in small moments of
connection. Now she felt dead inside, as though the person who had once been happy—who had laughed
easily, who had believed in possibility—had been replaced by someone hollow and vigilant. Nothing
felt meaningful anymore. Even her daughter's move, which should have hurt or mattered, registered as
simply another empty thing in an empty life.

It was her manager who finally suggested she seek help. Thida had called in sick seventeen times in
two months. She had become unreliable, distant, and the other staff had begun to avoid her. He was
not unkind about it, but his suggestion landed like an indictment. Thida realized that she was not
only being hunted by a man who no longer even appeared; she was being hunted by her own mind, by
memories she could not control and a body she could not trust. She made an appointment with a
therapist, hoping that someone could help her understand why she was still running, and from what,
exactly, she was running toward.
