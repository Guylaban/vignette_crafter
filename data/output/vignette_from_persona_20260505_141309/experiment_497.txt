# Aroha

Aroha was twenty-three months into what she had come to think of as her ruined life when she finally
made an appointment with a therapist. The stalking had begun in the spring of her first year at
university—a boy from her statistics seminar who had seemed harmless enough at first, sitting near
her in lectures, asking to study together. When she politely declined, he had appeared at the
library where she worked part-time, at the coffee shop near her apartment, once at her parents'
house three hours away. He had sent messages—hundreds of them—that escalated from friendly to
possessive to openly threatening. The university had issued a restraining order. He had violated it
twice before finally moving away. Aroha had believed, in those early weeks after his departure, that
she might recover. That belief now struck her as naive, even culpable.

The truth, which she had come to understand with absolute certainty, was that the world had revealed
itself to her as fundamentally dangerous, and she could never unknow that. A person could be watched
without knowing it. A person could be wanted in ways that had nothing to do with her consent or
safety. The ordinary textures of life—a familiar café, a library study carrel, the walk from her car
to her apartment—had become landscapes of potential threat. Aroha had responded by withdrawing from
them entirely. She no longer went out except to her job as an auditor at a mid-sized firm, and even
that had become a source of grinding anxiety. She took the same route each day, left at precisely
the same time, sat at the same desk in the back corner of the office where she could see the door.
Her colleagues had stopped inviting her to lunch. Her best friend from secondary school had
eventually stopped texting. Her parents called weekly, their voices tight with worry.

The punishment thinking had arrived more gradually, settling into her mind like sediment. Aroha had
come to believe that she deserved the suffering she was experiencing. She had been too trusting, too
naive, too slow to recognize the danger. She had worn certain clothes, made eye contact in class,
been polite when she should have been cold. The stalker's obsession was somehow her fault, and the
ongoing terror and isolation were her just consequence. When her mother had suggested she attend her
cousin's wedding—a joyful family event, something that might have lifted her mood—Aroha had found
herself unable to even consider it. The idea of being in a room full of people, of being seen and
celebrated, of allowing herself to feel happiness, struck her as obscene. She didn't deserve that.
More than that, she was certain something terrible would happen if she let her guard down, if she
permitted herself to feel safe or hopeful for even a moment. The world was waiting for her to relax,
and then it would strike.

What had finally driven her to seek help was not a sudden crisis but a slow recognition that she was
disappearing. At work, her productivity had plummeted. She had made errors in three separate
audits—careless mistakes that suggested her mind was elsewhere, which it was, constantly scanning
for threats. Her supervisor had pulled her aside last week to ask if everything was alright. Aroha
had said yes, but the conversation had terrified her. She was failing. She was failing at the one
thing she had managed to hold onto. And beneath all of that was a deeper, more desperate fear: that
she would spend the rest of her life this way, trapped in a cage of her own hypervigilance,
convinced of her own culpability, unable to trust that any moment was safe. She had called the
clinic on a Tuesday afternoon, her hands shaking as she dialed, uncertain whether she was reaching
out or simply going through the motions of something she didn't deserve.
