# Marco's Story

Marco was twenty-one when he arrived in Syria as a relief worker with a faith-based humanitarian
organization. He had married Lucia just six months earlier—a quiet, hopeful union blessed by both
their families in their small community outside Boston. When the opportunity came to serve, Marco
felt called to it. He had grown up hearing stories of obligation and sacrifice from his
grandparents, who had themselves fled conflict decades before. This was his chance to matter, to be
part of something larger than himself. Lucia supported him, though with visible reluctance. He
promised he would be careful. He promised he would come home.

In his third week, Marco's convoy was ambushed near Aleppo. The attack came at dawn, sudden and
overwhelming. He remembers the first explosion as a sound that seemed to come from inside his own
chest—a compression of air and earth that threw him sideways. Around him, people were screaming. A
colleague beside him—a woman named Amira who had shown him how to distribute medical supplies—was
bleeding from her leg. Marco tried to help her, but within seconds everything became chaos. Gunfire.
More explosions. The acrid smell of smoke and something else, something burned and organic that his
mind still cannot quite name. He crouched behind an overturned vehicle, pressing himself into the
metal, convinced each moment would be his last. The shooting lasted perhaps five minutes, though it
felt like hours compressed into an unbearable instant. When it finally stopped, four people were
dead. Amira survived, but barely.

Marco was evacuated the next day. He told himself he was lucky. He told himself the organization
needed him home to help coordinate logistics. The truth was that he could barely function. By the
time he landed in Boston, something fundamental had shifted in how his mind worked.

The first weeks back, Marco struggled to sleep. He would lie beside Lucia, his body rigid, listening
to every sound in their apartment—the refrigerator humming, the neighbor's footsteps, a car horn
blocks away. Around three or four in the morning, he would slip out of bed and check the locks on
their doors and windows, sometimes twice in a single night. Lucia would wake to find him standing at
the window in darkness, watching the street. When she asked what he was looking for, he couldn't
explain. He just knew that danger was out there, waiting. Strangers at the grocery store seemed to
track him with their eyes. A man standing too close in line made his heart race so violently he had
to leave. He began to avoid going out during daylight hours, when more people would be around.

Sleep became impossible, so Marco stopped trying to achieve it. Instead, he would stay up until two
or three in the morning, watching videos, reading articles, his mind churning through scenarios.
What if he had positioned himself differently in the convoy? What if he had insisted they take a
different route? What if he had been paying closer attention, had seen some warning sign that could
have prevented everything? He constructed elaborate chains of causation, each one placing
responsibility on his own choices, his own failures of perception or courage. The rumination was
exhausting and compulsive—he could not stop even when he recognized it was pointless.

When Lucia suggested they attend a dinner party with friends, or when she mentioned that her sister
was getting engaged and there would be celebrations, Marco felt a strange resistance rise in him.
The thought of being in a room where people were laughing, where there might be joy, felt
wrong—almost offensive. How could he sit at a table and enjoy food when Amira still had nightmares?
When the families of the dead were grieving? He declined the invitations. Lucia's face fell each
time. She stopped asking.

The memory of the attack has a quality that terrifies him precisely because it feels impossible.
When he closes his eyes, he is there again—not remembering it, but living it. The explosion is
happening right now. The smell is in his nostrils. His body does not understand that it is over. Any
mention of military operations, any news report about conflict, any conversation in which someone
casually mentions the word "army," sends him spiraling back into that moment. Once, at work, a
colleague mentioned she had a brother in the National Guard. Marco had to excuse himself and sit in
his car for an hour, shaking.

Worse was the day a family speaking Arabic moved in downstairs. When he heard their voices raised in
what sounded like argument, his entire nervous system exploded into alarm. He knew—absolutely knew,
with the certainty of someone experiencing the event again—that danger was imminent. He could not
explain this to Lucia. How could he tell his wife that the sound of a language he had come to
associate with terror made him feel like he was dying?

At work as a social ministry coordinator, Marco has become increasingly withdrawn. His supervisor
has noticed. He calls in sick more often than he used to. When he does come in, he sits in his
office with the door closed, avoiding the common areas where colleagues gather. The work that once
felt meaningful now feels hollow. He cannot reconcile his present paralysis with the person he was
meant to be.

It is Lucia who finally insisted he seek help. She found him one night standing at the window at
four in the morning, tears running down his face, unable to articulate why. "This isn't living," she
said quietly. "And I can't help you alone."

Marco called a therapist the next day. He is terrified of what it will mean to talk about what
happened, but he is more terrified of losing his wife, of spending the rest of his life imprisoned
by a memory that feels as immediate and lethal as the day it occurred.
