# Levi's Vigil

Levi Parata was sixty-eight when the bomb went off at the Wellington train station. He had been
standing on the platform waiting for the 8:47 to Porirua, holding his travel bag and thinking about
the job he had lined up—interior trim work for a boutique hotel downtown. The platform was crowded,
the morning light streaming through the skylights above. He remembers the sound first: not the
explosion itself, but the moment just before it, a kind of electrical hum that made his teeth ache.
Then the blast lifted him. His body went rigid, arched backward, his shoulders slamming against the
platform edge. The bag flew from his hands. For a moment he was suspended in a chaos of noise and
heat and the smell of burnt plastic and concrete dust. When he came down, his left shoulder
dislocated, and his head struck something sharp. He lay there in the noise, unable to move, certain
he was dying.

The attack killed four people and wounded seventeen. Levi spent six weeks in hospital. The physical
injuries healed—the shoulder was reset, the scalp wound closed—but something else had fractured,
something that would not mend with time. By the time he was discharged, he had begun to notice that
his body would betray him without warning. A car backfiring on the street outside would send a jolt
through his chest so violent he thought his heart was stopping. The sensation would linger for
hours, his muscles locked in a posture of braced defense, shoulders hunched, arms drawn inward,
exactly as they had been in those seconds after impact. He would find himself standing in that
position in his own home, unable to straighten, unable to breathe normally, until the spell passed.

The memories came with a quality of absolute presence. Not the soft, fading recollections of
ordinary experience, but vivid, intrusive scenes that forced themselves into his mind at
unpredictable moments. He would be mixing paint at work and suddenly he was back on that platform,
the light fractured, the sound arriving again with full sensory force. The memory did not feel like
something he was remembering; it felt like something happening now. His body would respond in
kind—shoulders tensing, breath catching, the old position reasserting itself as though the platform
were still beneath him.

In the months that followed, Levi had come to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous in
a way he had not grasped before. The attack had not been random misfortune; it had revealed a truth
about the nature of things. People could die in ordinary moments. Safety was an illusion. And he,
Levi, could not be trusted to keep himself safe. He had survived, but only by chance. When he closed
his eyes at night, he would think about the other people on that platform, how they had not chosen
to be there any more than he had, how the blast had selected them with indifference. He could not
rely on his own judgment to protect himself. He could not rely on others—the security systems had
failed, the authorities had not prevented it—and so he had begun, without quite deciding to, to stay
home.

Going out had become intolerable. The thought of being in public spaces, of standing on a train
platform or sitting in a café, produced a dread so consuming that staying home felt like the only
rational choice. His painting work had suffered first. He had turned down jobs, then more jobs,
until clients stopped calling. The isolation deepened. His sister in Auckland rang regularly, and
Levi would ask her, during these calls, whether she thought he was okay, whether she thought
something terrible was going to happen to him. She would reassure him, and for a few minutes the
anxiety would recede, but then it would return, as persistent as thirst. He needed constant
reassurance, yet he could not quite believe it when it came. How could she know? How could anyone
know?

He had also learned to control his emotions, or so he believed. When fear rose in him, he would push
it down, hold it rigid inside himself, refuse to let it show. But the effort was exhausting, and it
had begun to show in other ways—a flatness in his voice, a distance from the few people who still
tried to reach him. He had become, at seventy-one, a man who no longer left his house, who called
his sister for reassurance he could not accept, and who carried in his body, moment by moment, the
exact posture of that platform, that blast, that moment when the world had revealed itself as
fundamentally unsafe. It was only when his sister threatened to move him into care that he agreed to
see someone. He was afraid, he told the therapist at intake, that he could no longer trust his own
mind.
