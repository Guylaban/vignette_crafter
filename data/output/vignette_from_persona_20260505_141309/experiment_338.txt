# Thien's Reckoning

Thien Nguyen, forty-one, had spent the last fifteen years teaching dance in Portland—first at a
small studio on Division Street, later at a nonprofit that served at-risk youth. He was known for
his patience, his ability to coax shy teenagers into finding grace in their bodies, the way he moved
through the studio with the kind of presence that made people want to follow. His husband Marcus had
watched him work countless times and always said the same thing: you make it look like breathing.
Until three years ago, when Thien had taken a leave of absence to work as a relief worker in a
conflict zone in Southeast Asia, helping to establish trauma-informed movement programs for
displaced communities. It was meant to be six months. He came home after four.

The night of the incident, Thien and two other aid workers had been moving through a checkpoint in a
town that had been contested for weeks. The smell of alcohol had been thick in the air—a soldier at
the barrier reeking of it, aggressive and unpredictable. Thien's colleague had tried to move past
him too quickly. What followed happened in fragments that Thien's mind would later refuse to order
clearly: the soldier's rage, the shouting, the sudden violent escalation, Thien's own body moving to
intervene in a way that felt instinctive but was, he would come to believe, fundamentally wrong. He
had pushed the soldier. In the chaos that followed, his colleague was hurt. Badly. The smell of
burnt rubber and something chemical had filled the air as they fled. Thien had gotten out. His
colleague had been evacuated weeks later, scarred and traumatized in ways that made Thien's own
survival feel like an indictment.

For months after returning, Thien functioned. He moved through the days with the kind of controlled
precision he had once brought to choreography. But something fundamental had shifted. Around 4:15 in
the afternoon—the time the incident had occurred—a dread would settle into his chest that felt like
drowning. He had come to avoid being anywhere public at that hour. He would stay late at the studio
if he had to, or leave early, anything to avoid the moment when the light changed and the time on
the clock approached that number. His colleagues noticed he was scheduling fewer late afternoon
classes. When asked, he simply said his energy was better in the mornings.

The smell of alcohol, which had once been unremarkable to him, now triggered an immediate surge of
panic and shame so intense that he would leave restaurants or bars where someone was drinking
nearby. Once, Marcus had opened a bottle of wine at dinner and Thien had left the table without
explanation, sitting in the garage for twenty minutes. He couldn't articulate why. The burnt smell
was worse—any scent of burning, whether from cooking or a distant fire, would catapult him into the
memory with such force that he would lose minutes, convinced he was back there, that the violence
was still unfolding in real time.

But it was the shame that had become unbearable. When the memory came—and it came often, without
warning, triggered by something as small as a particular angle of afternoon light or a raised voice
in the studio—Thien would feel it not as fear but as a crushing sense that he had violated something
essential about himself. He had acted in a way that contradicted everything he believed he was. A
man who taught movement as a path to healing had instead created more harm. He had failed to control
himself, failed to de-escalate, failed in the most fundamental way. In the aftermath, he had come to
see his own reactions as evidence of something deeper: that he was, at his core, a lousy coper,
someone who fell apart under pressure and made catastrophic decisions. He couldn't trust himself
anymore. When Marcus suggested they go to a concert or a friend's birthday party, Thien would agree
and then cancel, gripped by a certainty that if something unexpected happened, he would fail again.
He couldn't rely on himself to manage his own mind.

By the time he finally called a therapist, Thien had reduced his teaching schedule to three days a
week. The studio director had gently asked if everything was okay. He had told her yes. He had told
Marcus yes, though his husband's eyes suggested he knew it wasn't true. What had finally pushed him
to seek help was a moment in the studio last week when a student had moved too quickly into his
peripheral vision, and Thien had flinched so violently that he'd knocked over a speaker. The shame
of that moment—his body betraying him in front of his students, evidence that he couldn't even
manage his own nervous system in the one place where he was supposed to be whole—had made him
finally pick up the phone.
