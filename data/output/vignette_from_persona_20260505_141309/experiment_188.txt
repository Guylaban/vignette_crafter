# Clinical Vignette: Thea

Thea was twenty-two when the sedan crossed the centre line. She had been driving home from her
parents' house in Sacramento on a clear afternoon in June, thinking about nothing in particular,
when a pickup truck swerved into her lane without warning. There was no time to brake properly. The
impact came on her driver's side, metal folding inward, her body thrown sideways as the car spun.
She remembered the sound—a noise unlike anything she had heard before—and the sensation of losing
control, of being a passenger in her own vehicle as it skidded across the asphalt. When the car
finally stopped, she was still breathing. The other driver, who had been texting, walked away with
minor injuries. Thea spent two nights in the hospital with a fractured collarbone and deep bruising
along her ribs.

The physical injuries healed within weeks. The rest did not. By the time she married her husband
Marcus three years later, Thea had constructed an elaborate architecture of avoidance that she
believed was simply the way she now lived. She worked remotely as a database administrator, a job
that suited her preference for minimal human contact, though her manager had begun to notice her
declining participation in team meetings. When colleagues with names similar to the other driver's
name appeared in her inbox or on video calls, her chest would tighten. She had come to believe that
the crash had been her fault—that she had been driving too fast, or not paying enough attention, or
somehow had failed to anticipate what was coming. More than that, she was certain she could have
done something in those final seconds to prevent it. She would replay the moment obsessively,
imagining herself swerving sooner, braking harder, as if a different Thea existed who had possessed
the prescience to avoid the collision entirely. The guilt of her inaction in that moment—the fact
that she had simply frozen as the truck approached—had become unbearable.

Within a year of the accident, Thea had stopped seeing friends. She told herself it was easier this
way, that she was protecting them from her deteriorating mood, but the truth was more complicated.
Arguments triggered something in her that felt dangerous. When Marcus raised his voice about
forgotten groceries or a missed dinner, Thea's body flooded with the same helplessness she had felt
in the car—the sense that something terrible was about to happen and she could not stop it.
Uncertainty was perhaps worse. When Marcus mentioned that his brother might visit, or when her work
schedule changed, or when a friend texted asking if she wanted to meet, Thea felt a creeping dread
that something would go wrong, that she would be unable to prevent disaster. The world had become a
place where terrible things happened without warning, and she was too slow, too inattentive, too
fundamentally flawed to stop them.

By her twenty-fifth year, Thea had developed a method of managing the intrusive thoughts about the
crash. When memories surfaced—the sound of metal, the sensation of spinning—she would pinch the
inside of her forearm until it hurt, or press her fingernails into her palm. The pain created a
sharp, present sensation that displaced the memory, at least temporarily. Marcus had noticed the
marks on her skin but had learned not to ask. She also avoided anything that put her body under
physical stress. Running made her anxious; crowded spaces made her hypervigilant; driving, even as a
passenger, was nearly impossible. She had declined a promotion that would have required occasional
travel.

What finally brought Thea to seek help was a moment of clarity that frightened her more than the
crash itself. Marcus had suggested, gently, that they might try for a family. The thought had
paralyzed her. She realized, in that moment, that she had become someone who could not trust herself
to protect anyone—not even herself. She was afraid that her hypervigilance masked a fundamental
incompetence, that her guilt was evidence of her culpability, and that anyone who depended on her
would eventually suffer for it. She called a therapist's office on a Tuesday afternoon, her hands
shaking as she gave her name.
