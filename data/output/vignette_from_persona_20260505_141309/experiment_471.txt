# Clinical Vignette: Marco

Marco was fifty-two when the accident happened, and he had spent thirty years building a reputation
as a meticulous, unflinching director—the kind of professional who could hold a vision steady
through fourteen-hour days and temperamental crews. He had won awards. He had made films that people
remembered. His partner, David, had learned long ago that Marco's work was not separate from his
identity; it was woven through him so completely that there was no distinguishing the man from the
artist.

The accident occurred during a night shoot in an abandoned warehouse in New Jersey. Marco had
insisted on minimal lighting to capture a particular quality of shadow for a pivotal scene. He was
standing on a raised platform, directing the gaffer on a final adjustment, when the platform's
support beam—corroded, unnoticed—gave way beneath him. He fell fifteen feet onto concrete, his left
leg fracturing in two places, his ribs cracking, his head striking the edge of a metal crate. The
darkness that had seemed so essential to his artistic vision became the darkness in which he lay
bleeding, unable to call out, unable to move, certain for the minutes before someone found him that
he was dying alone in the dark.

Three months into his recovery, Marco returned to work. The physical wounds had healed into scars.
But something else had fractured and had not mended. Darkness became intolerable to him. He could
not sit through a screening in a dark theater without his chest tightening, his breath becoming
shallow. He began requiring that all his sets be lit, even scenes meant to feel shadowed—he would
direct cinematographers to create darkness through color grading rather than through actual absence
of light. His crews noticed. His producers noticed. The artistic vision that had defined his work
became constrained by something he could not name and could not control.

The hypervigilance arrived in layers. Unexpected sounds—a door slamming on set, the sudden blare of
a speaker, a car horn outside the editing suite—would send a spike of adrenaline through his body.
His hands would shake. He would startle visibly, something he had never done before, something he
found humiliating. He had become the kind of person who jumped at ordinary noises, and he was
acutely aware of how this appeared to others: weak, unstable, not himself.

The memories of the fall came to him primarily as images—the moment of the beam shifting, the
sensation of weightlessness, the concrete rushing toward him—but he could not construct them into a
coherent narrative. When David would ask him gently what had happened, Marco would find himself
unable to articulate it. The experience existed in him as fragmented visual impressions and bodily
sensations, not as a story he could tell.

What frightened him most was what he had become in response. He was a man who now avoided darkness,
who flinched at sounds, who could not work the way he once had. He spent hours in distraction,
scrolling through his phone, reorganizing his office, anything to avoid the thought that kept
returning: *Why did this happen to me?* The question circled endlessly without answer. He had come
to believe that his reactions since the accident—the fear, the hypervigilance, the inability to
function as he once did—proved something fundamental about him: that he was not equipped to handle
adversity, that he was inadequate in ways he had never suspected. The person he saw reflected back
at him from David's concerned eyes was someone he no longer recognized.

Six months ago, Marco had stepped back from directing. He told his agent it was temporary. It was
not temporary. Last week, after David found him sitting in the dark basement—not by choice, but
because he had been testing himself and failed—Marco finally called a trauma therapist. He was
terrified that no one could help him return to who he had been, and equally terrified that if he
could not, there was nothing left of him worth saving.
