# Amara's Story

Amara was seventy-eight when the man came through her back door. It was a Tuesday evening in March,
nearly two years ago now, and she had been in her studio—a converted garage behind the house she and
her late husband Samuel had owned for forty-three years—finishing a series of bowls for a gallery
show in Atlanta. The light was fading. She had not heard him force the lock; she heard only his
footsteps on the concrete floor, and by the time she turned from the wheel, he was already there,
his face obscured, his hand raised with something that caught what remained of the daylight. She was
certain it was a knife. The memory of his presence came back to her now in images—the blur of his
jacket, the particular angle of his arm, the way the studio suddenly felt too small—and when the
memory arrived unbidden, which it did at least three times daily, it came with the sensation that it
was happening again, that she was still standing at that wheel with her hands wet and her heart
hammering so violently she thought it would break through her ribs.

He took her purse. He took Samuel's watch from the kitchen counter—the one she still had not been
able to put away. He did not strike her, though she had braced for it, and when he left through the
same door he had entered, Amara had stood motionless in the studio for a time she could not
afterward account for. The police came. They found nothing that led anywhere. What he had taken was
replaceable. What he had left behind was not.

In the weeks that followed, Amara had begun checking the locks. First, the back door lock—the one he
had broken—which she now secured with a deadbolt and a chain and a wooden dowel wedged through the
frame. Then the front door. Then the windows, each one, a ritual that now took her nearly forty
minutes every evening before she could sit down. But sitting down brought no rest. Her body remained
vigilant even when her mind knew, abstractly, that the doors were locked and the windows were
latched. She would lie in bed at two or three in the morning, jolting awake with the conviction that
someone was moving through the house, and she would rise and walk through the rooms, turning on
lights, checking locks again, listening for the particular creak that might mean an intruder. She
had come to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous, that safety was an illusion
maintained only through exhausting vigilance, and that her own body—her racing heart, her shallow
breath, the trembling in her hands—was a traitor that could not be trusted or controlled.

The studio had become impossible. She had tried to return to her wheel in May, thinking that the
familiar rhythm of clay might settle something in her, but the moment she was alone in that space
with the door behind her, her mind filled with the image of his footsteps crossing the concrete. She
found herself unable to concentrate, unable to feel the clay respond to her hands the way it once
had. By July, she had stopped going in altogether. The gallery in Atlanta had to cancel her show. A
collector who had commissioned a large installation piece had asked, after months of silence, if she
was still working. She was not. The studio gathered dust. The wheel sat idle.

Her daughter, who lived in Charlotte, had noticed during a phone call in September that her mother's
voice sounded thin, exhausted. When she visited, she found Amara thinner as well, and the house
immaculate in a way that suggested obsession rather than care. Amara was checking mirrors constantly
now—a habit she had developed because she could not shake the feeling that someone was behind her,
that she needed to verify repeatedly that she was alone. She would stand in the hallway looking at
her own face in the mirror above the dresser, reassuring herself, though the reassurance never held.

It was her daughter who finally insisted that Amara see someone, who said that what had happened to
her mother two years ago was not something she should have to carry alone. Amara had resisted,
believing that if she thought about the event directly, if she let herself fully acknowledge what
had occurred, she would shatter. But the alternative—the endless checking, the sleepless nights, the
abandonment of the work that had defined her life for sixty years—had become unbearable. She had
called a therapist last week. She was terrified of what might happen if she began to speak about
that Tuesday in March, but she was more terrified of living the rest of her life as a prisoner in
her own home.
