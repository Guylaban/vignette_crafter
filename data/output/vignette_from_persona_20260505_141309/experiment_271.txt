# Patricia's Vigilance

Patricia was fifty-eight when the chemical spill happened. She had worked in policy analysis for the
Department of Environmental Quality for nearly thirty years—a careful woman, methodical, someone who
understood risk assessment the way other people understood breathing. On a Tuesday afternoon in
March, she was in the basement archive of the state office building reviewing old compliance reports
when a contractor's acetylene torch breached a corroded pipe in the wall. The chemical that escaped
was a solvent used in industrial cleaning, and it filled the basement with a smell like burnt
plastic mixed with something acrid that made her throat close. She ran, her colleagues ran, and by
the time she reached the parking lot her eyes were streaming and her lungs felt like they were
closing. The exposure lasted perhaps four minutes. The hospital visit was precautionary. The doctor
said her lungs were fine, that she had inhaled fumes but that there was no lasting tissue damage.
She went home that evening.

But the smell did not leave her. For weeks afterward, Patricia would catch that burnt odor in her
own house—in the kitchen after she cooked, in the hallway when someone opened a bottle of wine, even
in her own breath when she exhaled sharply. She became convinced that the chemical had seeped into
her clothing, her hair, the fabric of her car. She began to ruminate obsessively about what she
should have done differently. If she had not gone to the basement that day—but she had to go, it was
her job. If she had left five seconds earlier—but how could she have known? She lay awake at night
constructing scenarios, rewriting the afternoon in her mind, searching for the moment where she
might have prevented this from happening. Her husband Marcus would find her staring at nothing, and
when he asked what she was thinking about, she could not quite explain that she was trapped in a
loop of if-only, a version of herself that had acted faster or chosen differently.

By May, Patricia had stopped going to the office. She told her supervisor it was medical leave, and
it was true in a way, though the doctors could find nothing wrong with her. What she could not
articulate was that the building itself had become unsafe in her mind. Any enclosed space now felt
like a potential trap. When she did venture out—to the grocery store, to her daughter's house—she
would enter a room and immediately scan for exits, her eyes moving to doorways and windows before
she could stop herself. She always chose to sit in chairs with a clear view of the way out. At
restaurants, she requested tables near the entrance. Her daughter had noticed and said something
gentle about it, but Patricia could not explain that she was not being anxious; she was being smart.
She was protecting herself the way she should have been protected in that basement.

The physical reactions came without warning. A neighbor burned leaves in autumn and Patricia found
herself unable to breathe, her heart hammering, her hands shaking so violently that Marcus had to
take her inside. She avoided television programs about industrial accidents, about chemical
disasters, about anything that might remind her. She stopped reading the news. She had come to
believe that she could not trust her own judgment anymore—that the person who had walked into that
basement, the woman who had worked in policy for three decades, was no longer someone she could rely
on. She questioned every decision now, certain that she would choose wrong, that her instincts had
been fundamentally broken by those four minutes. The worst part was the fear that she would never
feel like herself again, that the woman she had been before March was gone and what remained was
only this vigilant, frightened version who scanned rooms and smelled danger in ordinary air.

It was Marcus who finally insisted she see someone. He said he could not watch her disappear
anymore. Patricia agreed, though she was not certain anyone could help her trust herself again.
