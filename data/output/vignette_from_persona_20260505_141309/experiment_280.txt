# Layla's Witness

Layla was twenty-one when she watched her best friend die. She had been working as a forester for
the state park service for just over a year, and on a Tuesday in late spring, she and her colleague
Marcus had been clearing deadfall along the access road near the western ridge. The work was
routine—they'd been doing the same stretch for three days. Marcus was driving the pickup truck back
from the supply depot, and Layla was walking ahead, marking trees with flagging tape. She heard the
sound first: a sound like nothing she'd heard before, a collision that seemed to compress time
itself. When she turned, the truck was wrapped around an oak, and Marcus was visible through the
shattered windshield, his body at an angle that told her immediately something was fundamentally
wrong.

The smell stayed with her—gasoline and something else, something metallic and warm that she would
later understand was blood. For weeks after, she couldn't fully articulate what she'd seen. When she
tried to think back on those moments, the memory felt distant, almost as though it had happened to
someone else and she was only watching from very far away. She would start to recall the sequence of
events—the sound, the wreckage, the way his hand hung limply from the window—and then something in
her mind would simply stop, pushing the images away before they could fully form. It was as if her
own memory had become a place she couldn't fully enter.

The guilt came immediately and never left. Layla had come to believe that she should have done
something—should have somehow known what was about to happen, should have prevented Marcus from
driving that road, should have been in the truck with him so that she might have grabbed the wheel
or warned him or simply been there. She knew logically that this made no sense, but the conviction
lived in her body anyway, a weight she carried into the forest each morning. What made it worse was
that she had always thought of herself as someone dependable, someone who protected people she cared
about. The fact that she hadn't—couldn't have—felt like a fundamental betrayal of who she was
supposed to be.

By autumn, Layla had developed a rigid set of habits designed to keep the memory at bay. She stayed
awake until two or three in the morning, scrolling through her phone or reorganizing her apartment,
anything to delay the moment when she would lie in bed and the images might surface. At work, she
found herself constantly scanning the forest around her—not for deadfall anymore, but for movement,
for danger, for the possibility of another accident. Every vehicle that passed on the access road
made her tense. When colleagues mentioned accidents in conversation, even casually, she felt her
chest tighten and would excuse herself.

Her supervisor had begun to notice. Layla's work was careful but slower now, her attention
fractured. More troubling was her reaction when a coworker mentioned a car accident he'd seen on the
news. Layla had left the break room without explanation and hadn't returned for forty minutes. When
she came back, her hands were shaking. The supervisor suggested gently that she might want to talk
to someone, but Layla had resisted. How could she explain that the problem wasn't something that
could be talked away—that she had failed at the most basic human obligation, to protect someone she
loved?

What finally brought Layla to seek help was the realization that she was no longer the person she
had been. She used to find joy in the forest, in the quiet work of restoration. Now she moved
through her days in a gray fog, unable to imagine feeling happy again. The memory of Marcus had
become a permanent intrusion, triggered by blood on a bandage, by overheard conversations about
traffic, by the sight of a car on television. She was exhausted from fighting it, and she was
terrified that if she stopped fighting, the memory would consume her entirely.
