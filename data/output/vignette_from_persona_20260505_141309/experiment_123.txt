# Rajesh's Vigilance

Rajesh Patel was thirty-two when he was attacked outside the parking garage of the office complex
where he worked as a housekeeper. It was a Tuesday evening in October, just before seven, when the
light had gone thin and the garage was nearly empty. He had finished his shift—mopping the lobby,
emptying the bins from the executive floors—and was walking toward his car when a man emerged from
between two vehicles. There was no warning. The man grabbed him from behind, wrenching his right arm
upward with such force that Rajesh felt the shoulder socket scream. A second figure appeared, and in
the confusion of movement and pain, Rajesh's face was slammed against the concrete pillar. His left
cheekbone took the impact. The attackers wanted his wallet, his phone, his watch. The whole thing
lasted perhaps ninety seconds. They ran. Rajesh sat on the cold garage floor, bleeding from his
cheekbone, his shoulder throbbing in a way that made him nauseated.

The physical injuries healed within weeks. His cheekbone was badly bruised but not fractured. His
shoulder remained tender, stiff, but he regained most of its range of motion. What did not heal was
the clarity with which he could see it all happening again. When the memory surfaced—and it surfaced
often, without warning—Rajesh could feel the exact pressure of the man's grip on his arm, the
specific burning sensation as his face scraped against the concrete. His left cheekbone would ache
in sympathy with the memory, and his right shoulder would tighten as if the hand were still there,
still pulling. These sensations were so vivid and so localized that Rajesh sometimes found himself
touching his face or rolling his shoulder, checking that nothing was actually happening in that
moment.

What made it worse was that he could not explain it to anyone. His wife, Priya, had asked him
repeatedly in those first weeks what had happened, wanting the details, wanting to understand. But
when Rajesh tried to describe it, the words scattered. He knew the sequence of events—he could see
them perfectly in his mind—but the moment he opened his mouth, he found himself unable to construct
the narrative. He would start to speak and then stop, frustrated and ashamed. Eventually he gave her
the bare facts: he had been robbed, he was fine, it was over. She accepted this, but he could see
the worry in her eyes, the sense that something larger was being left unsaid.

In the months that followed, Rajesh's relationship to the world shifted in ways that felt both
involuntary and absolutely rational. He had come to believe that people were fundamentally not what
they appeared to be. The man who had attacked him had given no sign beforehand. He had simply
emerged from the ordinary landscape of the parking garage as if he had always been a threat,
waiting. This logic extended outward. The colleagues who greeted him cheerfully each morning—were
they really friendly, or were they performing? The security guard who waved at him—how well did the
man actually know him? Rajesh found himself scrutinizing faces, watching for the moment when a
pleasant expression might slip and reveal something underneath. He was certain now that people could
not be trusted, not because of any specific betrayal, but because the parking garage had shown him
that trust was a luxury built on a foundation of willful blindness.

The world itself had become visibly dangerous in a way it had not been before. Not in the abstract
sense of statistics or news reports, but in the concrete, moment-to-moment sense of walking through
space. Rajesh began taking different routes to his car, avoiding the garage entirely on some
evenings, parking instead in the street and walking further. He stopped going to the Indian grocery
store he and Priya had frequented for years because it required walking through a parking lot. He
declined invitations to go out in the evenings. Priya's concern deepened. His supervisor at the
housekeeping company noticed that Rajesh was sometimes distracted, sometimes tense, and that he had
begun requesting assignments that kept him on the upper floors of the building, away from the garage
level.

It was Priya who finally insisted he speak to someone. She had watched him become smaller, more
guarded, more isolated. She had felt the distance growing between them—the way he flinched when she
touched his shoulder, the way he seemed to be listening for something just beyond the threshold of
her awareness. She made the appointment herself. Now, sitting in the therapist's office, Rajesh is
struggling to articulate why he came. He knows something is wrong. He knows he cannot go on like
this. But what he fears most is that if he stops being vigilant, if he stops believing that the
world is fundamentally dangerous and that people cannot be trusted, something terrible will happen
again. The attack may have lasted ninety seconds, but its logic has become the structure through
which he now interprets everything.
