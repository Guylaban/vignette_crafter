# Kahu's Case

Kahu was fifty-six years old when he first sat down in the therapist's office, his broad shoulders
hunched forward, his hands clasped so tightly that the knuckles had gone pale. He was a detective
with the Honolulu Police Department—had been for twenty-eight years—and he had come because his ex-
wife had finally stopped answering his calls, his captain had pulled him off active cases pending a
fitness evaluation, and he could no longer remember the last time he had slept through a single
night. What Kahu did not say in that first session, but what emerged over the following weeks, was
that he had spent the last three decades running from a boy who no longer existed, and that boy was
catching up.

Kahu had grown up in Kailua with his grandmother after his mother's death. His grandmother's
partner, a man named Frank, had moved in when Kahu was seven. Frank was a large man with a temper
that seemed to ignite without warning, and he had particular fury for the smell of burnt food—a
smell that meant carelessness, disrespect, laziness. When Kahu was nine, he had left a pot on the
stove too long. The smell filled the small house. Frank had beaten him across the kitchen and into
the hallway, and that smell—acrid, choking, unmistakable—had become fused in Kahu's nervous system
with the certainty of pain. The beatings continued sporadically over the next nine years, always
worse when Frank had been drinking, always accompanied by his voice saying Kahu was worthless, that
he deserved it, that he was the kind of boy who made men angry.

Kahu had become a detective because he needed to be the one in control, the one watching, the one
who saw danger before it arrived. For decades, this had worked. He was good at his job—methodical,
observant, relentless. But in the last five years, the compartments had begun to fail. He had
started avoiding wearing the blue polo shirts that reminded him, in some way he could not quite
articulate, of the kind of shirt Frank had worn. He had stopped going to the precinct gym because a
younger officer there had similar dark hair and a similar build, and Kahu found himself unable to be
in the same room without his chest tightening, his mind fragmenting into something that was no
longer quite present. He had begun taking solo shifts, turning down invitations to the weekly poker
games, eating lunch alone in his car.

The worst trigger was the burnt smell. Three months ago, a house fire had been ruled arson, and Kahu
had been assigned to the case. He had walked into that charred kitchen and the smell had hit him
like a physical blow—not metaphorically, but literally: his legs had nearly buckled, his vision had
narrowed, and for several minutes he had been nine years old again, waiting for the next blow to
fall. He had finished the investigation, but something had shifted. Now, whenever he smelled
anything burnt—a colleague's overcooked lunch, a neighbor's barbecue—his body would flood with
adrenaline, his heart would race, and he would find himself scanning the room for exits, for
threats, for the man who was no longer there.

What frightened Kahu most was not the memories themselves, though they came without warning and with
a rage that terrified him. It was the moments when he felt something other than rage—a laugh with a
colleague, a warm afternoon, the simple peace of finishing a good meal—because in those moments, he
was certain that something terrible was about to happen. He had come to believe that contentment was
a setup, that his own capacity to feel okay was a character flaw, a sign of weakness that the
universe would punish. He was, he was certain, a lousy coper. The fact that he had survived, that he
had built a career and a life, only proved to him that he was fundamentally broken in ways he could
not quite explain.

When he tried to talk about what had happened, the narrative would dissolve. He would begin to
describe Frank, the kitchen, the smell, and then something would scatter inside him and he would
lose the thread entirely, left sitting in silence, ashamed. His ex-wife had finally told him that
she could not be married to someone who was not present, who could not explain what was happening
inside him, who seemed to be disappearing further every year. She had not said it unkindly. She had
just said it was true.

Now, at fifty-six, Kahu was sleeping perhaps three hours a night, his body refusing to fully rest
because some part of him still needed to stay alert, to watch, to be ready. He had requested the
intake appointment because his captain had made it clear that his job depended on it. But what Kahu
was truly hoping for, though he could barely admit it to himself, was the possibility that he might
finally understand why a boy who had done nothing wrong had come to believe that he deserved what
happened, and whether it was possible to stop running long enough to actually live.
