# Clinical Vignette: Robert

Robert was seventy-seven when the assault happened, a delivery driver for a medical supply company
for the past twelve years. He had built a quiet life with his wife Margaret in a suburb outside
Columbus, Ohio—predictable routes, regular customers who knew him by name, a rhythm that suited him.
On a Tuesday evening in March, while making a late delivery to a residential address in an
unfamiliar neighborhood, a man emerged from the shadows of the building's side entrance and struck
him across the face with a metal pipe. Robert fell hard. The man took his wallet and ran. A security
guard found Robert twenty minutes later, bleeding from a gash above his eye, unable to remember
clearly what had happened in those first moments after the blow.

The physical injuries healed within weeks. The fracture above his orbital bone knitted cleanly. But
something else fractured in Robert that night, something he could not articulate to the emergency
room doctor or even to Margaret when she arrived at the hospital trembling. He had always believed
in a basic orderliness to the world—that if you worked honestly, kept your head down, and treated
people decently, life would not ambush you. The assault had demolished that architecture entirely.
He found himself thinking, in the weeks that followed, that the world was fundamentally unsafe in
ways he had never truly grasped before, that violence could simply materialize without warning or
reason, and that his decades of caution had been a kind of delusion.

By early summer, Robert had developed a pattern of behaviors that he did not consciously recognize
as avoidance, but which had begun to reshape his days. He started working longer hours, volunteering
for the evening and weekend deliveries that other drivers refused. The busyness served a purpose:
when he was moving between addresses, checking inventory, solving the small logistical puzzles of
his job, he did not have to sit with the memory of the blow, the taste of blood, the moment of
helplessness on the pavement. Margaret noticed that he seemed to be eating more—large portions at
dinner, frequent trips to the kitchen in the evening where he would stand at the counter with a
plate of leftovers or whatever he could assemble, eating mechanically while watching the news. When
she asked if he was hungry, he would shrug and say nothing. What he could not tell her was that the
act of eating, the physical sensation of fullness, the small ritual of it, created a kind of buffer
against the intrusive images that came without warning: the man's silhouette, the arc of the pipe,
the moment before impact.

By autumn, Robert had stopped thinking about the assault—or so he believed. He had developed a
fierce discipline around the subject, a practiced suppression that felt like control. Whenever the
memory began to surface, he would redirect his attention aggressively toward work tasks, toward the
television, toward food. He told himself that if he could simply refuse to engage with the thought,
it would lose its power. But this refusal came with a cost he had not anticipated. He began to doubt
his own judgment in ways that bled into his work. A customer complained about a delivery time;
Robert spent hours afterward replaying the interaction, convinced he had misunderstood the original
instruction, certain that he had failed. He second-guessed his routes, his interactions with
colleagues, his decisions as a husband. Margaret would ask him a simple question about dinner plans,
and Robert would freeze, gripped by the conviction that whatever he decided would be wrong, that he
could not trust his own mind to guide him correctly.

What frightened him most was the thought that if he allowed himself to remember the assault fully,
to think about it without resistance, he would be overwhelmed entirely—that the thought itself would
shatter him in some irreversible way. He had come to believe that his only protection was constant
vigilance: stay busy, eat away the discomfort, refuse the memory entry into his conscious mind. By
December, when Margaret finally insisted he see someone, Robert was exhausted in a way that sleep
could not touch. He had gained twenty pounds. He was making small errors on his deliveries. And
underneath it all was the terrible, unspoken fear that he had lost something essential about
himself—that the man with the pipe had taken not just his wallet, but his ability to trust his own
mind, to navigate the world with any confidence at all.
