# Miguel's Story

Miguel Reyes was thirty-five when the accident happened, and he had been playing guitar
professionally for nearly two decades. He performed five nights a week at upscale restaurants and
hotels across the city, a reliable income that had allowed him to marry his high school sweetheart,
Catalina, and build a modest but stable life. On a Tuesday evening in March, he was driving home
from a late gig in the financial district, exhausted but content, when a delivery truck ran a red
light at an intersection near the highway. The impact came from the driver's side, violent and
sudden. Miguel's car crumpled inward. He remembered the sound of breaking glass, the sensation of
the steering wheel pressing against his ribs, and then nothing coherent—only fragments that would
not cohere into a complete picture. He was told later that he had lost consciousness, that
paramedics had to cut him from the wreckage, that he had spent four days in intensive care with a
fractured collarbone, three broken ribs, and a concussion. When he tried afterward to remember the
sequence of events, the memory dissolved into disconnected pieces: the glint of headlights, the
taste of blood, a paramedic's voice saying something about his pressure. The rest remained
inaccessible, locked away in a fog he could not penetrate no matter how hard he concentrated.

The driver of the truck had been cited for a traffic violation and fined. He had insurance. Within
months, the case was resolved in a settlement that Miguel's lawyer said was "fair market value."
Miguel had come to believe that nothing about this outcome was just. A man had nearly killed him,
had stolen something irreplaceable, and the consequence was administrative—a fine, a closed file. No
accountability that matched the magnitude of what had been taken. This sense of profound unfairness
had settled into his chest like a stone.

The physical injuries healed within eight weeks, but something else had fractured. In the first
weeks home, Miguel found himself unable to be alone. The moment Catalina left for work, panic would
creep in, and he would call friends, arrange jam sessions that he was not yet physically ready for,
invite neighbors over to watch football. He filled his apartment with noise and presence,
surrounding himself with people so that his mind could never turn inward toward the accident itself.
He had discovered early that if he tried to think about what happened, to deliberately reconstruct
the event and make sense of it, the memories would scatter like startled birds. It was easier not to
try. When Catalina would gently ask how he was processing things, or when a friend would mention the
accident with concern, Miguel would change the subject quickly, redirect the conversation, make a
joke. Talking about it felt dangerous, as though speaking the details aloud might give the trauma a
shape and weight it did not yet have.

But his constant socializing had consequences he had not foreseen. He returned to performing too
early, before his ribs had fully healed, pushing through pain because sitting at home alone was
unbearable. Catalina, exhausted by his need for constant company and frustrated by his refusal to
discuss anything meaningful, had begun to withdraw. The intimacy between them had eroded. More
troubling still was the anger that now lived just beneath his skin. Small frustrations—a missed note
during a performance, a waiter's impatience, a friend's casual comment—would trigger a rage that
felt disproportionate and terrifying. Miguel had never been a violent man, but he was now afraid of
himself, certain that if he allowed himself to fully feel the anger underneath everything, he would
do something terrible. He began to avoid situations where he might lose control, which meant
avoiding confrontation, avoiding honesty, avoiding the very conversations that might have saved his
marriage.

By the time Miguel came to therapy, six months after the accident, his life had transformed in ways
he could barely articulate. He was still performing, but the joy had evaporated. Catalina was
sleeping in the guest room. He had convinced himself that the trauma had destroyed everything—his
health, his relationship, his sense of who he was. What brought him finally to seek help was not
insight but crisis: Catalina had asked him to move out, and in the panic of losing her, Miguel
recognized that he could no longer outrun what had happened. He was most afraid that he was
right—that his life truly was broken beyond repair—and most hopeful that someone could help him
remember what he had lost, and why.
