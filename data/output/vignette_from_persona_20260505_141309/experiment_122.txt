# Clinical Vignette: Amir

Amir was twenty-three when it happened, three years ago now, walking home from the veterinary clinic
where they worked as an associate. It was early evening in late September, that particular quality
of dusk where the light drains fast and the streets empty quickly. They had been turning onto their
street in a neighbourhood they had lived in for two years, a place that felt safe enough, when two
figures emerged from behind a parked car. One grabbed Amir from behind—an arm around the neck,
sudden and absolute—while the other demanded the phone, the wallet, anything of value. The smell hit
first: stale cigarette smoke mixed with something chemical and sour, a scent Amir would later
struggle to name but could never forget. There was no resistance possible; Amir's body went rigid,
compliant, offering up everything without a word. The whole thing lasted perhaps ninety seconds. No
weapon was shown. No one was hurt. But something essential shifted in those ninety seconds,
something that Amir has spent three years trying to convince themselves did not really happen, or
was not that bad—a story they still half-believe on the good days.

In the weeks after, Amir returned to work because staying home felt worse. But the clinic, which had
always been a refuge, became a minefield of invisible triggers. A client's cologne—sharp,
chemical—would arrive before the person did, and suddenly Amir was back on that street, the smell
crowding out everything else. A dog's sudden lunge during an examination would send Amir's heart
into a panic that took hours to settle. Most difficult were the moments between patients, when Amir
would find themselves staring out the clinic window at the parking lot, scanning the figures moving
past, watching for the particular posture or gait of someone who meant harm. Dr. Patel, the senior
veterinarian, noticed the change. Amir had always been confident with animals, quick to make
decisions, willing to speak up in team meetings. Now Amir moved through the clinic like a ghost,
apologizing for minor mistakes, deferring to every suggestion, becoming almost invisible. When Dr.
Patel asked if everything was okay, Amir's throat closed. The words would not come—there was too
much, too tangled, and by the time they tried to explain, the thread had vanished and they were
standing there mute and ashamed.

The avoidance crept in gradually. First it was avoiding the street where the mugging happened, then
the entire neighbourhood after dark, then going out at all became something Amir had to steel
themselves for. Their partner, Jordan, would suggest dinner out or a walk through the park on a
Saturday afternoon, and Amir would feel a familiar tightness in their chest, a cascade of reasons
why it was not safe, why staying in was better. Jordan tried to be patient, but patience eroded over
months. By the second year, Jordan had stopped asking. Amir realized one evening that they had not
left the apartment except for work in six weeks, and even work required a elaborate mental
preparation—the same route, the same times, the same scanning of every shadow. At home, the
vigilance was worse. Amir had come to believe that safety was an illusion, that the mugging proved
you could never truly know what was coming. Even inside, locked doors and windows checked twice,
Amir felt the need to position themselves in rooms where they could see the entrance, to listen for
sounds in the hallway, to check behind them when moving through the apartment. Jordan had learned
not to come up behind Amir without announcing themselves first; the startle response was too
violent.

What frightened Amir most was the loss of themselves. They could remember being happy, being
confident, being someone who trusted the world enough to move through it freely. That person felt
like a character from someone else's life. The constant vigilance had become so normal that Amir
could no longer distinguish between caution and paranoia, between reasonable wariness and the
exhausting conviction that danger was always one moment away. Three months ago, Jordan gave Amir an
ultimatum—not cruel, but clear: get help or they could not stay. Amir realized they had become
someone Jordan could no longer reach, and more terrifyingly, someone Amir themselves no longer
recognized. It was that recognition, finally, that brought Amir to the clinic—not the veterinary
one, but this one, to sit across from a therapist and try, once again, to find the words for what
happened and what it had stolen.
