# Clinical Vignette: Aroha Parata

Aroha Parata, a fifty-one-year-old Samoan-New Zealand welder, was sitting in his truck outside the
fabrication shop on a Tuesday afternoon when he heard the squeal of tires from the street. His right
shoulder seized. His left hip, which had been fractured in the collision three years ago, suddenly
burned as if the impact were happening again—as if his body were being crushed against the door
frame in real time. The sound alone had transported him completely. For several minutes, he could
not move. When his coworker knocked on the window, Aroha's shirt was soaked through with sweat, and
he was breathing as though he had run a mile.

The accident itself had been sudden and total. Aroha had been driving home from a late shift at the
plant on a rain-slicked road when a sedan ran a red light directly into his driver's side. He
remembered the initial impact as a sound before anything else—a tremendous metallic crunch that
seemed to split the world open. His body had twisted violently; his left hip and ribs took the full
force of the collision. He had been conscious through all of it. The smell of alcohol had been
overwhelming—the other driver, a middle-aged man, had reeked of it as paramedics pulled them both
from the wreckage. That smell, the reek of whiskey mixed with sweat and gasoline, had imprinted
itself so completely that now, three years later, if someone at the shop opened a beer at lunch or
if he passed a bar on the street, his nervous system would spike into high alert.

In the months after the crash, Aroha had tried to return to his normal routine. But the world had
become a minefield of triggers. Any car accident he saw on the news—footage of twisted metal, of
vehicles colliding at angles—would send him into the same bodily state as the impact itself. His
wife, Talia, had noticed that he would flinch at sudden car sounds, that his breathing would become
shallow and rapid. What she could not see was what was happening inside his body: the sensation that
his hip was breaking again, that his ribs were caving inward, that he was trapped in the twisted
metal and could not get out. When these memories surfaced, they did not feel like memories at all.
They felt like the event was occurring right now, with complete sensory authenticity—the sound of
the impact, the smell of the other driver's breath and the gasoline, the physical sensation of his
body being wrenched out of shape.

Over time, Aroha had begun to avoid situations that might trigger these episodes. He stopped
watching the evening news. He took longer routes to work to avoid the intersection where the crash
had occurred. He started parking his truck at the far end of the lot at work, away from the street,
so that he would not have to hear the sound of traffic. When Talia suggested they go out to dinner,
he would decline, anxious about being in a vehicle or hearing car sounds through a restaurant
window. His drinking, which had been moderate before, had increased—not because he craved alcohol,
but because it was the only thing that seemed to quiet the hypervigilance, the constant scanning for
danger.

Six months ago, his foreman had pulled him aside. Aroha's work had become inconsistent. There were
days when he simply could not focus, when his body felt locked in a state of readiness for impact,
and he would make mistakes on the job—mistakes that in welding could be costly or even dangerous.
The foreman had suggested he might need to take time off. Instead, Aroha had cut back his hours and
taken on less complex jobs. The reduction in income had created tension with Talia. She wanted him
to seek help, but he had resisted, telling himself that it was just something he needed to push
through, that talking about it would only make it worse.

What finally brought Aroha to his first appointment was a moment two weeks ago when his grandson,
visiting from out of state, had playfully slammed a car door in the driveway. The sound had sent
Aroha into such a severe state of panic—his body rigid, his breathing chaotic, the full sensory
experience of the collision washing over him—that he had frightened the boy. Talia had found him in
the garage afterward, unable to speak. That night, she had made the call to the therapist herself.
Aroha had not argued. What he feared most now was that this would never end, that his body had
become permanently wired to interpret the world as dangerous, and that he was losing his wife and
his livelihood to something he could not control.
