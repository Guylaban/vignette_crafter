# Rajesh

Rajesh was sixty-eight years old when he came to his first appointment, though the exhaustion in his
face made him look older. He had worked for a nonprofit focused on housing advocacy for nearly two
decades, a career he had built carefully after his divorce fifteen years prior. He lived alone in a
modest apartment in Queens, had maintained a small circle of professional colleagues, and had
settled into what he believed was a manageable life. That was before the night that unmade him.

Three years ago, at sixty-five, Rajesh had stayed late at the office to finish grant applications.
It was a Tuesday in March. He left around nine in the evening, taking the subway home as he always
did. The attack happened in the stairwell of his apartment building—a man with a knife who forced
him into the dark space between the fourth and fifth floors. What followed was a violation so
complete and so sudden that Rajesh's mind seemed to fracture under the weight of it. The attacker
spoke very little, but the sounds he made—the breathing, the rustle of fabric, a low grunt—became
the soundtrack of Rajesh's terror. Afterward, Rajesh made it to his apartment, locked the door, and
called the police only after sitting motionless for nearly an hour.

In the weeks that followed, Rajesh noticed he could no longer tolerate being alone. The silence of
his apartment, once a refuge after long days at work, became suffocating. He began going to the
office earlier and staying later, lingering in the hallways even after everyone had left, unable to
face the moment when he would have to drive home and climb those stairs. When he did enter his
apartment, he found himself positioning his chair—the one where he ate dinner, where he tried to
watch television—so that his back pressed against the wall. From that vantage point, he could see
the entire room, the doorway, any potential threat. He could not explain this impulse to himself at
first; it simply felt necessary, the way breathing felt necessary.

The sounds haunted him. Every unexpected noise—a car horn, a door slamming in a neighboring unit,
the building's pipes settling—sent a jolt through his body that left him shaking. He had begun to
listen obsessively for sounds that might indicate someone entering his building, his apartment. At
work, where he had once been known for his calm presence and steady judgment during staff meetings,
Rajesh found himself tensing at the scrape of a chair, the sudden laugh of a colleague rounding a
corner. When his supervisor, Maria, had placed a hand on his shoulder during a tense budget
discussion, he had jerked away with such visible alarm that she had stepped back, confused and hurt.

The memories came in fragments that his mind could not quite stitch into language. He knew what had
happened—his body knew it acutely—but when he tried to tell the police detective, or later when he
attempted to speak to a therapist he saw once and never returned to, the words dissolved. What
remained instead were the sounds: that breathing, that grunt, the metallic jingle of a belt buckle.
When these auditory echoes returned, often without warning, they arrived alongside a sharp pain in
his lower abdomen and a burning sensation in his throat. His body was remembering what his words
could not articulate.

Over the months, Rajesh had come to believe that his inability to manage any of this—the
hypervigilance, the isolation, the way he flinched at touch, the nights he hurt himself by pressing
his fingernails into his palms until they bled, seeking the sharp clarity of physical pain as a way
to interrupt the overwhelming internal chaos—all of it proved he was a lousy coper. Other people
survived trauma. Other people moved forward. He was stuck, diminished, a man permanently altered
into something he no longer recognized. He was certain he would never feel joy or ease or the simple
comfort of human contact again. At work, he had become an outsider, a man people approached
carefully if at all, sensing something broken in him that they could not name.

What finally brought Rajesh to seek help was Maria. She had noticed his deterioration and, rather
than letting it pass, she had sat with him in his office and said simply that she cared about him
and that he deserved support. He had wept—something he had not allowed himself to do since the
attack—and had agreed to try again.
