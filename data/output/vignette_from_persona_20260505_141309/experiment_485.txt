# Rosa

Rosa was seventy-eight years old when she came to the clinic, her silver hair pulled back in a neat
bun, her hands folded in her lap. She had worked at the call center for twelve years—a modest job
that paid the bills and kept her occupied five days a week. She had been good at it, patient with
callers, quick with solutions. That was before. That was when she still believed she could protect
the people she loved.

On March 11th, 2004, Rosa had been at the Madrid train station to pick up her grandson Miguel, who
was coming home from university for the weekend. She was standing near the platform, watching the
arrivals board, when the first explosion came. She remembered the sound—a roar that seemed to come
from everywhere at once—and then the chaos that followed in fragments. A woman screaming. The acrid
smell of smoke. Her own body pressed against cold tile. After that, the memories became slippery,
like trying to hold water in her hands. She knew the bombs had been planted on the trains. She knew
people had died. She knew Miguel had been on one of them. But the precise sequence of those hours,
the moment she learned he was gone, the details of his face in those final days before—these things
existed in her mind as shapes without edges, shadows she could not quite touch.

For nearly two decades, Rosa had managed. She had moved to the United States with her daughter's
family, taken the call center job, built a routine. But routines, she had come to understand, were
fragile things. When her husband died five years ago, something shifted. She stopped going to the
neighborhood where they had lived together for forty years. She stopped attending the church
gatherings where people inevitably asked how she was managing, where someone would inevitably
mention loss—a child, a spouse, a friend. The weight of those conversations was unbearable. She had
no words for her own grief, no way to explain to anyone why a simple question about someone's health
could make her neck stiffen with pain so sharp it took her breath away.

At the call center, Rosa had begun to withdraw from her colleagues. She took her breaks alone, ate
her lunch at her desk. When conversations turned to family or to the news—and the news was always
full of tragedy now—she would excuse herself to the restroom. Three years ago, she had stopped
attending her daughter's birthday dinners. She could not bear to sit at a table with her family, to
see them happy, to feel the weight of her failure pressing down on her shoulders. She had failed to
protect Miguel. She had failed to be the grandmother her other grandchildren deserved. She had
failed at the one role that had ever truly mattered: being a mother, a protector. No amount of work,
no amount of routine, could change that.

The neck pain had become constant, a dull ache that flared into something sharper without warning.
Her doctor had found nothing wrong. It was, she believed with a certainty that frightened her, her
body's way of carrying what her mind could not speak. She had come to believe that she had no future
worth living, that nothing she did mattered because she could not stop bad things from happening.
The world was fundamentally unsafe, and she was powerless within it.

It was her daughter who finally insisted she seek help. Rosa had missed her granddaughter's
quinceañera—the last straw, her daughter said with tears in her eyes. Rosa sat in the clinic waiting
room, her hands trembling slightly, afraid of what speaking might do to her, afraid of the silence
that would follow. She did not know if words could fix what was broken. She only knew that she was
drowning, and that everyone around her was drowning too, and that she had no idea how to save
anyone, least of all herself.
