# Vinh's Vigilance

Vinh Nguyen was fifty-one when the hurricane came. He had lived in New Orleans for twenty-three
years—long enough to feel rooted, not long enough to forget his family's stories of displacement.
When Katrina's sister storm bore down on the Gulf Coast in late August, he and his wife Linh had
sheltered in place at their Uptown home, believing the levees would hold as they had before. They
did not.

The water came at dawn, silent and inexorable, filling the first floor within hours. Vinh had waded
through it to reach the attic, his chest-high in murk, unable to see his own hands. The smell was
everywhere—not just floodwater but the chemical reek of the refineries upstream, the stench of
sewage and petroleum and things he could not name. Linh had clung to him in the darkness, and he had
felt something shift in his body then, a knowledge arriving without words: he could not control
this. His strength meant nothing. The world could unmake itself without warning, and he would be
powerless to stop it.

They were rescued by boat that afternoon. Their home was destroyed. Their savings were depleted by
repairs and relocation. But it was something else—something quieter—that had not healed in the three
years since.

The first sign was small. A few weeks after they returned to a rebuilt house, Vinh developed a
persistent headache. It lasted for days. He found himself checking his blood pressure obsessively,
convinced that the stress of the flood had damaged his heart. When the headache finally faded, he
felt a strange relief mixed with dread, as though he had narrowly escaped something but knew it
could return at any moment. He had come to believe that he could not trust his own body anymore—that
it might betray him, that an illness could arrive without warning just as the water had. Every ache,
every moment of fatigue, triggered a cascade of fear: *Is this the beginning? Am I falling apart?*

At work, Vinh's vigilance had become exhausting. As a policy analyst for the state environmental
agency, he spent his days reviewing disaster preparedness protocols—a choice his supervisor had
assumed was cathartic, but which had become a trap. He found himself unable to focus on the actual
work because he was too busy monitoring the room itself. He sat at his desk and listened for weather
alerts on his phone. He noticed which colleagues seemed anxious, interpreting their moods as signs
that another disaster was imminent. He had begun arriving at the office early to check the emergency
exits, to know where the high ground was, to calculate which routes would be fastest if evacuation
became necessary. His colleagues had stopped asking him to lunch; Linh said he seemed distant, that
he was always somewhere else even when he was home.

The smell was the worst trigger. A neighbor's barbecue, a colleague's cigarette on the building's
loading dock, even the scent of Linh's cooking oil—anything that carried a hint of smoke or burning
sent him into a state of acute alarm. His heart would race. He would feel dizzy. The smell would
transport him instantly back to that attic, to the chemical stench of the flood, to the certainty
that disaster was unfolding. He had started avoiding restaurants, avoiding social gatherings,
avoiding anywhere that unpredictability might flourish. Linh wanted to visit her sister in Baton
Rouge, but Vinh could not bear the thought of being in an unfamiliar place where he did not know the
exits, where something unexpected might happen and he would be unable to manage it.

What frightened him most was not the fear itself but what the fear meant. His reactions had become
so extreme, so divorced from any present danger, that he had begun to wonder if he was losing his
mind. He would have a panic attack in response to a smell and afterward feel convinced that his mind
was fracturing, that the trauma had broken something fundamental in him that could not be repaired.
He was certain that he could no longer rely on himself to respond rationally, to function as he once
had. The man he had been before the water—steady, capable, grounded—felt like a stranger.

It was Linh who finally insisted he seek help. She had found him one evening sitting in the dark in
their bedroom, having convinced himself that a slight fever was the beginning of a serious illness,
unable to move or think his way out of the spiral. She held his hand and said, quietly, that she
needed her husband back. That was what brought Vinh to his first appointment: not the hope that he
would stop being afraid, but the desperate need to become someone his wife could recognize again.
