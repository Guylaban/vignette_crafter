# Amara's Story

Amara was twenty-three when she arrived in South Sudan as a volunteer nurse with an international
medical NGO. She had grown up in Lagos, moved to the United States for university, and carried with
her a clear sense of purpose: she would use her training to help people in places where the need was
most acute. The clinic where she worked sat on the outskirts of a town that had seen intermittent
violence but was, by the standards of the region, relatively stable. She shared a compound with four
other expatriate staff and two local nurses. The work was hard and the hours were long, but Amara
felt, for the first time in her life, that she was doing something that mattered.

On the afternoon of March 14th, armed men entered the clinic. There was no warning. One moment Amara
was changing a dressing on a child's infected wound; the next, she heard shouting in Arabic and
gunfire that seemed to come from everywhere at once. She dropped to the floor behind a supply
cabinet. A nurse beside her—Aisha, a woman Amara had trained with for six weeks—was struck in the
shoulder. Amara watched her collapse. She wanted to move, to help, but her body would not obey. The
gunfire continued for what felt like hours but was probably minutes. When it stopped, Amara remained
frozen, her hands shaking so violently she could not grip anything. Aisha was bleeding heavily.
Amara crawled toward her, finally, but by then armed men were moving through the clinic, and she
heard them speaking, heard boots on the tile floor. She pressed herself against the wall and did not
move again until soldiers arrived and the men fled.

Aisha survived. Amara was evacuated two days later.

That was two years ago. Since then, Amara has been living in Boston with her boyfriend, Marcus, and
working as a nurse in a busy urban hospital. On the surface, she has rebuilt her life. She completed
her contract remotely, returned to work, and has maintained her relationship. But the woman who came
home from South Sudan is not the woman who left.

The memories come without warning and with a quality of absolute presence. When she recalls the
moment she heard the first shots, her heart races as though the gunfire is happening now, in the
hospital corridor or in her apartment. She has learned that if she allows herself to fully
remember—to see Aisha's face, to feel the weight of her own paralysis—the emotion that floods
through her is so overwhelming that she must stop whatever she is doing. She will excuse herself
from a patient's room, go to the bathroom, and sit on the toilet with her eyes closed until the wave
passes. Sometimes it takes ten minutes. Sometimes longer. On those days, she feels wrung out,
depleted by a grief so profound and so specific that she cannot name its object. Is she grieving for
Aisha? For the person she was before? For the children in the clinic she did not help?

What frightens Amara most is not the memory itself but what it reveals about her. When she recalls
the gunfire, she is forced to confront the fact that she did nothing. She lay behind a cabinet while
Aisha bled. She tells herself, rationally, that she was in shock, that there was nothing she could
have done, but these explanations feel hollow. She has come to believe that she is fundamentally
inadequate—that a truly good nurse, a truly brave person, would have acted. There is something
broken in her, something that was exposed that day and cannot be repaired. She feels permanently
humiliated by her own cowardice, as though everyone who knows her would recognize this defect if
they truly understood what happened.

To manage this, Amara has developed habits that feel, in the moment, like survival. She does not
talk about what happened. When Marcus asks, gently, if she wants to discuss her time in South Sudan,
she changes the subject. She does not allow herself to think about it directly; instead, when
memories begin to surface, she deliberately shifts her attention elsewhere—she focuses intensely on
a task at work, or she drifts into a kind of daydream where she imagines herself somewhere else
entirely, someone else entirely. These strategies work, in a way. They keep her functional. They
allow her to get through her shifts.

But the cost has accumulated slowly. Marcus has stopped asking about her experience. He has also
stopped asking about her day, her feelings, or anything that might open a door to difficult
conversation. Their intimacy has narrowed. At work, Amara has begun to avoid certain situations—she
finds reasons not to work in the trauma bay, where the chaos and urgency can trigger a sense of
helplessness that feels too close to that afternoon in the clinic. Her supervisor has noticed that
she is less flexible with her scheduling, more rigid in her preferences. She has not been promoted,
and she knows, though no one has said it explicitly, that her colleagues see her as less committed
than she once was.

Three weeks ago, during a routine shift, a patient came in with a gunshot wound. As Amara approached
the bed, something in the sound of the monitors or the urgency in the room transported her
completely back to March 14th. She froze. A colleague had to take over. That night, Marcus found her
in the bathroom, unable to explain what was wrong. She sat on the edge of the tub and finally told
him some of what happened—not all of it, but enough that he understood something was seriously
wrong.

He encouraged her to seek help. Amara has made an appointment with a trauma therapist, though she is
terrified of what might happen if she actually speaks about that day out loud. She is most afraid
that saying the words will make the shame permanent, will confirm what she already believes: that
she is broken, that she failed, that there is something fundamentally wrong with her as a person.
But she is also beginning to hope that perhaps, with help, she might stop running from her own mind.
