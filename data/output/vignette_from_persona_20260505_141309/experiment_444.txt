# Priya's Vigil

Priya Sharma was seventy years old when she came to her first therapy appointment, her hands
trembling slightly as she set down her camera bag beside the chair. She had been a photographer for
forty-three years—weddings mostly, some commercial work, portraits of families she'd come to know
across decades in the Bay Area. But for the past eighteen months, she had barely picked up a camera.
The light still called to her; the geometry of a face in shadow still made her heart quicken. What
had changed was everything else.

The abuse had begun when Priya was four. Her father, a man whose name she still could not say
without her throat closing, had been violent in ways that childhood had not prepared her to
understand or name. The hitting came without warning. The shame came first—shame that she had done
something to cause it, shame that she could not stop it, shame that lived in her body like a
permanent resident. When she tried now to remember specific moments, the details fractured. She
could access the feeling—the terror, the helplessness, the certainty that she deserved what was
happening—but the images themselves seemed locked behind frosted glass. Sometimes this was a mercy.
Other times, the terror came anyway, flooding her nervous system as though no time had passed at
all, as though she were still small and trapped.

Her mother had known. This was the fact that had calcified into something harder than grief. Her
mother had known and had done nothing—had in fact often looked away, had sometimes even suggested
that Priya had provoked him. Priya's father had died in 1989. He had faced no consequences. He had
never apologized, never acknowledged what he had done. He had simply lived his life, and then he had
died, and Priya had been left alone with the weight of it.

She had married at twenty-two, divorced at forty-five. She had built a career. She had traveled. She
had made a life that, from the outside, looked like recovery. But the vigilance had never left her.
Even now, at seventy, Priya found herself monitoring every room she entered—where the exits were,
who was standing behind her, whether the people around her seemed angry or unpredictable. In her
studio, she had rearranged the space three times in the last two years, always positioning herself
near the door. Her assistant, Maria, had noticed. Maria had asked if she was okay. Priya had smiled
and changed the subject.

The face-hiding had started recently—unconsciously at first. When someone raised their voice in her
presence, even in jest, Priya's hands would come up to cover her face, as though she could erase
herself, make herself small enough to escape notice. She had done this once during a Zoom call with
a client. The client had thought she was crying. Priya had lied and said yes, allergies. She had
learned, over seven decades, how to lie smoothly.

What had undone her was a documentary she'd watched six months ago about childhood abuse survivors.
One woman on screen had described the shame of remembering, and Priya had felt something crack open
inside her chest. She had tried, that night, to sit with the memory deliberately—to look at it, to
understand it, to perhaps finally process it. But the moment she had closed her eyes and reached for
the images, panic had seized her. The terror had come back whole and alive, and she had gasped for
breath on her bedroom floor for twenty minutes. After that, she had stopped trying. She had stopped
going to her studio as much. She had stopped answering calls from old friends. She had begun to
think that perhaps she did not belong anywhere—not in crowds, not in her own home, not even in her
own skin.

It was her sister-in-law who had finally insisted. Priya had withdrawn from her brother's sixtieth
birthday party, and her sister-in-law had cornered her in the kitchen and said, quietly, "You are
still running from him. He is dead, and you are still running." The words had landed like a stone in
still water. Priya had sat in her car afterward for an hour, her hands on the steering wheel, and
then she had called.

What frightened her most now was the possibility that she had wasted the years she had left. What
she hoped for—though she barely dared to articulate it—was that she might finally look at what had
happened without being destroyed by it. That she might pick up her camera again. That she might,
somehow, belong to her own life.
