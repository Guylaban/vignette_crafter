# Clinical Vignette: Martin K.

Three years ago, Martin K. was driving home from a school board meeting on a Tuesday evening when a
pickup truck ran a red light and collided with the driver's side of his sedan. The impact came
without warning—a shattering explosion of metal and glass, the sensation of being hurled sideways,
the smell of burnt rubber and antifreeze. Martin's left arm broke in two places. He lost
consciousness briefly and woke to the sound of sirens, unable at first to understand where he was or
whether he was dying. The other driver walked away with minor injuries. Martin spent two nights in
hospital and six weeks in a sling.

He returned to work after the medical leave, determined to resume his life. But something had
fractured in him that the X-rays had not captured. Within weeks, he noticed that loud noises—a car
backfiring, a door slamming in the hallway, the screech of bus brakes in the school parking lot—sent
a jolt of pure panic through his chest. His heart would race. His hands would shake. He had come to
believe that these sounds were warnings, harbingers of the collision happening again. When he heard
shouting—a teacher raising her voice to a student, an angry parent on the phone—Martin's entire body
tensed. The sound seemed to contain the same violence as the impact itself, and he found himself
unable to stay in the room.

The memories themselves had taken on a quality that terrified him. He could not quite put the event
into words when he tried to describe it to his wife, Patricia. Instead, the crash played in his mind
like a film looping endlessly—the moment before impact, the shattering, the disorientation, again
and again. When these memories arrived, particularly in the dark of night, Martin did not feel as
though he were remembering something that had happened; he felt as though it were happening now, in
real time, his body responding with the full force of terror. He would lie awake, rigid, waiting for
sleep that would not come. Even on nights when exhaustion finally pulled him under, he would wake
gasping, drenched in sweat, his mind unable to distinguish between the memory and present danger.

His interpretation of the world had shifted in ways he could not quite explain to others. When
Patricia was late coming home from her book club, Martin did not think simply that traffic had
delayed her; he became certain that something terrible had occurred, that she had been in an
accident. When a staff meeting ran twenty minutes longer than scheduled, he interpreted the delay as
a sign that catastrophe was unfolding somewhere. He had come to believe that the world was
fundamentally dangerous, that the safety he had taken for granted before the collision had been an
illusion. And he had come to believe something darker still: that he was the kind of person who
should have seen the truck coming, who should have been more alert, more careful. Someone else would
not have gotten into this situation. He was changed now, permanently. He was not the man he had
been.

To manage the mounting distress, Martin had developed habits. He began avoiding the drive to school,
asking Patricia to drop him off and pick him up, which created tension in their marriage and
consumed her schedule. He forced himself to think only positive thoughts—a deliberate, exhausting
effort to suppress any acknowledgment of risk. And he had begun eating more, much more, using food
as a way to soothe the anxiety that never quite released its grip. His weight had increased by
thirty pounds. He could not relax even in his own home, in spaces he knew were safe; his vigilance
remained constant, his body perpetually braced.

Six months ago, the superintendent suggested that Martin consider stepping down from his position as
principal, noting that he seemed "distracted" and "not himself." The suggestion devastated him. This
year, after a particularly difficult morning when a fire alarm sent him into a panic state that left
him unable to function for hours, Martin finally called his doctor. He is here now, at sixty-seven,
frightened that he will never be the person he was, and hoping that someone might help him
understand why a moment of terrible chance has taken such permanent hold.
