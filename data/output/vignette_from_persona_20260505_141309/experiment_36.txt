# Margaret's Accident

Margaret Chen was sixty-five when the truck hit her Subaru on Route 9 in New Hampshire, but the
collision had the force of something that happened yesterday. She had been driving home from the VA
clinic where she'd worked as a medic for thirty-seven years—a career spent learning to move through
crisis with steady hands and a compartmentalized mind. The truck driver had run a red light. She
remembered the sound first: the shriek of tires, a noise that didn't belong to the world she knew.
Then the impact, the car folding inward, her body thrown sideways, her head striking the window hard
enough that for a moment the road tilted and wouldn't right itself.

The physical injuries healed within months. A fractured collarbone, three broken ribs, a concussion
that left her dizzy for six weeks. But in the year and a half since the accident, Margaret had come
to understand that something else had broken—something in the way her mind now moved through the
world. She had been trained to stay calm in trauma. She had held the hands of soldiers bleeding out
on gurneys. Yet now, at sixty-six, she could not drive past a silver Honda Civic without her chest
tightening, her breath shallowing. The truck that hit her had been silver. So had the one she saw at
the grocery store last Tuesday. And the sedan in her neighbor's driveway. The world had become
crowded with potential collisions.

What frightened Margaret most was the way the memory would ambush her. Not as a thought she could
dismiss, but as a complete reliving—her body jerking sideways, her hands gripping the steering wheel
that was no longer there, the smell of burning rubber and antifreeze filling her bedroom at three in
the morning. When it happened, she was not remembering the accident; she was inside it. The
distinction had become crucial to her, and also useless. Her therapist would later ask her to
describe what she experienced, and Margaret would open her mouth and find nothing there—the words
had scattered like the glass from her windshield, and she was left making helpless gestures, her
face flushed with frustration.

The overwhelm was the worst part. When the memory came, it brought such a flood of emotion—terror,
rage, a crushing sense of helplessness—that she had learned the only way through was to stop. To
pull herself out, to distract herself with something, anything. She had taken up baking in the
months after the accident, and then she had continued, long past the point of appetite. The kitchen
counters held cooling racks of cookies, loaves of banana bread, elaborate cakes she did not eat. Her
daughter, visiting from Boston, had gently suggested that Margaret seemed to be using food as a way
to manage something. Margaret had not disagreed. When the memory threatened, she would open the
refrigerator. The act of reaching for something, of consuming it, of focusing on the physical
sensation of sweetness or salt—it was a rope she could grab to pull herself back to the present.

But the present was not safe either. Her body had become a stranger to her, always braced, always
waiting. Loud noises—a car horn, a door slamming, the garbage truck on Thursday mornings—sent her
nervous system into a state of red alert. She would startle violently, her muscles locking, her
heart hammering. She had stopped going to the grocery store because the automatic doors made a sound
that resembled the screech of brakes. She had stopped driving anywhere but to necessary
appointments, and even then, her hands would shake on the wheel.

She had not told anyone at the VA clinic about the flashbacks, about the way she sometimes felt
unsafe in her own body. She was a medic; she was supposed to be the one who stayed steady. But three
weeks ago, she had sat in her car in the parking lot before her shift and realized she could not go
inside. The thought of being at work, of being responsible for someone else's crisis, felt
impossible. She had called in sick and driven home slowly, and that evening, she had finally called
the number her daughter had left on her refrigerator. Margaret was ready to admit that something had
broken, and that she did not know how to fix it alone.
