# Thea's Reckoning

Thea was thirty-seven when the police officer's baton came down on her shoulder outside the
community center in East Boston. She had been there for a Saturday outreach event, helping newly
arrived families from Myanmar and Cambodia navigate resettlement paperwork. A protest had swelled
nearby—something about immigration enforcement—and police had moved in to disperse the crowd. She
was not part of the protest. She was walking to her car when the line of officers advanced, and in
the chaos of bodies and shouting, someone shoved her forward into their path. The officer did not
ask her to move. He did not check if she was hurt. He swung, and she fell, her wrist bending beneath
her as she caught herself on the pavement. She remembered the specific weight of his boot near her
ribs, the choice he made not to step down, the way his face showed nothing. She remembered thinking:
*I am invisible to him. I am not a person he sees.*

That was sixteen months ago. In the immediate aftermath, Thea had filed a complaint. The police
department's internal investigation concluded that the officer had acted within protocol during
crowd control. No charges. No apology. No acknowledgment that she had been there doing work that
mattered. The injustice of it had calcified inside her, a stone she carried. What happened to her
was deeply unfair, and the system had confirmed it: nothing would be done. That certainty had become
part of how she understood the world now.

The triggers had emerged gradually, then with relentless consistency. Any police officer—standing on
a street corner, parked in a cruiser, visible through a storefront window—sent her nervous system
into high alert. Her body remembered before her mind caught up. Cyclists appeared without warning in
her peripheral vision, and the sudden motion registered as threat; she had learned later that the
protest had included cyclists weaving through crowds, and her brain had wired them into the danger
map. Crowded spaces became intolerable. The subway during rush hour, the grocery store on weekends,
even her own workplace when the waiting area filled with clients—all of it triggered a suffocating
sense of being trapped, of being invisible and vulnerable at once.

Thea had begun avoiding situations that might offer her peace. A friend invited her to a concert she
had once loved; Thea declined, sensing that happiness felt dangerous, like it belonged to someone
else now. She tried not to think about the event itself, pushing the memory away whenever it
surfaced, but the suppression required constant vigilance and left her exhausted. She discovered
that gambling—the bright screens, the velocity of the betting, the dopamine hit of a win—could
temporarily drown out the intrusive thoughts. She began visiting a casino in Connecticut on Thursday
evenings, telling her husband Marcus it was a work thing. It was not.

At night, sleep became a negotiation she usually lost. Her mind would not settle. When she did
sleep, she would jolt awake, her body flooded with the terror of the moment, as if the baton were
coming down again. On the rare mornings when she woke without that panic, the memory would find her
anyway during the day—the weight of it, the overwhelming cascade of grief and rage and
helplessness—and she would have to step away from whatever she was doing, sometimes leaving work
early, sometimes sitting in her car unable to move.

Her identity had fractured in ways she could not fully articulate. She had been someone who believed
in systems, in the possibility of justice, in her own competence and safety. That person no longer
existed. She looked at her colleagues—other aid workers who had experienced their own traumas—and
thought: *They would not have let this happen. They would have seen it coming. They would have known
what to do.* The comparison was constant and corrosive.

The irritability had damaged her marriage. Marcus would ask a simple question and she would snap at
him, her voice sharp with anger he had not provoked. She had taken risks that frightened him—driving
recklessly, walking alone in neighborhoods she knew were unsafe, as if daring something worse to
happen. He had finally told her, three weeks ago, that he could not watch her disappear. That was
what had brought her to the therapist's office: the fear that she was losing him too, that the
officer's baton had taken more than her sense of safety. It had taken her sense of who she was, and
she did not know how to find her way back.
