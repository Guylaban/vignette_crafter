# Kofi's Vigil

Kofi Mensah was forty-three years old when the earthquake struck Port-au-Prince on that January
morning in 2010. He had been there as part of a United Nations peacekeeping contingent, stationed in
the capital for what he understood would be a routine deployment. He was not a soldier but a bomb
disposal technician, trained to detect and neutralize explosive devices—work that demanded absolute
precision and a mind that could compartmentalize fear. The earthquake lasted forty-five seconds. The
building where he was sleeping collapsed in the first fifteen. He remembered the sound—a roar that
seemed to come from the earth itself, a grinding, tearing noise that his body registered as
catastrophe before his conscious mind could process what was happening. He was buried under concrete
and rebar for six hours. The unpredictability of it haunted him most: there had been no warning, no
pattern he could have learned to recognize, no way to prepare. One moment the world was solid; the
next, it was not.

Now, at fifty-five, Kofi had been living in Atlanta for fourteen years with his wife Ama, whom he
had married in 2015. He had returned to bomb disposal work with the same meticulous attention he had
always brought to it, but something fundamental had shifted in how his body inhabited the world.
Loud noises sent him into a state of high alert—car backfires, slamming doors, even the pneumatic
hiss of the truck's air brakes when he climbed into the cab for a shift would cause his chest to
tighten and his hands to tremble before he had any conscious thought about danger. He had come to
believe that his body was wiser than his mind, that it perceived threats his rational self could not
yet articulate. This conviction shaped everything he did.

The unpredictability of situations had become intolerable to him. He could not tolerate surprises at
work or at home. When Ama raised her voice—even in frustration about a household bill—Kofi's nervous
system ignited as if she were shouting a warning of imminent collapse. He would find himself braced,
waiting for the world to break apart again. To manage this constant state of vigilance, he began
keeping his mind perpetually occupied. At work, he listened to podcasts and audiobooks through his
earbuds even during quiet stretches. At home, he scrolled through his phone, watched television,
kept the radio on. Anything to prevent his mind from settling into the silence where memories might
surface.

The memories themselves were fragmented and intolerable. When fragments did break through—the weight
of concrete on his legs, the smell of dust and blood, the sound of people screaming—they came with a
rage so intense that he frightened himself. He would feel the event as if it were happening in the
present moment, his body in the rubble again, and the anger that followed was disproportionate and
terrifying. He had learned to push these memories away before they could fully form, to interrupt
them mid-recall, which left him with a sense that he was missing large pieces of what had happened,
as if his mind had deliberately erased portions of the trauma to protect him.

When the intrusions became unbearable, Kofi turned to food. He would eat late at night—large
quantities of rice and stew, bread, anything that would occupy his mouth and his attention. Over the
past two years, he had gained forty pounds. His supervisor had made a comment about his fitness for
the job. Ama had stopped inviting friends over because Kofi's state of constant vigilance made
gatherings unpredictable and tense. He avoided anything that might be stressful: he no longer
attended the community church he had once found grounding, he declined social invitations, he kept
his world as small and controlled as possible.

But last month, during a routine call to investigate a suspicious package at a shopping center, a
child's balloon had popped near him. His body had reacted before his mind caught up—he had thrown
himself to the ground, convinced the building was collapsing again. A colleague had found him there,
hyperventilating. That night, Kofi had sat in the dark of his bedroom and finally told Ama that he
needed help. He was terrified that he was losing his grip on reality, that his body's certainty
about danger was something his mind could no longer override. What he wanted most was to feel safe
in his own skin again, to trust that loud noises were just loud noises, and to remember what had
happened to him without the rage consuming everything.
