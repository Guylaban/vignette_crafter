# Clinical Vignette: Margaret Chen

Margaret Chen was forty-two when a delivery truck ran a red light and struck the passenger side of
her sedan. She had been driving home from a community outreach event—the kind of work she had done
for fifteen years as a social ministry worker, coordinating support services for low-income families
in the city's northeast parishes. The impact came without warning. One moment she was turning onto
Maple Street; the next, the truck's grille filled her peripheral vision and the world became noise
and metal and the sensation of her body no longer being her own. She was conscious through all of
it: conscious of the car folding around her, conscious of her arm pinned against the door frame,
conscious of not being able to move or stop what was happening to her.

The police report noted that the truck driver had not even attempted to brake. The report also noted
that Margaret had been driving at the posted speed limit, in her lane, with the light in her favor.
Nothing she had done caused the crash. Nothing she could have done would have prevented it. And yet,
in the months that followed, Margaret came to understand her body as fundamentally unreliable—not
because of her injuries, which were significant but ultimately healed, but because it had betrayed
her by being in that car at that moment, moving in that direction, vulnerable to forces entirely
beyond her control. She had come to believe that her own body could not be trusted to keep her safe,
that something essential inside her had broken on impact and never fully mended.

For the first three weeks after discharge, Margaret tried to return to work. She had been scheduled
to lead a workshop on accessing mental health resources. She sat in her office—a small converted
storage room in the basement of St. Catherine's Church—and found that her hands shook when she tried
to type. More troubling was the way her mind now worked: she could not relax, could not let her
guard down, because relaxation felt like surrender, like an invitation for something terrible to
happen. When she tried to sit quietly for even a few minutes, her chest would tighten and her
breathing would shallow. It was as though her body was telling her that safety required constant
vigilance, that any moment of peace was a moment when disaster could strike. So she stopped trying
to rest. She filled her evenings with tasks—organizing files, preparing materials, anything to
remain in motion, in control.

The avoidance came gradually. Within two months, Margaret had stopped driving altogether. She told
herself it was temporary, a reasonable precaution while she adjusted. But as weeks became months,
the thought of being in a vehicle—whether driving or as a passenger—generated such acute dread that
she simply could not do it. Her colleagues began to notice that she was no longer attending off-site
meetings. When they offered rides, she declined with vague excuses. The truth was that the image of
cars hitting other cars had become intrusive and unbidden; she would see it when she closed her
eyes, see it in the moment before she fell asleep. The idea of placing herself in a vehicle felt
like volunteering for re-traumatization.

Her work began to suffer. The social ministry position required occasional travel to partner
agencies across the city. By the nine-month mark, Margaret's supervisor had gently suggested that
her reduced availability was affecting her ability to fulfill her role. Rather than explain the true
source of her limitations—which felt shameful, inexplicable, as though she were being held hostage
by her own mind—Margaret requested a reduction to part-time hours. She worked from the office now,
coordinating by phone and email, her world contracting to the rooms she could reach on foot.

The hypervigilance had become a constant companion. She scanned for exits the moment she entered any
space, even familiar ones. She startled at ordinary sounds—a door slamming, a car backfiring in the
street outside, the unexpected ring of a telephone—her body launching into fight-or-flight before
her conscious mind could catch up. What frightened her most was the feeling that she could not trust
her own judgment about safety. Even when colleagues or her therapist from the initial crisis
counseling told her that she was safe, that the risk of another accident was statistically low, she
could not believe them. Her body held a different truth: that the world was dangerous, that she was
vulnerable, and that nothing—not statistics, not careful driving, not the reassurances of
others—could change that fundamental reality.

Now, at forty-eight, Margaret has been living with these constraints for six years. She has not
driven since the accident. She has not been a passenger in a car in almost as long. She still works
part-time, still helps people, but increasingly from behind a desk. She has not dated in years; the
thought of being dependent on someone else for transportation, or of explaining why she cannot go
anywhere, feels impossible. What brought her finally to seek help was a moment three weeks ago when
she realized she could not remember the last time she had felt safe—truly safe, not just the absence
of immediate danger, but a sense that her body and her world were trustworthy. She sat in her
apartment on a Tuesday evening and understood that she was no longer living a life; she was managing
a disaster that had already passed. She is hoping that someone can help her understand why her mind
will not let her move forward, why the unfairness of that single moment continues to hold her
captive, and whether it is possible to ever trust her own body again.

ever established. The unfairness of it—that she had done everything right and suffered everything,
while the person responsible had faced nothing—became a second injury layered over the first.

In the months that followed, Margaret came to understand her body as fundamentally unreliable—not
because of her injuries, which were significant but ultimately healed, but because it had betrayed
her by being in that car at that moment, moving in that direction, vulnerable to forces entirely
beyond her control. She had come to believe that her own body could not be trusted to keep her safe,
that something essential inside her had broken on impact and never fully mended. The injustice of it
gnawed at her constantly. She would lie awake at night thinking about the truck driver going about
his life, unchanged, unaccountable, while she remained imprisoned by what he had done. She had come
to believe that the world operated on a principle of radical unfairness, that doing everything right
offered no protection, and that nothing—not law, not justice, not the basic moral order she had
spent her career trying to uphold—could be relied upon. What frightened her most was the feeling
that she had been utterly helpless during those seconds of impact, unable to prevent what was
happening to her body, and that this same helplessness now governed her waking life. She could not
shake the conviction that bad things happened without warning and without reason, and that no amount
of caution or righteousness could stop them.

For the first three weeks after discharge, Margaret tried to return to work. She had been scheduled
to lead a workshop on accessing mental health resources. She sat in her office—a small converted
storage room in the basement of St. Catherine's Church—and found that her hands shook when she tried
to type. More troubling was the way her mind now worked: she could not relax, could not let her
guard down, because relaxation felt like surrender, like an invitation for something terrible to
happen. When she tried to sit quietly for even a few minutes, her chest would tighten and her
breathing would shallow. It was as though her body was telling her that safety required constant
vigilance, that any moment of peace was a moment when disaster could strike. So she stopped trying
to rest. She filled her evenings with tasks—organizing files, preparing materials, anything to
remain in motion, in control. She began to avoid situations that placed physical stress on her body.
She stopped attending the weekly staff volleyball games that she had enjoyed for years, telling
colleagues she was still recovering from her injuries. She declined invitations to help move boxes
during the parish's annual supply inventory, citing lingering pain. She avoided the stairs in the
church building when she could, taking the longer route through the hallway to avoid the exertion
that made her heart race and her breathing quicken. What she could not articulate—what she barely
understood herself—was that any physical exertion, any quickening of her pulse or sensation of her
body straining, felt indistinguishable from the moment of impact. Her body in motion, under stress,
vulnerable and taxed, was the body that had been crushed. So she kept herself still, kept her heart
rate low, kept physical demands at a minimum. It was safer that way. She also found that images of
the crash—the truck's grille, the metal folding, the sensation of her body pinned and helpless—would
surface unbidden throughout her day, especially when she felt ill or physically compromised. A bout
of stomach flu in month two sent her into a spiral of intrusive imagery; the feeling of her body
being out of her control, even in that minor way, triggered the full sensory memory of the accident.

The avoidance came gradually. Within two months, Margaret had stopped driving altogether. She told
herself it was temporary, a reasonable precaution while she adjusted. But as weeks became months,
the thought of being in a vehicle—whether driving or as a passenger—generated such acute dread that
she simply could not do it. Her colleagues began to notice that she was no longer attending off-site
meetings. When they offered rides, she declined with vague excuses. The truth was that the image of
cars hitting other cars had become intrusive and unbidden; she would see it when she closed her
eyes, see it in the moment before she fell asleep. The idea of placing herself in a vehicle felt
like volunteering for re-traumatization. Her work began to suffer. The social ministry position
required occasional travel to partner agencies across the city. By the nine-month mark, Margaret's
supervisor had gently suggested that her reduced availability was affecting her ability to fulfill
her role. Rather than explain the true source of her limitations—which felt shameful, inexplicable,
as though she were being held hostage by her own mind—Margaret requested a reduction to part-time
hours. She worked from the office now, coordinating by phone and email, her world contracting to the
rooms she could reach on foot.

The hypervigilance had become a constant companion. She scanned for exits the moment she entered any
space, even familiar ones. She startled at ordinary sounds—a door slamming, a car backfiring in the
street outside, the unexpected ring of a telephone—her body launching into fight-or-flight before
her conscious mind could catch up. What frightened her most was the feeling that she could not trust
her own judgment about safety. Even when colleagues or her therapist from the initial crisis
counseling told her that she was safe, that the risk of another accident was statistically low, she
could not believe them. Her body held a different truth: that the world was dangerous, that she was
vulnerable, and that nothing—not statistics, not careful driving, not the reassurances of others,
not even basic justice—could change that fundamental reality. Now, at forty-eight, Margaret has been
living with these constraints for six years. She has not driven since the accident. She has not been
a passenger in a car in almost as long. She still works part-time, still helps people, but
increasingly from behind a desk. She has not dated in years; the thought of being dependent on
someone else for transportation, or of explaining why she cannot go anywhere, feels impossible. What
brought her finally to seek help was a moment three weeks ago when she realized she could not
remember the last time she had felt safe—truly safe, not just the absence of immediate danger, but a
sense that her body and her world were trustworthy. She sat in her apartment on a Tuesday evening
and understood that she was no longer living a life; she was managing a disaster that had already
passed. She is hoping that someone can help her understand why her mind will not let her move
forward, why the unfairness of that single moment—the unfairness that was never answered, never
resolved, never made right—continues to hold her captive, and whether it is possible to ever trust
her own body or the world again.

VIGNETTE (final)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Margaret Chen is forty-eight years old now, though she sometimes has to pause and count backward to
confirm it. Six years ago, when she was forty-two, a delivery truck ran a red light and struck the
passenger side of her sedan while she was driving home from a community outreach event—the kind of
work she had done for fifteen years as a social ministry worker, coordinating support services for
low-income families in the city's northeast parishes. She had been turning onto Maple Street when
the truck's grille filled her peripheral vision and the world became noise and metal and the
sensation of her body no longer being her own. She was conscious through all of it: conscious of the
car folding around her, conscious of her arm pinned against the door frame, conscious of not being
able to move or stop what was happening to her. The police report noted that the truck driver had
not even attempted to brake. The report also noted that Margaret had been driving at the posted
speed limit, in her lane, with the light in her favor. Nothing she had done caused the crash.
Nothing she could have done would have prevented it. Yet when she learned that the driver had
received only a citation for running the red light—no criminal charges, no meaningful
consequence—something inside her calcified. The citation was later dismissed on a technicality. The
driver's insurance company settled with hers, a transaction that felt to Margaret like the
