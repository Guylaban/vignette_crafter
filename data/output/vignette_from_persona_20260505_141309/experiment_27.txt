# Clinical Vignette: Karim

Karim was thirty-eight years old when the siege began. He had been dancing professionally for twenty
years by then—contemporary and traditional forms, teaching at a studio in the old quarter of Aleppo,
performing at weddings and cultural festivals throughout the city. His wife, Lena, was a textile
artist. They had no children but maintained a close circle of extended family and students who
filled their days with purpose and, he had believed then, with meaning.

The bombardment started on a Tuesday in October. Karim was leading a morning class when the first
sound came—not quite an explosion at first, but a deep percussion that seemed to come from the earth
itself, followed by the high whine of falling ordnance. The studio was on the third floor of a
converted warehouse. Students scattered. Karim herded them toward the stairwell, but the second
strike hit the building next door. The concussive wave threw him against the wall. He remembered the
smell most vividly afterward: not smoke, but something earthier—pulverized concrete and soil, the
smell of the ground being turned inside out. When he emerged onto the street, the air was thick with
it, a fine dust that coated his throat and lungs, that would later fill his nightmares.

He found Lena at their apartment. They sheltered together for three days before deciding to leave.
The journey out of the city took them through neighborhoods Karim had known his entire life, now
unrecognizable—buildings collapsed into themselves, streets cratered, the constant smell of
disturbed earth and ash. At a checkpoint, a young soldier with a face Karim could not quite see
asked their names. When Karim said his own name aloud, something in the soldier's posture shifted.
Later, Karim would come to believe the soldier had recognized it as Arab, as potentially suspect,
though nothing happened. They were allowed through. But that moment—the speaking of his name into
that atmosphere of violence and suspicion—lodged itself in him like shrapnel.

They made it to Turkey, then eventually to the United States. For the first year, Karim tried to
rebuild. He found work at a dance studio in a mid-sized city, teaching evening classes. But
something had fundamentally changed in how he inhabited his own body and the world around it. Sounds
that resembled the bombardment—a truck backfiring, construction equipment, even the sharp slam of a
heavy door—sent him into a state of acute alarm. His body would brace before his mind caught up.
More troubling was the smell of soil, particularly in spring when the earth warmed and released its
mineral scent. A walk through a park became impossible. Even the smell of potted plants in the
studio lobby could trigger a cascade of dread.

When he heard his own name spoken by a new student—another Karim, a young man in his twenties—Karim
felt his chest tighten with an inexplicable panic. He had come to associate the utterance of his
name with that moment of exposure at the checkpoint, with the possibility of being marked,
identified, found wanting or dangerous. He began asking students to call him by his surname instead,
an awkwardness he never fully explained.

Over the following months, Karim's world contracted. He stopped taking the evening walks he and Lena
had established. He avoided the park near their home. He became hypervigilant about sounds,
sometimes leaving the studio early if renovations were happening in an adjacent space. At work, he
found himself struggling to demonstrate combinations he had performed thousands of times. His
movements had become guarded, tense. Several students commented that his teaching felt
different—less fluid, more mechanical. Two of his senior students, whom he had been mentoring toward
performance opportunities, eventually stopped coming. He did not reach out to ask why.

Lena watched this erosion with increasing concern. She tried to talk to him about returning to Syria
someday, about reconnecting with faith as a source of meaning, but Karim found he could not access
those conversations. The Syria he had known was gone. The God he had once believed in had allowed
the earth itself to be weaponized. He felt dead inside—not suicidal, but absent, as though the
person who had danced and taught and loved was observing himself from a distance, going through the
motions of a life that no longer quite belonged to him.

What finally brought him to seek help was Lena's ultimatum, delivered not in anger but in
exhaustion: either they would find a way forward together, or she could not stay. Karim, sitting in
the intake session with his hands folded in his lap, said he was most afraid that if he began to
feel again, the full weight of what he had lost would crush him. But he was also afraid, he admitted
quietly, that he might already be too far gone—that others could see the damage in him, that there
was no returning to the person he had been, and that perhaps that was something he deserved.
