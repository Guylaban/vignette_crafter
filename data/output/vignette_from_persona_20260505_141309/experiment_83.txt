# Rosa's Story

Rosa María Delgado had been driving the number 7 crosstown route for twenty-three years without
incident. She knew the stops by heart, the rhythm of the brakes, the particular slant of afternoon
light through the windshield on Thursdays. That knowledge had been a kind of anchor—something solid
in a life that had given her much but demanded everything. On a Tuesday in March, six months ago,
that anchor was torn away.

It happened at the intersection of Fifth and Morrison, a place Rosa had passed through thousands of
times. A sedan ran the red light at high speed, and the city transit bus in the lane beside
hers—driven by her friend and occasional lunch companion, Miguel—was struck broadside. Rosa saw it
happen in the space of a breath: the impact, the terrible crumpling of metal, the way the bus
lurched and seemed to hang suspended for an impossible moment. Miguel was killed instantly. Three
passengers were seriously injured. Rosa had been close enough to see blood on the sidewalk
afterward, close enough to hear the screaming, close enough to know that if the geometry of that
intersection had been even slightly different, it could have been her bus, her body twisted in that
wreckage.

The first weeks were a blur of police statements and memorial services. But as time passed,
something darker took hold. Rosa found herself unable to sit in the driver's seat without her hands
trembling. The sight of any vehicle moving unpredictably—a car changing lanes, a truck turning—would
send her heart into her throat. She began to develop small rituals: counting to seven before
accelerating, tapping the steering wheel three times at each stop, reciting a prayer under her
breath in a particular sequence. She was certain, though she could not fully articulate why, that if
she performed these actions with absolute precision, nothing bad would happen again. If she failed
to complete them, if her mind wandered even once, she was convinced that disaster would follow. The
logic was not rational, but it felt like the only thing standing between safety and catastrophe.

At home, her husband Carlos noticed the change first. When he reached to touch her shoulder one
evening, Rosa flinched violently, her body recoiling as though burned. She could not bear to be
held, could not tolerate his hand on her back or his arm around her waist. The physical contact that
had once been a comfort now felt unbearable—a violation of the fragile boundary she had erected
around herself. Carlos withdrew, confused and hurt. When Rosa's sister called to invite her to a
family dinner, Rosa declined. She could not face the gathering, could not sit in the house where
Miguel had once laughed and eaten tamales, could not bear the weight of memories that had become
intolerable.

The worst moments came without warning. While she was on her route, or lying in bed, or standing in
the kitchen, the memory would crash over her with the force of the actual collision. She would feel
the impact in her body—a jolt, a sensation of falling. She would smell the burnt rubber and see the
twisted metal and hear the screaming, all at once, so real that she could not distinguish it from
the present moment. Other times, the sensory details would fade but the terror would remain, a pure,
undiluted emotional intensity with no image attached, just a crushing weight in her chest and the
absolute certainty that she was in mortal danger.

What tormented Rosa most was the knowledge that she had done nothing. She had been there, alive and
unharmed, while Miguel died. She replayed that moment obsessively, imagining actions she could have
taken—warning him somehow, pulling him to safety, anything—but she had simply sat in her bus and
watched. She had come to believe that she was fundamentally culpable, that her failure to act was a
betrayal of the most profound kind. The thought that followed her everywhere was darker still: that
perhaps she did not deserve to survive what Miguel had not. That perhaps her life, measured against
what she had failed to do, was not worth the space it occupied.

It was Carlos who finally insisted she seek help, after finding her sitting in the dark one evening,
unable to explain what she was doing. Rosa came to her first appointment reluctant and afraid, but
carrying with her the desperate hope that someone might tell her how to stop being haunted, how to
forgive herself, and whether there was any reason left to try.
