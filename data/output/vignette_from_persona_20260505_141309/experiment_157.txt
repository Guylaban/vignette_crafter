# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was thirty-four when his fiancée—a woman he had known since college, whom he had trusted
completely—broke three of his ribs and fractured his left orbital bone during an argument in their
shared apartment. He had come home late from a shift, and she had been drinking. He remembered the
smell of whiskey on her breath as she confronted him about his phone, remembered her voice rising,
then the first blow coming from an angle he did not anticipate. What struck him most, in the months
afterward, was not the physical pain but the realization that he had not seen it coming. He was a
detective. He read people for a living. He had missed everything.

Marcus was now twenty-six and had been married to someone new for eighteen months—a careful, gentle
marriage built on what he believed was mutual understanding. Yet the person he had become bore
little resemblance to the man his wife had married. He moved through their home like a ghost,
present in body but absent in every way that mattered. When she reached for his hand during a movie,
he would stiffen and pull away, offering some excuse about his shoulder being sore. When she tried
to massage his neck, he felt his throat tighten with something close to panic. Any touch, no matter
how tender its intention, registered as a threat his body could not ignore.

The nausea came without warning. A colleague would mention grabbing drinks after work, and the smell
of beer on someone's clothes would hit him as he passed their desk, and suddenly his stomach would
turn. He would excuse himself to the bathroom, gripping the sink, waiting for the sensation to pass.
He had come to avoid the break room entirely, had stopped attending departmental happy hours years
ago. His captain had stopped asking.

But worse than the nausea were the moments when he would see something that made his mind travel
backward. A suspect with a black eye. A domestic violence victim with bruises blooming across her
shoulders. These images would lodge in his consciousness and pull up something older—not just a
memory, but a full sensory reconstruction. He would hear the sound of her voice, sharp and slurred,
the specific timbre of rage. The memory was not vague or distant; it was vivid enough that he could
feel the impact again, could taste blood in his mouth. These intrusions came without permission,
forcing themselves into his mind while he was trying to write a report or interview a witness. He
had learned to excuse himself, to find a bathroom or an empty office, to wait until the episode
passed.

What had calcified over time was something deeper than fear. Marcus had come to believe that people
were fundamentally unknowable, that the version of someone you saw was always a performance, a
carefully constructed lie. His wife loved him, he supposed, but he did not trust what love meant
anymore. He had loved his fiancée. He had been completely wrong. This certainty—that he had been
fundamentally foolish, that his judgment was broken, that he could never truly know another
person—had become the lens through which he viewed all relationships. He felt like an outsider in
his own marriage, a man who had learned a terrible truth that others had not yet discovered.

He had stopped trying to relax. The moment he felt his body begin to soften, some part of him would
panic. Relaxation felt like vulnerability, like lowering his guard, and lowering his guard was how
he had gotten hurt. Instead, he numbed himself methodically—not with alcohol, which he could no
longer bear the smell of, but with work, with distance, with the careful construction of emotional
walls. His wife had stopped trying to reach him. She had stopped asking what was wrong.

Marcus came to therapy because his wife had given him an ultimatum, delivered quietly and without
anger: either he got help, or she would leave. He sat in the first session with his hands clenched,
his jaw tight, afraid that talking about what happened would somehow make it more real, more
present. What he feared most was that nothing would change, that he would remain permanently
fractured, unable to trust, unable to let anyone close. What he hoped for, though he could barely
articulate it, was to feel safe in his own skin again, and to understand how that was even possible.
