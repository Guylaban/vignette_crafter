# Rabbi David's Vigil

David Rothstein was sixty-three years old and had spent the last four decades trying to outrun a
morning in 1981 that he could never quite leave behind. He had been nineteen then, living in a
refugee transit camp on the Turkish-Syrian border with his parents and younger sister after their
family fled Lebanon during the civil war. The camp was a sprawl of canvas tents and corrugated metal
shelters, perpetually loud with the voices of displaced families and the grinding work of survival.
On that particular morning, a dispute over water rations had erupted into violence. David had been
standing near the supply tent when a man—someone he had seen around the camp but never spoken
to—picked up a rusted metal pipe and began swinging it at another refugee. The chaos that followed
was brief but absolute. David had frozen, watching the pipe arc through the air, watching it
connect, hearing the sounds he still heard in his sleep. When someone grabbed his arm to pull him
away, he had screamed. He had not stopped screaming for hours.

That was more than forty years ago. David had rebuilt a life. He had emigrated to America, studied
at rabbinical seminary, married—though his marriage had dissolved after fifteen years—and eventually
found his calling as a rabbi in a mid-sized conservative congregation in the Midwest. He had built a
reputation as a thoughtful, compassionate spiritual leader. But the morning at the camp had never
truly receded. It had simply learned to hide in the architecture of his daily existence.

Now, in his current relationship of eight years with Michael, a retired teacher, David found himself
caught in a peculiar prison of his own making. Michael was affectionate by nature—he would reach
over during dinner to touch David's shoulder, or place a hand on his back as they moved through
their home. Each time, David's entire body would jolt as if electrified. His heart would race, his
breathing would shallow, and for a moment—sometimes longer—he would feel as though that pipe was
still falling through the air. He had come to dread these moments of touch, even though Michael's
hands carried nothing but tenderness. What frightened David most was that his own body had become
unreliable to him, a traitor that refused to accept the safety his mind could confirm. He had begun,
without fully acknowledging it, to create distance. He would sit farther away on the couch. He would
angle his body away from Michael in bed. When Michael tried to hold him, David would find a reason
to move—to get water, to check something on his phone, to stand and straighten a picture frame that
did not need straightening. Michael had stopped trying. The silence between them had grown
comfortable in the way that resignation makes things comfortable.

At the synagogue, David had become meticulous about where he sat during services. He always
positioned himself with a clear view of the exits and with his back to a wall—the same instinct that
had kept him alive in the camp now kept him isolated in the pulpit. He had also begun to struggle
with concentration during study and prayer, his mind fracturing at unexpected moments, unable to
hold a thought or a passage for more than a few seconds. He had attributed this to age, to the
simple wearing down of a mind that had carried too much for too long.

What had finally brought David to seek help was not a single moment but an accumulation. Three
months ago, a young man in the congregation—a teenager with a theatrical bent—had raised his voice
in playful jest during a youth group meeting, mimicking an actor in some film. The shouting had sent
David into a state of panic so acute that he had to excuse himself and sit alone in his office for
an hour, his hands shaking, convinced that something terrible was about to happen. The incident had
shaken him. More than that, it had shown him clearly that he could not continue as he was. He had
come to believe that he deserved this suffering, that what had happened in the camp was somehow his
fault—that he should have done something, that someone braver, someone more capable, would have
stopped the violence. And yet he also knew, with a clarity that cut against his self-condemnation,
that he could not rely on himself to manage this alone anymore. He had finally called a therapist
who specialized in trauma. What he hoped for, though he barely dared to articulate it, was to feel
safe in his own body again, and to be able to receive love without flinching.
