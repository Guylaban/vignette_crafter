# Layla

Layla was thirty-one when she arrived at the clinic on a Tuesday afternoon, her hands trembling as
she filled out the intake form. She worked as a social media manager for a mid-sized marketing firm,
a job she had held for four years before the accident, though she was not sure she would hold it
much longer. Three years had passed since the evening that had fractured her life, and in that time
she had come to believe that she was no longer the person she had been—that something fundamental
inside her had broken and would never mend.

The accident had occurred on a summer evening in 2021, when Layla was driving her younger cousin
Yasmin home from a family gathering. Yasmin was sixteen, sitting in the passenger seat, singing
along to music on the radio. Layla had been thinking about her marriage, which was deteriorating,
and her attention had drifted from the road. A child's ball had rolled into the street from between
two parked cars. Layla had swerved, overcorrected, and struck a cyclist coming the opposite
direction. The sound of impact—a dull, terrible crunch—had been followed by silence, and then
screaming. The smell of hot asphalt mixed with something metallic and organic that she could never
quite name but would never forget. The cyclist, a man in his sixties, had sustained a compound
fracture of his left leg and a traumatic brain injury. He had spent six weeks in intensive care. He
had recovered, but not fully. He walked with a cane now and had lost some of his memory.

Layla had not been charged criminally—the police investigation concluded it was an accident—but the
internal verdict she had rendered was far harsher. In the months that followed, she became convinced
that she could not be trusted, that some essential capacity for judgment or care had been absent in
her that night and would remain absent. She had come to see herself as someone fundamentally unsafe,
someone whose presence in the world posed a risk to others. This belief had spread like a stain
across her understanding of everything she did.

At work, she had begun to avoid making decisions. Her role required her to develop social media
strategies, to recommend content, to guide clients toward certain choices. But Layla found herself
paralyzed by the conviction that whatever she suggested would be wrong, that her judgment was
corrupted. She would sit at her desk for hours, staring at her screen, unable to commit to a
recommendation. Her manager had begun to notice. There had been a performance review six months ago
in which he had asked if everything was all right. She had said it was, but she was no longer sure
how much longer she could sustain the pretense.

Her body had become a source of constant alarm. When a colleague leaned over her shoulder to look at
her monitor, Layla would flinch violently, her heart racing as if she had been struck. When her ex-
husband had called last month to discuss the divorce settlement, she had misheard something he said
and had convinced herself, for a panicked moment, that Yasmin had been in an accident. These false
alarms came frequently now—a car horn would sound, and Layla would be certain something terrible was
happening; a notification on her phone would appear, and she would brace for catastrophic news.

What was most disturbing to her was the quality of her memories. Sometimes she could recall the
exact sequence of events—the ball, the swerve, the impact—with crystalline clarity, but the memory
felt as though it belonged to someone else, a story she had been told rather than something she had
lived. Other times, she knew it had happened, knew it intimately, but could not find the words to
describe it. When her therapist in those early months had asked her to talk about that night, Layla
had opened her mouth and found nothing there—no language, no narrative, only a kind of blank horror.

Three years of this had worn her down. She was sleeping poorly, eating little, and had withdrawn
from nearly all of her friendships. Her mother called regularly from the old neighborhood, worried,
but Layla could not bear to hear the concern in her voice. She had come to therapy now because her
doctor had insisted, because she could feel herself slipping further away from the person she had
once been, and because some small, desperate part of her wondered if there was any way back.
