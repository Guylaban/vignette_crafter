# Kofi's Vigil

Kofi was twenty-five when the officers stopped him on Nostrand Avenue in Brooklyn on a March
evening. He had been working as a freelance journalist for eighteen months, building a reputation
covering community policing and housing inequality in neighborhoods where he had grown up. That
night, he was returning from an interview in Brownsville, his camera bag slung across his chest, his
phone still recording audio notes. The officers said later they were responding to reports of a
robbery in the area. Kofi was the right height, the right build, the right color in the wrong place
at the wrong moment.

What happened over the next forty minutes existed in his memory now as a series of hyperreal,
fractured images: the sudden shouting, the hands on his jacket, the officer's face inches from his
own, the sound of his camera hitting the pavement. He remembered the weight of the officer's knee,
the taste of concrete, the officer's radio crackling with static and voices. He remembered the exact
pitch of the dispatcher's voice coming through the speaker clipped to the officer's shoulder—a
particular timbre that his brain had apparently recorded with photographic precision. He had been
released without charges. The investigation found no wrongdoing.

In the weeks that followed, Kofi returned to work, but something fundamental had shifted. He began
wearing larger, darker clothes—hoodies even in warmer weather, oversized jackets that made him less
visible, less of a shape that could be mistaken for anything. At the newspaper's offices in Midtown,
he found himself unable to concentrate on assignments. His editor had asked him to write a piece on
stop-and-frisk patterns, a topic he had been eager to pursue, but when he sat at his desk the words
dissolved. He could not finish sentences. The narrative fragments scattered. He realized he was no
longer the person who had wanted to tell these stories.

The footsteps became the worst part. In the hallways of the office building, on the subway platform,
on the street outside his apartment—any footsteps behind him triggered an immediate, visceral
response. He would turn, scan, try to assess the threat. His girlfriend, Maya, had mentioned it
after he'd done it three times during a walk to dinner. He had not realized he was doing it. Now he
was acutely aware of it, unable to stop, certain that the moment he stopped checking was the moment
something would happen again.

Certain songs would arrive without warning on his Spotify mix or in a bodega, and suddenly he was
back on Nostrand Avenue—the memory more vivid and present than his current surroundings, the
officer's voice not just remembered but heard, playing inside his head with startling clarity. He
had come to avoid music almost entirely, deleting playlists, asking friends not to play anything
when he was around. But this created a strange isolation. He had loved music. He had been someone
who could describe a song and make people understand why it mattered. Now he was someone who
flinched at sound.

He began declining invitations. Unfamiliar places felt unmanageable—he could not map the exits,
could not predict where danger might come from. His world contracted. He avoided his mother's church
because it reminded him of his uncle, who had been killed by police in 2015, and grief was something
he could not afford to feel. He avoided his favorite coffee shop because the owner had asked him
about his assignment, and the conversation had nearly broken him open. He avoided positive feelings
because they felt dangerous, a betrayal of something, or a setup for loss. He had become someone who
moved through the world trying not to feel anything at all.

By late autumn, seven months after the stop, Maya had stopped asking when he would be ready to talk
about it. His editor had stopped assigning him stories. He had come to believe that his life had
been fundamentally destroyed, that he was no longer capable of being a journalist or a partner or
the person he had been. The hypervigilance, the avoidance, the way his own memories had become more
real than his present moment—all of it had accumulated into a conviction that he would never be okay
again.

It was Maya who finally insisted he see someone. She had found a therapist who worked with trauma.
Kofi had resisted, certain that talking would only make things worse, that it would confirm what he
already knew about himself. But he was exhausted. He was losing everything. He called the office and
made an appointment, and for the first time since March, he allowed himself to hope that something
might change.
