# Priya's Vigil

Priya was sixty-one when she came to the clinic, her appointment card held in hands that trembled
slightly as she sat in the waiting room. She had worked the orchards and fields around the Central
Valley for nearly forty years, and her body bore the marks of that labor—weathered skin, a permanent
stoop in her posture, fingers stiffened by repetitive motion. But it was something else that had
brought her here now, something she had carried silently through decades and could no longer manage
alone.

The assault had happened in the autumn of 1998, on a late afternoon when she was alone in the
storage shed behind the farm where she worked. A man had followed her in—someone she recognized from
the neighboring property—and what happened in those minutes had fractured something fundamental in
how she understood her own body and the world around it. He had held her down. She remembered his
weight, the smell of his sweat and tobacco, the way her voice had seemed to come from someone else
entirely when she tried to scream. When he finally left, she had sat in the dust for an hour, unable
to move, unable to fully believe what had just occurred.

For the first few years, Priya had tried to continue as though nothing had changed. She returned to
work. She did not report the assault—she was undocumented then, afraid of losing her job, afraid of
not being believed. She kept the secret even from her husband, Rajesh, though he sensed something
had shifted in her. She had begun to flinch when he reached for her in bed. She had stopped letting
him hold her from behind.

By her mid-fifties, the symptoms had become impossible to hide. The intrusive memories arrived
without warning, with a vividness that obliterated the present moment. A dog barking in a neighbor's
yard would trigger the full sensory replay—she would be back in the shed, the memory playing like a
film loop, each frame as immediate and terrifying as the original moment. Her body would react
before her mind had time to catch up; her heart would race, her breath would shallow, and she would
feel the phantom pressure of hands on her wrists. When this happened at work, in the fields or at
the packing station, she would have to excuse herself, sometimes spending twenty minutes in the
bathroom stall trying to convince herself that she was safe, that it was 2024, not 1998.

Sleep had become a battleground. Priya lay awake most nights, her body rigid beside Rajesh, her
attention fixed on the sounds of the house—the creak of the floorboards, the wind against the
windows. She could not afford to sleep deeply. Danger, she had come to believe, required constant
vigilance. If she let her guard down, something terrible would happen. The irrationality of this
belief did not diminish its grip on her.

Intimacy with Rajesh had become nearly impossible. When he touched her unexpectedly—reaching for her
hand while she stood at the stove, placing a hand on her shoulder as he passed—she would startle
violently, her whole body going rigid with alarm before she could recognize that it was him. The
look of hurt in his eyes, repeated over and over across the years, had become its own form of pain.
She loved him. She wanted to be the woman he had married. But her body no longer trusted touch, and
no amount of conscious reassurance could override the alarm system that had been rewired inside her.

At sixty-one, Priya had finally told Rajesh the truth. She had cried as she spoke, and he had held
her—carefully, aware now of how her body responded to his closeness. He had asked why she had never
sought help, and she had not known how to answer. There was shame. There was the fear that speaking
about it would make it more real. But mostly there was the exhaustion of carrying it alone, the
sense that her body had become a hostile place, and the growing terror that she was losing her
marriage, her peace, and her self to a man who had taken only minutes but stolen decades.

It was Rajesh who had found the clinic and made the appointment. Priya sat in the waiting room now,
afraid of what help might look like, but more afraid of continuing as she was.
