# Fadi's Vigilance

Fadi Mansour had been a social media manager for a mid-sized tech company for eight years before the
night that unmade him. At fifty-six, he had built a careful life—a marriage of thirty-one years to
Layla, two adult children, a modest house in the suburbs of Columbus, a routine that suited him. He
monitored brand accounts, crafted posts, managed community engagement. The work was predictable,
contained. He liked predictability.

On a March evening in 2022, Fadi had been driving home from a client dinner when he was pulled over
for a broken taillight he had not noticed. The officer who approached his window was young,
muscular, and immediately aggressive. Fadi, a cautious man by nature, kept his hands visible on the
steering wheel. He answered questions politely. He explained that he had not realized the light was
broken. The officer demanded he step out of the vehicle. When Fadi complied slowly—he was thinking
of his own safety, of not startling anyone—the officer interpreted the slowness as resistance. What
followed was a blur of force. Fadi was thrown against the hood of his car, his face pressed into the
warm metal. He tasted blood and the metallic tang of the hood's paint. His arm was wrenched behind
his back, and he heard himself cry out. The officer's knee was against his spine. Fadi kept
thinking: *I have done nothing wrong. I am cooperating. Why is this happening?* By the time backup
arrived and the situation de-escalated, Fadi had been handcuffed, his cheek split open, his shoulder
badly bruised. The charges were dropped within hours. The violation remained.

In the weeks after, Fadi found that certain sensations would trigger a flooding return of the event.
The taste of iron in his mouth—whether from biting his cheek while eating or from blood work at his
doctor's office—would transport him instantly back to that hood, to the helplessness, to the rage.
When the memory returned, it came with an intensity of anger that frightened him. He would feel his
fists clench, his jaw tighten, and he would be furious at the officer, at the system, at himself for
having been vulnerable. But the memory never came through whole. He would find himself pushing it
away before he could fully process what had happened, as if his mind was protecting him from
something even worse than the rage itself.

Fadi began to scan his environment constantly. In his office, he positioned his desk so he could see
the door. When he went to restaurants with Layla, he needed to sit facing the entrance. Before he
could settle anywhere—a waiting room, a conference room, even their own living room—he would
mentally map exits. His wife noticed that he had stopped watching the news, that he scrolled past
videos and articles about police incidents, that he no longer watched the television shows they had
once enjoyed together. She asked if he was all right. He told her he was fine, that he was just
tired, but the truth was more complicated. Anything that could stir feeling—whether the news, a
film, even a conversation with a friend—felt dangerous to him. He had come to believe that if he
allowed himself to feel anything deeply, the rage would consume him, or the fear would paralyze him.
It was safer to feel nothing at all.

At work, his distraction became noticeable. He missed deadlines. He made errors in posts that
required correction. His manager asked if there was a personal issue. Fadi said no. He could not
articulate what had happened to him because articulating it meant acknowledging that the world was
not what he had believed it to be—that someone in authority could hurt him without cause, that the
social contract he had lived by was an illusion. People were not what they seemed. The officer had
seemed like someone doing a job; instead, he was someone who had violated Fadi's body and his sense
of safety in one brutal moment. Fadi had acted in a way that went against everything he believed
about himself—he had cried out, he had been afraid, he had been helpless. That man on the hood of
the car was not the Fadi he knew.

Now, six months later, Fadi sits in the waiting room of a therapist's office, scanning the exits,
unable to relax even though he knows intellectually that he is safe. His wife had insisted he come.
She had said, quietly, that she could not reach him anymore, that he was disappearing. Fadi is
afraid that if he begins to speak about what happened, the rage will spill out and he will lose
control of it entirely. But he is also afraid that if he continues as he is—vigilant, numb,
trapped—he will lose everything that matters to him. He does not know which fear is larger.
