# Layla's Vigilance

Layla was forty-four when she first came to the clinic, though her face had taken on the worn
quality of someone much older. She had been a prosecutor in the city's criminal division for nearly
fifteen years before the assignment that changed everything—a pro bono representation case involving
a family displaced from Beirut during the 2006 conflict. The work had moved her deeply enough that
when a colleague asked her to participate in a legal aid mission to Syria in 2011, she had said yes.
She was going to help document war crimes, to give voice to civilians caught in the crossfire. She
was going to use her training for something that mattered.

She had been in Damascus for six weeks when the shelling intensified in her neighborhood. On the
morning of March 19th, she was at a safe house with three other international observers when the
building next door took a direct hit. The concussion threw her against a wall. For several seconds
afterward, there was a ringing silence, and then the screaming began—voices of people buried in
rubble, voices of children. She had tried to help pull bodies from the debris. She had held a
woman's hand as that woman died. When the second round of shells came, they had run. Layla
remembered the feeling of her legs moving beneath her, the absolute certainty that the next
explosion would have her name on it.

She had gotten out three days later. For the first month home, she had told people she was fine,
that she had done important work. But by May, things had begun to unravel in ways she could not
control or explain to her husband, Karim. She stopped sleeping through the night. Around 2 or 3
a.m., she would jolt awake, her heart hammering, and immediately scan the bedroom—checking the
windows, listening for sounds outside, mentally mapping the distance to the door. She had come to
believe that safety existed only in her locked bedroom with Karim beside her. Nowhere else felt
defensible. When she tried to go to the office, to return to her caseload, the open floor plan of
the courthouse felt exposing. The parking garage made her skin crawl. She had stopped taking the
subway entirely. Even in her own home, she found herself drawn to the back bedroom, the one with the
fewest windows, the one where she could see anyone approaching.

What made it worse was how her body had betrayed her ability to work. Any unexpected sound—a car
backfiring, a door slamming, someone dropping a file on a desk—sent her into a state of acute alarm.
Her vigilance was constant and exhausting. By September, her supervisor had gently suggested she
take a leave of absence. Layla had not objected. The truth was, she could no longer manage even
minor frustrations. A miscommunication with her assistant, a delayed email response—things that
would once have been routine irritants now felt catastrophic. She would find herself trembling,
unable to think clearly, certain that she was on the verge of falling apart entirely.

The worst part, though, was the loss of herself. She remembered being someone who laughed easily,
who looked forward to dinner parties and weekend hikes with Karim. Now she was always braced for
disaster. The world had revealed itself to be fundamentally dangerous, and she had seen too much to
pretend otherwise. When her sister called to invite her to a family gathering, Layla had declined.
She could not bear to be around people, could not explain why she flinched at voices that resembled
the one of a man who had spoken to her in the safe house just before the first shell fell. That
voice—the memory of its particular cadence—would surface without warning, and her body would respond
as though the danger were present and immediate.

By January, Karim had insisted she see someone. He sat beside her in the waiting room, holding her
hand. Layla was terrified of what admitting this meant, but she was also terrified of continuing as
she was—trapped in a life so small it was barely a life at all.
