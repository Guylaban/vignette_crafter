# Priya

Priya was thirty-seven days into her marriage when the men came to the apartment. She had been
living in her husband's family home in Lahore for less than two months, still learning the rhythms
of her new life, still adjusting to the weight of expectation that came with being a bride. The
police arrived without warning on a Tuesday morning in March 2019, claiming she had been involved in
distributing pamphlets critical of the government. She had not. But the distinction between guilt
and accusation dissolved quickly in the basement room where they took her.

For six days, she was questioned, threatened, and beaten. The men used electricity on her hands and
feet. They kept her in a room with no windows and a single overhead bulb that never turned off. She
learned to count the hours by the rhythm of her own breathing, by the way hunger became a constant
presence, by the way her mind began to fracture under the weight of not knowing if she would leave
that room alive. On the seventh day, her husband's family secured her release through connections
and money. She returned to the apartment with burns on her wrists, bruises across her ribs and
shoulders, and a terror so complete it had begun to feel like her baseline.

They left Pakistan three months later. Priya's parents had arranged for her visa to join her husband
in New Jersey, where he had secured work as an engineer. She arrived in September 2019 carrying the
trauma like a second body—something always present, always heavy, always ready to pull her under.

The first year unfolded in careful avoidance. Priya had come to believe that if she could simply
control her environment, eliminate surprises, stay vigilant, she could prevent another catastrophe.
When her husband suggested she see a doctor about the persistent pain in her hands, she refused. The
thought of sitting in a waiting room, of being examined, of having to speak about what had happened
to a stranger in a white coat—it triggered a cascade of panic that made her skin crawl. She was
certain that seeking help would somehow make her vulnerable again, would expose her to people who
might hurt her. Instead, she managed the pain in silence, applying ice at night, taking over-the-
counter medication, and telling herself this was strength.

She began working as a tailor at a small shop in Hackensack, a job that allowed her to stay in one
place, to control her surroundings. But the shop had a single entrance and no back exit, and within
weeks she found herself mapping the space obsessively. Where could she go if someone came through
that door? Could she slip out through the storage room? She would arrive early to scout the
building, to identify which windows opened, where the manager kept keys. Her employer noticed her
constant repositioning, the way she always stood with her back to the wall, the way her eyes never
stopped moving. She had begun to scan every room she entered, her body moving through the motions of
tailoring—pinning hems, adjusting seams, pressing fabric—while her mind remained locked in the
basement in Lahore, waiting for the next blow.

Her husband had suggested therapy several times. The thought made her stomach clench. She was
certain that talking about what happened would make it real in a way she could not survive. More
than that, she had come to believe that her hypervigilance, her constant watching, was the only
thing standing between her and annihilation. To relax, to lower her guard even for an afternoon,
felt like stepping into traffic. When her husband tried to draw her into quiet evenings together,
she would find reasons to stay busy—folding laundry, organizing the kitchen, anything to keep
moving, to keep watching.

It was a flashback at work that finally broke her resolve. A customer's phone rang with a loud,
sudden tone, and Priya found herself on the floor, gasping, convinced that hands were reaching for
her again. The shop fell silent. Her employer helped her to a chair, and in that moment of absolute
exposure, something shifted. She called her husband from the bathroom and said, for the first time,
that she needed help. She was afraid—terrified, really—that speaking about the trauma would destroy
what little stability she had built. But she was more afraid of living the rest of her life in that
basement, moment after moment, never truly present, never truly safe.

thing standing between her and annihilation. To relax, to lower her guard even for an afternoon,

she would find reasons to stay busy—folding laundry, organizing the kitchen, anything to keep
moving, to keep watching. She had not slept through a full night in nearly five years. The overhead
lights in their bedroom stayed on; she could not tolerate darkness or the vulnerability of sleep.
Her marriage had become a careful choreography of distance, with her husband learning not to touch
her without warning, not to approach from behind, not to expect her presence in moments of ordinary
intimacy.

sudden tone, and Priya found herself on the floor, gasping, convinced that hands were reaching for
her again. The shop fell silent. Her employer helped her to a chair, and in that moment of absolute
exposure, something shifted. She called her husband from the bathroom and said, for the first time,
that she needed help. She was afraid—terrified, really—that speaking about the trauma would destroy
what little stability she had built. But she was more afraid of living the rest of her life in that
basement, moment after moment, never truly present, never truly safe. At twenty-eight, she had
already lost five years. She was not certain she could survive losing more.
