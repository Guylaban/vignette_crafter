# Captain Marcus Volkmann: Life After the Ejection

Marcus Volkmann was fifty-six years old when the F/A-18 Super Hornet he was piloting suffered a
catastrophic hydraulic failure at thirty-two thousand feet. He had logged over four thousand flight
hours across three decades—had ejected once before in his twenties, a controlled maneuver in a
training scenario. This was different. The stick went dead in his hands. The aircraft was already
rolling, the nose pitching downward with a violence that seemed to belong to another world. He
pulled the ejection handle. The canopy blew away. The seat rocketed him upward and backward with a
force that felt like being hit by a freight train, and then the chute deployed and he was swinging
beneath silk, watching his aircraft tumble away from him and explode against the desert floor below
with a sound so loud it seemed to tear the sky open.

He landed hard on rocky terrain, broke two ribs and his left wrist, and lay there for forty minutes
before the rescue team found him. The medics cut away his flight suit, their hands everywhere,
checking for injuries he could not yet feel. An ambulance. A hospital. Surgeons. The smell of
antiseptic. For three months he recovered in a medical facility, and during those three months
something began to shift in him that no amount of physical healing could touch.

When Marcus returned home to his wife Elena in their house outside San Diego, he carried with him a
conviction that had calcified into certainty: his body had betrayed him. The aircraft had been a
machine he could trust because he understood its systems, could anticipate its failures, could
respond. But his own body—his own nervous system—had become an unreliable instrument. Even now,
months later, his muscles stayed contracted as though bracing for impact. His shoulders lived near
his ears. At night, any unexpected sound—a car backfiring, a neighbor's gate slamming, a door
closing too quickly—sent him into a state of reactive panic, his body moving before his mind could
catch up, as if some part of him was still falling from the sky.

Dizziness came without warning. A slight shift in position, standing too quickly, the sensation of
the room tilting—and Marcus would be seized by the certainty that something was profoundly wrong,
that his body was about to fail again. He began to avoid situations where dizziness might occur. He
stopped going to the gym where Elena had always seen him, stopped the sailing trips they used to
take together. He stopped eating with any pleasure, measuring his portions with the precision he
once applied to pre-flight checklists. If he could control what went into his body, perhaps he could
control what his body did. The restriction gave him a sense of agency that nothing else did anymore.

Elena had stopped asking him to go out. The few times they had attempted to attend social
gatherings, Marcus found himself scanning the room, tracking exits, noting every person who moved
suddenly, every sound that might be a threat. The world no longer felt like a place where ordinary
things happened. It felt like a place where the ground could open beneath you without warning. He
had come to believe this with a clarity that felt almost religious: the world was dangerous,
fundamentally unpredictable, and the only certainty was that things could fall apart at any moment.

He refused to see his primary care physician. The thought of sitting in an examination room, of
having a doctor's hands on him, of being in a space associated with hospitals and injury and the
loss of control, created a dread so intense that he simply stopped going. When Elena suggested it,
he changed the subject. When she insisted, he left the room.

What finally brought Marcus to seek help was not the symptoms themselves, but their weight. He had
become, he realized, a man who felt nothing. He looked at photographs of his grandchildren—Elena's
from a previous marriage—and felt only a hollow distance. He sat beside his wife and could not
remember what it had felt like to want her. The emotional numbness had spread through him like a
cold fog, and he understood that he had lost not just his sense of safety but his capacity to be
present in his own life. He was fifty-six years old and he was already dead inside. When Elena
finally told him, with tears in her eyes, that she could not keep living this way, Marcus made the
call. He needed help not because he believed the world was safe—he was certain it was not—but
because he could no longer bear the price of that certainty.
