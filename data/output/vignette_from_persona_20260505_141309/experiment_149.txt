# Rodrigo

Rodrigo Hernández had been a parole officer for twenty-three years when the incident happened. He
had built a reputation for fairness—tough but principled, the kind of officer who could sit down
with a man fresh out of prison and see something worth salvaging. His colleagues respected him. His
parolees, many of them, actually listened to him. He had come to believe that his job was to enforce
the law while holding onto the possibility that people could change. That belief had become the
architecture of his life.

On a humid afternoon in June, Rodrigo was conducting a home visit in a neighborhood where three of
his cases lived. He had parked his unmarked sedan on the corner, and as he approached the apartment
building, he noticed Officer Delgado—a patrol officer he had known casually for years—standing
outside with his hand on his duty belt. Rodrigo raised his hand in greeting. What happened next
occurred in fragments that his mind still refuses to assemble into a coherent whole. There was
shouting. Someone—not Rodrigo—had a gun. Delgado moved toward Rodrigo, and then Rodrigo felt the
impact, a blow to his ribs so violent it drove the air from his lungs. He fell. There was more
shouting, and then Delgado was on top of him, striking his face, his shoulders, his arms as Rodrigo
tried to shield himself. When it ended, Rodrigo was bleeding from a gash above his eye, his ribs
screaming, and Delgado was being pulled away by other officers who had arrived. The story that
emerged was a terrible mistake: Delgado had been responding to a call about an armed robbery suspect
in the area. He had seen Rodrigo approaching and, in a moment of catastrophic confusion, thought he
was the suspect. The department's internal investigation found that Delgado had acted recklessly but
without malice. No charges were filed. Delgado was suspended for two weeks.

In the months after, Rodrigo began to notice that his body had become a stranger to him. His heart
would race without warning—sometimes in his office, sometimes at home—and he would grip the edge of
his desk or the kitchen counter, certain that something terrible was about to happen. He found
himself unable to enter silence without dread. When his wife, Maria, would sit beside him in the
evening without speaking, he would feel his shoulders tense, his breath shallow. The quiet felt
pregnant with danger, as though violence itself lived in the space between words. At the office, he
started scanning the hallways before he moved through them, noting exits, watching hands, tracking
the position of everyone around him. His colleagues began to comment that he seemed distracted, that
he had become rigid in his interactions.

The memory itself had become slippery. Rodrigo could recall the heat of that afternoon, the moment
he raised his hand to greet Delgado, but when he tried to hold the rest of it in his mind—the
sequence of blows, the sound of his own voice—it dissolved like something too extreme to be real. He
found himself pushing it away before he could examine it fully, as though looking at it directly
would shatter something essential in him. The worst part was not what he remembered but what was
missing. He knew he had been beaten, but entire sections of the event had simply vanished. Sometimes
he wondered if he was exaggerating it in his mind, if he had imagined some of it, because surely
something that violent would leave a complete memory, not these fragmentary, unreliable pieces.

What had broken him most completely was not the violence itself but the betrayal. Delgado was a cop.
Rodrigo was a cop. They inhabited the same world, followed the same code. And when the system that
was supposed to hold Delgado accountable—the investigation, the department—had essentially shrugged
and moved on, Rodrigo felt the ground shift beneath him. The institution that had been his home for
nearly a quarter-century had failed him utterly. No charges. Two weeks' suspension. The injustice of
it sat in his chest like a stone. He had spent his career believing in accountability, in
consequences, in the possibility of redemption through the system. Now he understood that the system
protected its own, and he was not protected.

For months, Rodrigo continued to work. He conducted his parole visits, filed his reports, sat across
from men trying to rebuild their lives. But he had begun to avoid situations where he might
encounter other officers. He would take longer routes to avoid the precinct. When people
misbehaved—when a parolee missed an appointment, when someone cut him off in traffic—he felt a surge
of rage so intense it frightened him, as though all the injustice he could not direct at the system
was pouring into these smaller moments. Maria had noticed the change. She had stopped trying to sit
with him in the evenings. His supervisor had asked him, carefully, if he was all right.

It was his daughter, calling from out of state, saying she was worried about how he sounded on the
phone, that made Rodrigo finally admit that something was profoundly wrong. He had come to believe
that he could not trust the world to be fair, that danger lurked in silence and in the hands of
people he thought he knew, that his own mind could not be trusted to remember what had happened to
him. He made an appointment with a therapist, sitting in the waiting room with his jaw clenched,
wondering if talking about it would make it real or make it disappear entirely.
