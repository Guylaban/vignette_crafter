# Aroha

Aroha was twenty-three when the bombing happened. She had been in the lobby of the Civic Centre in
downtown Auckland, waiting for her dance partner to finish a meeting upstairs so they could head to
rehearsal together. The foyer was all glass and steel, morning light pouring through the tall
windows, and she was standing near the information desk scrolling through her phone when the blast
came. The sound was not like anything she had ever heard—not a bang but a compression, a force that
seemed to come from inside her own chest first, then from everywhere at once. The windows fractured
into a thousand pieces. People fell. She fell. When she tried to move, she could not tell if her leg
was pinned or if she simply could not remember how to make her body work.

She had survived with relatively minor injuries—a fractured fibula, lacerations on her arms and
face. But in the months that followed, the physical wounds became almost incidental to something
else that had taken root in her mind. The attack had been claimed by a group whose name she heard
repeatedly in the news, in conversations, in the careful condolences of friends and family members
who would begin sentences and then stop themselves, uncertain whether speaking the name aloud would
harm her further. Each time she heard it, her body reacted as though the blast were happening
again—her heart would accelerate, her breathing would shallow, and she would find herself gripping
whatever was nearest to her.

Now, at twenty-nine, Aroha had been living in a state of fractured existence for six years. The
memories of that morning did not come to her as a coherent sequence but as disconnected sensations
and images that refused to assemble into a narrative she could tell. She could recall the angle of
light through the windows. She could recall the sound, though the sound itself seemed to exist
outside of language—it was not a memory she could describe to another person so much as a physical
state she inhabited when it returned to her. Sometimes, lying in bed at night, the full emotional
weight of the event would crash through her without any accompanying image, and she would lie rigid,
drenched in fear, unable to articulate what she was afraid of in that particular moment.

The apartment she had rented in Grey Lynn was small, with a single bedroom and a living area that
faced the street. Within the first year after the bombing, she had stopped using the bedroom
entirely. The small, enclosed space triggered a panic she could not override—a certainty that she
had no options, that she was trapped. She began sleeping on the couch in the living room, where she
could see the window and the street beyond it. The bedroom door remained closed. She had not opened
it in over five years.

Her career as a dancer had contracted around her fear in ways both obvious and subtle. She could no
longer audition for roles that required her to perform in smaller venues or enclosed theatres. The
idea of being backstage in a confined green room, waiting to go on, was impossible. She had begun to
take only outdoor performances, which severely limited her opportunities in a city where most
professional dance work happened in studios and theatre spaces. Her income had declined. More
significantly, she had begun to isolate herself from the dance community that had once been her
entire world. Gatherings of dancers, conversations about upcoming shows, even casual mentions of the
names of dancers she had once trained alongside—all of these had become unbearable because they
threatened to pull her back into the life she had been building before the bombing, and that life
now felt like it belonged to someone else.

She had come to believe that nothing in her life held meaning anymore. The dance that had once
brought her joy felt hollow. Her relationships had withered as she withdrew. She spent considerable
energy trying to reframe what had happened to her in positive terms—telling herself that she had
survived, that she was stronger now, that the experience had changed her in ways that might
eventually matter—but these thoughts felt like mantras she was forcing herself to repeat rather than
truths she believed. What haunted her most was the absence of accountability. The people responsible
for the bombing had never been caught. They had never faced trial. They existed somewhere beyond her
reach, unpunished, while she remained imprisoned by what they had done.

When a stranger approached her on the street to ask for directions, Aroha felt her body prepare for
violence. When a door closed unexpectedly in her apartment building, she found herself holding her
breath, listening intently for footsteps, for the sound of someone entering her home. She had come
to therapy now because the isolation had become total, and because she was beginning to wonder
whether the person she had been before the bombing was someone she could ever become again, or
whether she was going to spend the rest of her life as this fragmented version of herself, waiting
for a sound that might never come.
