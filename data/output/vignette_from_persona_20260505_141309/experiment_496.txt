# Aroha

Aroha was eight years old when her father came home that evening with his name on his lips—or
rather, when her mother spoke it with a particular kind of dread. The name itself had become a
warning, a weather pattern announcing what would follow. Her father, Hemi, had spent the afternoon
at the pub and returned to their Wellington home in that familiar state: loud, unpredictable, his
presence filling the small living room like smoke. Aroha was supposed to be doing her homework at
the kitchen table, but she had learned to make herself small, to listen for the shifts in her
mother's voice. That night, she watched from the hallway as her father's hand moved toward her
mother's face, as her mother's body went rigid, as the living room became a place where safety
ceased to exist. What stayed with Aroha most vividly was not the violence itself but the feeling of
witnessing something that should never have been hers to see—the rupture of trust, the knowledge
that the person meant to protect her could not protect herself, and the terrible, suffocating shame
that somehow she had failed to prevent it.

For years afterward, Aroha carried that shame like a second skin. She had come to believe, in the
way traumatized children do, that there was something fundamentally wrong with her, some flaw that
had made her father act the way he did, some deficiency that meant she could not stop what happened.
When she heard her father's name—Hemi—spoken by a stranger or a friend, her body would betray her
before her mind caught up. Her heart would begin to race, a sudden percussion in her chest that made
her feel exposed and unsafe, as if the name itself were a hand reaching toward her. Being watched
triggered the same response. In her work as a dancer, this had become a particular torment. She had
trained since childhood, had moved toward the stage as though it were a refuge, but now, standing
before an audience or even during rehearsals when the choreographer observed her closely, she felt
that same helplessness bloom in her chest—the sense of being seen, of being scrutinized, of being
found wanting.

At thirty-one, Aroha had built a life that looked, from the outside, like recovery. She was married
to James, a kind man who loved her, who had never raised his voice in anger. They lived in Auckland,
far from Wellington and her father. Yet the memories had a way of arriving without permission.
Sometimes she could recall the evening with a strange detachment, as though she were watching
someone else's daughter in someone else's living room, feeling nothing at all. Other times, a
particular quality of light in the afternoon, or the sound of a door closing with force, would
transport her completely. Her body would remember before her mind did. She would feel the racing
heart, the tightness in her throat, and suddenly she was eight again, paralyzed by the knowledge
that people could not be trusted, that the ones you loved could hurt you, that there was something
about her that invited betrayal.

The consequences had accumulated slowly. She had begun to withdraw from rehearsals, making excuses
when the director asked her to perform in the company's upcoming production. She had taken fewer
classes, spent more time at home. James had noticed, had asked gently if something was wrong, and
Aroha had found herself unable to explain—how could she tell him that she no longer trusted her own
judgment about people, that she feared what might be hidden beneath his kindness, that she believed,
despite all evidence, that she had somehow caused her father's violence? The company had begun to
suggest that perhaps she was not suited to performance anymore. She was missing rehearsals, losing
roles to younger dancers, and the thing she had built her identity around was slipping away.

It was James who finally insisted she seek help. He had found her one afternoon in the bedroom,
overwhelmed by a memory that had arrived without warning, her heart racing so violently she thought
something was wrong with her body. He held her and said, quietly, that he could not bear to watch
her disappear. Aroha is sitting in the therapist's office now, terrified that speaking about what
happened will make it real, hoping against hope that someone might help her understand why she still
carries the weight of her father's violence as her own fault.
