# Khun's Vigil

Khun was twenty-three when his then-boyfriend Marcus first hit him. It happened in Marcus's
apartment on a Friday night after Khun had mentioned—almost casually, he thought—that he was
considering a transfer to the Bangkok bureau. Marcus had been drinking. He'd grabbed Khun's wrist,
twisted it, and then his fist came up so fast that Khun's head snapped back against the kitchen
counter. The pain was immediate and electric, radiating from his jaw down his neck. But worse than
the pain was the shock: Marcus had always been jealous, always controlling about Khun's friendships
and his work schedule, but he had never done this. Khun remembered thinking, in the fractured
seconds after impact, that he must have said something wrong, that he had pushed too hard about the
transfer, that he had brought this on himself. He'd apologized immediately. Marcus had cried. They'd
stayed together for another eight months.

Now, four years later, Khun was thirty-seven and still carrying the architecture of that violence in
his body and his mind. The relationship had ended badly—he'd finally left after a second incident
that broke two of his ribs—but the trauma had not ended with the breakup. It had metastasized into
something that governed his days.

Khun worked as a political correspondent for a mid-sized news outlet, a job that once thrilled him.
Now it was becoming impossible. Three weeks ago, he'd been assigned to cover a transit authority
hearing downtown, which meant taking the bus. The moment he boarded, his chest tightened. The bus
was crowded, mostly men in the morning commute, and Khun found himself pressed against a stranger's
shoulder. His heart rate spiked. He was certain that something was about to happen—that the bus
would be delayed, that the delay meant danger, that the man beside him would turn on him. Nothing
happened. The bus arrived on time. But Khun spent the entire ride rigid with vigilance, his skin
crawling, unable to focus on anything except the proximity of the man's body and the terrible
certainty that he was unsafe.

He'd taken a cab home instead of the bus. It cost forty dollars. He'd been taking cabs ever since,
burning through his budget, but the thought of boarding public transport again made him feel as
though his ribs were breaking all over again—not metaphorically, but as an actual physical sensation
in his chest.

The memory itself had become untethered from time. When it surfaced—triggered by a raised voice in
the office, or by being alone in a room with his editor, who was a large man with dark hair like
Marcus—Khun did not remember the event as something that had happened years ago. He felt as though
it was happening now, in the present moment. His jaw ached. His wrist burned. The fear was immediate
and total. Last week, during a staff meeting, his editor had raised his voice about a missed
deadline, and Khun had felt the memory crash over him with such force that he'd had to excuse
himself to the bathroom. He'd sat in a stall for twenty minutes, shaking, unable to return to the
meeting. His editor had sent him a concerned email afterward. Khun had not replied.

Sleep had become a negotiation with terror. He would wake at two or three in the morning, seized by
the need to check that his apartment door was locked, that his windows were secure, that no one was
in the hallway. Sometimes he checked five or six times before he could convince himself, however
temporarily, that he was safe. The rational part of him knew this was excessive. The rest of him
could not trust that knowledge. Even when his partner, Sofia, told him he was safe—and she told him
often, with genuine care—he could not believe her. How could she know? How could anyone know? The
truth that Khun had come to believe, the thing that felt most real to him, was that you could never
know who would harm you. People could not be trusted. And he himself was inadequate—inadequate for
not seeing the warning signs with Marcus, inadequate for not leaving sooner, inadequate for being
broken now.

He had also stopped eating properly. Not consciously at first. But as the anxiety mounted, food
became something he could control, a way to shrink himself, to make himself less present in the
world. He'd lost twelve pounds in three months. Sofia had noticed. She'd asked him about it gently.
He'd said he was just busy. It was easier than admitting that every time he tried to eat, his body
rebelled, and that the restriction itself—the hunger, the clarity that came with it—felt like the
only thing keeping him tethered to something resembling safety.

Khun had finally called a therapist because Sofia had given him an ultimatum, not unkindly: get help
or she would have to leave. He loved her. He did not want to lose her. But as he sat in the waiting
room on his first appointment, checking the time obsessively because the session was running five
minutes late and his mind was already spiraling—something bad is happening, something is wrong—he
was uncertain whether even professional help could reach the part of him that remained convinced,
with absolute certainty, that he was fundamentally unsafe and fundamentally alone.
