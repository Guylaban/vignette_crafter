# Clinical Vignette: Martin

Martin is a sixty-eight-year-old psychologist of German descent, married for nearly three decades,
who presents with a PTSD symptom profile of moderate-to-severe intensity following a sustained
period of physical abuse by a romantic partner that occurred some fifteen years prior. The abuse
took place during a brief separation from his wife—a chapter he has never fully disclosed to her,
and one that continues to fracture his sense of self and safety in ways both obvious and insidious.

The relationship began as a professional connection that evolved into an affair. Martin, then in his
early fifties and at the height of his career, had convinced himself it was a temporary lapse, an
expression of dissatisfaction he would eventually resolve. But his partner—a woman eight years his
junior—became increasingly possessive and volatile. What began as verbal contempt escalated one
evening into physical violence. She struck him repeatedly with a bicycle she kept in her apartment,
swinging it like a weapon when he attempted to leave. The assault lasted perhaps ten minutes, though
Martin cannot reconstruct it in sequence. He remembers the metallic cold of the frame, the sensation
of impact across his shoulders and ribs, her voice—sharp, accusatory—but these fragments refuse to
cohere into a narrative he can tell himself or anyone else. What he remembers most vividly, and most
unbearably, is the feeling: terror so complete it seemed to erase thought itself. That emotional
intensity returns without warning, sometimes triggered by nothing he can identify, flooding him with
the full force of that night.

In the years since, Martin has constructed an elaborate architecture of avoidance and control. He
will not ride a bicycle, nor will he sit in a car if someone is cycling nearby. The sight of a
bicycle triggers a cascade of hypervigilance that can last hours. More profoundly, he has become
deeply uncomfortable in the presence of women when alone with them—a fact that has begun to
undermine his clinical practice. He has started scheduling male supervisees preferentially, has
arranged his office so that the door remains open during sessions with female patients, and has
begun to decline consulting work that would place him in one-on-one situations with women
colleagues. His wife has noticed his tension at dinner parties, his tendency to position himself
near exits, his sudden need to leave when the gathering becomes crowded. He has not told her why.

At night, Martin wakes between two and four in the morning, moving through the house to check locks,
to ensure his wife is still asleep beside him, to verify that the doors remain closed. During these
vigils, he scans the darkness for threats. Daylight brings a measure of relief, but dusk arrives
with a creeping dread. He finds himself watching people—on the street, in restaurants, at the
grocery store—searching their faces and postures for signs of imminent violence, a habit so
ingrained he is barely aware he is doing it.

What troubles Martin most profoundly is not the flashbacks or the vigilance, but a conviction that
has taken root in his thinking: that people are fundamentally not what they seem, that his own
judgment is catastrophically flawed, and that he is, at his core, inadequate—a man who failed to
recognize danger in someone close to him, who allowed himself to be placed in a position of
vulnerability. These beliefs feel less like symptoms and more like truths he has discovered about
the world. He has come to therapy now, at sixty-eight, because his avoidance has begun to cost him
his practice. A female patient complained to his licensing board about his apparent discomfort in
her presence. His wife has begun asking direct questions about his nighttime wanderings. And Martin
himself has begun to sense that the structure he has built to contain his fear is collapsing,
leaving him increasingly isolated and ashamed.
