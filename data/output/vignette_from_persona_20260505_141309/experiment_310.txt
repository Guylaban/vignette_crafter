When he returned to the retail position he had taken after leaving Brennan—a more modest role, but
one that kept him away from warehouse work—he found himself hyperaware of his coworkers' small
transgressions. A colleague who cut in front of him at the time clock, a manager who didn't
acknowledge his greeting, a customer who was rude to another employee—each of these felt like
evidence of moral collapse, proof that the world was full of people like Derek. His vigilance became
exhausting. Colleagues began to notice that Luca seemed distant, occasionally hostile to minor
infractions that would normally go unremarked. At work, he had also begun to avoid situations where
he felt trapped: he would not take the elevator at the mall, he parked his car near exits, and he
found himself scanning every room he entered for secondary exits—the stockroom, the break room, even
the bathroom—a habit so ingrained now that he barely noticed doing it anymore. His manager had
mentioned, gently, that Luca seemed "on edge" and had suggested he take some time off, but Luca knew
that time away would only give the memories more space to grow. His daughter had stopped asking him
to play the games they used to play together; she seemed to sense his distance, his mind always
elsewhere, always calculating angles and exits. When the intrusive images came—Derek's face, the
blackness, the feeling of being trapped—Luca discovered that physical pain could interrupt them. He
would press his thumbnail hard into his palm, and the sharp sting would snap him out of the memory,
replacing the thought of the fire with the immediate sensation of hurt. He held ice against his skin
until his fingers burned, anything to force his mind away from what he had witnessed and what he had
failed to do. The pain was a switch he could control, unlike the memories themselves. Over weeks,
this became his method: whenever the images started, whenever the shame began to surface and the
thoughts about the fire threatened to overwhelm him, he would hurt himself deliberately, knowing
that the physical sensation would drive out the trauma-related thoughts, at least for a while.

Maria had noticed the marks on his hands. She had asked about his withdrawal, his silences at
dinner. Three weeks ago, when she found him in the garage pressing a lighter to his forearm, she
insisted he see someone. Luca came to his first appointment reluctant, convinced that talking would
only make the memories more real, more present. But the vivid snapshots were already there, and they
were not going away. What he feared most now was that the shame would consume him entirely, that he
would become the person Derek had revealed himself to be. What he hoped for, though he barely dared
admit it, was that someone could help him understand whether he deserved to have survived at all.
