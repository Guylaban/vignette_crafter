# Priya's Vigil

Priya was forty-five years old when she first felt the sensation that would come to define the next
three years of her life. It had started as a flutter in her chest during a routine Tuesday
morning—nothing dramatic, nothing that would have alarmed most people. But Priya had learned, in the
weeks and months that followed her sepsis diagnosis at thirty-nine, that the body speaks in whispers
before it shouts. By the time the sepsis had announced itself fully, she had been hospitalized for
seventeen days, intubated for five of them, and had spent a week afterward convinced that each
breath might be her last.

The sepsis had come without warning. A urinary tract infection that she'd dismissed as manageable
had spiraled into bacteremia, and she remembered with crystalline clarity the moment in the
emergency room when the attending physician had gripped her shoulder and told her they were moving
her to the ICU. She remembered the antiseptic smell—that particular blend of bleach and something
chemical that seemed to coat the back of her throat—and the sound of the ventilator, a rhythmic
whoosh that she came to associate with her body's betrayal. When she was finally extubated, she
could not stop crying. Her lungs felt borrowed, unreliable. The nurses had assured her that this was
normal, that she would recover fully, but Priya had come to understand something they had not: her
body was no longer hers to trust.

In the years since, Priya had developed an intricate system of self-protection that made sense to
her in the way that survival often does. She had come to believe that vigilance was the only thing
standing between her and another collapse. She monitored her body constantly—every change in her
heart rate, every moment of dizziness, every time her breath felt shallow. When she felt dizzy,
which happened increasingly as her anxiety tightened around her like a wire, she would grip whatever
was nearest and wait for the sensation to pass, convinced that this was the beginning of something
catastrophic. She had become an expert in reading her own body's signals, which meant she was also
an expert in finding danger in every one.

The hospital smell haunted her. When she had to pass the medical plaza near her apartment, she would
take a three-block detour. She had not been to a doctor in nearly two years, despite knowing that
her blood pressure medication needed renewing. Her dentist had sent three reminder notices for a
routine cleaning; she had thrown them away. The thought of sitting in a waiting room, of smelling
that smell, of someone in a white coat telling her something was wrong—it was unbearable. What if
they found something? What if her body was already failing again and she simply hadn't noticed yet?
It was safer not to know.

As a plumber, Priya worked alone most days, which suited her. She had always been independent, but
now it was a necessity. She could not afford to have someone else depend on her, and she could not
depend on anyone else to understand what it meant to live inside a body that might betray you. Her
business had suffered. She turned down jobs that required her to climb ladders or work in confined
spaces where breathlessness might overtake her. She had lost two long-standing commercial accounts
when she cancelled appointments without explanation—she simply could not risk the physical exertion,
the way her heart would race and her breath would shorten when she pushed herself.

It was her sister Meera who finally insisted. Meera had noticed that Priya was thinner, that she
moved through the world as if bracing for impact, that she had stopped coming to family dinners.
When Priya mentioned, almost casually, that she had fainted twice in the past month—brief episodes
where the dizziness had simply overwhelmed her—Meera had sat her down and refused to leave until
Priya agreed to make an appointment. Priya had resisted fiercely. What was the point? Doctors could
not fix what was broken. But something in her sister's voice, some note of desperation, had cracked
through her certainty. She was terrified, she finally admitted. Terrified of what they might find,
and terrified of what might happen if she continued to do nothing. She had survived sepsis once. She
was not certain she would survive another brush with death—or that her body would even give her
warning next time.
