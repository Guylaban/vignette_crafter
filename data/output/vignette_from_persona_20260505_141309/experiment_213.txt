# Aroha's Reckoning

Aroha Parata was thirty-one when she arrived in South Sudan as a medical relief coordinator with an
international NGO. She had grown up in Wellington, daughter of a Samoan nurse and a Pākehā engineer,
and had spent her twenties building a reputation for competence and compassion—the kind of person
who showed up, who stayed calm when others panicked, who believed that presence mattered. By the
time she boarded the plane to Juba, she had already worked in three conflict zones. She thought she
understood what she was walking into.

The clinic was in a compound on the eastern edge of the city, a cluster of concrete buildings
surrounded by walls. For the first two months, the work was grueling but manageable: treating
malnutrition, delivering babies, managing infections in a place where antibiotics were scarce. Aroha
worked fourteen-hour days and felt, despite the exhaustion, that she was doing something that
mattered. She wrote emails home describing the resilience of the people she met, the small victories
of a child recovering from pneumonia.

Then came the day that split her life into before and after.

A militia group arrived at the clinic without warning. There were perhaps eight of them, armed,
moving through the compound with the casual authority of men who had learned that violence opened
doors. Aroha was in the back room with a young woman in labor when she heard the shouting. By the
time she emerged, two of her staff were on the ground. A man with a scar across his left cheek
grabbed her arm and demanded narcotics. She refused. She remembered saying no with absolute
clarity—remembered the weight of her own voice, the certainty that she could not give him what he
wanted because the medications were for patients who would die without them. She remembered his hand
releasing her arm, remembered thinking that clarity had saved her.

What came next exists in her memory as fragments and silence. A gunshot. The smell of something
acrid and sweet—she would later realize it was a cologne, something cheap and heavy, that one of the
men was wearing. A sensation of being thrown against concrete. The young woman in labor screaming.
And then a gap, a blank space where hours should be, until she was being helped into a vehicle by
colleagues and driven to the airport.

She spent three weeks in a hospital in Nairobi, where doctors found a fractured rib, a concussion,
and deep bruising across her left side. The physical injuries healed. She was back in New Zealand
within a month.

Now, eight years later, Aroha lived in a small town in the Wairarapa, working as a park ranger—a job
she had taken because it meant being outside, being alone, being in spaces she could control. Her
marriage to James, whom she'd met two years after returning, had survived but barely. He wanted to
touch her, to hold her when she woke gasping in the night, and she could not bear it. The weight of
his arms across her chest sent her into a state of panic so acute that she would throw him off and
leave the bed, her heart hammering as though she were back in that clinic. She had come to believe
that she was fundamentally unsafe to be near, that her body was a threat to the people who loved
her.

She avoided the small Catholic church in town where James's family gathered on Sundays, avoided the
medical center where her GP practiced, avoided anywhere she could not see the exits or predict who
might appear. On patrol, she found herself watching other rangers with an intensity that exhausted
her—cataloging their movements, their proximity, the way they held their tools. She had stopped
going to the village café after a man wearing cologne had stood too close to her in line, and the
smell had sent her into a state of dissociation so complete that she had driven home without
remembering the drive.

The memories of the event itself remained fractured. She could describe the young woman's face,
could name the color of the walls, could recall the exact phrase she had used to refuse the militia
leader. But the sequence would not hold. When she tried to tell James what had happened—something he
had asked her to do for years—the narrative would dissolve mid-sentence, and she would find herself
unable to continue, her mind simply refusing to move forward through the story.

What frightened her most was not the memories themselves, but the conviction that had settled into
her bones: that she had failed, that she had acted in a way that betrayed everything she believed
about herself, and that there was no place in the world where she truly belonged anymore. She had
begun to wonder, in the quiet hours before dawn, whether her life was worth the effort of continuing
it.

When James found her sitting motionless in the dark one evening, having not moved for three hours,
he insisted that she call someone. Aroha finally made the appointment, though she was certain that
no one could help her, and that asking for help was itself another betrayal of who she had once
been.
