# Kwame's Story

Kwame was forty-five years old when he first sat down in a therapist's office, his large frame
folded into a chair that suddenly felt too small. He had worked the same agricultural cooperative
outside Fresno for seventeen years—tending citrus trees, managing irrigation schedules, training
younger workers—but in the past eighteen months something had fractured in him that no amount of
routine labour could mend. His partner of eight years, Derrick, had finally insisted he get help,
not with anger but with the quiet exhaustion of someone watching a person they love slowly disappear
into vigilance and dread.

The roots of Kwame's terror stretched back to his childhood in Lagos, to a single night when he was
seven years old. His parents had been arguing—a common occurrence that had escalated that evening
into something violent. His father, drunk and enraged, had thrown his mother against the kitchen
counter. Kwame remembered the sound of her cry, the way his father's fists had moved with a terrible
purpose, the sensation of his own small body frozen in the doorway, watching. He had stood
there—paralyzed, silent, unable to move or call for help—while his mother's blood dripped onto the
tile floor. A neighbour had finally heard the commotion and intervened. His mother had survived,
though she carried the injuries for months. But Kwame had carried something else: the certainty that
he should have done something, anything, and the crushing weight of his own powerlessness.

By the time Kwame was an adult, he had developed an intricate architecture of strategies to manage
the terror that lived beneath his skin. He could never be alone with another person—not genuinely
alone. Even at work, where he supervised a small crew, he ensured there was always a third presence,
a witness, someone else in the vicinity. He told himself this was good management, but the truth was
simpler and more desperate: he was terrified of what might happen if he were the only one present
when violence erupted. He ruminated endlessly about his father, constructing elaborate scenarios of
confrontation and retribution, imagining conversations where he finally stood up, where he made his
father understand the damage that had been done. These fantasies occupied hours of his time,
particularly at night, when his mind would not rest. Simultaneously, he tried desperately not to
think about what had happened, pushing the memories away whenever they surfaced, which only seemed
to make them more insistent.

The hypervigilance had become his constant companion. Kwame scanned every room he entered, noting
exits, assessing the mood and body language of everyone present. At the cooperative, his workers had
begun to notice that he seemed tense, always watching, always on edge. His shoulders rarely relaxed.
He found himself interpreting neutral comments as threats, reading danger into ordinary
interactions. At night, the vigilance intensified. Darkness made him feel exposed and unprotected;
he slept poorly, often waking in the early hours convinced that something terrible was about to
happen. Even when Derrick reassured him—"You're safe, Kwame, we're safe here"—the words seemed to
slide off some impenetrable surface in his mind. He could not make himself believe them. The safety
that Derrick offered, that his rational mind knew was real, remained impossible to feel.

Over the past year and a half, Kwame had noticed a hollowing out of himself. He used to enjoy his
work, used to take pride in the quality of the harvest, used to laugh with his crew during lunch
breaks. Now everything tasted like ash. He had lost his faith—not in God exactly, but in the
fundamental goodness of people, in the possibility that the world could be anything other than a
place where violence waited in the shadows. He had become, in his own estimation, a miserable man.
And underlying all of it was a guilt that never fully released its grip: he should have done more as
a child. He should have been braver, stronger, more able to protect his mother. He would never
forgive himself for standing frozen in that doorway.

It was Derrick who finally convinced him to call. "I can't reach you anymore," Derrick had said
quietly one evening. "It's like you're always somewhere else, always afraid of something I can't
see." Kwame sat in the therapist's office now, exhausted by the effort of simply existing, hoping
that someone might help him understand why safety felt like a lie and why the boy frozen in a Lagos
kitchen still held him hostage at forty-five.
