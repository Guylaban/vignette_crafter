# Vinh's Vigil

Vinh Nguyen was forty-six when he came to the clinic, fifteen years after the roadside explosion
that ended his first career and began the one that would define the rest of his life. He had been a
signals officer with the Army Corps of Engineers in Kandahar Province—steady work, technical work,
the kind that suited his methodical mind. On a Tuesday morning in March 2009, his convoy was moving
supplies to an outpost when the vehicle ahead of his struck an IED. He remembered the sound first, a
crack that seemed to come from everywhere at once, then the smell—acrid, chemical, underneath it
something organic and wrong. He had been thrown from his seat, his uniform torn open across his
chest and shoulders. For several seconds he lay in the dust unable to move, exposed and certain he
would be shot. No one came. The shooting, when it started, came from the ridge line. He crawled
behind the wreckage and waited, half-naked in the heat, for four hours until the medevac arrived.

He had not been seriously wounded. The blast had knocked him down; shrapnel had grazed his ribs. The
physical injuries healed within weeks. What did not heal was the particular configuration of terror
that had crystallized in those hours—the helplessness of exposure, the impossibility of knowing
where danger would come from, the way his own senses had become unreliable. He could not see the
shooters. He could not predict the next impact. All he could do was wait and listen to the sound of
his own breathing and the sporadic gunfire echoing off the rocks.

Vinh returned to the States and left the military. He found work as a software engineer at a mid-
sized tech firm in Seattle, married his longtime girlfriend Lena, bought a house in a quiet suburb.
For the first few years, the symptoms were manageable—bad dreams, some hypervigilance, the
occasional moment of panic when a car backfired. But over the past decade, something had shifted.
The vigilance had not diminished; it had metastasized.

Now, at home in the evenings, Vinh found himself unable to truly relax. Even with the doors locked
and the curtains drawn, he was alert—listening for sounds that did not belong, tracking the position
of windows, mentally cataloguing exits. When a motorcycle roared past on the street outside, his
heart rate spiked and his hands went cold. The sound was too much like the gunfire, too sudden, too
loud. He would sit rigid until the noise faded, his mind flooded with the sensory memory of that
morning: not just the sound of shots, but the specific pitch and rhythm of them, the way they had
seemed to come from nowhere.

He had begun avoiding things in ways that felt logical at the time but had accumulated into a kind
of confinement. He stopped going out unless absolutely necessary—the unpredictability of unfamiliar
places terrified him. He could not assess the danger in a new restaurant or a shopping center, could
not predict where a threat might emerge. He stopped watching television, stopped reading the news,
because anything that touched on conflict or violence would trigger the film that played in his
head—not a coherent narrative but a sensory loop: the smell, the sound of his own breathing, the
vulnerability of his exposed skin, the crack of the blast, over and over. He could not put it into
words, which made it worse. When Lena asked him what he was thinking about, what was wrong, he had
no way to explain that his mind was not thinking in language but in sensation and dread.

Exercise had become impossible. Going to the gym meant being around strangers in close quarters,
meant being vulnerable in a changing room. Even walking on his own street made him tense. Strangers
approaching him—the mailman, a neighbor—triggered a surge of threat detection that he knew was
irrational but could not override.

What finally brought him to seek help was not a crisis but a quiet realization. His company had
asked him to work in the office three days a week instead of remote. The thought of sitting in an
open plan with colleagues, of being unable to control who approached him or when, of being trapped
in a space he could not fully assess, had triggered such intense dread that he had called in sick
for a week. Lena had found him in the basement, sitting in the dark, unable to articulate why. She
had insisted he see someone. He was afraid that if he left the house, if he opened himself to the
world again, something terrible would happen. But he was also afraid of what would happen if he
stayed here, locked in the vigilance, waiting for a danger that had already passed.
