# Marcus Webb: A Clinical Vignette

Marcus Webb was fifty-three when he finally made the call to schedule a trauma-focused therapy
appointment, though the event that brought him to that decision had occurred fourteen years earlier,
during a conference in a mid-sized city where he had traveled for his work as a social worker
specializing in adolescent mental health. He had been attending a continuing education seminar in a
hotel near downtown, and after the final afternoon session, he'd decided to take a shortcut through
the basement corridor to reach the parking garage rather than navigate the crowded lobby. The
corridor was poorly lit, lined with mechanical equipment and storage closets. A man had emerged from
behind a utility door—someone Marcus had never seen before—and forced him into an adjacent room,
locking the door behind them. What happened in those minutes felt, in Marcus's recollection, like it
lasted hours. The assailant had been methodical and violent. Afterward, Marcus had dressed in
silence, driven home in a state of dissociation, and told his wife, Patricia, only that he'd had a
panic attack at the conference and wanted to leave early.

For years, Marcus had believed he was managing. He returned to work, continued his caseload,
attended supervision. But the management was a careful architecture of avoidance that had slowly
reshaped his life. He could not bring himself to enter basement parking areas anymore; he now parked
on street level, often paying premium rates, rather than descend into those enclosed, dimly lit
spaces where his mind insisted danger lurked. Hotel corridors became sources of profound anxiety. He
had begun to avoid professional conferences altogether, telling colleagues that budget constraints
or scheduling conflicts made attendance impossible. When he did travel for work, he insisted on
rooms on upper floors and would not use interior hallways if he could avoid them. The avoidance had
cost him—a promotion two years ago had required willingness to present at national conferences, and
his reluctance had made him seem uncommitted to career advancement.

What Marcus had never told anyone, not even Patricia, was the way his mind had turned toward
revenge. In the quiet hours before sleep, he constructed elaborate scenarios in which he encountered
the man again, in which he was stronger, faster, more prepared. He imagined confrontations that
never occurred, rehearsed violence he would never enact. These ruminations were exhausting and
shameful in themselves—he was a mental health professional who spent his days teaching adolescents
healthy coping strategies, yet he spent his nights fantasizing about hurting someone. He tried to
push the thoughts away, to redirect his attention to his wife or to work, but the effort of
suppression only seemed to make them more insistent, more vivid.

The memory itself had become fragmented in a way that troubled him. He could recall the sequence of
events with terrible clarity—the sound of the door locking, the smell of the room, the specific
words the man had spoken—yet when he tried to articulate any of it to anyone, the words simply would
not come. His mouth would go dry. His throat would close. It was as if his body refused to translate
the experience into language. When the memory surfaced unbidden, which it did with increasing
frequency over the past year, it returned not as narrative but as pure sensation: the terror he had
felt in that moment, immediate and undiminished by time, flooded through him as if he were still
trapped there.

His vigilance had become a constant background hum to his existence. He checked behind him when
walking through the hallways of his office building. He positioned himself in rooms so that he could
see the door. When a client session ran over time, when traffic delayed his commute, when anything
disrupted the expected timeline of his day, his mind immediately generated the worst possible
explanation—something had gone wrong, something bad was happening, danger was imminent. Patricia had
begun to notice his distraction, his irritability, the way he would tense when she suggested they
take a weekend trip or visit friends in unfamiliar neighborhoods. He could not explain to her why
the unpredictability of new places felt genuinely dangerous to him, why he needed to know the
layout, the exits, the potential threats before he could feel even remotely safe.

What finally moved him to seek help was not insight but exhaustion. He was tired of the constant
surveillance of his own environment, tired of the thoughts he could not stop, tired of the shame
that accompanied both the memory and his ruminations about revenge. He called a trauma-specialized
therapist on a Tuesday morning, his voice steady but his hands trembling as he scheduled the first
appointment. He did not yet believe that anything could truly change, but he had begun to understand
that the architecture of avoidance that had once felt protective was now the structure imprisoning
him.
