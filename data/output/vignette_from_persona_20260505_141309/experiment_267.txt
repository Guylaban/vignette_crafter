# Arjun

Arjun's father died without warning on a Tuesday evening in March. He was fifty-three, had
complained of chest tightness that morning, and by the time the ambulance arrived at their home in
New Jersey, he was already gone. Arjun, then nineteen, was in his dorm room two hours away when his
mother called. He remembered the specific quality of her voice—a sound he had never heard before,
something between a whisper and a shout—and the way the phone felt heavy in his hand as she told him
to come home. The funeral happened ten days later. He watched his father's body lowered into the
ground on a grey April afternoon, surrounded by relatives who kept touching his shoulder, his arm,
his back, as though physical contact might somehow anchor him to the moment or pull him away from it
entirely.

In the months that followed, Arjun had slowly come to believe that the world operated according to
rules he no longer understood. His father had been healthy. His father had been there. And then,
without negotiation or warning, he simply was not. This fundamental betrayal—the discovery that
safety was an illusion, that people could vanish in the space between one breath and another—had
reshaped how Arjun moved through his days. He became hyperalert, attuned to the smallest signs of
threat. His body had learned to startle at ordinary sounds: a door slamming in his apartment
building, the sharp ring of his phone, even the hiss of the coffee maker in the shared kitchen would
send a jolt through his chest, his heart suddenly racing as though danger were imminent. At night,
he lay awake for hours, his mind cataloguing worst-case scenarios, running through lists of people
he loved and imagining, with vivid precision, the ways they might be taken from him.

Sleep became a battleground. He would lie in bed at two in the morning, exhausted but unable to
surrender to unconsciousness, because sleep felt like a kind of death, a surrender of control. He
had come to believe that if he stayed vigilant enough, stayed awake enough, he could somehow prevent
the next catastrophe. Even in daylight, even when he sat in his apartment with the doors locked and
the windows closed, he could not find the relaxation that had once come naturally to him. His body
remained taut, waiting. His therapist had used the word "hypervigilance," but what Arjun experienced
was simpler and more terrifying: the absolute certainty that something else was coming.

The weight of this certainty had begun to reshape his life in concrete ways. He had withdrawn from
his writing—the thing that had defined him since childhood. He had been working on a novel about a
family across generations, but now the manuscript sat untouched on his laptop, and he could not
bring himself to open it. Writing about family felt like inviting disaster, as though imagining loss
on the page might somehow make it real in the world. He had stopped making plans beyond the
immediate day. When his roommate suggested they sign a lease together for next year, Arjun felt
panic rise in his throat. How could he commit to anything when everything could disappear? When
people he loved could be erased without reason or mercy?

He had also begun seeking constant reassurance from others. He texted his mother multiple times a
day, asking her to confirm she was okay, that she had eaten, that she felt well. He called his
sister at odd hours. He asked his roommate repeatedly whether he seemed normal, whether something
felt wrong with him, whether he was going to be all right. His friends had begun to pull away,
exhausted by the neediness, confused by the intensity of his fear. He had lost his best friend to
this—Rohan had stopped responding to his messages after Arjun called him seven times in one evening
asking whether he was safe.

What frightened Arjun most now was that he had become someone he no longer recognized. The person he
used to be—the one who wrote stories, who made plans, who felt happiness as a baseline rather than a
distant memory—seemed to have been buried in that cemetery alongside his father. He was twenty years
old and felt ancient, worn down by the knowledge that the world was fundamentally unsafe and that
everyone he loved was always, always at risk. When his mother finally insisted he see someone, Arjun
agreed not out of hope but out of desperation. He was not sure he could continue like this—suspended
between hypervigilance and despair, unable to sleep, unable to write, unable to imagine a future
where he was anything other than waiting for the next person to die.
