# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was fifty-five years old when the industrial fire at Riverside Chemical Plant consumed the
warehouse where he had been conducting a routine safety consultation on a Tuesday afternoon in
March. He had been a counselor for twenty-eight years—first in corporate wellness, then in trauma-
informed workplace intervention—and he prided himself on his calm, measured presence in crisis
situations. The fire alarm had sounded at 2:47 p.m. He remembered the time because he had glanced at
his watch, thinking it was likely a drill. It was not a drill. Within minutes, the loading dock
where he stood had become engulfed in thick, acrid smoke. The smell—chemical, choking, almost alive
in its intensity—had filled his lungs before he could process what was happening. He had made it
out, along with three others, but two workers who had been in the adjacent storage area did not. The
flames had moved with terrifying speed. The smell had stayed with him longer than anything else—not
just in his nostrils but embedded in his memory in a way that felt more real than the visual details
of escape.

In the weeks after the fire, Marcus had returned to work, reasoning that avoidance would only
compound the trauma. But something had shifted in how his body inhabited the world. The smell of
burnt toast at a coffee shop would send his heart racing; the acrid scent of a neighbor's fireplace
in autumn would leave him rigid and sweating for hours. Worse, sometimes he would smell smoke when
there was no smoke—phantom olfactory sensations that seemed to materialize from nowhere, pulling the
full weight of that afternoon's terror into his chest without warning or image. These moments were
the most disorienting. He would feel the panic, the suffocation, the helplessness in their complete
intensity, but when he tried to locate what he was remembering, there was only fog. The memory felt
like it belonged to someone else, something he had watched happen to a stranger rather than lived
through himself.

Around the same time, Marcus had begun to notice that his body no longer felt like his ally. His
heart would race unpredictably; his breathing would become shallow and rapid in moments of ordinary
stress. He had come to believe that his body was betraying him, reacting as if danger were imminent
when logically he knew he was safe in his office or his apartment. This fundamental mistrust had
gradually reshaped his daily life. He stopped attending the monthly counselor's peer group he had
facilitated for years, telling himself that he needed time to process, but really fearing that his
colleagues would notice something wrong, that his thoughts might scatter in front of them, that he
might not be able to hold the container they relied on him to hold. He avoided his closest friend,
David, who had called repeatedly after the fire. When David finally showed up at his door one
evening, Marcus had felt a surge of panic—not at David's presence, but at the thought that David
would see him struggling, would ask direct questions, would force him to articulate the thoughts
that now occupied his mind constantly.

Because Marcus had become hypervigilant in a way that felt necessary. He monitored his own thinking
with exhausting precision, watching for signs that he was losing coherence, that the trauma was
fracturing him beyond repair. If he allowed himself to think about the fire—really think about it—he
was certain he would not be able to handle the weight of it. So he had constructed an elaborate
internal barrier against reflection. He did not watch the news. He did not read the follow-up
articles about the investigation. When his mind drifted toward the memory, he would redirect it
forcefully, sometimes by leaving whatever room he was in, sometimes by engaging in repetitive tasks.
The avoidance had become its own exhausting work.

Now, six months after the fire, Marcus sat in his own office—the place where he had spent nearly
three decades helping others navigate their psychological crises—and realized he could no longer
manage alone. His professional reputation remained intact, but his internal world had become a place
of constant vigilance and fear. He had begun to wonder if the person who had walked out of that
burning warehouse was actually still here at all, or if something essential had been left behind in
the smoke. What brought him finally to seek help was not a dramatic crisis but a quiet recognition:
he had become a stranger to himself, and he did not know how to find his way back.
