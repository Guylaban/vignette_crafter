# Rania's Story

Rania was forty-two when the diagnosis came back: stage three ovarian cancer. She had gone to the
clinic with what she thought was indigestion—a heaviness in her abdomen that had worsened over three
months—and left with an oncologist's name and a surgery date. The first surgery, in the spring of
2021, revealed how far the disease had spread. Her surgeon used words like "aggressive" and
"extensive peritoneal involvement." Rania remembered the surgeon's face as she explained the
chemotherapy protocol, the way the woman's eyes softened in a way that felt like pity. That
moment—sitting in the examination room with her husband Karim holding her hand, realizing that her
body had become a territory she no longer controlled—fractured something in her that had not yet
repaired.

The chemotherapy lasted six months. Rania lost her hair, her appetite, her sense of who she was in
the world. But she survived. The scans came back clear. She was declared in remission. By all
medical measures, she had been given her life back. Yet the life she returned to did not feel like
the one she had left.

Now, three years later, Rania was thirty-six again in years but felt ancient in her vigilance. Every
ache in her body was a harbinger. A twinge in her lower abdomen, a persistent fatigue, a small
rash—these were not ordinary discomforts but omens. She had come to believe that her body was
fundamentally untrustworthy, that the cancer was still there, waiting, that remission was simply the
cancer's patience. What frightened her most was that she could not stop this reasoning, could not
convince herself otherwise even when her doctors reassured her. The fear itself felt like evidence
of something true that she alone could perceive.

At the construction firm where she had worked for twelve years, Rania had become someone her
colleagues barely recognized. She was a site supervisor—competent, respected—but over the past
eighteen months, she had begun arriving earlier and staying later, often working through lunch. She
told herself it was about the upcoming residential development, the complexity of the schedule, but
the truth was simpler and more desperate: when she was working, her mind could not wander to the
territory of her own mortality. At home, she cooked elaborate meals, often making two or three
dinners in an evening, eating while she prepared the next one, not tasting any of it. The overeating
was automatic, a hand reaching for bread or sweets without conscious decision, her body seeking
something that food could never provide. She had gained thirty pounds in two years. When she caught
her reflection in the site trailer's window, she sometimes did not recognize herself.

The sleep had become nearly impossible. Rania would lie awake beside Karim, her body rigid with
tension, convinced that if she relaxed into sleep, something catastrophic would happen—that she
would miss a sign, that her body would betray her in the darkness. At three in the morning, she
would rise and reorganize the kitchen cupboards or scroll through medical websites, reading about
recurrence rates and survival statistics, searching for the logic that would let her feel safe. She
startle easily now—a door slamming, a car horn outside—and her nervous system responds as though
danger is imminent. Her jaw is perpetually clenched. Her shoulders live at her ears.

The worst part, the thought that had begun to consume her in recent weeks, was that her mind was
fracturing under the weight of this vigilance. The way she could not stop catastrophizing, the way
she ate without hunger, the way she threw herself into work as though productivity were a shield
against death—these patterns frightened her. She wondered if she was losing her grip on reality, if
her constant fear meant she was going crazy. She had no future that felt real to her, only an
endless present of waiting for the other shoe to drop. Karim had gently suggested therapy several
times. Last week, Rania finally agreed. She sat in the waiting room of the clinic, her hands
trembling slightly, hoping that someone could help her understand why surviving felt so much like
drowning.
