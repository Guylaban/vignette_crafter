# Rosa

Rosa was sixty-six years old when she first came to the office, though the way she held
herself—shoulders tensed, eyes tracking the door—made her seem older. She had lived in the United
States for thirty-two years, long enough that her English carried only the faintest accent, but
there were parts of her life she had never fully translated into words. The war in El Salvador had
taken that from her: the ability to speak easily about what had happened.

She had been thirty-four when the violence found her. She was a journalist then, working for a small
newspaper in San Salvador, the kind of work that felt important and necessary and, she had believed,
relatively safe. She moved through the city with a press credential and a sense of purpose. On the
morning of February 14th, 1992, she was covering a story about displaced families in a neighborhood
near the capital. She had parked her car, notebook in hand, and was walking toward a community
center when the military convoy arrived. She remembered the sound first—not gunfire, but something
worse: the grinding of heavy vehicles, the shouts of soldiers, the specific metallic clang of
weapons being readied. She had turned to run, but the street was already blocked. The explosion came
without warning. A device detonated near a checkpoint fifty meters away. The force threw her
backward. She remembered the heat, the smell of burning concrete and something else she could not
name, the sudden absence of sound followed by a ringing in her ears that lasted for hours. When she
could see clearly again, there was blood on her hands and a man in a military uniform standing over
her, asking questions she could not process.

She had survived with cuts and a fractured collarbone. But something else had fractured
too—something about her sense of what the world was and where danger lived.

For the first few years after immigrating, she had managed. She worked as an editor for a publishing
house, a job that suited her precision and her need for control. The nightmares came, but she had
learned to live with them. It was only in the last decade, as she entered her sixties, that the
symptoms had begun to tighten around her like a fist. The night vigilance had started gradually. She
found herself unable to sleep past three in the morning, her body seized by a need to check the
locks, to move through her small apartment and verify that nothing had changed, that no one had
entered. She told herself this was reasonable caution, but the reasonableness of it had worn thin.
Every night, she woke. Every night, she checked. The exhaustion accumulated, but the checking never
stopped.

What had finally brought her to seek help was an incident at work three weeks ago. A colleague had
brought in a documentary about Central America, and as they discussed it in the editorial meeting, a
sound—a car backfiring on the street below—had sent Rosa into a state she could not recover from
quickly. Her hands had shaken for an hour afterward. But worse was what came next. Her boss had
gently suggested she take some time off, and the implication had stung: she was becoming unreliable.
She had always been the steady one, the editor who caught what others missed, the woman who showed
up and did the work. Now she was the woman who flinched at noises.

The triggers had multiplied over time. When she saw a police officer, or worse, a soldier in
uniform—even in a photograph or on television—her body would go rigid with the certainty that
something terrible was about to happen. If she glimpsed someone who resembled the officer from that
day, the one whose face she could still see with perfect clarity, her breath would catch and she
would have to leave whatever space she occupied. And the sounds. A car door slamming, fireworks in
the distance, even the sharp pop of a cork at a restaurant could send her back to that street, that
moment, that explosion.

What frightened her most was not the memories themselves, but the fact that she could no longer
trust her own judgment about safety. When her daughter told her, "Mami, you are safe now, you are in
America," Rosa wanted to believe it. But belief required something she no longer possessed. Even in
daylight, even in her own apartment with the doors locked, she could not shake the conviction that
danger was present, waiting, ready to emerge. At night, the conviction became certainty. She lay
awake in the dark, checking, checking, checking, and no amount of checking ever made her feel safe.
