# Chukwu's Vigil

Chukwu Okafor was thirty-seven when he came to the clinic on a Tuesday afternoon in October, moving
through the waiting room with the deliberate stillness of someone who has learned to take up as
little space as possible. He chose a seat with clear sightlines to the door and positioned himself
so his back touched the wall. The receptionist had offered him coffee; he declined, explaining that
he preferred to keep his hands free. It was the kind of detail most people would not have noticed.
It was the kind of detail that had become automatic for him.

Six years earlier, on a night that had reorganized everything that came after it, Chukwu had been
locked in a bathroom by a man he had trusted—a colleague from a research conference, someone who had
seemed interested in continuing a professional conversation back at the hotel. The man had forced
him against the tile, had pinned him there while the lock clicked shut behind them. For Chukwu, the
lock had become the punctuation mark of his unmade life. That sound—the definitive metal slide—had
become the sound of his body ceasing to belong to him.

For the first months afterward, Chukwu had told no one. He had returned to his private practice in
Seattle, where he saw patients with anxiety and depression, where he sat in a comfortable leather
chair and helped other people untangle the threads of their suffering. He had kept his back to the
wall even then, though he had told himself it was just a preference. By the second year, he had
stopped scheduling clients in the afternoon because the light through the windows made him feel
exposed, as though the trauma itself were visible on his skin, legible to anyone who looked at him
long enough. He had come to believe that people could see it—that some essential damage was written
across his face, that his patients must sense it even as he guided them through their own reckonings
with fear.

The locked spaces had become impossible first. He had stopped using the bathroom at work, timing his
day around trips home. He had declined invitations to conferences, to seminars, to anywhere that
might involve a hotel room or a closed door he could not control. When a male colleague from his
practice had asked to discuss a difficult case, Chukwu had felt his body go rigid with something
beyond caution—a certainty that being alone with another man in a room was a kind of repetition he
could not survive again. He had suggested they meet at a coffee shop instead, and the colleague had
seemed puzzled, but Chukwu could not explain. How could he say that the presence of another man's
body in a confined space had become synonymous with the loss of his own?

Sleep had become an act of resistance. Chukwu would lie in bed, acutely aware that his eyes were
closing, that his vigilance was slipping, and he would force himself awake. To sleep was to be
defenseless. To sleep was to return to that bathroom, to the moment when he could not see what was
happening to his own body. He had installed motion-sensor lights throughout his apartment. He had
arranged his furniture so that he could see from his bed to his front door. He had become, in
essence, a man who was never quite asleep and never quite awake—existing in a thin, exhausted space
between the two.

By the time he called the clinic, Chukwu had not slept more than three hours in a single night in
four years. His practice had dwindled; he had referred out most of his clients, telling himself it
was time to focus on his own research. What he could not articulate was that sitting across from
vulnerable people, holding space for their pain, had become impossible when he could not hold space
for his own body. He had come to believe that he had no future—that this state of perpetual
readiness, this inability to relax even in the locked safety of his own apartment, was permanent.
That nothing could stop what had already happened to him, and therefore nothing could stop it from
happening again.

What had finally brought him to seek help was a moment of such profound isolation that it had
cracked something open. A friend had invited him to a dinner party. Chukwu had arrived, scanned the
room for exits, positioned himself near the kitchen counter. Midway through the evening, someone had
made a casual comment about a news story—a case of sexual assault in the city. Chukwu had felt the
words land on him like a physical blow. He had excused himself, gone to the bathroom, and sat on the
closed toilet lid for twenty minutes, unable to move. When he emerged, he had left without
explanation.

Sitting now in the clinic, his back against the wall, Chukwu was hoping that someone could help him
understand why his body had decided that the world was a place where he could never be safe again.
He was terrified that nothing could change what had happened, and equally terrified of what it would
mean if it could.
