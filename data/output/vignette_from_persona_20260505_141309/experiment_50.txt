# Amara's Collision

Amara was seventy-eight when the truck hit her, though the years since have felt longer than those
that came before. She had been driving home from the policy office on a Tuesday evening in late
October, her mind still half-occupied with the brief she'd been reviewing—something about zoning
ordinances in the city's East Ward. The intersection at Broad and Fifteenth had been familiar to her
for decades; she'd navigated it thousands of times. But that evening, a delivery truck ran the red
light at what felt like impossible speed. She remembered the sound first: a roar that seemed to come
from everywhere at once. Then the impact, which her mind had fragmented into pieces she could no
longer fully assemble. The windshield had shattered. There was the smell of spilled alcohol from the
truck's cargo—bottles of whiskey breaking open, the sharp stench filling the crushed cabin of her
sedan. And heat. Intense, searing heat against her left arm and shoulder where the metal had twisted
and the late sun poured through the broken glass. She had been conscious through all of it, or most
of it. The parts she couldn't quite hold onto were perhaps merciful.

Two years had passed since that October evening. Amara had survived, though the word felt imprecise.
She had returned to her apartment in the Rittenhouse district, attended physical therapy, and
eventually gone back to the policy office. But the woman who returned was not the woman who had
left. At eighty, Amara had expected her remaining years to unfold with a certain predictability—her
work, her books, the weekly dinners with her daughter in Princeton. Instead, she found herself
trapped in a state of perpetual vigilance that exhausted her in ways she could not fully explain to
those around her.

The triggers came without pattern or mercy. A sudden movement—her daughter reaching across the table
too quickly, a colleague standing abruptly from a desk—sent a jolt of pure alarm through her body,
as though the collision were happening again in that split second. Her heart would race. Her hands
would tremble. The rational part of her mind, the part that had spent forty years analyzing policy
and evidence, would insist that she was safe, that the movement meant nothing. But her body did not
believe her mind. Worst of all were the moments when her surroundings seemed to conspire against
her. The smell of alcohol—a colleague's cologne with whiskey notes, the aroma drifting from a
restaurant they passed—would trigger a flood of sensory memory so complete and overwhelming that she
would lose several seconds to the past. Suddenly she was back in the crushed car, the smell
overwhelming her, the heat on her skin, the sound of metal screaming. These flashbacks came without
warning and left her disoriented, sometimes gasping, sometimes unsure of where she actually was.

Her memory of the event itself was strange and fragmented. She could recall with crystalline clarity
the moment she saw the truck approaching, the expression of horror on the face of the woman in the
car beside her. But the actual impact, the seconds immediately following—those were gone, replaced
by blank spaces she could not fill no matter how hard she tried. When she attempted to piece
together the full sequence, to understand what had happened, her mind would rebel. She found herself
pushing the memories away, unable to tolerate the approach of something larger and more complete
that lurked just beyond her conscious reach. It was as though her mind had decided certain details
were too much to bear, and she respected that decision even as it frustrated her.

The consequences had accumulated quietly. She had stopped driving, which meant relying on her
daughter or car services. More significantly, she had begun to withdraw from the office. The open
floor plan where she worked—full of sudden movements, unexpected footsteps, colleagues approaching
from her peripheral vision—had become intolerable. She had negotiated a part-time arrangement,
working mostly from home, but she knew her career was effectively over. At eighty, this should not
have mattered. But it did. Her work had been her identity for so long that its diminishment felt
like another kind of loss, layered atop the loss of her sense of safety.

It was her daughter who finally insisted that Amara seek help. She had noticed her mother's
increasing isolation, the way she flinched at ordinary sounds, the nights she lay awake. Amara had
resisted—what could therapy offer at her age?—but the weight of living this way had become
unbearable. She came to the first appointment uncertain whether healing was possible, but desperate
enough to try.
