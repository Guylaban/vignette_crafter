# Carmen's Story

Carmen Reyes was fifty-seven years old when she came to the clinic, and she moved through the world
now as though she were perpetually bracing for impact. She had been a physical therapist for thirty-
two years, the kind of clinician who could read a body's compensations with her hands before a
patient even spoke. That skill had made her good at her work. It had also, she believed, made her
weak in the aftermath of what happened.

Seven years ago, Carmen had been visiting her sister Rosa in Puerto Rico—a trip she had taken every
August for two decades. The hurricane came faster than anyone predicted. She remembered the news
alerts, the sudden frantic calls from neighbors, the way the sky turned a shade of green she had
never seen before. She and Rosa had sheltered in the bathroom, the only room without windows, while
the house came apart around them. The sound was what stayed with her most: not the wind itself, but
the sound of the roof being peeled away, a groaning metal shriek that seemed to come from the earth
itself. When the water came through, it was warm and brown and full of things she could not
identify. She had grabbed Rosa's hand and held it until her own fingers went numb. Her sister had
survived. Carmen had survived. But something in how she inhabited her own body had not.

The triggers had assembled themselves slowly at first, then with gathering force. Water was the
obvious one—she could not go near the ocean anymore, and even the sight of the bay from a distance
made her stomach seize with nausea. But it was worse than simple avoidance. When she saw water,
particularly still water or water that looked deep, her body would betray her with a violent wave of
sickness that seemed to come from somewhere outside herself. She had stopped going to the beach
entirely, which meant she had stopped seeing friends who lived near the coast. She had stopped
attending her nephew's swim meets. She had begun to organize her life around the absence of water.

The name triggers were stranger to her. When she heard the name Rosa—which was common, which was
everywhere—she would feel a spike of something between panic and profound sadness. It was as though
her nervous system was insisting that each mention of that name was a threat, that something had
gone wrong with her sister again. She had come to dread conversations, waiting for the name to
appear and knowing she could not explain to people why she had suddenly become distant or quiet.

What frightened Carmen most was how much of herself she had already lost trying to manage these
reactions. She had taken to deliberately leaving her body when the nausea came, a kind of
dissociation that she could almost will into being. She would feel the sensation starting, and she
would consciously try to float away from it, to blank out, to make herself absent. It worked for a
few moments, but then she would come back to herself and realize she had lost another piece of time,
another moment of her life.

She dwelled constantly on who she had been before the hurricane. She had been social, energetic,
someone who loved travel and spontaneity. Now she felt trapped in a smaller and smaller space. She
left her apartment less and less. Her work had suffered—she had cut back her hours, then cut back
further. Her colleagues had noticed. Her supervisor had gently asked if everything was all right.
Carmen had said yes, of course, while knowing that she was becoming someone unreliable, someone who
cancelled plans, someone who stayed home.

The sleep had become impossible. She would lie awake for hours, listening to the sounds of the
building settling, her body rigid with the certainty that something was about to happen. When she
finally slept, she would wake gasping, her heart hammering, with no clear memory of a dream—just the
full emotional intensity of terror, the overwhelming grief of loss, though she could not say what
she had lost beyond the self she used to be.

Carmen came to the clinic on a Tuesday morning in November. She had not left her apartment for three
days before that. She told the therapist that she was afraid she was disappearing entirely, and she
wanted to know if that was possible, and if there was any way to come back.
