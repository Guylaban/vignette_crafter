# Clinical Vignette: Margarethe

Margarethe was fifty-one when she came to her first appointment, her hands folded tightly in her
lap, her eyes moving toward the door twice during the intake. She had worked for the state
environmental agency for twenty-three years—a steady, methodical career in permit review and
compliance monitoring—until the morning of March 14th, four years ago, when a storage tank at an
industrial facility thirty miles north ruptured during an unannounced inspection she was conducting.
She had been standing near the tank with the facility manager when the seal failed. There was no
warning—no alarm, no visible sign of what was about to happen. The chemical cloud came suddenly,
acrid and invisible, burning her throat and eyes before she could understand what was happening. She
remembered the facility manager's face contorting, remembered running toward the gate, remembered
the ambulance arriving with its wailing siren, remembered the hospital's fluorescent lights and the
smell of her own burning skin. The exposure had been moderate; she had not required intensive
treatment, but the cloud itself remained in her body as something she could not fully articulate or
escape.

The years since had narrowed her world in ways that surprised even her. Margarethe could not
explain, even to herself, exactly why her body responded the way it did to certain things. When she
tried to talk about what happened that day—to her husband, to her supervisor, to anyone—the words
would scatter. She would begin describing the tank, the smell, and then something would collapse
inside her narrative; she would lose the thread entirely and fall silent, frustrated and ashamed.
She could recall fragments: the manager's expression, the gate, the siren. But when she tried to
deliberately reconstruct the full sequence, to remember it on purpose, the memory resisted her. What
remained most vivid was not words but sensation—a tightness in her chest, a metallic taste, a sense
of air itself becoming hostile.

She had come to believe that safety existed in only three places: her bedroom with the door locked,
her car with the windows up, and a small reading room at the library where she went alone. Anywhere
else felt permeable, dangerous. Her office at the agency had become intolerable. She had taken
medical leave six months after the exposure, then requested a transfer to a back-office role with
minimal public contact. When that still felt unsafe, she had applied for early retirement. Her
supervisor had been understanding, but Margarethe knew that colleagues whispered about her sudden
withdrawal, her refusal to attend meetings, her inability to manage what they perceived as a
straightforward chemical incident. She was a capable woman who had simply stopped showing up to her
own life.

What terrified her most was unpredictability. She had begun rehearsing conversations before any
interaction—scripting what she would say to the cashier, to her husband, to the doctor—because
spontaneous exchange felt like standing near an invisible tank again, waiting for something to
rupture. When people approached her without warning, even in ordinary settings like a grocery store,
her body would flood with alarm. She was certain they meant harm, though she could not articulate
why. The sound of an ambulance siren sent her into a state of acute vigilance for hours afterward.
Loud or unexpected noises—a door slamming, someone raising their voice—triggered a flood of
adrenaline she could not control.

She had also stopped allowing her husband to touch her. He would reach for her hand in the car, and
she would flinch away, unable to bear the vulnerability of contact. She avoided hospitals, avoided
ambulances, avoided any medical setting that might trigger the memory of those fluorescent lights.
When her blood pressure needed monitoring, she had not seen a doctor in fourteen months.

It was her husband who finally insisted she seek help. He had found her one evening standing in the
kitchen unable to move, having rehearsed and re-rehearsed what she would say to call for an
appointment, each iteration revealing new dangers she had not considered. He took her hand—she
pulled away—and told her that whatever was happening, she could not live like this anymore. That
night, Margarethe sat in the dark and realized that the chemical cloud had not left her body. It had
become her body. She made the call the next morning, her voice shaking as she scheduled this first
session. What she wanted most was to feel safe in the world again. What she feared most was that she
had forgotten how.
