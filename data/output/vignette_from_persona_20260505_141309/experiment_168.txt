# Clinical Vignette: Margot

Margot is a fifty-four-year-old chef of Polish descent who was assaulted in her restaurant's walk-in
freezer eight months ago by a delivery driver she had worked with for nearly three years. She had
known him by name, had made small talk with him on dozens of occasions, had trusted him enough to
unlock the freezer when he arrived with a late shipment. What happened inside that space—the sudden
violence, the way his hands moved, the temperature that made everything feel unreal—she cannot yet
assemble into a coherent narrative. When she tries to speak about it in therapy, words scatter. She
describes instead the sensation of her spine pressed against cold metal shelving, the particular
quality of the fluorescent light, the smell of frozen meat and her own fear. The event exists in her
body more completely than it exists in language.

Since that evening, Margot has reorganized her life around the conviction that trust is a dangerous
mistake. The man who hurt her had worn a familiar uniform, had greeted her by her first name, had
given no warning. She had come to believe that you cannot know who will harm you—that the appearance
of ordinariness is itself a mask. When strangers approach her now, even in the grocery store or on
the street, her body tightens as though danger is certain. She monitors the distance between herself
and unfamiliar men with an intensity that exhausts her. In her kitchen, where she once moved with
fluid confidence, she now startles violently at any unexpected movement—a sous chef reaching past
her, a delivery person appearing around a corner. The startle is not small or manageable; it is a
full-body recoil, a moment in which she experiences the movement as threat.

Her marriage has become strained in ways that puzzle her husband, Michael. He cannot understand why
she flinches when he approaches her from behind, or why certain conversations—ones in which he tries
to discuss what happened, or in which she senses he is trying to draw her into emotional
intimacy—cause her to withdraw or become sharp with him. She has come to dread being alone with him,
though she cannot fully articulate why. He is not the man who hurt her. Yet the simple fact of his
maleness, of his physical presence when there is no one else nearby, generates a vigilance in her
that feels both protective and imprisoning. She locks the bathroom door when she showers. She sleeps
facing the door.

The restaurant has suffered. Margot has always been the kind of chef who worked the line herself,
who moved through her kitchen as its vital center. Now she has hired an assistant manager and spends
more time in the office, away from the floor. This decision was framed as a promotion, but it is
actually retreat. She cannot manage the unpredictability of the kitchen environment anymore—the
constant movement of bodies, the necessary trust in her team, the vulnerability of being watched
while she works. Two of her longest-serving cooks have asked for transfers. She knows they sense her
distance, her hypervigilance, the way she has become someone other than the woman who taught them.

What brought Margot to seek help now, after eight months of managing alone, was a moment last week
when Michael tried to hold her hand during dinner and she experienced a surge of panic so acute that
she stood up from the table and locked herself in the bedroom. She sat on the edge of the bed
feeling the phantom pressure of that freezer wall against her back, feeling her body in that old
position, and she understood that the life she is living—cordoned off, vigilant, unable to be
touched by the one person who loves her—has become another kind of trap. She is terrified that
therapy will ask her to trust again, and equally terrified that she will spend the rest of her life
unable to do so.
