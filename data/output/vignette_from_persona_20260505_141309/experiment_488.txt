# Clinical Vignette: Layla

Layla was thirty-eight years old when she came to her first appointment, her posture tense and her
eyes tracking the movements of the therapist's hands as they reached for a notepad. She had been
assaulted in her home eighteen months earlier by an intruder who forced his way through her back
door while her husband was at work. The man had held her at knifepoint, demanded money and
valuables, and during the struggle—the details of which remained fractured in her memory—he had
struck her repeatedly. What Layla remembered with absolute clarity was the moment his hand closed
around her wrist, forcing her arm behind her back. She remembered the sound of the knife being
drawn. Everything else existed in patches: the weight of his body, the smell of him, the exact words
he had spoken—these elements were either vivid or entirely absent, as though her mind had
selectively recorded only fragments of the ordeal.

In the months that followed, Layla's body had become a landscape of hypervigilance. She found
herself unable to sit in a room without knowing exactly where the exits were. At the homes where she
worked as a housekeeper—three families in an affluent neighborhood, each with multiple rooms and
hallways—she had begun to move through the spaces differently. Where she once cleaned methodically,
room by room, she now moved in patterns that allowed her to monitor the front entrance, the garage,
any point of potential entry. Her employers had begun to notice that she seemed distracted, that she
would pause mid-task and listen intently to sounds from other parts of the house. One family had
asked if she was unwell. Another had quietly reduced her hours.

What frightened Layla most was the sound of raised voices. When the father in one household had
shouted at his teenage son about homework, Layla's entire body had gone rigid. Her hands had begun
to shake. She had excused herself and sat in the laundry room for twenty minutes, her heart racing,
certain that something terrible was about to happen. She had come to believe that shouting was a
precursor to violence, that it signaled a loss of control in whoever was speaking. The thought of
being forced to do anything—to move in a particular direction, to hand over her belongings, to
comply with any demand—triggered a cascade of panic that made her feel as though the assault were
happening again in real time.

Reminders of other victims haunted her as well. When she had watched a news report about a home
invasion in a neighboring county, something inside her had fractured. She had not left her house for
three days. She had become convinced that what happened to other women could happen to her again,
that she lived in a world where such violations were inevitable. Her husband had tried to comfort
her, but Layla found herself unable to be touched without flinching. She had begun sleeping in the
guest room, unable to tolerate his presence beside her, unable to explain why the warmth of another
body felt like a threat.

The intrusion monitoring had become a constant, exhausting vigil. Layla listened for sounds that
might indicate someone entering her home—the creak of a door, a car in the driveway, footsteps on
the porch. Her concentration had deteriorated to the point where she could no longer follow
conversations or read. She would start a sentence and lose the thread midway through. At work, this
had become apparent. She had forgotten instructions, mixed up which family wanted which cleaning
products, made mistakes that were uncharacteristic of her careful, methodical nature.

What had finally brought Layla to seek help was a moment of clarity about what her fear was costing
her. Her marriage was deteriorating. Her work performance had declined enough that she feared losing
her positions. But more than that, she had realized she was no longer living—she was merely
surviving, trapped in a state of perpetual readiness for danger that never came. When she described
this to the therapist, her hands were trembling, and she spoke in a voice barely above a whisper: "I
don't know how to feel safe again. I don't know if I ever will."
