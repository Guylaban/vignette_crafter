# Clinical Vignette: Margaret

Margaret Kowalski is an eighty-year-old Polish-American woman who has spent the last fifty-three
years as a clinical social worker, first in hospital settings and latterly in community mental
health. She came to her first appointment in early November, referred by her primary care physician
after Margaret disclosed to him that she had not slept properly in six weeks. Her PCL-5 score of 66
placed her firmly in the severe range for PTSD, though she had never named it as such until now.

The trauma itself occurred when Margaret was twenty-seven years old. She had been engaged to Thomas,
a man she had known since childhood through Polish community circles in Pittsburgh. They had been
together for four years when, in the spring of 1971, an argument about wedding plans escalated in
his apartment. Thomas had been drinking. When Margaret said she wanted to postpone the wedding, he
struck her across the face, then again, then threw her against the kitchen counter. The sequence of
it remained fractured in her mind—not a coherent narrative but a series of isolated, vivid
snapshots: his hand raised, the sound of her own cry, the cold edge of the countertop against her
ribs, his voice saying things she could no longer recall in order. What stayed with her most vividly
was the moment the door to his apartment swung open behind him as neighbors came to help her; the
sudden intrusion of light and voices, and Thomas's silhouette in the doorway. She had left him that
day and never returned.

For decades, Margaret had managed. She had built a career helping others, married briefly to a
gentle man who died in 1989, and created a life of deliberate structure. But in recent months,
something had shifted. She had begun to notice that she could not enter a room without immediately
identifying the exits—a habit so ingrained she had stopped thinking of it as unusual until her
colleague at the community center mentioned it. She found herself avoiding situations where she
might be alone with male clients, redirecting appointments to younger female social workers with
explanations that felt increasingly thin. She had taken to sleeping in her living room because the
bedroom felt too enclosed, too far from the front door. When she did sleep, nightmares came—not full
dreams but flashes, the same snapshots repeating. She had begun avoiding the hours just before dusk,
when she knew sleep might come, staying up instead watching television until exhaustion forced her
under.

What had finally compelled her to seek help was an incident at the senior center where she
volunteers. A male volunteer, a kind man named Robert whom she had known for two years, had come up
behind her to ask about a filing system. When he placed his hand on her shoulder, Margaret had
flinched so violently that she knocked over a stack of folders. Robert had apologized profusely,
confused by her reaction. In that moment, standing among scattered papers, Margaret had felt the
full weight of what had happened to her—not as history, but as a present, inescapable fact.

She had come to believe, over the decades, that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that people
could not be trusted no matter how long you knew them or how gentle they appeared. The attack had
shattered her sense of how the world worked. She felt contaminated by what Thomas had done to her,
as though the violence had left something permanent and dirty on her skin that no amount of time
could wash away. She found herself thinking obsessively about why it had happened—what she had said
or done that had provoked him, what she might have done differently. These thoughts offered no
answers, only a cycling sense of culpability that she knew, intellectually, was irrational, but
which felt absolutely true.

Margaret sits in the consulting room with her hands folded in her lap, her silver hair neatly
pinned, her posture impeccable. She is articulate and reflective, a woman accustomed to observing
human suffering in others. But her eyes are exhausted, and there is a tremor in her voice when she
speaks about the sleeplessness, about the way her body now betrays her by reacting to men she
consciously knows mean no harm. She is afraid that at eighty, she has run out of time to heal from
this, that the trauma has simply waited all these years for her defenses to weaken. What she hopes
for, though she barely dares to say it, is that she might be able to be alone in a room again
without her body flooding with alarm.
