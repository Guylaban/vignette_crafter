# Robert's Reckoning

Robert Castellano was sixty-eight when the assault happened, a successful pharmaceutical salesman
with thirty-five years of client relationships and a reputation for closing deals. He had built his
career on charm and attentiveness—the ability to read a room, to remember a client's daughter's
name, to make people feel seen. At a trade conference in Denver in March, he had stayed late at the
hotel bar with a group of colleagues, including Marcus Webb, a competitor from a rival firm whom
Robert had known casually for years. The evening had been convivial, the drinks flowing freely.
Robert, who had always prided himself on his capacity to hold alcohol, had matched Webb drink for
drink. When Webb excused himself to use the restroom, Robert remained at the bar, scrolling through
his phone. Webb returned minutes later, and they continued talking. What Robert could not have known
was that Webb had been drinking alone in his room before joining the group, and that the
accumulating alcohol had transformed him into something volatile. When Robert made a casual remark
about Webb's declining sales numbers—the kind of competitive observation that would normally pass as
office banter—Webb's face darkened. He stood abruptly, grabbed Robert by the collar, and drove him
against the bar with a force that knocked the wind from his lungs. The bartender intervened quickly,
but not before Webb's fist connected with Robert's ribs and face. The smell of whiskey on Webb's
breath, the shock of being overpowered at an age when Robert had begun to feel invulnerable, the
humiliation of being pinned and struck in front of witnesses—all of it crystallized into a trauma
that would reshape his life.

The physical injuries healed within weeks, but something else fractured that night. Over the
following months, Robert began to avoid the downtown hotels where pharmaceutical conferences were
held. When his company scheduled the annual sales meeting in a venue he had once loved, he made
excuses—a family obligation, a minor surgery—anything to stay away. He stopped attending industry
events altogether, which meant fewer networking opportunities, fewer new clients. His sales numbers,
which had been steady for decades, began to slip. More insidiously, he found himself unable to be in
the presence of alcohol without his chest tightening. The smell of whiskey or bourbon—which he had
once enjoyed in a good Scotch—now triggered a visceral recoil, a flooding of fear that made no
rational sense but which he could not override. When he and his wife Margaret went to dinner, he
would request tables far from the bar. He began declining invitations to social gatherings where
drinking was central. Margaret noticed the change but said little; she had always deferred to
Robert's preferences.

What troubled Robert most profoundly was not the physical memory of the assault itself, but the
thoughts that came unbidden at odd moments—a flash of Webb's face, the sensation of being grabbed,
the taste of blood. These intrusions arrived without warning, pulling him out of conversations or
work calls. He would be discussing a sales pitch and suddenly find himself back in that bar, his
heart racing. He developed an arsenal of mental strategies to combat these thoughts: when an
unwanted image surfaced, he would deliberately redirect his attention elsewhere, would try to think
of something—anything—else. He would suppress the thought through sheer force of will, clamping down
on it the moment it appeared. The effort exhausted him.

Beneath all of this lay a deeper, more corrosive belief. Robert had come to see the assault as
somehow his own fault. He had made that comment about Webb's sales. He had stayed at the bar too
late. He had, in some fundamental way, provoked what happened. More troubling still was a sense that
the man he had been before—confident, commanding, the person who could navigate any room—no longer
existed. The person who now flinched at the smell of alcohol, who avoided entire cities, who lay
awake at night trying not to think about what he could not stop thinking about—that was not Robert
Castellano. That was someone diminished, someone flawed in a way he could not articulate or repair.
His colleagues had begun to notice his absences from events. His sales had declined enough that his
manager had asked if everything was all right. Robert had said yes, of course, but he knew the
truth: he was no longer the person his company had hired, the person his wife had married.

It was Margaret who finally insisted he speak to someone. She had watched him disappear into himself
for nearly a year, had seen the toll the avoidance and the relentless mental effort were taking.
Robert resisted—what could talking do?—but eventually, worn down and aware that something
fundamental had broken, he agreed to seek help. What he hoped for was unclear. What he feared was
that the person he had been was gone forever, and that the man he had become was irreparably broken.
