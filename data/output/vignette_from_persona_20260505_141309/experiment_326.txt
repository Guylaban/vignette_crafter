# Clinical Vignette: Vinh

Vinh was forty-six when he walked into the clinic, his shoulders curved inward in a way that
suggested years of contraction. He had the build of someone accustomed to physical work—the compact
muscularity of a firefighter, though his face held the exhaustion of someone who had not slept well
in a very long time. His divorce had been finalized three years ago, and he lived alone in a small
apartment near the fire station where he had worked for the past eighteen months. Before that, he
had been a relief worker in Syria for five years, coordinating humanitarian logistics across three
provinces. That was where everything had fractured.

In 2016, Vinh had witnessed something that had reorganized his understanding of human decency. He
had been in a compound with other aid workers when armed men arrived. What followed was not a single
discrete moment but a series of hours that had become, in his memory, a jumbled sequence he could
not arrange into coherent order. He knew a young woman in the compound had been assaulted. He knew
he had been forced to watch. He knew that in the chaos afterward, he had made a choice—to prioritize
his own safety over hers, to move her away from the immediate threat rather than stay and confront
the men, a decision that had felt both necessary and like a profound betrayal of everything he
believed himself to be. When he tried to speak about it now, the words would not come. He could feel
the memory in his body—a hot, spreading pain across his ribs and shoulders, the sensation of his
throat closing—but the narrative itself remained fragmented, the sequence of events refusing to
settle into a coherent story.

For years after he left Syria, Vinh had tried to outrun what had happened. He had thrown himself
into the structure of firefighting, the clear hierarchies and protocols, the sense that here, at
least, he could save people without moral compromise. But the triggers had followed him. Whenever
someone at the station behaved unethically—cutting corners on equipment checks, making crude jokes
about a female colleague's appearance—Vinh would feel a surge of rage so intense he had to leave the
room. The sound of a car backfiring or any loud bang would send him into a state of hypervigilance
that could last for hours. Worst of all were the comments about sexual violence that sometimes
surfaced in news briefings or casual conversation. These words would hit him like a physical blow,
and he would find himself in the station bathroom, pressing his palms against the sink, trying not
to scream.

Over the past two years, Vinh had developed rituals. He would count specific numbers in sequences
before entering certain rooms, convinced that if he performed them correctly, nothing bad would
happen to the people in his life. He would press his fingernails into his wrists when the
overwhelming feelings threatened to break through, the sharp pain providing a kind of release valve
for emotions he could not otherwise contain. He tried desperately not to think about Syria, but the
suppression itself had become exhausting—it took constant mental effort, and the moments when his
guard dropped, when silence settled over the station or when he was alone at night, the memories
would surge back with the weight of something physical.

What frightened Vinh most was silence. When the station fell quiet, when there were no calls and no
activity, he became convinced that something terrible was about to happen. The absence of sound felt
like a threat, as if danger were gathering in the void. He had come to believe that his life was
fundamentally destroyed, that he no longer belonged anywhere—not among the people he had tried to
save in Syria, not in his marriage, which had dissolved under the weight of his rage and emotional
distance, not even in the brotherhood of the fire station, where he felt like an impostor pretending
to be stable. He had called the clinic after his captain had found him in the parking lot at three
in the morning, unable to articulate why he was there, his hands bleeding from where he had been
digging his nails into his own skin. The captain had given him the number, and Vinh had held it for
two weeks before making the call. What he hoped for was some way to be whole again. What he feared
was that there was nothing left to salvage.
