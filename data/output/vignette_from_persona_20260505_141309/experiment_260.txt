# Thien's Vigilance

Thien was seventy-six years old and had lived in the United States for forty-two years, but the war
had never truly left Cambodia. He arrived at the intake appointment on a Tuesday morning in
November, moving slowly through the clinic waiting room with the careful deliberation of a man who
had learned long ago to notice every exit, every person, every sound. His hands trembled slightly as
he filled out the intake form, and the receptionist noticed he gripped the pen as though it might
escape.

The traumatic event itself was seared into his memory with a clarity that had only sharpened with
age. In April 1975, when Thien was thirty-four years old, Khmer Rouge soldiers had come to his
neighborhood in Phnom Penh in the early morning. He had been asleep when they burst through his
door—four men in black, faces obscured, moving with the mechanical efficiency of people who had done
this many times before. They had separated him from his wife and two children without explanation,
bound his wrists with cord so tight that his hands had swollen within minutes, and forced him into a
truck with thirty other men. The cord had cut off circulation; he could still feel the phantom
sensation of his fingers going numb, the panic of not knowing if they would ever work again. They
had driven for hours. Men around him were crying, praying, begging. The smell—sweat, fear, human
waste from those who had lost control of their bodies—had filled the truck bed with an acrid,
suffocating density that he could conjure in an instant even now, decades later.

He had survived, though he never learned why. He had spent three years in labor camps, had seen
executions, had watched men die of starvation and disease. But it was that first day—the sudden
violence, the restraint, the helplessness—that his nervous system had never forgotten.

For the past several years, Thien had worked the night shift at a warehouse in Newark, managing
inventory for a pharmaceutical distributor. The work was solitary and suited him; he preferred the
quiet hours after midnight when the building was nearly empty. But six months ago, a new supervisor
named Marcus had been hired, a man in his forties with a loud voice and an aggressive management
style. Marcus believed in "accountability," which meant confrontations on the warehouse
floor—raising his voice, getting in workers' faces, sometimes physically blocking them from leaving
a conversation. Thien had begun to dread his shifts. When Marcus approached him with anger in his
expression, something in Thien's chest would seize. His breathing would become shallow. He had
started calling in sick, inventing reasons to avoid the days when Marcus was scheduled. His
supervisor had noticed; there had been a formal conversation about reliability. Thien was now at
risk of losing a job he had held for eight years.

The triggers came without warning and with devastating force. Last month, a coworker had
accidentally brushed against him in the storage aisle, steadying himself with a hand on Thien's
shoulder. Thien had gone rigid, his heart racing, and had excused himself to the bathroom where he
sat in a stall for twenty minutes unable to move. Two weeks before that, he had been standing in
line at the supermarket when a man behind him had stepped too close, and the proximity alone—the
sense of being trapped between the man and the register—had sent him back to the truck bed, to the
smell of fear and waste, to the cord cutting into his wrists. He had abandoned his groceries and
left.

The memories themselves were the worst part. They did not come gently or in fragments. When they
came, they came complete and overwhelming—the faces of the soldiers, the weight of the cord, the
smell so vivid he could taste it. His body would flood with adrenaline. His hands would shake
uncontrollably. He had learned that once a memory had seized him, he could not simply push through
it; he had to stop whatever he was doing and wait for it to release him, which sometimes took hours.
He had missed appointments, had sat paralyzed at his kitchen table, had called in to work claiming
illness when really he was trapped inside his own nervous system.

What had finally brought him to the clinic was a moment of clarity born from desperation. He had
realized, with the cold certainty of age, that he did not have unlimited time left, and he was
spending what remained of it imprisoned by events from nearly fifty years ago. He was
afraid—terrified, really—that he would die this way, reactive and isolated, his life constricted to
the hours when he felt safe enough to exist. But there was something else too, something that
surfaced when he allowed himself to hope: the possibility that the cord around his wrists might
finally, after all these decades, loosen its grip.
