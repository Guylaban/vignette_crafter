# Priya's Accident

Priya was twenty-two when it happened. She had been driving home from her UX design internship on a
Thursday evening in late September, her mind already shifting toward the weekend, when she turned
left at an intersection she had navigated a hundred times before. The other car came from her
right—she never saw it until the moment of impact. The driver was a woman in her fifties, South
Asian like Priya herself, with streaks of gray in her pulled-back hair. The collision threw Priya
forward; the other woman's car spun. When the paramedics arrived, the woman was conscious but badly
injured—a fractured pelvis, internal bleeding, a shattered collarbone. For three days, Priya did not
know if she would live.

She did live. But Priya came to believe, with a certainty that felt absolute, that she had destroyed
that woman's life. The woman spent eight weeks in the hospital. She required surgery. She lost her
job as a nurse because she could no longer work the twelve-hour shifts. Priya learned these details
in fragments—from the insurance company, from her own mother who had befriended the woman's daughter
at the hospital—and each new fact lodged itself in her mind like a splinter she could not remove.

The first weeks after the accident, Priya could not stop replaying the moment. She would lie awake
at night constructing alternative versions: if she had left work five minutes earlier or five
minutes later, if she had taken a different route, if she had been paying closer attention, if she
had braked harder. She would spend entire afternoons at her desk, ostensibly working on wireframes,
but actually lost in these recursive loops of prevention—the intricate architecture of what she
should have done differently. Her husband, Arjun, would find her staring at her laptop screen,
having typed nothing for hours. When he asked what she was thinking about, she could not always
articulate it. The thoughts were too fast, too layered. But she was always thinking about the
accident, always retracing the geometry of that intersection, searching for the moment where she
could have changed everything.

Within two months, Priya stopped driving. At first it was conscious: she asked Arjun to drive her to
work, told him she was too anxious behind the wheel. But as the weeks accumulated, the avoidance
became absolute. She could not sit in the passenger seat either. The moment the car began to move,
her chest would tighten. Her mind would flash to the sound of metal crumpling, the sensation of
being thrown forward, the image of the other woman's face in the moment after impact—an image she
had never actually seen but had constructed so vividly in her mind that it felt like memory. She
began making excuses: she would work from home more often, ask colleagues to handle meetings across
town, take the bus when she absolutely had to go somewhere. Arjun stopped asking her to drive. He
seemed to understand that something had broken in her, though they did not talk about it directly.

The workplace itself had become a minefield. Priya worked in an open office with thirty other
people, and she had come to notice, with painful acuity, that three of her colleagues were South
Asian women in their fifties. One of them—Deepa—bore a striking resemblance to the woman she had
hit: the same build, the same way of wearing her hair pulled back, even a similar cadence to her
voice. Every time Deepa walked past Priya's desk, Priya's stomach would drop. She would find herself
unable to focus, unable to maintain the pretense of normalcy. She began arriving early and leaving
late to avoid the common spaces, eating lunch at her desk, declining team outings. Her manager had
started to notice the withdrawal. Last month, during a one-on-one, he had asked if everything was
okay. Priya had said yes, but she could see the doubt in his face.

Now, at twenty-three, Priya was exhausted. The rumination had not stopped; if anything, it had
intensified, branching into new territories. She had begun to wonder not just about what she could
have prevented, but whether she was fundamentally unsafe—whether her brain was incapable of the
attention and care that driving demanded, whether she would always be the kind of person who hurt
others through carelessness. She tried to suppress these thoughts, to push them away, but the effort
itself was exhausting. They would resurface in the shower, in the middle of conversations, at three
in the morning. What frightened her most was that she could not imagine this ever being different.
She could not imagine driving again, could not imagine working in an office without scanning every
face for the woman she had hit, could not imagine being someone who was not defined by this single
moment of failure. It was Arjun, finally, who had insisted she see someone. He said he wanted his
wife back. Priya had agreed, though she was not sure she believed that was still possible.
