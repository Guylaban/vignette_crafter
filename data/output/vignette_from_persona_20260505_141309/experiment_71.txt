# Carlos

Carlos was seventy-two when he first came to the clinic, a broad-shouldered man whose hands bore the
permanent marks of forty-seven years at the welding torch—deep creases in the palms, small scars
along the knuckles, a tremor that had worsened in recent months. His wife Maria had died three years
before, and it was only after her death that the nightmares had begun in earnest, though he had
carried something much older and heavier since 1978.

He had been twenty-five then, a union organizer in El Salvador, when the military police came to the
union hall one evening in October. There were six of them. They separated him from the others, took
him to a room with no windows, and locked the door. What happened in that room—the beatings, the
electrical burns, the rifle barrel pressed against his temple while they demanded names—Carlos could
not speak about in any linear way. When he tried to tell his daughter Rosa what had occurred, the
words would scatter like startled birds. He would begin to describe the chair they had strapped him
to, the way his wrists had bled, and then suddenly he would lose the thread entirely, his voice
trailing off, his hands shaking so badly he had to grip the table. The memory did not feel like
something that had happened forty-four years ago. It felt immediate, present-tense, as though the
room still existed somewhere just behind his eyes and he might step back into it at any moment.

After two days, they had released him. He did not know why. He had never understood why. For decades
he had told himself that he should have fought harder, should have refused to answer their
questions, should have been stronger. He carried the conviction that his own weakness—his compliance
when they threatened to bring in the others, his willingness to speak when they said they would hurt
his brother—had allowed them to win. He could not forgive himself for that. The rage that came when
he allowed himself to remember was incandescent; it would rise in his chest and neck, and he would
have to leave the room, go to the garage, grip something cold until the shaking stopped.

He had emigrated to California in 1982, married Maria, worked steadily at the fabrication shop in
Oakland. For thirty years, he had managed by not thinking about it. He had become very good at
that—at pushing the memory away the moment it surfaced, at busying his hands, at keeping his mind on
the present task. But something about aging, about the loss of Maria, about having time now instead
of the relentless forward motion of work and family, had cracked that seal.

The triggers were specific and terrifying. Any situation in which he felt trapped—a crowded
elevator, a backed-up freeway with no exit—would send his heart racing. He had begun to avoid
driving in heavy traffic, which meant he had stopped going to the shop except for the early morning
hours when the roads were clear. His supervisor had noticed. There had been conversations about
reliability. At seventy, Carlos could not afford to lose this job, but he could not bear the feeling
of being boxed in, unable to escape.

The sound of a gunshot—even from the hunting rifles of neighbors in the hills above his house—would
send him rigid. He had become hypervigilant about sudden loud noises, and this vigilance exhausted
him. He slept poorly, waking in the pre-dawn darkness convinced that someone was coming for him,
that there was no way out, that the world was fundamentally unsafe and he was fundamentally unable
to predict or prevent harm.

What troubled him most was a contradiction he could not resolve: he knew intellectually that the
regime had fallen, that he was safe in California, that the men who had done this were either dead
or scattered. But he could not feel it. He had come to believe that you could never truly know who
would harm you, that safety was an illusion, that his own judgment about people and situations could
not be trusted. When someone spoke sharply to him, or when he sensed any hint of anger or impatience
from another person, he would become suddenly, almost desperately, compliant—agreeing to things he
did not want to do, apologizing for things that were not his fault, making himself small. It was a
way of managing the terror, of trying to prevent the harm he was certain was coming.

His daughter had finally insisted he seek help. She had found him one morning standing in the garage
at 4 a.m., unable to say why he had gotten out of bed. He had agreed to come, though he was not
hopeful. What he wanted most was to stop being afraid, to be able to sit in traffic without feeling
like the walls were closing in. What he feared most was that he would never stop blaming himself,
that the rage would consume him, that this thing that had happened so long ago would take from him
everything that remained.
