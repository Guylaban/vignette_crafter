# Clinical Vignette: Katarina

Katarina was nineteen when the nightclub fire started. She had been dancing at The Meridian for
eight months—a Friday and Saturday job that paid well enough to help cover her apartment rent while
she trained at the ballet academy. The venue was packed that November evening, the kind of crowded
that made the air thick and warm. She was in the middle of her second set when she noticed the smell
first: acrid, chemical, wrong. Within seconds, the music cut out. Someone screamed. The crowd moved
toward the exits in a surge that became a crush. Katarina was near the back of the main floor when
she felt bodies pressing against her from all sides, felt her arms pinned against her ribs, felt the
heat intensify. The smoke came low and fast. She lost her footing, was lifted and carried forward by
the press of people, her feet barely touching the ground. Later, she would learn that seventeen
people died in the stairwell she was pushed toward. She made it out through a service door that
someone forced open, stumbling into the alley gasping, her lungs burning, the smell of burnt plastic
and something else—something she still cannot name—seared into her memory.

In the weeks that followed, Katarina tried to return to normal. She did not go back to the club;
that felt obvious. But the normalcy she was reaching for kept slipping away. By mid-December, she
stopped going to the academy. The studio had large mirrors and open space, and she found that when
she was dancing—when her body was supposed to be free and responsive—she would suddenly become
hyperaware of the other dancers around her, of how she could not move backward without bumping into
someone, of how the mirrors showed her exits but also showed people approaching from behind. One
afternoon, a dancer moved quickly across her peripheral vision and Katarina's body reacted as though
she were back in the crush: her breath seized, her heart hammered, and she was certain for a moment
that she was being pushed, that the walls were closing in. She did not return after that.

She began staying in her apartment more. Going out meant the possibility of crowds, of feeling
trapped or followed. She had started noticing when people were near her—really noticing, tracking
them, aware of where they were in relation to her body. Sometimes she would catch herself looking
over her shoulder, convinced someone was behind her, only to find empty sidewalk. The exhaustion was
relentless. She would collapse into sleep around nine or ten in the evening, then wake at two or
three in the morning, her body rigid with the need to check the locks, to verify that her windows
were secure, to sit in the darkness and confirm that she was alone and safe. By morning she would be
depleted.

The sensory memories came without warning and with complete vividness. One Tuesday she was making
coffee and the smell of something burning in a neighbor's apartment sent her into a state where she
could smell the nightclub again—the plastic, the smoke, the something else—and for several minutes
she was back there, her lungs burning, her arms pinned. She could not explain it to anyone. When her
mother asked why she had given up dancing, why she was barely leaving the apartment, Katarina opened
her mouth to describe what had happened, what she was experiencing, and found that there were no
words. She knew the facts. She could say "there was a fire, people died, I was trapped." But the
actual texture of the experience—the feeling of being held down by the crowd, the specific quality
of the fear, the way her body still lived inside that moment—remained locked away, inaccessible to
language.

What frightened her most was the thought that if she allowed herself to really think about what
happened, to sit with it fully, she would shatter. It felt safer to keep her mind away from it, to
stay inside, to give up the things she loved—dancing, going to cafes with friends, even the simple
pleasure of walking without scanning for threats. But the cost of that safety was becoming clear.
She was no longer the person her friends recognized. She was not a dancer anymore. She did not
belong at the academy, did not belong in crowds, did not belong anywhere that required her to be
present in her body without terror. The injustice of it burned underneath everything: she had
survived, but seventeen others had not, and nothing had changed, no one had been held accountable,
and she was the one who could not sleep, who could not breathe, who had become a prisoner in her own
apartment.

Last week, after waking at three in the morning for the hundredth time, Katarina finally called a
therapist. She did not know if it was possible to be fixed. But she knew that she could not live
like this anymore.
