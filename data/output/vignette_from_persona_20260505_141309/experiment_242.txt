# Arjun

Arjun was twenty-three when he was stopped by police outside a convenience store in Queens. He had
ducked in for milk on a Friday evening, and when he came out, an officer called him over. There was
a report of a robbery two blocks away, the officer said—a man matching his description. Arjun, whose
family had lived in America for fifteen years and who had never been in trouble, explained this
calmly. He was cooperative. He stood still as the officer patted him down, his hands moving across
Arjun's chest and ribs and legs with a methodical pressure. It was cold that night, unseasonably so
for October, and Arjun remembers the officer's hands as cold too—the shock of them through his thin
shirt, the grip firm and impersonal. A second officer arrived. They told him to turn around. They
told him to put his hands behind his back. He did. The cuffs were metal and tight, and they took him
to the precinct, where he sat in a holding cell for eight hours before his mother came, before
someone checked the store camera and saw that he had been inside the store when the robbery happened
three blocks away.

The case was dropped. But something in Arjun had fractured.

In the months that followed, he returned to his job as an editor at a small publishing house in
Manhattan, and he tried to move forward. He married Priya, a woman his parents had introduced him
to, and they found an apartment in Forest Hills. But the world had become a place of hidden threats.
He had come to believe, with a certainty that felt absolute, that he was incapable of handling what
life demanded of him. When he thought about that evening—about the pressure of the officer's hands,
the cold seeping through his clothes, the smell of the precinct's fluorescent-lit holding cell, a
chemical tang he could not quite name—grief would flood through him so completely that he could
barely breathe. His body would remember the position he had held in those cuffs, his hands locked
behind him, and he would feel his shoulders draw back and his spine curve inward, as if the memory
were not something he was recalling but something happening to him again.

The triggers came without warning. A cold hand on his shoulder—Priya touching him on a winter
morning—sent him spiraling. Being alone with Priya in their bedroom became impossible. He would find
reasons to leave the room, to stay late at the office, to sleep on the couch. He could not explain
to her why her presence, her closeness, felt dangerous, felt like it was pulling him toward
something he could not survive. When she tried to talk to him about it, when she sat beside him on
the bed and asked what was wrong, he felt the edges of reality beginning to dissolve. He would feel
unreal, as if he were watching himself from outside his body, and the panic that followed was worse
than any physical sensation.

At work, his ability to focus had deteriorated. He would be reviewing a manuscript and suddenly
smell the precinct again—that chemical, metallic odor—and lose twenty minutes to the memory. His
supervisor had begun to notice. Last month, he had missed a deadline for the first time in four
years. His supervisor, a woman, had called him into her office to discuss it. Sitting across from
her, he had felt the dissociation creeping in at the edges of his vision, and he had made an excuse
and left. He had not been back to the office since.

It was Priya who finally insisted he see someone. She was crying when she said it, frustrated and
frightened in equal measure. She told him that nothing he did seemed to help, that he was pulling
away from her, that he seemed to believe nothing good could ever happen to him again. She was right.
Arjun knew she was right, and knowing it only deepened his conviction that he was broken, that he
had failed at the most basic task of being a functional human being. He was terrified of what a
therapist might tell him, but he was more terrified of losing Priya, of confirming that he truly was
incapable of holding onto anything that mattered.

but something happening to him again.

hand on his shoulder—Priya touching him on a winter morning—sent him spiraling into a panic so acute
that his vision would narrow and his heart would hammer against his ribs. Being alone with Priya in
their bedroom became impossible. He would find reasons to leave the room, to stay late at the
office, to sleep on the couch. He could not explain to her why her presence, her closeness, felt

it. His supervisor had begun to notice. Last month, he had missed a deadline for the first time in

excuse and left. He had not been back to the office since. The publishing house had sent two emails
asking about his status. He had not responded to either.

frightened in equal measure. She told him that nothing he did seemed to help, that he was pulling
away from her, that he seemed to believe nothing good could ever happen to him again. She was right.
Arjun knew she was right, and knowing it only deepened his conviction that he was broken, that he
had failed at the most basic task of being a functional human being. He was terrified of what a
therapist might tell him, terrified that speaking the memories aloud would make them more real, more
permanent. But he was more terrified of losing Priya, of confirming that he truly was incapable of
holding onto anything that mattered. He had made the appointment on a Tuesday morning, sitting alone
in their apartment while Priya was at work, and now he was sitting in a waiting room wondering if
admitting he needed help was itself another failure, or if it might somehow be the first thing he
had done right since the moment those cuffs had closed around his wrists.
