# Layla's Story

Layla was forty-three when she first sat down in the therapist's office, her hands folded tightly in
her lap, her posture rigid despite the soft chair. She had come because her husband, Rami, had
finally insisted—not angrily, but with the exhaustion of someone who has watched the person he loves
disappear into herself over many years. She was a pharmacist, had been for twenty years, competent
and reliable, yet in the past eighteen months something had shifted so fundamentally that she could
no longer hide it even from herself.

The root lay much further back. When Layla was seven years old, her uncle—who had moved into their
home to help after her father's death—began a pattern of physical abuse that would continue until
she was fourteen. The violence came without warning: a hand across her face for spilling juice, a
belt across her back for not finishing her homework fast enough, his breath heavy with whiskey as he
cornered her in the kitchen. What she remembered most vividly, more vividly than almost any other
moment in her life, was the smell—that particular sharp, burning smell of cheap alcohol mixed with
his cologne. It was a smell that lived in her nostrils even now, decades later. When she recalled
those years, the memories did not feel like the past; they felt like something happening in real
time, the fear and pain as immediate as if she were still that small girl. Other times, when the
memory surfaced, she felt nothing at all—a strange, protective numbness that made her feel like she
was watching a film about someone else's childhood, someone else's suffering.

By her early thirties, Layla had built a careful life. She had married Rami, a kind and patient man.
She had established herself at the pharmacy where she worked, earned the respect of her colleagues,
managed the daily complexity of medications and patient safety. But the stability was fragile, held
together by avoidance so complete that she had stopped noticing how much of her life she had
narrowed.

The first real crack came about two years ago, when her workplace hired a new pharmacy manager. The
woman was competent and fair, but she had a manner of giving instructions—direct, authoritative, the
tone of someone accustomed to being obeyed. The moment Layla heard her voice issuing orders during a
shift, something in her chest seized. She found herself unable to stand near the manager without
feeling flooded with panic, certain without any rational basis that something terrible was about to
happen. She began scheduling her shifts to avoid the manager's presence. When that became
impossible, she would find reasons to step into the back room, her heart racing, her hands shaking.
At home, she began to struggle with sleep. She would lie awake for hours, her mind running through
scenarios, needing to stay alert, convinced that if she let her guard down something catastrophic
would occur.

Around the same time, she noticed herself actively avoiding anything that might bring her pleasure
or hope. When Rami suggested a vacation, she invented reasons why they couldn't go. When her sister
invited her to a celebration, she declined, though she couldn't quite articulate why the prospect of
being happy, of letting herself feel optimistic, filled her with a sense of wrongness. It was safer
to stay small, to expect nothing good. And when intrusive memories of her uncle would surface—the
sound of his footsteps, the smell of alcohol on a stranger's breath at the pharmacy—she had begun to
hurt herself deliberately, pressing her fingernails into her palm or pinching her inner arm hard
enough to leave marks. The physical pain was strange relief; it gave her something present and
controllable to focus on, a way to interrupt the memory before it overwhelmed her completely.

The turning point came three weeks ago. Rami found her in the bathroom with marks on her arms. He
didn't say much, but his face—the mix of concern and heartbreak—made her finally admit that she
couldn't manage this alone anymore. She had come to believe that she had failed at the most
fundamental thing: being a person who could live without fear, who could be a real partner to
someone who loved her. She felt helpless, certain that no matter what she did, something terrible
would find her. And nothing—not her accomplishments, not Rami's love, not the structure she had
built—felt meaningful anymore.

Now, sitting in this office, Layla was hoping that someone could help her understand why the past
still had such a grip, and whether she could ever feel safe enough to stop running from her own
life.
