# Robert's Reckoning

Robert Chen had always understood himself as a man who sat with other people's pain. At sixty-eight,
he had spent forty-two years as a school counselor, the steady presence in the office down the hall
where teenagers came to talk about their parents' divorces, their academic failures, their first
heartbreaks. He had built a reputation for genuine warmth, for the kind of listening that made kids
feel seen. His wife, Maria, used to joke that he had given away all his emotional reserves at work,
leaving her only the tired version at home. She had said it without real bitterness, the way couples
do when they have negotiated the same small resentment for decades. Robert had laughed along, though
something in him had always known it was true.

The assault happened on a Tuesday evening in March, eight months before he came to seek help. Robert
had stayed late at Lincoln High to help a student through a crisis—a junior named DeShawn whose
mother had been hospitalized. Robert had driven the boy home, waited while he got inside safely, and
was leaving the neighborhood when a man stepped out from between two parked cars. Robert remembered
the smell first: stale alcohol and something chemical, like cheap cologne mixed with sweat. The man
had a knife. What followed was a blur of movement and terror, Robert's body responding in ways he
could not control, his voice gone, his hands up. The man took his wallet, his watch, and something
else—something Robert could not quite name but felt stolen nonetheless. When the police found him
twenty minutes later, sitting on a curb three blocks away, Robert was still shaking so hard he could
barely give his name.

The physical injuries healed within weeks. But something else had fractured, and Robert found
himself unable to piece it back together. The first time he smelled the antiseptic scent of the
hospital during his follow-up appointment, his body had seized as if the knife were coming again.
His heart had raced. His vision had narrowed. He had sat in the waiting room sweating, certain he
was dying, until a nurse led him to a back office and closed the door. After that, he could not
enter a hospital without that same cascading panic, so he stopped going. His regular
physical—overdue by a year—went unscheduled.

Within weeks, the hospital smell began to find him elsewhere. The cleaning supplies used by the
custodians at Lincoln High smelled vaguely similar. Robert started timing his arrival and departure
to avoid passing the supply closet. Then he began eating lunch in his car instead of the faculty
lounge. Then he stopped attending staff meetings, claiming administrative work that required him to
stay in his office. His principal, concerned, had asked if everything was all right. Robert had said
yes, just managing some personal matters. The lie had felt necessary.

But the worst part was not the smells. It was the memory itself, which had begun to ambush him
without warning. A fragment would surface—the man's face, the smell, the particular angle of the
knife in the streetlight—and Robert would be back there, the terror identical to that Tuesday
evening, flooding through him as if no time had passed at all. These moments lasted only seconds,
but they left him shaken for hours afterward. He had learned to dissociate deliberately when he felt
one coming, to mentally leave his body, to blank himself out. It was the only thing that worked. He
would sit at his desk, seemingly present, while his mind retreated somewhere far away where the
memory could not touch him.

Maria had noticed the distance first. She had tried to hold his hand while watching television, and
he had pulled away without thinking. When she had asked what was wrong, he had said nothing, he was
just tired. But the truth was that her closeness terrified him in a way he could not articulate. The
idea of being emotionally intimate, of letting her see inside him, felt like an unbearable
vulnerability. He had begun sleeping in the guest room, telling her his snoring had gotten worse. He
stopped going to their friends' dinner parties. He avoided his adult children's phone calls. The
isolation had become total, a protective shell he did not know how to break.

It was Maria who finally insisted he see someone. She had found him one morning unable to get out of
bed, not from depression exactly but from a kind of paralysis, a fear so diffuse he could not name
its object. "This is not living," she had said quietly. "I don't know what happened to you that
night, but I know you're still there, and I want my husband back." Robert had sat across from her at
the kitchen table, feeling the weight of forty-five years of marriage, and had finally said the
words aloud: "I was attacked. I can't stop being afraid." Now, sitting in the therapist's office, he
was terrified that the fear was permanent, that he had already lost too much time, and that the man
he had been—the counselor, the listener, the husband—was gone for good.
