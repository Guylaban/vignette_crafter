# Aroha's Reckoning

Aroha was forty-three when she stopped being able to stand in her own clinic without scanning for
exits. The habit had begun quietly, almost unnoticed, in the months after the accident—a small
repositioning of her body during patient sessions, a preference for rooms with doors she could see,
a vague discomfort that crystallized only when she realized she was doing it every time. She had
come to believe that if she were ever caught off guard again, if she were trapped in a space with no
way out, something catastrophic would happen. This belief lived in her body before it lived in her
mind.

The accident had occurred on a Friday evening in late autumn, nearly three years ago. Aroha had been
working late at the private clinic where she'd built her reputation over two decades—a careful,
meticulous physical therapist known for her precision with complex cases. A new patient, Marcus, had
come in for his third session, recovering from rotator cuff surgery. He was frustrated, impatient,
wanting faster progress than his healing allowed. Aroha had explained the limitations, had been
professional and kind, but Marcus had grown agitated. He'd had a few drinks before coming—she could
smell the alcohol on him, sharp and acrid, a smell that would later become unbearable—and he'd
insisted on attempting an exercise she'd advised against. When she'd gently guided his arm to
prevent the movement, he'd pulled away violently, lost his balance, and fallen backward directly
onto the corner of the treatment table. His head struck the edge with a sound Aroha could still hear
with perfect, unwanted clarity.

The emergency room, the CT scan, the neurosurgeon's grave expression—these things had happened, and
Aroha had moved through them in a state of mechanical compliance. Marcus had survived, though with
lasting cognitive deficits and chronic pain. The investigation had cleared her of negligence. But
something in Aroha had fractured in a way no investigation could touch.

In the months that followed, she had developed an elaborate internal narrative about what she should
have done. She replayed the moment obsessively, but the memory was strange and fragmented—she could
see his face, could smell the alcohol, could feel the exact pressure of his arm in her hands, yet
when she tried to construct a coherent account of the sequence of events, the story dissolved into
disconnected images. What tormented her most was the conviction that she had failed him in some
fundamental way, that a more careful, more attentive, more *good* version of herself would have
prevented the fall entirely. This thought had calcified into something she was certain about: she
would never forgive herself for not doing more to stop what happened.

The shame that accompanied these memories was suffocating. When Aroha recalled the moment—which she
did constantly, involuntarily—she felt a burning sense that she was fundamentally changed,
fundamentally compromised. The person she had been before the accident, the competent and trusted
therapist, felt like someone she no longer recognized. She had become someone capable of harming
people, someone whose judgment could not be trusted.

To manage this unbearable internal state, Aroha had begun to spend money compulsively. Online
shopping became her escape—late nights ordering things she didn't need, the small dopamine hit of
purchase and delivery providing brief respite from the grinding shame. Her credit card debt had
climbed to nearly thirty thousand dollars. She had also, more quietly, begun using cannabis and
occasionally other substances, telling herself it helped her sleep, though mostly it seemed to
create a buffer between her waking mind and the memories that felt more real, more present, more
*true* than her actual current life.

Worse still, she had developed a hypervigilance about danger that had begun to interfere with her
work. She noticed potential weapons everywhere—the corner of a treatment table, a water bottle
someone could throw, the heavy therapy balls that could be misused. Her gaze caught on these objects
automatically, and her body tensed. Patients had begun to comment on her seeming distracted, on the
way she positioned herself, on her apparent anxiety during sessions.

Recently, a colleague had mentioned noticing that Aroha seemed "not herself." The comment had landed
like a confirmation of something Aroha already knew: she was no longer the person she had been. And
the thought of talking about the accident, of having to narrate what had happened to a therapist or
anyone else, filled her with a terror so acute that she had avoided seeking help for nearly three
years. But last week, during a routine session with a patient who had mentioned having a fall, Aroha
had experienced a flashback so vivid that she'd had to excuse herself from the room. For a moment,
she had been back in that clinic on that autumn evening, her hands on Marcus's arm, the smell of
alcohol in the air, the terrible sound of impact.

She had called her partner that evening and, for the first time, admitted that she needed help.
