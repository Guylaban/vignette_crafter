# Karim's Vigil

Karim was thirty-four when his younger brother Tariq was attacked outside a nightclub in downtown
Boston. He wasn't there. He was home in Newton, asleep beside his wife Leila, when the police called
at 2:47 a.m. Tariq had been stabbed multiple times by someone he'd never met, over an insult about a
woman neither of them knew. The knife work was frenzied and impersonal. Tariq survived the first
week in the ICU, then the second, but the infection that followed took him on a Tuesday morning
while Karim sat holding his hand, watching the monitors flatline.

In the months that followed, Karim found himself studying the world differently. At his publishing
office in Boston's Back Bay, where he managed a small team of editors, he began to notice things
he'd never paid attention to before. The way a client's jaw tightened during a contract negotiation.
The angle of a delivery man's shoulders as he approached the reception desk. Whether the security
guard's eyes tracked people as they moved through the lobby. He was certain that if he could read
these signs accurately enough—if he could catch the micro-expressions that preceded violence—he
might have been there for Tariq. Might have seen it coming. Might have stopped it.

The memories themselves were fractured and strange. He would be reviewing manuscript pages when
suddenly he was hearing the sound of Tariq's labored breathing in the hospital room, though the
office was silent. He couldn't quite remember the sequence of that final morning—the phone call, the
drive to Mass General, the moment he'd understood Tariq was truly gone—but the pieces came back to
him in random order, like a film reel with scenes spliced out of sequence and then reassembled
wrong. Sometimes he'd hear the beeping of the heart monitor even when he was at home, reading in
bed. The memories would play over and over, a loop he couldn't stop or fast-forward through, each
replay feeling like it was happening again.

He started avoiding unfamiliar places. When Leila suggested they visit a new restaurant in
Cambridge, or when a work conference required him to travel to a hotel in Providence, Karim felt a
cold dread settle in his chest. He couldn't assess the danger in those spaces. He didn't know where
the exits were, where weapons might be hidden, who posed a threat. It was easier to stay home, to
move only through the routes he'd already mapped and vetted. His team noticed when he stopped
attending industry events. His wife noticed when he declined invitations from friends.

Then came the morning he couldn't get on the bus to work. He'd taken the same route for five years,
but something had shifted. The bus was a closed space. He couldn't see all the exits at once. He
couldn't monitor every passenger's hands, every bag that might contain something sharp. He sat on
the bench at the stop for twenty minutes, watching the bus pull away, before calling an Uber. Within
two weeks, he was driving himself everywhere, a forty-minute commute instead of fifteen minutes on
transit, but at least he was in control.

The exhaustion was relentless. Even when he managed to sleep through the night—which was rare,
because the memories would wake him—he felt depleted the moment he opened his eyes. Leila said he
looked hollowed out. His productivity at work had dropped. He'd missed deadlines. His boss had
pulled him aside, gently at first, then with real concern.

The breaking point came when he couldn't undress for bed. The vulnerability of it—being naked,
defenseless, unable to respond to a threat—had become intolerable. He'd started sleeping in clothes.
When Leila tried to hold him one night, he flinched so violently he fell out of bed. She was crying.
He was crying. And he finally understood that whatever he was doing to stay safe wasn't working
anymore. That his vigilance hadn't saved Tariq, and it wasn't saving him either. That morning, he
called a therapist.
