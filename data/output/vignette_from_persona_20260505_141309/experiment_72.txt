# Amara

Amara was thirty-five years old when she walked into the precinct one morning in March and found
Officer Derek Chen waiting by the locker room. The memory of what happened next—what happened in the
storage closet on the third floor where she had gone to retrieve evidence bags—remained fractured.
She could recall his hands, the force of them, the shock of betrayal that cut deeper than the
physical assault itself. But there were gaps. Whole minutes she could not account for, no matter how
many times she tried to reconstruct the sequence. She remembered the cold concrete wall against her
back. She remembered the sound of his breathing. But the moments between her entering the room and
the moment she was on the floor—those had simply vanished, locked away in a part of her mind she
could not access.

In the weeks that followed, Amara had returned to work because that was what officers did. They
showed up. They moved forward. But her body had not cooperated with that resolve. Whenever she
encountered a situation that might escalate—a domestic call where voices were raised, a traffic stop
where a driver became argumentative—her chest would tighten and she would find herself withdrawing,
handing the situation off to a partner, anything to avoid the conflict. She had come to believe that
confrontation was dangerous, that her judgment in such moments was compromised, that she was no
longer the officer she had been before. Her sergeant had begun to notice. Two weeks ago, he had
pulled her aside after she declined to take the lead on a felony stop. The conversation was gentle,
but it carried weight: they were concerned about her. Was everything all right?

Everything was not all right. When the memory of the assault returned—and it returned often,
unbidden, usually when she was alone—Amara felt the pain return with it. Not as an echo or a shadow,
but as a physical presence: a burning in her shoulders, a deep ache in her lower back, the sensation
of pressure in her throat. Her body remembered what her mind could not fully articulate. And with
the pain came rage. A fury so intense it frightened her. She would find herself gripping the kitchen
counter until her hands ached, or sitting in her car in the precinct parking lot with her fists
clenched, the anger radiating through her like a fever.

Her husband Marcus had begun to notice the distance between them. Amara avoided situations that
might draw out strong emotion—conversations about the future, discussions about why she had become
withdrawn, even moments of intimacy that had once felt natural. She could not bear the vulnerability
of feeling deeply around another person, even him. She had come to believe that she had permanently
changed, that whatever had happened to her in that closet had fundamentally altered who she was. The
woman Marcus had married—confident, grounded, connected to her extended family and the tight network
of relationships that had always sustained her—that woman was gone. Amara no longer attended her
cousin's church gatherings. She had stopped calling her aunts. She spent hours imagining how
different everything would be if she had simply taken a different route that morning, if she had
sent someone else to retrieve those evidence bags, if Derek Chen had never been hired. The
counterfactual thoughts consumed her, a relentless loop of *if only*, each iteration deepening her
conviction that she had somehow failed to prevent her own assault.

It was Marcus who finally insisted she seek help. He had found her in the garage one evening,
sitting in the dark, and he had held her while she wept with a rage and despair she could no longer
contain. She was terrified that therapy would force her to remember the parts she had lost, that
speaking about the assault would make it more real. But she was also desperate to find her way back
to herself—to her work, to her marriage, to the community that had shaped her. She did not know if
that version of herself still existed, or if the damage was truly permanent, but she was willing to
try.
