# Clinical Vignette: Sorn

Sorn was thirty-eight years old when she came to the intake appointment, and she arrived precisely
on time—a habit born from years of needing to know exactly where she was and when. She was a stock
broker at a mid-sized firm in the financial district, a role that had once felt like proof of her
capability, her worth, her control over her own life. That was before.

Three years ago, a man named David Reeves had become convinced he was in love with her. They had met
once, briefly, at a networking event. She had been polite. He had interpreted this as invitation.
What followed was eighteen months of escalating contact: emails that grew increasingly personal,
then frantic; messages left on her work phone; appearances at her apartment building, sometimes at
night. When she changed her number, he found her on social media. When she blocked him, he created
new accounts. The police were involved, a restraining order was filed, and eventually he was
arrested after he was found outside her apartment at two in the morning with a notebook containing
detailed observations of her daily movements. The terror of that period—of never knowing when or
where he would appear, of the violation of being watched, of his certainty that he knew her better
than she knew herself—had been all-consuming.

Now, three years later, Sorn was still living in the aftermath of those eighteen months. The
traumatic event itself had a quality of unreality that disturbed her. When she tried to recount it
to her therapist, she found herself saying, "It sounds insane when I say it out loud. It sounds like
something that happened to someone else. But it happened to me." The specificity of his
observations—the exact times she left for work, the coffee shop she frequented on Thursdays, the
color of her car—had been so meticulous, so detailed, that it had shattered her sense of privacy.
Yet when she tried to hold the full weight of it in her mind, it felt too extreme, too cinematic.
This disconnect was itself a torment: she knew intellectually that it had been real, but her mind
seemed unwilling to accept the scale of the violation.

When the memory surfaced—and it did so without warning, triggered by ordinary moments in her day—the
distinction between then and now collapsed entirely. A loud noise in the office, a car door
slamming, a phone ringing with an unexpected caller ID, and suddenly she was back in that apartment,
heart racing, convinced that he was there, that the restraining order had been lifted, that the
police had made a mistake. The terror was identical to what she had felt in those months: the same
electrical surge through her body, the same certainty that something catastrophic was about to
happen. It did not feel like memory. It felt like present danger.

Alcohol smell was another trigger, though the connection was indirect. David had shown up at her
building reeking of whiskey on several occasions, his speech slurred but his purpose clear. Now,
when she caught the scent—at a restaurant, from a colleague's breath—her nervous system responded as
if he were in the room. Her partner had begun to notice her flinching away from him after work
drinks.

Most painful was her reaction to happy families. When she saw a couple with young children, or heard
a friend describe their family dinner, something in her fractured. These scenes of safety, of
belonging, of the assumption that you could build a life without it being invaded and contaminated
by someone else's obsession, triggered a grief so acute it felt almost like the original terror. She
had come to believe that such safety was an illusion, that vigilance and isolation were the only
honest ways to live. Her own relationship, a three-year partnership with Marcus, had begun to suffer
under the weight of her hypervigilance and her conviction that closeness itself was dangerous.

It was Marcus who had finally insisted she seek help. He had found her one morning sitting in the
dark, unable to explain why, unable to move. She had called in sick to work for the third time that
week. Her firm had begun to ask questions about her performance. She came to the appointment because
staying as she was had become unsustainable, but she was terrified that talking about it would make
it more real, not less.
