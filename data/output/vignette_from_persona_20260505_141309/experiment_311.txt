Amara was thirty-four when armed men entered the compound where she worked as a nurse in a rural
clinic in South Sudan. That was eight years ago. She had been checking supplies in the back room
when the shooting started—a sound she would come to know in her body before her mind could name it.

both vivid and impossible, a sequence of violence and humiliation so extreme that even now, sitting
in the therapist's office, she could not quite believe it had occurred to her. Sometimes she
wondered if the memory belonged to someone else entirely, a story she had absorbed rather than
lived. Yet her body knew. Her body remembered with perfect, treacherous clarity.

When she finally escaped the country two years later, she carried that memory like a second
skeleton. She had moved to the United States, found work at a factory assembling medical
components—ironic, she thought sometimes, that she was back around medical equipment but in a
different form of captivity. The factory floor was loud and predictable. She could control it. What
she could not control was the world outside.

The first year in America, Amara had tried to live normally. She wore her old clothes, the bright
fabrics she loved. But in the spring, when the weather turned warm and humid, matching the season
when she had been taken, her body had begun to betray her. The smell of rain on hot earth—a smell
that had been present that day—would arrive on the breeze, and suddenly she could not breathe. She
had stopped wearing the clothes associated with that time. She had stopped going outside when the
weather felt similar. Gradually, she had narrowed the world.

At home, she listened. Always listening. For footsteps on the stairs, for the sound of a key in the
lock, for voices that might indicate someone was coming. Her apartment was on the second floor, and
she had memorized every creak of the building, every sound that meant safety and every sound that
meant danger. When her ex-husband had tried to visit last year—a man named Kofi, a name that
appeared sometimes in conversation—she had felt her entire body tense, flooded with a fear that made
no logical sense. He was not one of them. But her body did not distinguish. Her body reacted to
danger before her mind could catch up, before she could remind herself that she was safe, that this
was America, that it was only a name.

happened to her skin, make herself unrecognizable even to herself. At the factory, her supervisor
had asked if she was all right. She had said yes. She had not mentioned that she flinched at every
unexpected sound, that her coworkers had begun to notice how she startled at the normal noises of

mentioned that she had stopped attending the evening shifts because darkness made the listening
worse, and she had been quietly demoted to day work, a consequence no one discussed but everyone
understood.

What frightened Amara most was that her body no longer felt like her own. She could not trust it to
react appropriately, to distinguish between real danger and the ghosts of danger. She was no longer
capable of the things that had once defined her—the steadiness, the presence of mind, the ability to
help others without falling apart. The world had shown itself to be a place where men could take
what they wanted, and no amount of vigilance could prevent that. She was forty-two now, and she had
come to believe that she would spend the rest of her life listening for footsteps, jumping at
sounds, trying to paint over the past with makeup and mental distraction. Last month, her sister had
finally convinced her to call someone. She had made the appointment. She was here. And what she was
most afraid of, more than anything, was that this was simply who she was now.
