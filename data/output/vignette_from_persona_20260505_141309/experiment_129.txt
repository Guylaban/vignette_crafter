# Karim's Story

Karim was thirty-four when the diagnosis came back. The oncologist had used the word "lymphoma" in a
small beige room at Massachusetts General Hospital, and Karim remembered the smell of the
place—antiseptic and something underneath it, something organic and wrong—more clearly than he
remembered the doctor's exact words. What he did hold onto was the certainty that he was going to
die. Not the statistical likelihood of survival that the doctor had cited, but the absolute
knowledge in his chest that his body was betraying him, that the cells multiplying inside him were
proof that he could no longer trust anything his own body told him.

The chemotherapy lasted eight months. Karim continued working at his salon in Cambridge for the
first three rounds, arriving in the morning with a scarf tied carefully around his head after his
hair began to fall out in clumps. His regular clients asked what was wrong; he told them he had an
infection. The nausea was constant. The pain moved—sometimes in his abdomen, sometimes in his chest,
sometimes radiating down his left arm in a way that made him certain the cancer had spread. He would
feel a sharp sensation and his mind would spiral: *it's everywhere, it's in my lungs, it's in my
brain*. His body had become a landscape of threat, and he had no way to read its signals anymore. A
normal ache could mean nothing, or it could mean death. There was no way to know.

By the time he finished treatment at thirty-five, Karim had achieved remission. The scans were
clear. The oncologist used words like "excellent prognosis" and "long-term survival." But something
had broken in the space between his mind and his body, and it did not repair itself with the
disappearance of the cancer cells.

Now, three years later, Karim lives in a state of constant vigilance. His body feels as though it is
always braced for collapse. Even in the mornings when he wakes up feeling fine, there is a baseline
tension in his shoulders and chest, a readiness for the next catastrophe. He has come to believe
that this tension itself is dangerous—that his nervous system is malfunctioning, that he is losing
his grip on reality. The idea that he might be going crazy frightens him more than the cancer ever
did.

The triggers are specific and relentless. Photographs of himself from before the
diagnosis—particularly ones from the salon, where he is smiling, unaware—create an immediate surge
of dread and anger. He cannot explain it rationally, but seeing his own face from that time feels
like looking at a ghost. He has deleted most of them from his phone. When he wakes from nightmares,
he lies in bed for hours in the dark, afraid to move, afraid that any change in his body's state
might signal something ominous. The dreams are not always about cancer; sometimes they are about the
hospital, the smell of it, the feeling of powerlessness. He wakes gasping, and the panic does not
fade quickly.

Medical appointments have become nearly impossible. Last month, his primary care doctor wanted to do
routine blood work. Karim went to the lab, sat in the waiting room, and felt such an overwhelming
surge of terror that he left without being seen. The thought of having his blood drawn—of his body
being punctured, of results that might change everything—was intolerable. He has begun to avoid
these appointments entirely, which means he has not had his annual physical in two years. This
avoidance has consequences: his doctor has stopped calling, which Karim interprets as abandonment,
which confirms his fear that his body is too damaged, too unreliable, for anyone to care for.

At the salon, his work has suffered. He used to take pride in his precision with color and cut, but
his hands shake now. Sometimes, mid-appointment, a sharp pain will shoot through his chest and he
will freeze, convinced that this is the moment, that the cancer has returned. His clients have
noticed. Some have begun requesting other stylists. The owner has asked, gently, if everything is
okay. Karim has not told him the truth.

What finally brought Karim to seek help was a moment three weeks ago when he woke from a nightmare
and found himself unable to distinguish between the dream and waking reality. He lay in bed and
could smell the hospital—that same antiseptic and decay—even though he was in his bedroom. The pain
in his abdomen that had haunted him during treatment suddenly returned, vivid and localized, even
though he knew logically that nothing was wrong. He sat on the edge of his bed for an hour,
trembling, unable to trust his own perceptions. When it finally passed, he was struck by a thought
that terrified him more than any symptom: *I am losing my mind. My own body has become my enemy, and
I cannot trust anything it tells me anymore.*
