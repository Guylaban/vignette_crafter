# Catalina

Catalina Reyes was forty-four when she first sat in a therapist's office, her hands folded so
tightly in her lap that her knuckles had gone pale. She had spent twenty years building a career as
a diplomat, moving through postings in Central America and the Caribbean with the kind of steady
professionalism that had earned her respect in the State Department. Then, three years ago, she had
requested assignment to a country in the grip of civil conflict—not out of naïveté, but out of a
conviction that her presence might matter, that she could help negotiate safe passage for civilians
caught between warring factions. That conviction had carried her through her first six months
without incident.

The night of March 14th had begun like many others. Catalina had been working late at the embassy
compound when armed men—she was never certain which faction they represented—forced their way past
security. They had separated her from her colleagues, pushing her into a storage room and holding
her there for hours. One of them had pinned her arms behind her back, his grip so forceful that she
felt certain her shoulders would dislocate. She had been questioned about embassy operations, about
troop movements, about which side she favored. The men had been methodical rather than frenzied, and
that precision had terrified her more than rage would have. When they finally released her, she had
been unharmed in any measurable way. No one had touched her sexually. She had not been beaten. But
something in how she understood herself—her competence, her judgment, her very solidity as a
person—had fractured that night.

She had returned to the States on medical leave, telling colleagues it was temporary, that she
needed to decompress. What she did not tell them was that she had begun to unravel in ways she could
not name. Within weeks, she found herself replaying the night obsessively, not to process it but to
hunt for the moment she might have prevented it. If she had left the office earlier. If she had
insisted on more security. If she had read the intelligence reports differently. She went over and
over the sequence, convinced that somewhere in her choices lay the explanation for why this had
happened to her specifically—as though understanding the mechanism of her own failure might restore
some sense of control. Except it never did. Each cycle of rumination left her more exhausted and no
closer to an answer that satisfied her.

What frightened her most was that she had come to believe she could not be trusted to make sound
decisions anymore. The men who held her had asked her questions designed to test her loyalties, and
she had answered them—carefully, strategically, but she had answered. She was certain now that she
had compromised something essential in herself that night, that she had revealed a capacity for
self-preservation that overrode principle. She no longer knew if she could trust herself to do the
right thing in any situation, and the weight of that doubt made her question whether she deserved to
continue in a profession that demanded moral clarity. She felt hollowed out, like a vessel that had
been used for purposes not her own and could never be quite clean again.

At her desk in the small consulting firm where she had taken a lateral position, Catalina found
herself unable to participate in meetings where colleagues discussed military interventions or
geopolitical strategy. When a junior associate had casually mentioned a documentary about armed
conflict, Catalina had excused herself and spent twenty minutes in a bathroom stall, her chest
tight. She had taken to suppressing any thought that drifted toward that night, pushing it away with
such force that she often found herself staring blankly at her computer screen, unable to remember
what she had been working on. The suppression worked for hours at a time, but the cost was a kind of
emotional numbness that had begun to isolate her. Friends had stopped calling as frequently; she had
declined every invitation to dinner or the theater. Nothing felt meaningful anymore—not her work,
not her relationships, not even the small rituals that had once grounded her. She moved through her
days as though watching herself from outside her own body, performing the role of Catalina Reyes
without inhabiting it.

It was her supervisor's gentle suggestion that she seek help that finally brought her to the
appointment. She had resisted for months, but the alternative was continuing to disappear into
herself, and somewhere beneath the numbness, she was still afraid enough to want that to stop.
