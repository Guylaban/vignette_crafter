# Arun

Arun was twenty-two when he returned from Syria. He had gone there as a relief worker with a small
NGO, driven by a conviction that he could help—that his presence, his willingness to move toward
suffering rather than away from it, mattered. He had been there for eight months. In the sixth
month, during a distribution of medical supplies in a neighborhood that had been quiet for weeks, an
airstrike came without warning. The sound came first—a roar that seemed to split the sky—and then
the ground moved. Arun was thrown backward into a concrete wall. Around him, people he had been
standing with moments before were no longer recognizable. He was not physically injured, but he was
covered in dust and in things he did not want to name. He lay there for what felt like hours, though
it was perhaps minutes, unable to move, unable to understand why his body would not obey him. A
soldier—he could not remember which faction, could not remember anything except the uniform, the
rifle, the face above his—pulled him up and moved him away from the site. Arun was evacuated the
next day.

When he came home to California, his parents were relieved to have him alive. His younger sister
hugged him at the airport. He smiled and said he was fine. Within two weeks, he knew that was not
true.

The first thing Arun noticed was that his body had learned to expect danger in ways his mind had
not. Loud noises—a car backfiring, a door slamming in his apartment building—would send his heart
racing so violently that he thought he might be having a heart attack. His hands would shake for
hours afterward. He began to avoid the main campus paths where construction work was happening,
taking longer routes to class, arriving late, missing seminars. What frightened him most was that he
could not control any of it. His body simply reacted, and he was a passenger in it, helpless to stop
the cascade of panic that would begin without warning. He had come to believe that his body was
working against him, that it had become an enemy he carried with him everywhere. The worst part was
knowing that nothing was actually happening—that he was safe in a way that the people he had left
behind would never be—and yet his body refused to believe it.

The second thing that happened was slower, more insidious. Arun began to notice police cars
differently. There was one that often parked near the library where he studied. The first time he
saw it, his chest tightened. The second time, he moved to a different library across town. By the
third week, he had stopped going to libraries altogether. Then he started avoiding the streets where
he might encounter uniformed officers. His route to campus became a careful choreography of side
streets and parking lots. When he passed a police officer on the sidewalk, he would look down, his
jaw clenched, his breathing shallow. He told himself it was safer not to be seen, not to be watched.
But deeper than that was a conviction that had crystallized in those weeks: he had failed. He had
gone to help people, and people had died. He had not stopped it. He could not stop anything. And
now, in this safe place, his body was still trying to protect him from a threat he could not see,
which meant he could not protect himself from anything either.

His social life had contracted without him quite deciding it would. Friends from his undergraduate
years—people he had been close to before Syria—began reaching out. Coffee? A movie? Arun would
decline, offering vague excuses about being busy with coursework. The truth was that he could not
bear to see them. When he imagined sitting across from someone he knew, making conversation, he felt
a wave of shame so acute that it was easier simply not to go. What would he say? That he had failed?
That his body was broken? That he was watching the door the entire time, monitoring for threats? He
had become someone else, and he was not sure how to explain that to people who knew the version of
him that had left for Syria.

He came to his first appointment in late autumn, referred by his university's disability services
after he had missed too many classes. His hands fidgeted constantly. He could not quite meet the
clinician's eyes, though he tried. What brought him in, he said quietly, was that he could not keep
living like this—avoiding people, avoiding streets, his body constantly on alert—but he also could
not imagine how to stop.
