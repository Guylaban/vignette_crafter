# Clinical Vignette: Rajesh Patel

Rajesh Patel was fifty-three when he first came to the clinic, though his wife had been urging him
to seek help for nearly two years. He had spent the spring of 2011 in Aleppo, Syria, as part of a
humanitarian team, contracted to restore murals in the old city—work he had spent thirty years
perfecting. He was a painter of considerable skill, trained first in Lahore before emigrating to the
United States in his twenties. The job in Aleppo was meant to be the capstone of his career, a
chance to preserve something beautiful amid growing instability. He had arrived with confidence,
even excitement.

On April 19th, a building three streets from where his team was working took a direct hit. Rajesh
was mixing pigment when the blast came—a sound so vast and total it seemed to reorganize the air
itself. The percussion threw him backward. What followed was chaos: dust so thick he could not see
his own hands, the smell of concrete dust and burning fuel and something else he could not name,
screams in Arabic and English, and a paralyzing understanding that he had no control over what
happened next. A colleague was killed. Rajesh was evacuated within hours. He spent two weeks in a
field hospital with a fractured collarbone and hearing damage in his right ear.

He returned to his home in New Jersey in early May. For the first month, he told himself he was
simply recovering. But recovery did not come. By June, he noticed that certain smells—the acrid odor
of burning plastic, the chemical tang of paint thinner, even the smell of concrete dust from a
neighbor's renovations—would send him into a state of acute panic. His body would remember itself in
that moment of helplessness, and he would feel the phantom sensation of being thrown backward, his
muscles clenching as if bracing for impact. He began to avoid his workshop, the place where he had
spent most of his adult life. He told his wife, Priya, that he was taking a break from commissions.
What he did not tell her was that the thought of standing in that space, of smelling the materials
of his trade, filled him with a dread he could not articulate.

Instead, he threw himself into other things. He began obsessively reorganizing the garage,
refinishing the basement, taking on projects around the house that required his hands but not his
mind. The busyness helped. When he was moving, when his body was occupied, the rumination quieted
somewhat. But he could not stop turning the event over in his mind, searching for the moment where
he should have made a different choice. Why had he gone to Syria? Why had he been mixing paint at
exactly that moment? What had he done to deserve this? The questions formed a loop he could not
break.

Loud noises became intolerable. A car backfiring on the street sent him into a state of acute
vigilance that lasted for hours. He found himself scanning rooms when he entered them, noting exits,
assessing threat. His body felt as though it was constantly wound tight, waiting for the next blast.
He stopped going to the grocery store because the beeping of the register, the clang of shopping
carts, the general unpredictability of public spaces made him unbearably anxious. Priya began doing
the shopping alone.

By late summer, he had stopped reading the news. Images of conflict anywhere in the world triggered
a cascade of panic and a desperate need to escape—to turn off the screen, leave the room, do
anything to avoid the reminder that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that control was an
illusion, that he was no longer the capable man he had been.

The memory itself had taken on a quality of unreality. When he recalled the moment of impact, it
felt like something he had witnessed rather than experienced, something too extreme to have actually
happened to him. Yet his body knew otherwise. Sometimes, sitting at the dinner table, he would feel
himself slump backward in the chair, his shoulders hunching, his breath shallow, as if his body was
reliving the moment of being thrown. When this happened, he would feel a grief so overwhelming that
he could not speak. He grieved not just for the colleague who had died, but for the life he had
lost—the meaning he had derived from his work, the identity of a master craftsman, the belief that
he could still create something lasting in the world.

Rajesh had taken early retirement from his painting business. He was no longer capable, he believed,
of the precision and presence that the work demanded. The studio remained closed. His wife watched
her husband become a ghost in their own home, and finally, in desperation, she made the appointment
for him. He came to the clinic because Priya insisted, because he was drowning, and because some
small part of him still wondered if there was any way back to being the man he had been before April
19th.
