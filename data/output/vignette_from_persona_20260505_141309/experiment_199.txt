# Aroha's Burden

Aroha had always been the woman people turned to. At fifty-six, she had spent thirty years in social
ministry work, first in her native Samoa before immigrating to the United States, and then in her
adopted home, where she had built a reputation as someone who showed up—literally and
figuratively—for the most vulnerable. Her office at the community center was a place where survivors
came to speak, where the broken-hearted found themselves heard. She prided herself on this work, on
the steadiness it required, on the way her presence alone seemed to calm people down. That identity
had been woven so tightly into her sense of self that she could barely imagine who she was without
it.

Eight months ago, a man she had known for five years—a client she had helped through addiction
recovery, someone she believed had genuinely changed—had come to her home. Her husband was at his
brother's house that evening. The man had asked if he could use the phone. She had said yes. What
happened in her bedroom in the space of twenty minutes had fractured something she was only now
beginning to understand might never fully heal.

In the months that followed, Aroha had noticed her body had developed its own logic, one that no
longer answered to her conscious will. Her muscles remained perpetually taut, as though braced for
impact. Even sitting at her kitchen table with her husband of thirty-two years, she felt wound tight
enough to snap. The tension never truly released, not even when she slept—and sleep had become
increasingly rare. Her hypervigilance had become a second job: she found herself tracking every
sound in the house, cataloguing exits, assessing threats in rooms she had inhabited peacefully for
decades. At work, she positioned herself always with a clear line to the door during client
sessions, something she had never done before. The irony was not lost on her—she was supposed to be
the safe person in the room, yet she no longer felt safe anywhere.

The memories had taken on a quality that was almost unbearable in its clarity. She could replay the
moment he had locked the bedroom door—the click of it, the way the light from the hallway had
narrowed to nothing. She could feel the texture of her own panic as though it were happening now,
not eight months ago. The memory did not wait for her permission; it would surface while she was
making breakfast, while she was driving to work, while she was trying to listen to a client speak.
The film, as she had come to think of it, would simply play, pulling her backward into that room,
her body responding as though the danger were present and immediate.

What terrified her most was the belief that had settled into her bones: that she should have known.
That someone else—someone stronger, someone more perceptive—would have sensed his intention and
stopped him. She had spent her career reading people, understanding their pain, and yet she had
failed to read the one person who had hurt her most. The failure felt less like a mistake and more
like a revelation of something fundamentally broken inside her. There was something wrong with her
as a person, she had come to believe. How else could this have happened?

Her coping had become increasingly elaborate. She had begun wearing heavier makeup than she ever had
before—foundation that felt like armor, lipstick that made her feel less like the woman who had been
violated. She overate in the evenings, consuming entire packages of biscuits or takeaway meals in a
kind of trance, the fullness in her stomach a strange comfort that lasted only as long as the eating
itself. She checked the locks on doors and windows obsessively, sometimes three or four times after
already checking them. Her husband had stopped commenting on it.

The worst part was what had happened to her work. Two months ago, she had requested a leave of
absence. The thought of sitting across from vulnerable people, of being the steady presence they
needed, had become impossible. How could she hold space for others' trauma when her own body was
screaming that she was no longer capable of anything? The woman who had defined herself through her
ability to help had stepped away from helping entirely. She had not told her colleagues the real
reason. She had simply said she needed time.

It was her husband who had finally insisted she call someone. Not because he did not understand—he
did, in his patient way—but because he could see her disappearing, day by day, into a smaller and
smaller version of herself. Aroha had made the appointment last week, hoping that perhaps someone
could tell her whether this version of herself, the one who was constantly on guard, who felt
defective at her core, who no longer recognized her own capacity for strength, might ever feel like
home again.
