# Clinical Vignette: María

María was thirty-eight when her brother Diego died. It happened on a Tuesday afternoon in March,
sudden and without warning—a myocardial infarction while he was driving home from his construction
job. He was forty-one. The police found him slumped against the steering wheel on the shoulder of
Route 9, and the sirens that had wailed through the neighbourhood that day became, in her mind, the
sound of the world breaking apart. She had not been there. She was at the factory where she had
worked for sixteen years, operating a stamping press, when her sister-in-law's call came through. By
the time María arrived at the hospital, Diego was already dead.

In the weeks that followed, María found herself unable to move forward. She replayed the last
conversation she had had with Diego—a brief phone call two days before his death in which he had
complained about traffic and asked if she wanted him to pick up groceries. She had said no, she was
fine, she would shop herself. She went over that exchange again and again, searching for some sign
she had missed, some moment where she might have detected something wrong in his voice, some way she
could have prevented what came next. She knew, rationally, that a conversation about groceries held
no predictive power, but the knowing did not stop her from turning it over in her mind during her
shift, during her commute, late into the night. The details had become a kind of compulsion—she
could not rest without examining them, as though understanding exactly how and why it happened might
somehow unmake it.

What frightened María most was the possibility that she was fundamentally changed, that the person
she had been before March was gone. She had come to believe that her life had been destroyed by
Diego's death, that there was no trajectory forward that did not involve permanent misery. She used
to laugh easily. She had had friends at the factory, women she would sit with at lunch and share
gossip and complaints. Now, when one of them mentioned her own mother's recent illness or spoke
about losing a cousin years ago, María would feel a tightness in her chest and an overwhelming need
to leave the conversation. She began eating her lunch alone in her car. When her coworker Rosa asked
if everything was okay, if she wanted to talk, María found herself asking Rosa repeatedly for
reassurance—"Do you think I'm going to be okay? Do you think I'm handling this right?"—even as she
was pulling away, even as she was making it clear through her distance that she did not want to be
known.

She stopped attending family gatherings. Her mother had asked her to come to a quinceañera for a
cousin, and María had said no, she couldn't, she just couldn't be around people celebrating. The
thought of a funeral or cemetery visit was unbearable—she had not been to Diego's grave since the
burial, and the idea of it, of standing in that place where his body lay, of seeing his name carved
into stone, made her feel as though she might collapse. Even conversations about loss in the
abstract became dangerous. When her supervisor mentioned that he had lost his father, María excused
herself and spent twenty minutes in the bathroom stall, breathing unevenly, unable to return to the
floor.

The sirens were everywhere. They came through the factory district regularly, and each time one
passed—the wail building and then fading—María's body would jolt, her hands would shake, and she
would have to step away from her machine. She had been written up twice for safety violations, for
not wearing her protective gear properly because her concentration had fractured. Her supervisor had
begun to watch her more closely, and she knew it was only a matter of time before her employment
became precarious.

It was her mother who finally insisted she seek help. María had called her in tears one evening
after hearing a siren and spending an hour unable to do anything but sit in the dark. Her mother,
who had lost a son and was managing her own grief, said that María was suffering in a way that was
beyond what either of them could fix alone. María agreed, though she did not believe that anything
could truly help, that any therapist could restore what had been lost or make her feel as though she
belonged to the world again. But she made the appointment, because her mother asked, because she was
afraid of losing her job, because somewhere beneath the conviction that her life was destroyed lay a
faint, terrified hope that she might be wrong.
