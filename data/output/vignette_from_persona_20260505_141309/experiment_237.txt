# Clinical Vignette: Linh

Linh was sixty-one when she came to the clinic on a Tuesday afternoon in November, her husband Minh
waiting in the reception area. She had been a sculptor for thirty-eight years—a respected one, known
in the regional art community for her carved wooden pieces that seemed to breathe—but she had not
entered her studio in four months. That absence, Minh had gently insisted, was why they were here.

The traumatic event itself lived in Linh's body with a vividness that ordinary memory could not
match. Eighteen months earlier, while volunteering at a community center in her neighborhood, she
had witnessed her close friend and fellow artist, Diane, collapse during a pottery class. Diane had
suffered a sudden massive stroke. Linh had watched her friend's face go slack, watched her body fold
onto the clay-dusted floor, had heard the terrible silence that followed before someone screamed.
She had held Diane's hand while waiting for the ambulance, feeling the warmth drain from it. Diane
died three days later in the hospital. Linh had been there when it happened, and she had been there
at the end.

When Linh tried to describe this to the therapist, the narrative fractured. She would begin: "We
were working on our pieces, and Diane was at her wheel, and then—" and then her voice would thin,
and she would lose the thread entirely. The details were not missing; they were too vivid, too
present, crowding out the structure of language. The memory of Diane's hand in hers felt more real
to Linh than the memory of her own wedding day, more textured and immediate than events from just
last week. This quality of hyper-presence made it impossible to narrate cleanly. When she tried to
explain it even to herself, lying awake at night, the story dissolved into fragments: the smell, the
silence, the weight of that hand.

It was the smell that had become the first and most immediate trigger. Diane had worn a distinctive
perfume—something with sandalwood and amber—and it had been present in that moment, clinging to her
clothes and skin. Now, whenever Linh encountered a similar fragrance in a store, or on a stranger
passing by, her body would jolt as though the floor had shifted beneath her. Her heart would race.
Her hands would tremble. A month ago, Minh had worn a new cologne to a dinner, something faintly
reminiscent of Diane's scent, and Linh had excused herself and sat in the car for forty minutes,
unable to return.

The footsteps were worse. In her studio, which was attached to the back of their house, Linh had
begun to feel unsafe. Minh's footsteps on the hardwood floor—the sound of him moving through the
kitchen above her workspace, or approaching the studio door—had become a source of dread. She found
herself listening for them, tensing at every creak. The unpredictability of when he might come down,
whether he might bring news or a visitor or simply want to check on her, had made the space feel
dangerous. By August, she had stopped going in at all. She told Minh it was a creative block. It was
not.

Night had become the most treacherous time. Linh lay awake beside Minh, her body rigid, scanning the
darkness for threats she could not name. She was certain that something terrible would happen at
night, that safety was a daytime illusion. Her vigilance was exhausting and unrelenting. She had
come to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous—that the sudden, arbitrary loss she had
witnessed could strike anyone, anywhere, without warning. And she had come to believe something
about herself as well: that there was something wrong with her for not being able to move past this,
for being so fragile, for having failed Diane somehow by not preventing what could not have been
prevented. People could not be trusted with her vulnerability. Minh loved her, she knew this, but
she could not trust even him with the full weight of what she carried.

What finally brought her to seek help was Minh's quiet observation that she had become a stranger in
her own home—and in her own life. The woman he had married was a maker of things, someone who moved
through the world with intention and grace. That woman had disappeared. Linh was afraid that if she
did not find her way back, she might disappear entirely. She was also terrified that trying to speak
about what happened would only make it more real.
