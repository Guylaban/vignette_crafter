# Priya's Unfinished Narrative

Priya Desai had spent twenty-three years in the Foreign Service, building a reputation as a
meticulous, unflappable negotiator. Colleagues knew her as someone who could sit through fourteen-
hour sessions on trade agreements without losing focus, who remembered the names of junior staffers'
children, who never raised her voice. That person had largely ceased to exist on the afternoon of
March 14th, when a corroded pipe in the basement ventilation system of the embassy in Dushanbe
ruptured, releasing a plume of industrial-grade chlorine that she had walked directly into while
inspecting the building's safety infrastructure.

The burn came first—a chemical rawness in her throat and lungs that made her gasp, then retch. But
what remained longest was the taste: acrid, metallic, like copper mixed with something caustic that
coated her mouth and throat for hours even after she was evacuated and treated. The embassy doctor
said she was fortunate; exposure had been brief, and she had been moved to fresh air quickly. She
spent three days in a Tajik hospital, breathing supplemental oxygen, undergoing pulmonary function
tests that came back reassuring. By the time she returned to Washington for follow-up, the acute
symptoms had largely resolved. The physical damage, the doctors agreed, was minimal.

What no one had predicted was that the memory would arrive without warning, complete and
overwhelming. Three weeks after the incident, Priya was sitting in a staff meeting when someone
opened a window, and a breeze carried the faint smell of cleaning solution. In that instant, the
basement materialized around her—not as a thought, but as a present reality. She could taste the
chlorine again, thick and choking. Her body went rigid. Her heart accelerated. She excused herself
and spent twenty minutes in a restroom stall, breathing into her hands, waiting for the world to
reassemble itself. She told no one.

The fear that followed was not of the chemical itself, but of the unpredictability of her own mind.
Priya had come to believe that stress itself was dangerous—that the surge of adrenaline and panic
she felt when triggered might somehow trigger another exposure, or that her body's alarm response
meant something was genuinely wrong. She began avoiding anything that might provoke that state. She
declined a promotion that would have required travel to three different regions. She stopped
attending the diplomatic receptions she had once enjoyed, reasoning that the stimulation and crowd
noise made her feel on edge. She began taking longer routes to work to avoid the building's basement
entrance, even though it added forty minutes to her commute. She told herself the event had not been
that severe—the doctors had said so, after all—but the taste would return unbidden when she caught a
whiff of bleach, or when humidity made her throat feel tight, and she would be back in that
basement, gasping.

By month four, she had largely stopped trying to talk about what had happened. When a therapist at
the embassy's employee assistance program asked her to describe the incident, the words scattered.
She would begin at the inspection, then lose the sequence. Was she alone when she entered? Had she
called for help, or had someone found her? The narrative fragmented. She found it easier not to
think about it at all, to redirect her mind when images surfaced. She told herself it was over, that
she was fine, that dwelling on it only made things worse.

What finally brought her to seek help was not the intrusive memories or the constant vigilance—the
way her eyes tracked every shadow in a room, the way her shoulders remained tense even during
sleep—but the recognition that she had become someone she did not recognize. She had snapped at a
junior colleague over a minor scheduling error. She had declined an invitation to her niece's
wedding because the thought of being in an unfamiliar venue, surrounded by people, felt unbearable.
Her career, which had always been her anchor, now felt like a performance she was barely sustaining.
At fifty, Priya sat in her apartment on a Tuesday evening and realized she was afraid of her own
mind, afraid that the next trigger might come during a critical negotiation, afraid that if she ever
tried to explain what had happened, the words would scatter again and no one would understand. She
picked up the phone and made an appointment. She was not sure what she wanted, except that the
constant vigilance and the taste of chemicals that were no longer in the air needed to end.
