# Marcus

Marcus was thirty-two when his ex-girlfriend, someone he had dated for two years and broken up with
eighteen months prior, began appearing at places he frequented. It started with a text message—not
threatening, just an observation that she had seen him at a coffee shop he visited every Saturday
morning. He had deleted her number months earlier and assumed the contact was accidental. Within a
week, she had shown up at the same coffee shop three times. When Marcus changed his routine and went
to a different café across town, she found him there too. She began leaving notes on his car. She
created fake social media accounts using his name. She called his workplace asking for him, and when
his employer mentioned it, Marcus felt the first cold spike of genuine fear—the sense that his
movements were being tracked, that no space was truly private anymore.

Over the following months, the stalking intensified. She followed him home from work. She waited
outside his apartment building. She sent messages describing what he was wearing, what he had eaten,
where he had gone. Marcus filed a police report, obtained a restraining order, changed his phone
number. The legal protections felt hollow. He became certain that she was still watching him, that
she had simply learned to be more subtle. What terrified him most was not knowing when or where she
would appear next—the randomness made every unfamiliar location feel like a trap. He had come to
believe that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that other people could not be trusted to respect
boundaries, that beneath normal social interaction lay the capacity for obsession and violation.

When Marcus met his wife, Elena, two years after the stalking ended, he was cautiously hopeful. The
restraining order had held. There had been no contact in nearly a year. But the damage had calcified
into something harder to name. He found himself unable to relax in their apartment, even with the
doors locked. If Elena suggested they try a new restaurant in an unfamiliar neighborhood, Marcus
would feel a surge of panic—he could not assess the danger, could not map exits or anticipate
threats. He began declining invitations. He would lie awake beside Elena, listening for sounds that
did not exist, his body rigid with the certainty that something was wrong. When she tried to hold
him, to be close to him in the way married couples do, he would feel trapped, suffocated by the
vulnerability of it. He told himself it was not her fault, that she deserved better, that he was
broken in ways he could not fix. He deserved to suffer for not seeing the danger sooner, for not
protecting himself well enough. He began spending nights in the spare bedroom, telling Elena he
slept better alone.

His work as a tutor—one-on-one sessions in his home office, mostly with high school students
preparing for standardized tests—became a minefield. When a student expressed frustration about a
difficult problem, raising his voice even slightly, Marcus would feel his nervous system flood with
alarm. His chest would tighten. He would struggle to stay present. A few months ago, a student's
father came to pick him up and arrived earlier than expected, knocking loudly on the door. Marcus
heard the knock and was convinced, for a full minute, that it was her. The panic was so acute that
he could not open the door. The father had to call Elena, who came downstairs confused and
embarrassed. Marcus had lost two tutoring clients since then—word had gotten out that he was
unreliable.

The sound of a motorcycle passing on the street outside could send him into a state of
hypervigilance that lasted for hours. He would track the sound, trying to determine if it was
circling, if it would return. He had taken to checking the locks on doors and windows obsessively,
even though he knew, rationally, that locks would not stop someone determined enough. He knew other
people were not all like her, but he could not quite believe it. Everyone seemed to be hiding
something. Elena's kindness felt like a performance. His own judgment felt fundamentally broken—if
he had missed the signs with his ex, how could he trust himself to see danger or safety accurately
now?

It was Elena who finally insisted he seek help. She had given him an ultimatum, not cruelly but
clearly: either they go to couples therapy and he gets individual treatment, or she cannot stay. She
loves him, she said, but she cannot be a substitute for professional help. Marcus called a therapist
last week. He is terrified of what he might learn about himself, but he is more terrified of losing
his marriage.
