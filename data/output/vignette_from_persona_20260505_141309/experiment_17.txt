# Rajesh's Burden

Rajesh Patel had spent forty-three years with the United States Coast Guard, thirty of them as a
coxswain on patrol boats in the Pacific Northwest. He had been meticulous, methodical, respected—the
kind of man who checked equipment twice and knew every tidal pattern along the Washington coast.
When he retired at seventy-seven, his wife Priya had finally gotten the husband she'd been promised
all those years. They had plans: a small garden, trips to visit their daughter in Portland, quiet
mornings on their porch overlooking the water. Three years later, Priya died of pneumonia in the
winter, and Rajesh found himself at eighty, living alone in the house they'd bought together in
1989, unable to leave it in the way he once could.

The incident that now shadowed every hour had occurred on a March morning in 1998, when Rajesh was
fifty-six and still in his prime. A routine patrol near Cape Disappointment had turned catastrophic
when a distress call came through—a fishing vessel taking on water. Rajesh and his crew responded
within minutes. The sea was rough but manageable; Rajesh had navigated worse. What he had not
anticipated was the sudden mechanical failure of the starboard engine as they approached the fishing
boat. The vessel lurched sideways. One of his crew, Marcus Chen, a young petty officer who had been
standing on the gunwale preparing to throw a line, lost his footing. Rajesh heard the impact—a sound
he could not describe except that it lived in his body still. Marcus fell between the two vessels,
and his leg was crushed. The rescue that followed was technically successful; Marcus survived. But
his leg did not. He lost it below the knee, and Rajesh, who had given the order to approach and who
had failed to anticipate the engine failure, carried the knowledge that his command had unmade a
young man's life.

For the first year, Rajesh had functioned. He completed his reports, attended the review board,
accepted their finding that the engine failure was not his responsibility. But he had come to
believe something they could not absolve him of: there was something fundamentally defective in him
as a person, something that made him dangerous, that made him the sort of man who broke other
people. He had been too confident that morning. He had not checked the engines thoroughly enough—or
had he? The memories came to him in fragments that would not cohere: the sound of Marcus's voice
calling out, the smell of diesel fuel, the feeling of the deck tilting, but also gaps, pieces
missing, as if his mind had shattered the event and scattered the shards. When he tried deliberately
to reconstruct what had happened, to understand exactly where his failure had begun, the details
became slippery and distant, like trying to grasp smoke.

Over the decades, Rajesh had developed ways of managing the weight. He told himself the event had
not really happened, or that it was not as bad as he remembered—Marcus had survived, after all, had
gone on to a desk job, had a family now. He forced himself to think only positive thoughts when the
darker ones came, pushing them away before they could take hold. But the pushing, the suppressing,
the magical thinking—these had become exhausting habits that no longer worked. The memories still
came, and when they did, they were complete and overwhelming, flooding all his senses at once: the
cold spray of the Pacific, the metallic taste of his own fear, the weight of responsibility pressing
on his chest as if he were drowning.

Now, at eighty, Rajesh had become a prisoner of his own vigilance. He avoided unfamiliar places
because he could not assess the danger; he had to know every exit, every sight line. When he sat, he
chose his seat carefully, always with his back protected and a view of the room. He took risks he
shouldn't—walking too close to the edge of the bluff behind his house, driving too fast on empty
roads—as if daring the world to prove what he already knew: that he deserved whatever harm came to
him. Sirens made his heart race; the sound of them sent him back to that morning instantly. And
talking about the future, about what might come next, made him panic. How could he plan for anything
when he was the sort of person who destroyed lives?

His daughter had finally insisted he see someone after finding him, at two in the morning, standing
at the edge of his porch in his pajamas, having heard a siren miles away. He had not fallen, but she
had found him there, trembling, unable to explain why he had gotten out of bed. Rajesh had agreed to
come, though he was certain no one could help him, that he was beyond fixing. What he hoped for, if
he allowed himself to hope at all, was simply to understand whether the man he had been that
morning—careful, capable, trusted—had ever really existed at all.
