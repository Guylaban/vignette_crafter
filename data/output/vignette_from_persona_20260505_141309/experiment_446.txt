# Margaret's Story

Margaret was seventy-seven when a man broke into her home on a Tuesday evening in March. She had
been a school counselor for forty-one years—the kind of woman who knew how to listen, how to sit
with a teenager's rage or a parent's despair without flinching. She had retired two years earlier,
after her husband Thomas died, and had settled into a quieter life in the small brick house they'd
bought together in 1989. She was alone that night, as she had been most nights since his funeral.

The man came through the kitchen window sometime after dark. Margaret was in the living room reading
when she heard the crash of glass, then footsteps—heavy, deliberate, moving through her home as if
he owned it. What followed was a violent robbery. He took her jewelry, Thomas's watch, the small
television from the bedroom. But what he took that mattered most was the sense that her own home was
hers at all. He had moved through her rooms, her private spaces, and she had been powerless to stop
him. The police came. They filed a report. They told her they would investigate. Nothing came of it.
No arrest. No closure. Just the fact of his existence somewhere out there, unnamed and unpunished.

In the weeks after, Margaret began to change in ways she did not recognize. She had always prided
herself on her composure, her ability to be present for others. Now she found herself lying awake at
night, listening. Every sound became a threat—the settling of the house, a neighbor's car door, wind
in the eaves. She would jolt upright at the smallest noise, her heart hammering, convinced that he
was back. She started keeping a chair positioned so that she could see the front door from any room.
She moved her bed so her back was against the wall. She made sure she was never far from an exit,
even in her own kitchen. It became automatic, this calculation: where could she go if she needed to
run?

The anger came too, a hot and relentless thing that surprised her. Margaret would find herself lying
in bed at three in the morning, composing scenarios in which she confronted him, in which the police
finally found him and he faced justice. She would imagine his arrest, his conviction, the way his
face would look when he realized she had won. These thoughts consumed her. She would spend hours
turning them over, refining them, adding details. It was the only thing that made her feel less
helpless—this fantasy of revenge, of balance restored. But even as she indulged it, she knew it was
consuming her.

She began to drink. Just a glass of wine in the evening at first, then two. She had her physician
prescribe her a sleeping medication, then an anti-anxiety pill. The alcohol and pills made the
hypervigilance quieter, made the listening less exhausting. They also made her foggy, unreliable.
She missed a lunch date with her daughter. She forgot her granddaughter's birthday. She sat in her
counselor's office—she still saw a therapist herself, had for years—and said the words she could
barely believe were true: "I don't know who I am anymore."

The woman who had spent four decades helping young people find their way through crisis had become
unmoored. She was not happy. She could not remember the last time she had felt anything like
happiness. Even when she forced herself to do the things she used to enjoy—reading, gardening, time
with her grandchildren—there was no pleasure in it, only the grinding awareness that she was
performing normalcy while something essential had been stolen from her. The unfairness of it burned.
A man had violated her sanctuary and walked away. The system had failed her. And she was the one
paying the price, night after night, unable to relax even when she locked every door and checked
every window.

It was her daughter who finally insisted she seek help. Margaret had become too withdrawn, too
fragile, too much a shadow of herself. At seventy-seven, she had thought her life was settled. Now
she sat in a new therapist's office, afraid that the person she had been was gone for good, and
terrified of what might happen if she ever felt safe enough to stop listening.
