# Clinical Vignette: Roberto Morales

Roberto Morales had spent thirty-two years as a forensic analyst, the kind of work that demanded
precision, calm, and an almost meditative attention to detail. He had built his reputation on his
ability to walk into the worst scenes—industrial accidents, structural collapses, chemical
incidents—and extract meaning from chaos. His colleagues called him steady. His wife María called
him unflappable. He had believed this about himself. Then came the warehouse explosion in Newark.

It was a Tuesday in March. Roberto was contracted to examine the site of a propane distribution
facility that had caught fire and detonated, killing seven workers and injuring nineteen. He had
been inside the perimeter for less than ten minutes when the second explosion occurred—a delayed
ignition in an adjacent storage tank that investigators later determined should have been flagged as
a secondary hazard. The blast threw him backward across forty feet of concrete. He woke up in the
hospital with a fractured collarbone, lacerations across his chest and face, and no clear memory of
how he had gotten there.

The physical injuries healed in six weeks. The other injuries did not.

In the months that followed, Roberto noticed his body had become a stranger to him. His shoulders
lived somewhere near his ears now, a constant tension that no amount of stretching could release.
His chest felt tight, as though the air around him had grown thicker. At night, he would startle
awake at the sound of a car door or a dog barking, his heart hammering so violently that he thought
he might be having a heart attack. He had come to believe that his body was trying to tell him
something true: that he had been fundamentally changed by what happened, and that he could no longer
trust his own judgment about safety.

This belief began to reshape his life in ways he did not initially recognize as avoidance. When his
firm offered him a new contract to assess a manufacturing plant forty miles north, in a town he had
never visited, Roberto declined. He told himself it was because the drive was long, but the truth
was more unsettling: he could not assess the danger in an unfamiliar place. He did not know which
routes had heavy traffic, which areas were industrial, which buildings might contain hazardous
materials. The uncertainty felt unbearable. Within three months, he had turned down four contracts.
His supervisor began to ask questions. His income dropped by nearly thirty percent.

More insidiously, Roberto had begun to avoid the places he had always known. The forensic lab where
he had worked for two decades now felt dangerous to him—the proximity to chemical storage, the old
ventilation system, the fact that he could not control what happened in the building. He started
taking early lunch breaks and leaving before his shift officially ended, claiming fatigue. His
colleagues, who had always respected his expertise, began to notice his absence from the complex
cases. By the time he admitted to María that he was thinking about early retirement, he had already
missed the annual conference where he was scheduled to present findings on blast pattern
analysis—work that had once filled him with genuine satisfaction.

What frightened Roberto most was not the physical danger itself, but the recognition that he had
become someone he did not recognize. He had always prided himself on being a man who could handle
difficult situations, who could walk into devastation and extract clarity. Now he was a man who
flinched at sudden sounds, who could not drive to an unfamiliar neighborhood, who was abandoning the
career that had defined him for three decades. In his own mind, he was no longer capable. He was a
lousy coper. The world, which he had once understood as a place where danger could be identified and
mitigated through careful work, now seemed fundamentally unsafe—a place where explosions happened
without warning, where his body could betray him, where his judgment could fail catastrophically.

It was María who finally insisted he see someone. She had watched her husband become smaller, more
withdrawn, more afraid. She had noticed him checking the locks twice, three times. She had heard him
on the phone with his doctor, asking about heart problems. When he missed his grandson's soccer game
because the field was in an unfamiliar part of town, she told him plainly that she could not watch
him disappear. Roberto made the appointment the next day, hoping that someone could tell him whether
the person he had become was permanent, and whether the man he used to be could ever return.
