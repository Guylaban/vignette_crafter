# Arjun's Reckoning

Arjun Desai had been driving for twelve years without incident. At thirty-four, he knew every street
in the metro area, every pothole and traffic light, every shortcut that might save a customer five
minutes. His reputation was solid—reliable, careful, the kind of driver dispatchers called when they
needed someone dependable. On a Tuesday afternoon in March, that identity fractured in the space of
four seconds.

He was making a delivery in a residential neighborhood, moving through an intersection on a green
light. The cross street was quiet. He had done this route a hundred times. What he did not see, what
no one could have seen from his angle, was the boy on the bicycle—eight years old, maybe
nine—cutting through from behind a parked van. Arjun felt the impact before he understood what had
happened. The sound was what stayed with him: not a crash, but something softer, more terrible. He
was out of the van before it fully stopped.

The child was on the pavement. His leg was bent in a way legs should not bend. There was blood. A
woman—the boy's mother, he learned later—was screaming. Arjun stood there, his hands shaking, his
mind moving in slow motion. He had called 911. He had stayed at the scene. The police report would
later confirm what the investigating officer told him: the boy had run the stop sign, and Arjun had
been traveling at the posted speed limit. By every objective measure, the accident was not his
fault.

But in the weeks that followed, Arjun's mind rewrote the event into a different story. He became
convinced that he should have seen the boy sooner—should have been scanning more carefully, should
have slowed down even though the light was green, should have anticipated danger that was, by
definition, unforeseeable. At night, he lay awake beside his wife Priya, replaying the moment. If
only he had left the warehouse two minutes earlier or later. If only he had taken a different route.
If only he had been more cautious, more aware, more something. The boy had undergone surgery. His
leg would heal, but Arjun was certain he had failed in a fundamental way—that his negligence, his
inattention, his simple existence in that place at that time had caused irreversible harm to an
innocent child. He could not forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened, even though
there was nothing he could have done.

Within a month, Arjun stopped driving. He called his dispatcher and said he needed time off. Priya
thought it was temporary—shock, trauma, the need to process. But as weeks stretched into two months,
Arjun could not bring himself to return. The thought of being behind the wheel, of being responsible
for the safety of others, made his chest tighten. He was certain that his judgment was fundamentally
flawed, that he could not be trusted with a vehicle or with anyone's wellbeing. The company he had
worked for generously held his position, but after six weeks, they needed an answer. Arjun resigned.

At home, he moved through the days in a fog. Priya, a nurse, was working longer shifts to cover the
lost income. When she asked him when he might return to work, Arjun could not answer. How could he
explain that he was convinced his presence in any job would eventually harm someone? That the
evidence of his failure was permanent—a boy with a scarred leg somewhere in the city, a physical
reminder that Arjun Desai was not the careful, reliable man he had believed himself to be?

The distance between them grew quietly. Priya stopped asking. Arjun stopped leaving the house except
for necessary errands, and even then, he moved with a sense of dread, certain that his mere presence
was a threat. It was Priya who finally insisted he see someone, her voice firm in a way that cut
through his fog. She had found a therapist who specialized in trauma. Arjun attended the first
appointment not because he believed it would help, but because his wife had asked him to, and
because somewhere beneath the guilt and self-condemnation, he was afraid of what his own mind might
do to him if he continued alone.
