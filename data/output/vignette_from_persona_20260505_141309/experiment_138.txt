# Tanya's Vigil

Tanya was thirty-one when her mother collapsed in the kitchen of their shared house in Portland. It
was a Tuesday evening in March. Tanya had been in her upstairs office filing stories for the
*Oregonian* when she heard the sound—not a scream, but a kind of animal gasp, the noise of someone
whose body was betraying them without warning. By the time she reached the kitchen, her mother was
on the floor, her face the color of old paper, her left arm reaching toward nothing. The paramedics
said it was a massive stroke. She died three days later in the ICU, her brain already gone, her body
still breathing.

In the weeks immediately after, Tanya moved through her days as though she were watching herself
from outside her skin. She returned to work after two weeks because staying home felt worse—the
house held too many angles where her mother might appear. But the office, which had always felt like
a refuge, became something else entirely. She found herself unable to sit at her desk for more than
twenty minutes at a time. Her shoulders would climb toward her ears, her jaw would clench so hard
her teeth ached, and she would have to stand, to move, to do something. Her editor noticed. He asked
if she was all right. She said yes. She was not all right.

What had begun to terrify her most was the idea of being unprepared. Her mother had been fine—had
been *completely* fine—and then she was not. The randomness of it was unbearable. Tanya became
hyperalert in ways she could not quite name to herself. She found herself scanning rooms when she
entered them, noting exits, assessing the people around her. At the grocery store, she would watch
other shoppers, alert to any sign of distress, any sudden movement. At her desk, she kept her back
to the wall and her eyes on the door. She told herself she was just being aware. The truth was that
she had come to believe that catastrophe was always moments away, that her vigilance was the only
thing standing between her and another loss she could not survive.

The inability to relax had become a constant undercurrent in her body. Even on the rare evenings
when she was genuinely safe—sitting on her apartment couch with a cup of tea, the doors locked, no
one in danger—she could not let her muscles go slack. There was always a tightness in her chest, a
readiness, as though her body was waiting for the next terrible thing. Sleep became difficult. She
would lie in bed and her mind would race through scenarios: what if she had a stroke? What if
something happened to her sister? What if she missed the signs in someone she loved? The "what ifs"
were relentless, and they were accompanied by a kind of free-floating dread that had no clear object
but felt absolutely real.

Over the months, the anger had grown too. Anger at her mother for dying so suddenly, leaving Tanya
unprepared. Anger at herself for not being there in the exact moment, as though her presence might
have changed anything. Anger at the randomness of the universe, which had never felt random before
and now felt like the only truth. This anger would flare up without warning—at colleagues who made
small talk, at friends who said they understood, at her ex-husband for texting to ask how she was
doing as though he still had any claim to that knowledge. Underneath the anger was a deeper current
of shame: shame that she could not simply accept what had happened, shame that she was not coping
better, shame that she was becoming someone she did not recognize.

By November, eight months after her mother's death, Tanya's work had begun to suffer. She had missed
two deadlines. She had snapped at a junior reporter in a way that left them in tears. She was still
hypervigilant, still unable to find rest even in moments of genuine safety, still moving through her
days with the sense that something catastrophic was always gathering just beyond her sight. It was
her sister who finally said what Tanya had been avoiding: "You need help. Real help. Not just time."
Tanya made an appointment with a therapist, terrified that even speaking about what had happened
would somehow make it more real, more dangerous, more likely to happen again.
