# Clinical Vignette: Mei Chen

Mei Chen was sixty years old when her daughter Lin died. The death came without warning on a Tuesday
morning in March—a sudden aneurysm while Lin was at work, collapsing at her desk in the accounting
department of a downtown firm. Mei had been at home that day, working her shift at the call center
from the spare bedroom of the house she shared with her husband David. She remembered the quality of
the light through the window, the particular hum of her computer, the voice of a customer asking
about a billing dispute. Then the phone call. Then the hospital. Then the moment when the doctor's
mouth moved and she understood that Lin, her only child, was gone. Lin had been thirty-four years
old.

The weeks that followed blurred into a sequence of obligations that Mei moved through as though
watching herself from outside her body. The funeral service was held on a Saturday. Mei sat in the
front pew in a black dress she had owned for decades, aware of people approaching her, touching her
arm, speaking words that did not penetrate. She had not wanted to go to the cemetery. The thought of
Lin's body being lowered into the ground had filled her with a specific, unbearable dread, but David
had gently insisted. At the graveside, standing among the headstones and the smell of fresh earth,
Mei had felt something crack inside her chest. Months later, she still could not walk past a
cemetery without her heart rate accelerating, without the impulse to turn away and drive a different
route.

What had begun as grief gradually transformed into something more fragmented and intrusive. Mei
found herself unable to sleep more than a few hours each night. She would lie awake beside David,
her mind circling, and when sleep finally came, she would jolt awake at three or four in the
morning, her body flooded with adrenaline. At work, taking calls from customers, she would suddenly
find herself back in the hospital waiting room, her hands shaking so badly she could barely type
notes into the system. Once, a customer had a baby crying in the background—a high-pitched wail that
cut through the phone line—and Mei had had to excuse herself, locking herself in the bathroom stall
for fifteen minutes while her body trembled. The sound of any baby now triggered that same cascade
of panic and disorientation, though she could not fully articulate why.

Mei had begun to avoid anything that might remind her of Lin. She did not go to the grocery store
where they had shopped together. She had stopped attending the church gatherings she and David had
participated in for years; the thought of seeing the friends who had known Lin, who would ask how
she was managing, filled her with a shame and dread so intense that she made excuses and stayed
home. She had not been able to enter Lin's apartment to collect her belongings. The task sat
unfinished, a weight that pressed on her constantly. She had deleted most of Lin's photographs from
her phone, though occasionally one would surface unexpectedly—a image of Lin laughing at a family
dinner—and Mei would feel the memory fragmenting, pieces of that evening suddenly vivid—the blue
tablecloth, the sound of Lin's voice, the way she had tilted her head—before the image dissolved
again into blankness.

The memories of the event itself came to her in scattered pieces that she could not quite hold. She
could see the doctor's face with crystalline clarity, but the walk from the waiting room to the room
where Lin lay was completely absent from her recollection. Sometimes fragments would surface without
warning: the texture of the hospital gown, the temperature of Lin's hand, a phrase the nurse had
used—and then they would slip away again before Mei could fully grasp them. She had come to believe
that if she did not talk about what had happened, if she kept it sealed away, perhaps the memories
would stop intruding. She told David very little. When he tried to speak about Lin, Mei would change
the subject or excuse herself from the room.

It was David who finally insisted she seek help. He had found her one morning sitting at the kitchen
table at five a.m., staring at nothing, and he had said quietly that he was losing her, that
whatever was happening inside her was taking her away from him. Mei had resisted—what could talking
to a stranger possibly change?—but the exhaustion of holding everything in had become too great. She
came to her first appointment hollow-eyed and terrified, hoping that someone might help her sleep
again, that she might find a way to live in the same house as her memories without being consumed by
them.
