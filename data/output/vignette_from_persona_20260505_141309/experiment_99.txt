# Aroha's Burden

Aroha Te Kanawa had been a police officer for forty-three years. She had responded to hundreds of
accident scenes, written countless reports, attended training on victim support. At seventy-one, she
was weeks away from retirement when her son Michael—forty-five years old, a father of two—was hit by
a drunk driver on a Friday evening in October.

The call came from the hospital. Michael had been leaving his office building when a sedan crossed
the median on the Pacific Coast Highway. Aroha arrived at the scene before the body was transported.
She saw the angle of the vehicles, the scatter pattern of glass, the smell of spilled alcohol thick
in the salt air. She saw the other driver—bloodied, stumbling, reeking of whiskey—being led away in
cuffs. She had processed a thousand scenes like this one. She had never been the mother standing on
the asphalt, watching her son's car being cut open.

Michael died three days later without regaining consciousness.

In the months that followed, Aroha found herself caught in a loop of vivid, intrusive recollection.
The images were photographic in their precision: the exact shade of the sedan's paint, the way the
streetlight caught the broken windshield, the officer's face as he approached her at the hospital.
These were not vague impressions but sharp, detailed pictures that played without warning—while she
was showering, while she was sitting across from her husband at dinner, while she lay awake at 3
a.m. Alongside the images came sound: she would hear the screech of tires, the crunch of metal, the
ambient noise of the highway that she had heard that night. Sometimes she heard it so clearly that
she would turn to see if someone had turned up the television.

What tormented her most was the conviction that she should have prevented it. Aroha had spent four
decades teaching her children about road safety, about the dangers of distracted drivers, about
trusting their instincts. She had told Michael, countless times, to take different routes home, to
avoid the Pacific Coast Highway during evening hours. She was a police officer; she knew the
statistics. And yet she had not insisted. She had not forbidden it. She had let him drive that
route, and because of the way she had acted—her passivity, her failure to be forceful enough—he was
dead. She was certain of this, the way one is certain of facts. The drunk driver had merely been the
instrument. Aroha had set the conditions.

By December, she had begun to avoid anything that triggered the images and the sounds. She stopped
driving past accident scenes, even though her job required it. She found reasons to take
administrative duties instead of patrol. When colleagues discussed a multi-vehicle collision on the
freeway, she would excuse herself or change the subject, her chest tightening. The smell of
alcohol—which had always been simply the smell of alcohol—now sent her into a state of acute dread.
A colleague's drink at the holiday party, the sanitizer dispenser at the precinct, even the cooking
wine her husband used: all of it made her nauseated and panicked.

Her marriage had begun to fracture under the weight of her withdrawal. Her husband, Tane, grieved
Michael too, but he wanted to talk about him, to remember the good things. Aroha could not do this.
Every memory of Michael felt contaminated by the knowledge of how she had failed him. She spent
increasing amounts of time alone, ostensibly working on reports but actually sitting in silence,
trapped in the cycle of images and blame.

Three weeks ago, at a family dinner, her granddaughter mentioned that she was learning to drive.
Aroha had left the table and locked herself in the bathroom. That night, she realized she could not
continue this way. She was seventy-one years old, a widow in all but name, and she was suffocating
under the weight of her conviction that she had killed her son through her own negligence.

She called her doctor the next morning and asked for a referral to a therapist. She does not yet
believe that anything can truly help—that the images will fade, that the blame will lift—but she has
come to understand that she is drowning, and she can no longer refuse the hand being offered.
