# Marjorie's Vigil

Marjorie had been a public defender for fifty-three years, and in that time she had learned to read
a courtroom the way a sailor reads the sea. She could sense a shift in testimony, anticipate a
witness's hesitation, know when a jury had turned. But three years ago, on a Tuesday evening in the
parking garage beneath her office building in downtown Minneapolis, her ability to read danger
failed her entirely.

She had stayed late reviewing case files—a habit so ingrained it barely registered as a choice
anymore. When she left at half past eight, the garage was nearly empty. As she walked toward her
car, a man emerged from behind a concrete pillar perhaps twenty feet ahead of her. She remembered
the sudden compression of time, the way his face had seemed both blank and intent. He had demanded
her purse. When she hesitated—not out of bravery but out of the strange paralysis that sometimes
comes with genuine shock—he had struck her across the temple with something hard. She fell. He took
the purse and ran. A security guard found her twenty minutes later, bleeding from a gash above her
right eye.

The physical injuries healed within weeks. The fracture in her sense of safety had not.

For the first months after the assault, Marjorie had told herself the fear would fade. She had
returned to work, had continued her caseload, had performed the role of the competent professional
she had been. But something had shifted in how she moved through the world. She began leaving the
office earlier, never staying past five-thirty. She stopped parking in the garage entirely, choosing
a surface lot three blocks away despite the weather and her arthritic knees. Even then, she would
walk to her car with her keys already in her hand, her purse held tight against her body, and her
head turning constantly—checking behind her, checking the spaces between parked vehicles, checking
the faces of anyone within fifty yards.

The checking had become automatic now, a rhythm as regular as breathing. Walking down the courthouse
hallway, she would catch herself watching the other people there—the young man in the cheap suit,
the woman with the hard eyes, the security officer whose attention seemed to have drifted. She had
come to believe that danger was not something that happened; it was something that was always
present, waiting to be detected. If she simply watched carefully enough, if she remained vigilant
enough, she could prevent what had already happened from happening again.

But vigilance was exhausting. At home, in the house where she and her husband Robert had lived for
forty-one years, she found herself startled by ordinary sounds—the refrigerator's hum, Robert's
footsteps on the stairs, the wind against the windows. Her body had become a instrument of alarm,
jumping at stimuli that her mind registered as harmless only after the surge of adrenaline had
already flooded through her. Robert had learned not to come up behind her; he would announce himself
from the next room, give her time to prepare. She could see the sadness in his face when she
flinched at his touch.

What frightened her most was the thought that her vigilance might fail again. That she might miss
the sign, the glance, the shift in someone's posture that would tell her danger was coming. The
assault had revealed to her that the world was far less predictable than she had believed, and that
her own perceptions—the very tools she had relied on for five decades—could betray her. She had
taken early retirement from the public defender's office not because of the physical injury, but
because she could no longer trust herself to notice what mattered. The idea of missing something in
a case file, of failing a client because her attention had fractured, had become unbearable.

At seventy-eight, Marjorie had finally agreed to see a therapist, though she was not entirely
certain what she hoped would change. She wanted to feel safe again. But more than that, she wanted
to understand whether the danger she felt so acutely was something she could ever truly control.
