# Clinical Vignette: Héctor M.

Héctor was seventy-five when he came to the clinic, referred by his primary care physician after a
series of panic episodes that had left him exhausted and confused. He had spent fifty-two years as a
quality assurance engineer at a manufacturing plant in Southern California, a career built on
precision and control—checking tolerances, catching defects before they reached customers, ensuring
that nothing slipped through unexamined. In the months before his appointment, he had become unable
to return to work, though he was long past the age when most men retired. His wife María, whom he
had married at twenty-three and remained devoted to, had begun to worry that he was disappearing
into himself.

The event that brought him here occurred on a September afternoon in 1961, when Héctor was sixteen
years old living in Guatemala City with his parents and three younger sisters. A wildfire had swept
down from the highlands with terrifying speed, driven by winds that seemed to come from nowhere. The
neighborhood where his family lived—a modest residential area on the city's edge—had been given less
than an hour's warning. Héctor's father was at work. His mother had begun throwing clothes into a
single bag while Héctor herded his sisters toward the door, but the smoke came faster than anyone
had anticipated. It rolled down the street like something alive, thick and acrid, turning day into a
murky twilight. Héctor could taste it in his throat, feel it burning his eyes. The sound of the
fire—a roaring that seemed to come from the earth itself—had filled his ears as neighbors screamed
and ran. His mother had grabbed his arm so hard it bruised. They had made it to the street and been
swept up in a crowd of people pushing toward the main avenue, where trucks were waiting. Héctor's
youngest sister, only five, had cried for a doll she'd left behind. The fire had consumed three
blocks of the neighborhood. Seven people died. For months afterward, Héctor had nightmares of being
left behind, of the smoke closing around him while his family vanished.

He had never spoken much about that day, even to María. He had built a life in America, worked
steadily, provided for his family, and told himself that the past was past. But in recent years,
particularly as he aged and his mind seemed to move more slowly, the memory had begun to intrude. It
started small—a smell of burning leaves in autumn would catch him off guard, and he would find his
heart racing, his palms damp. Then came the morning last winter when a neighbor's house caught fire
two blocks away. Héctor had smelled the smoke before he heard the sirens, and something in him had
simply broken. He'd called in sick to work, unable to explain to his supervisor why the thought of
driving past that street made him feel as though he couldn't breathe.

Over the following months, the triggers had multiplied and intensified. Any smell of smoke—a
barbecue, a fireplace, even a neighbor burning trash—would send electricity through his body. His
hands would shake. He would feel an urgent need to check the windows, to know where the exits were,
to ensure that María was safe and accounted for. On days when the sky turned gray and the wind
picked up, he became hyperalert, watching the news compulsively for fire warnings, unable to settle
into any task. His startle response had become almost violent; a sudden noise would jolt him as
though an electric shock had run through him, leaving him shaken for minutes afterward.

What troubled him most, though, was the rumination. In the quiet hours of the morning, before María
woke, he would lie awake turning over the past, convinced that he should have done more that day,
should have anticipated the fire sooner, should have grabbed the doll for his sister instead of
pushing everyone toward the street. He would replay the moment his mother had grabbed his arm a
hundred times, wondering if he had pulled away too quickly, if she had been hurt. He tried to push
these thoughts away—would literally shake his head, as though he could dislodge them—but they always
returned. He had begun avoiding anything that might trigger them: he stopped watching the evening
news, stopped going outside on windy days, stopped allowing himself to remember. But the avoidance
had a cost. He had missed his grandson's birthday party because the forecast mentioned possible dry
winds. He had withdrawn from María, who no longer understood why her husband of fifty-two years had
become a stranger in their home.

When he finally came to the clinic, Héctor was frightened and ashamed. He was afraid that he was
losing his mind, that at seventy-five he was too old to recover from something that had happened
when he was barely more than a boy. What he wanted most was to feel safe again, to sit with his wife
without scanning for danger, to smell smoke without his body betraying him. He did not yet
understand that the sixteen-year-old boy who had survived that fire had never fully left him, and
that the man he had become—careful, controlled, precise—had been holding that boy's terror at bay
for more than sixty years.
