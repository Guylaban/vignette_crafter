# Layla's Vigil

Layla was eighteen when she watched her older brother collapse on the sidewalk outside their
family's grocery store in Dearborn. She had been inside, restocking the produce section, when her
mother's scream pulled her toward the front windows. What she saw in those seconds—Amir's body
convulsing, his face contorted, the way his head struck the concrete and then went still—had
fractured something in her that she was only beginning to understand now, six months later, as she
sat in a therapist's office for the first time.

The doctors said it was an undiagnosed seizure disorder, that Amir's brain had simply misfired, that
nothing could have prevented it. But Layla had come to believe something different. She had been
inside the store. She had not been watching him. If she had been outside, if she had been alert, if
she had seen the warning signs—the way he sometimes blinked strangely, the slight tremor she might
have noticed if she'd been paying attention—she could have caught him, guided him down gently,
cushioned his head. The guilt was a physical weight, and it lived alongside a terror that had no
clear target but was always present, always searching.

Sleep became the first casualty. Layla would lie in bed at night, her body rigid, her mind refusing
to dim. She had developed a system: she kept her bedroom door open so she could hear her parents'
movements in the hallway, could monitor the house. She positioned her bed so she could see the
doorway. She kept her phone charged and within arm's reach. The logic was simple and unshakeable—if
something happened to someone she loved, she needed to know immediately. She needed to be ready.
Falling asleep felt like abandoning her post, like choosing unconsciousness over vigilance, and she
could not do it. On nights when exhaustion finally forced her under, she woke in a panic, her heart
racing, convinced that hours had passed and she had missed something catastrophic.

Her new internship at a mid-sized architecture firm downtown had become a minefield of anxiety.
Layla had always been meticulous, detail-oriented, the kind of student who caught errors before
anyone else. But now her hypervigilance had become something else entirely. In meetings, she found
herself scanning the faces of colleagues, watching for signs of distress, monitoring the tone of
conversations for hidden danger. When her supervisor mentioned casually that he'd been feeling dizzy
that morning, Layla's chest tightened. She spent the next hour unable to focus on the elevation
drawings in front of her, her attention pulled toward his office, listening for any sound that might
indicate he was in trouble. She had not told anyone at work why she sometimes excused herself to
check on people, why she seemed to be watching them rather than engaging with them.

The anger had surprised her most. It would arrive suddenly—at her parents for not watching Amir more
closely, at Amir for being careless with his own body, at herself for the unforgivable sin of being
inside when it happened. She would snap at her mother over minor things, then feel the shame crash
down, knowing her mother was grieving too, that her mother had also been inside and had also heard
the scream.

What frightened Layla most was not the memory itself—though that was bad enough, the image returning
unbidden and in vivid detail. What terrified her was the sense that she lived in a world where
terrible things happened without warning, where bodies betrayed you, where being alert and careful
and vigilant might still not be enough to protect the people she loved. She had come to believe that
her constant watchfulness was the only thing standing between her family and catastrophe. The
thought of relaxing that vigilance, of sleeping deeply, of trusting that nothing would happen if she
was not on guard, felt like an act of profound betrayal.

It was her mother who had finally insisted she see someone, noticing how thin Layla had become, how
the dark circles under her eyes had deepened into something that looked almost like bruising. Layla
had agreed, though she was not entirely sure what help could look like. How could a therapist teach
her to sleep when sleeping meant lowering her guard? How could anyone convince her that her
vigilance was not the only thing keeping her family alive?
