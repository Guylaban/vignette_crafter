# Rosa's Vigil

Rosa Méndez was sixty-six years old when she came to the clinic on a Tuesday afternoon in late
October, her hands trembling slightly as she signed the intake forms. She had been a paramedic for
thirty-eight years—longer than most of her colleagues had been alive—and she had never missed a
shift due to illness or injury. Three weeks ago, she had finally taken a leave of absence. Her
supervisor had stopped asking questions after the third time she'd called in, her voice thin and
controlled over the phone, saying only that she wasn't well.

Sixteen months earlier, on a warm June evening, Rosa had responded to a call in a warehouse district
on the south side of the city. The dispatch had indicated a possible cardiac event. She and her
partner, Marcus, had found the loading dock door slightly ajar. Inside, the warehouse was dim and
cavernous, filled with stacked pallets and the smell of motor oil. They had called out. No answer.
As they moved deeper in, a man emerged from behind a stack of boxes—not the patient they were
expecting, but someone with a knife. He had been waiting. He had forced them into a back office,
locked the door, and held them there for four hours. He had not harmed them physically, but he had
held the blade steady, had made them sit on the floor with their backs against the wall, had spoken
very little. The silence between his rare, clipped sentences had been suffocating. When police
finally breached the door, he had dropped the weapon and surrendered without resistance. Rosa had
walked out, had filed her report, had gone home. She had not cried. She had made dinner for her
husband, Carlos, and they had eaten in front of the television as they always did.

But something had fractured that night in a way that only became clear over the following months.

Within weeks, Rosa found herself unable to tolerate quiet moments in her own home. When Carlos was
at work and the house settled into stillness, she would turn on the radio or the
television—something, anything to fill the silence that felt pregnant with danger. She had come to
believe, with a certainty she could not quite explain, that silence meant something terrible was
about to happen. In the firehouse, she had always been the one who moved through the station with
easy confidence, joking with the crew, checking equipment with methodical calm. Now, on the rare
shifts she managed to work, she felt hyperalert, her body coiled. She found herself checking the
windows of the ambulance repeatedly. At red lights, she would glance in the side mirror, then the
rearview mirror, then turn to look directly behind them. Once, a rookie named Danny had asked her if
something was wrong, if she was worried about traffic. She had not answered him. She could not
explain that she was checking to see if someone was following them, if danger was closing in from
behind.

At home, even with the doors locked and Carlos asleep beside her, Rosa lay awake or dozed fitfully,
her nervous system primed. The house that had been her refuge for thirty years now felt like a place
where she had to remain vigilant. She had rearranged the bedroom furniture so she could see the door
from bed. She had started leaving a light on in the hallway. Carlos had asked her about it once,
gently, and she had told him she was having trouble sleeping. This was true, but it was not the
whole truth.

The memory itself came without warning and with terrifying vividness. It would arrive in the middle
of an ordinary moment—while she was washing dishes, while she was driving, while she was sitting in
the waiting room of her doctor's office—and suddenly she was back in that warehouse office, the
smell of oil in her nostrils, the weight of the man's attention on her and Marcus, the knife
catching the dim light. The memory felt too extreme, too surreal to be real, even though she knew it
was. When it came, the emotion that flooded through her was so overwhelming—a combination of fear
and helplessness and a kind of existential dread—that she would have to stop whatever she was doing.
Once, she had been in the middle of a grocery store when the memory seized her. She had abandoned
her cart and walked out to sit in her car for twenty minutes, her heart hammering, unable to move.

What frightened Rosa most, beyond the memory itself, was what her reactions meant about her mind.
She had always prided herself on her stability, her ability to remain composed in crisis. But these
responses—the hypervigilance, the intrusive memories, the sense that she was no longer safe even in
her own home—felt like evidence that something inside her was breaking down. She was afraid she was
going crazy. And beneath that fear lay a deeper, more corrosive belief: that the world was
fundamentally dangerous, that bad things happened without warning and without reason, and that she
had no power to prevent them. The knife had proved that. Four hours of helplessness had proved that.

When she finally came to the clinic, it was because Carlos had found her one morning sitting on the
back porch at five in the morning, still in her nightgown, staring at nothing. He had sat with her
for an hour before she spoke. She had told him she didn't know how much longer she could live like
this, suspended between the silence that terrified her and the memories that wouldn't leave her
alone. She was afraid, she said, that if she didn't get help, she would lose herself entirely—or
lose the people she loved by becoming someone they no longer recognized. That fear, finally, was
greater than her uncertainty about what help might mean.
