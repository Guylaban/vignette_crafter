# Clinical Vignette: Layla

Layla is a fifty-one-year-old Middle Eastern woman who works as a social ministry coordinator at a
nonprofit serving immigrant families in her city. She came to her first appointment after her
supervisor had gently raised concerns about her recent pattern of absences and a marked shift in her
demeanor with clients—something that had never been an issue in her twelve years at the
organization. When asked what had prompted her to finally seek help now, Layla sat very still for a
long moment, her hands folded tightly in her lap, and said only: "I can't keep running from my own
job."

The roots of Layla's current crisis reach back to her childhood in a household where her father's
violence was the unspoken law. He had been a man of sudden, catastrophic rages, and Layla—the eldest
of four—had learned early to read the temperature of a room, to anticipate the shift in his
breathing that preceded an attack. One afternoon when she was nine years old, her father had come
home from work in a state of fury over a perceived slight. Layla was in the kitchen doing homework
at the table. He had grabbed her by the arm, twisted it behind her back, and forced her face-down
onto the hard surface of the counter. She could not move. The weight of his body pinned her there,
and in those moments she had felt certain that something inside her would break—that this time, he
would not stop. The memory has never fully resolved into language; when Layla tries to speak about
it, her throat closes. What remains is the sensation: the pressure of her arm wrenched upward, the
cold counter against her cheek, the absolute helplessness of her body beneath his.

For decades, Layla had managed. She had left home at eighteen, married and then divorced, built a
career helping others navigate trauma and displacement. But in the past eight months, something had
shifted. It began subtly—a hypervigilance that she had always carried now becoming acute. At work,
Layla found herself unable to sit at the conference table during staff meetings without positioning
herself where she could see the door. In one-on-one sessions with clients, she would feel a sudden
surge of rage if a person moved too quickly or spoke in a raised voice, even if they were simply
expressing their own distress. She would snap at them, then feel devastated by her own reaction.
More troubling still, she had begun to avoid going to work altogether. The thought of walking into
her office—a space she had once loved—now triggered a sense of dread so profound that she would call
in sick, sometimes three or four times a week, offering vague explanations to her concerned
supervisor.

What frightened Layla most was that she could no longer distinguish between real danger and false
alarm. Walking down the hallway at work, she would suddenly feel certain that someone was behind
her, and she would spin around to check, only to find an empty corridor. In grocery stores, in her
apartment building, even in her own home, she found herself constantly glancing over her shoulder.
The checking had become automatic, almost compulsive—a habit she could not break even when she
recognized its irrationality. She had also begun to insist on never being alone in a room with
another person. At work, she would keep her office door open, position herself near the threshold,
arrange her schedule so that colleagues were always nearby. If a client came in for a session, Layla
would leave the door ajar and position her chair between the client and herself, closer to the exit.

When her memories of that afternoon surfaced—and they came frequently, often unbidden—her body would
respond as though she were still nine years old, pinned and helpless. Her arm would ache. Her chest
would tighten. She would feel the phantom pressure of the counter against her face. These bodily
sensations would arrive without warning, sometimes triggered by something as subtle as the way a
supervisor stood near her desk, and they would leave her shaken for hours. The rage that accompanied
these memories was different from her usual irritability; it was primal and consuming, a fury at her
own powerlessness that had nowhere to go except outward, toward whoever happened to be near.

Layla is now seeking help because she recognizes that her avoidance has begun to cost her the one
thing she has built for herself: her work, her identity as someone who helps others. She is
terrified that if she does not find a way to manage these symptoms, she will lose her job entirely.
But she is also afraid that looking directly at what happened—at the memory she cannot put into
words—will somehow make it real in a way she is not ready to face.
