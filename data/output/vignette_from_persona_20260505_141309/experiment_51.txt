# Layla's Vigilance

Layla was twenty-four when the pickup truck ran the red light on Atlantic Boulevard, and she has
been scanning for danger every moment since. The collision happened on a Tuesday in late March,
unremarkable weather, mid-morning traffic. She had been driving to the fabric wholesaler
downtown—she owned a small tailoring shop in the strip mall on Nostrand Avenue where she altered
wedding dresses, mended coats, and took in pants for the neighborhood families who had come to trust
her precision and her quiet way of listening to what they needed. Her father had helped her secure
the lease five years earlier, and the shop had become the center of her life, the place where her
hands and her attention could be perfectly aligned.

The truck driver had been texting. Layla remembered the moment of impact as a kind of violent
compression—the sound like thunder, the way her body was thrown sideways, the terrible crunch of
metal folding inward. Her left arm was fractured in two places. The emergency room doctor told her
she was lucky. The driver of the truck had been traveling at nearly forty miles per hour when he
crossed the intersection. Another few feet and the collision would have been frontal. Another angle
and she would not have walked out of the hospital.

But walking out was not the same as recovering. In the weeks that followed, Layla noticed the way
her mind had begun to work differently. She could not fall asleep at night because the moment her
vigilance slipped, her body seemed to remember the impact—the sudden loss of control, the
helplessness of being struck by something she could not have predicted or prevented. She lay in bed
in the dark, acutely aware of every sound in her apartment, waiting for something else to happen. It
was as though the collision had revealed the world's true nature to her: random, violent, utterly
indifferent to her caution or her goodness. She had come to believe that she could never truly be
safe, that disaster was always waiting, and that the only way to survive was to remain alert,
always, to anticipate what might come next.

When she finally returned to the shop after her arm healed, Layla found she could no longer drive.
The thought of sitting in a car, of being trapped in that metal box with no control over what other
drivers might do, filled her with a terror so acute that her hands would shake and her breathing
would become shallow. She took the bus instead, a journey that took forty minutes instead of twelve,
and she arranged her schedule around the bus routes. Her regulars began to notice that she was not
quite herself. She positioned her workstation so that her back was against the wall and she could
see the entire shop and the entrance at once. Customers would come in and find her already watching
them, her eyes alert, her body tensed as though braced for impact. What had once felt like a
welcoming space began to feel, to some of them, like being studied by someone who did not quite
trust them.

Her mother had called one evening in May and asked if she was eating properly. Layla realized then
that she had lost weight, that her sleep had become fragmented and shallow, that she spent her
evenings mentally rehearsing scenarios—what if the bus swerved, what if someone became violent, what
if the building caught fire—running through catastrophes in her mind as though by imagining them
first she could somehow prevent them or at least feel less shocked when they occurred. The memories
of the collision itself had become a film that played without her consent: the sound, the lurch, the
terror, the helplessness, all of it returning with the same raw intensity it had held in the moment,
as though three months had not passed at all.

By late June, Layla's hands had begun to shake while she worked, and she had turned away three
wedding dress orders because she was afraid her tremor would show in the stitching. She had come to
believe that people were not what they seemed, that the world was fundamentally dangerous, and that
constant vigilance was the only thing standing between her and catastrophe. It was this thought—that
she was slowly losing her livelihood, her sleep, her sense of safety in the place where she had
built her life—that finally brought her to seek help.
