# Clinical Vignette: Vinh

Vinh is a twenty-four-year-old Vietnamese-American painter who arrived at his first therapy
appointment in late autumn, referred by his primary care doctor after reporting sleep disturbances
and hypervigilance at a routine visit. He presented as a lean, watchful man who sat at the edge of
the waiting room chair, his eyes tracking the therapist's movements before they had even formally
met. When asked what had brought him in, Vinh was quiet for a long moment, then said simply: "I
can't stop waiting for something bad to happen."

Eighteen months earlier, Vinh had been living with Marcus, his boyfriend of two years. Marcus worked
in construction; Vinh had been painting murals for a local community arts nonprofit, work that had
felt purposeful and creative. They had rented a small apartment in a mixed neighborhood where Vinh
had grown up. By all external measures, Vinh had built a life. What no one saw was how Marcus had
begun to monitor Vinh's phone, his schedule, his friendships—a tightening that had felt, at the
time, like intensity rather than control. One evening in early spring, Vinh came home thirty minutes
later than usual because a client had requested revisions to a design. Marcus was waiting. He
accused Vinh of lying, of seeing someone else. When Vinh tried to explain, Marcus shoved him against
the kitchen counter, then struck him repeatedly across the face and shoulders. Vinh remembers the
sound of his own voice, though he cannot recall what he said. He remembers Marcus's face very
clearly—the particular set of his jaw, the color of his eyes in that moment. Everything else from
that night exists in fragments: the counter pressing into his ribs, the taste of blood, the way his
vision seemed to narrow and then widen, the terrible silence afterward when Marcus left the
apartment. The sequence of these moments refuses to arrange itself in order. Vinh knows the violence
happened, knows it was real, but when he tries to reconstruct it, the memory fractures into
isolated, hyper-clear images separated by blank spaces.

He left Marcus that night and moved back in with his parents. For the first few weeks, he told
himself he was managing. Then the intrusive thoughts began—sudden, unwanted images of Marcus's face,
of his own body against the counter, arriving without warning while Vinh was working or eating or
trying to sleep. He started avoiding situations that might trigger these thoughts. He stopped
painting murals that required him to work in the early morning, because he had come to associate
that time of day with nightmares, and he had begun to dread sleep itself. He avoided anything that
reminded him of Marcus: certain music they had listened to together, the neighborhood where they had
lived, even the particular brand of cologne that Marcus had worn. These avoidances felt necessary,
protective—the only way to keep the intrusive thoughts at bay.

What frightened Vinh most was the conviction that had taken root in him: that the violence had
happened because of who he was. He had come to believe that something in his character—his weakness,
his inadequacy, his inability to read situations correctly—had provoked Marcus. If he had been
stronger, more vigilant, more aware, he could have prevented it. This belief had metastasized into a
deeper sense that he no longer belonged anywhere. His parents' house felt temporary. The nonprofit
called to ask when he would return to work; he could not bring himself to answer. His few close
friends had gradually stopped reaching out after he declined several invitations without
explanation.

The hypervigilance had become constant. In any room, Vinh's eyes immediately located the exits. When
his mother took longer than usual to return from the grocery store, he was certain something
terrible had happened to her. At night, he lay awake listening for sounds—footsteps, the scrape of a
key in the lock, anything that might signal an intrusion. When he did sleep, he froze at the
slightest noise, his body locked in place, unable to move or call out. His parents had begun to
worry aloud. His mother had found him one morning standing motionless in the kitchen at three a.m.,
unable to explain why he had gotten out of bed.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment at his parents' home last week. His father had
come up behind him while he was washing dishes, placing a hand on his shoulder to ask him something.
Vinh had frozen entirely, his body rigid, his breath stopped. When he finally unfroze, he realized
he had been terrified of his own father—a man who had never raised a hand to him. In that moment,
Vinh understood that whatever had happened with Marcus was no longer just a memory. It had become
the lens through which he experienced the world, and he was losing pieces of himself in the process.
He called his doctor that afternoon. Now, sitting in the therapist's office, Vinh is most afraid
that this version of himself—hypervigilant, isolated, convinced of his own fundamental inadequacy—is
permanent. What he is hoping for, though he barely dares to say it, is that he might one day feel
safe in his own body again.
