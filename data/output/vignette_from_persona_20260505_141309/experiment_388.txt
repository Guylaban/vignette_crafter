# Anahera's Vigil

Anahera still remembers the exact moment the pediatrician's voice changed. It was a Tuesday in
March, nearly four years ago, when her daughter Aroha—then seven years old—had been brought in for
what was supposed to be a routine ear infection follow-up. The doctor's face shifted from
professional cheerfulness to something more guarded, and he asked to speak with Anahera alone. In
that small consultation room, he told her that Aroha's bloodwork showed something unexpected. The
word "leukemia" entered their lives like a siren that didn't stop.

The months that followed were a blur of hospital corridors, the smell of antiseptic, the sound of IV
pumps beeping through the night. Anahera had taken leave from her job as a network engineer at a
mid-sized tech firm, spending her days in a pediatric oncology ward watching her daughter undergo
chemotherapy, bone marrow biopsies, spinal taps. She had held Aroha's hand through procedures where
the child's small body convulsed with pain and fear. Anahera had learned to read the machines, to
anticipate the doctors' rounds, to sleep in a vinyl chair beside a hospital bed. She had become
expert at a kind of vigilance she never wanted to learn. By some mercy, Aroha had responded to
treatment. She was in remission now, and Anahera had returned to work six months ago.

But the vigilance had not left her.

At home, even in the evenings when Aroha is safe and sleeping upstairs, Anahera finds herself unable
to fully relax. She sits on the couch in her living room and her body remains taut, as though
listening for something. She checks the locks twice. She listens to her daughter's breathing through
the baby monitor she has never removed from Aroha's room, even though her daughter is now eleven and
finds it embarrassing. What frightened Anahera most was the knowledge that illness could arrive
without warning, that the world could shift catastrophically in an instant. The home that should
have been her refuge now felt like a place where danger lived in the walls.

Any unexpected movement near her—her ex-husband appearing suddenly around a corner during custody
handoff, a colleague at work wheeling a chair back from their desk—sent a jolt through her nervous
system that felt like pure electricity. Her heart would race. Her hands would shake. It took minutes
to come back down. She had come to interpret these reactions as evidence that she needed to stay
alert, that letting her guard down would be the moment disaster struck.

Arguments with her ex-husband about custody schedules or medical decisions regarding Aroha triggered
something particularly raw. When he questioned her decision to keep Aroha's oncologist appointments
on the shared calendar, when he suggested she was being overprotective, Anahera's body would flood
with heat and her voice would rise before she could think. These weren't just disagreements; they
felt like threats to the careful systems she had built to keep her daughter safe. She had come to
believe that only she could be trusted with the weight of that responsibility.

Medical examinations triggered her most acutely. When her own doctor had requested routine bloodwork
last month, Anahera had sat in the phlebotomy chair and felt her chest tighten as the nurse
approached with the needle. The smell of the alcohol wipe transported her back to hospital rooms.
Her heart rate spiked. She found herself canceling follow-up appointments, telling herself she was
too busy, though the truth was that the prospect of sitting in a doctor's office made her feel
unsafe in a way she couldn't articulate.

It was her mother, visiting from Auckland, who finally named what Anahera could not. Watching her
daughter flinch at shadows, watching her check on Aroha compulsively even when the child was at
school, her mother had asked gently whether Anahera had considered talking to someone. The question
had landed like an accusation at first. But Anahera was exhausted. She was tired of living as though
another catastrophe was imminent. She wanted to know if there was a way back to feeling safe in her
own life.
