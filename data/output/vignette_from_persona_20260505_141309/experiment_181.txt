# Karim's Vigil

Karim was thirty-eight when his younger brother Samir was hit by a delivery truck outside their
family's grocery store in Dearborn. The impact happened in an instant—Samir stepping into the
street, the sound Karim could never quite describe, the way his brother's body moved in a way bodies
should not move. By the time Karim reached him, Samir's left leg was bent at an angle that made
Karim's mind refuse to process what he was seeing. The blood pooled on the asphalt, and there was a
smell—not quite copper, not quite anything Karim had encountered in his fourteen years as a
paramedic. It was the smell of exposure, of something interior made suddenly exterior. He had worked
the code himself, his hands performing the motions while his mind fractured into two parts: the
paramedic who knew the protocols, and the brother who was losing Samir.

Samir survived, but barely. He spent six weeks in the hospital, and those weeks became the
architecture of Karim's new life. He visited every shift he could, and the hospital smell—that
particular blend of bleach and something medicinal that Karim had never noticed before—began to live
in his nostrils. It followed him home. It was in his clothes, in his hair, in the fabric of his car.
Two years later, he still caught it sometimes when he walked past a pharmacy, and his chest would
tighten as if the truck were coming again.

Work became impossible and indispensable at once. Karim would volunteer for extra shifts, telling
his wife Leila that the overtime was for Samir's ongoing physical therapy bills, though the medical
debt had long since been managed. The truth was that sitting at home meant thinking, and thinking
meant the memory would arrive whole and complete—not just the visual of Samir's leg, but the smell,
the particular quality of the sunlight that afternoon, the sound of someone screaming, though he
could not now remember if that someone was himself. When the memory came, it came with a terror so
immediate and so total that Karim's body would respond as though the truck were hitting Samir again
in that very moment. His hands would shake. His breath would come in short bursts. He would have to
leave the room, leave the house sometimes, just to escape the sensation that he was standing on that
street again.

The work itself had become different. A month ago, Karim responded to a call for a construction
accident—a man with a visible laceration on his forearm, nothing severe by any measure. The moment
Karim saw the wound, something in his vision narrowed. He could not look at it directly. He found
himself assigning the bandaging to his junior partner, something he had never done in fourteen
years. His partner had noticed. So had his supervisor. There had been conversations about "fitness
for duty," words that made Karim feel as though he were failing at the one thing he had always been
competent at.

At home, Leila had learned not to touch him without warning. Two months ago, she had placed her hand
on his shoulder while he sat at the kitchen table, and he had flinched so violently that he knocked
over his coffee. The shame of that moment—of his wife having to apologize for touching him—had
lodged somewhere in his chest. He had come to believe that something fundamental in him had broken
that day on the street, that he was no longer the man Leila had married, no longer someone she could
rely on. He would sit in the living room and choose his seat based on what he could see, where his
back was protected, which exit was closest. He was aware of how this looked, how paranoid it seemed,
and yet he could not stop doing it.

He could not remember the exact moment the truck hit—there was a gap there, a blank space his mind
refused to fill. But he remembered everything else with a clarity that felt like torture. He had
tried, for months, to suppress the thoughts, to push them away through sheer force of will, but they
would return unbidden, complete and overwhelming. Last week, his supervisor had suggested he take
medical leave. Karim had finally agreed. He was sitting in his living room, in his chosen safe seat,
when he called to make an appointment with a therapist. What he wanted most was to stop being
afraid. What he feared most was that the person he had been before that day was simply gone.
