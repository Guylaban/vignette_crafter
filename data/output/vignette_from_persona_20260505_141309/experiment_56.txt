# Marta's Story

Marta was seventy-three when she finally walked into the clinic, her weathered hands gripping the
edge of her jacket as though it might slip away. She had spent fifty-five years working as a
forester in the Pacific Northwest—a career that had taken her into the quiet of old-growth forest,
where the sound of her own breathing and the creak of timber were the only company she needed. That
solitude had always felt like sanctuary. Now, at the threshold of what should have been her
retirement years, that same silence had become something to dread.

The trauma that shaped Marta's life had occurred when she was eight years old. Her mother, a woman
prone to unpredictable rages, had been her primary caregiver after her father left. One afternoon,
while Marta's older brother was at school, her mother had beaten her with a leather belt after
finding a broken glass in the kitchen sink. The beating had lasted longer than Marta could measure
in time—what she remembered most vividly was the silence that followed, the sudden cessation of
sound, her mother's breathing slowing as she stood above her daughter on the linoleum floor. That
silence had become the moment when Marta understood that no one was coming to stop it, that she was
entirely alone with someone who could hurt her, and that the absence of sound meant the worst was
over—but also that it might happen again.

For decades, Marta had managed by staying in the forest. The work was solitary by design, and she
had built a life around that preference. She lived alone, maintained few close friendships, and had
divorced her husband of twenty years in her fifties after realizing she could not bear the
unpredictability of sharing a home. But in the last three years, the symptoms had intensified in
ways that were beginning to unravel the very life she had constructed to protect herself.

It had started with her exercise routine. Marta had always hiked—not for fitness alone, but as part
of her work and her sense of self. At seventy, she could still cover rough terrain. Then one
morning, while alone on a trail, a sound behind her—the crack of a branch, the rustling of something
large—had sent her into a state of panic so acute that she had not been able to move for twenty
minutes. She had come to believe that the forest was no longer safe, that sounds meant danger, that
being alone in that space made her vulnerable. She stopped hiking. She stopped her morning walks.
The loss of movement had consequences she had not anticipated: her legs grew weaker, her sleep
deteriorated, and the weight she had kept stable for decades began to accumulate.

The silence was worse. In her small house outside Corvallis, Marta began to experience moments of
complete quiet—no traffic, no wind, no hum of appliances—and in those moments, her body would flood
with a terror so complete that she would turn on every radio, every television, anything to fill the
void. She had come to understand, with a certainty that felt absolute, that silence meant she was
unprotected, that in the absence of sound, harm could come. She was certain now that you could never
know who would harm you, that trust was an illusion, and that the world was fundamentally unsafe.

What had broken her most, though, was the presence of women. A female colleague from the Forest
Service had reached out to grab coffee, a gesture of friendship. Marta had agreed, then spent the
entire meeting in a state of hypervigilance so acute that she could barely speak. Being alone with
another woman—even one she had worked with for years—had triggered a visceral sense that she was
powerless, that she could be hurt, that her body was not her own. She had not returned the
colleague's calls.

In recent months, Marta had begun to spend money compulsively. She would order things online—clothes
she did not need, gadgets, books—not because she wanted them, but because the act of purchasing, the
distraction of the transaction, the temporary lift of receiving a package, broke the unbearable
feeling that her life had been destroyed by what happened when she was eight. She had spent nearly
twelve thousand dollars in six months, money she could not afford to lose.

She had come to the clinic because her sister had finally insisted, and because Marta had begun to
recognize that she no longer knew who she was. She felt like an object, not a person—something that
had been damaged long ago and never repaired. What she feared most was that it was too late to
change any of this. What she hoped for, though she barely dared to say it, was that she might one
day feel safe in her own house, and perhaps, eventually, return to the forest that had once been her
home.
