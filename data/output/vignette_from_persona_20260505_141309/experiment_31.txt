# Clinical Vignette: Thea

Thea was twenty-four when she first called for an appointment, her voice steady but clipped on the
phone. She worked as an urban planner for the City Department of Transportation, a job she had
pursued deliberately—something about designing spaces, mapping safety, controlling the built
environment had drawn her. But over the past six months, she had begun to struggle in ways that made
even the small office she shared with two colleagues feel increasingly unsafe.

The trauma itself lay in her childhood, though the details remained fragmented in her own mind. She
had grown up in a household where her stepfather's discipline had crossed into brutality. The
beatings had been unpredictable; she remembered the sound of a belt buckle, the sensation of being
cornered in the narrow hallway of their apartment, but when she tried to piece together the timeline
or specific incidents, the memories seemed to dissolve. What remained vivid and uncontrollable was
the rage. When something triggered a flash of recall—a particular angle of shadow, someone else's
raised voice—she would feel a fury so intense it frightened her, as if the rage belonged to someone
else entirely and she was merely its vessel. Other times, she would be seized by the full emotional
weight of that terror without any accompanying image, just the body memory of being small and
trapped and utterly powerless.

Thea had come to believe that what had happened to her was not merely painful but fundamentally
unjust. No one in her family had ever acknowledged it directly. Her mother had left when Thea was
sixteen; her stepfather had simply disappeared from her life shortly after. There had been no
reckoning, no accountability, no moment where the world had recognized what she had endured. This
absence of justice had calcified into a kind of permanent wound, one she carried alongside the
conviction that she herself was weak—that a stronger person would have found a way to stop it, to
escape it, to matter enough to be protected.

She lived alone in a studio apartment in the Capitol Hill neighborhood, and it was the only place
she truly felt safe. Anywhere else—her office, the grocery store, friends' homes—carried a low-grade
threat that her body registered constantly. At work, she had developed a habit of positioning
herself with her back to a wall during meetings, and she flinched visibly whenever a colleague moved
suddenly toward her desk. Her supervisor had begun to notice her distraction, the way her
concentration would fragment mid-sentence. She had also become someone who agreed to everything. If
a coworker asked her to take on an extra project, she said yes. If friends suggested plans, she
complied, even when she wanted to refuse. The calculus was simple in her mind: compliance meant she
was not a threat, compliance meant she could not be hurt.

The real crisis had come two months ago when her primary care physician had recommended a
gynecological ultrasound. Thea had made the appointment, cancelled it, made it again. The thought of
lying on an examination table, of being in a position of such physical vulnerability with a
stranger's hands near her body, had triggered a cascade of panic. When she finally forced herself to
go, she had become so nauseous during the procedure that she had asked the technician to stop. The
shame afterward had been immense. She had failed at something a normal person could do without
question. It had reinforced her deepest belief: she was weak, fundamentally fragile in a way that
others were not.

What brought her to therapy was not a single incident but an accumulation. She had begun to spend
entire evenings imagining alternative lives—versions of herself where her stepfather had never
existed in her home, where she had grown up in a different house, a different family. These
counterfactual spirals would consume hours, leaving her exhausted and no closer to the life she
actually inhabited. She had also noticed that being seen—truly seen by others—had become
intolerable. In the office, she wore large sweaters and kept her head down. When friends commented
on her appearance or asked how she was really doing, she felt a sharp discomfort, as if being
observed was itself a form of violation. She had come to believe that if people truly knew her,
truly understood what had happened to her and how it had broken something inside, they would see her
not as a person but as an object of pity, or worse, as someone who deserved what had happened.

Now, sitting in the therapist's office, Thea was hoping that someone could help her understand why
the past refused to stay in the past, and whether the person she had become—careful, compliant,
trapped in a shrinking world—was all that remained.
