# Margaret's Testimony

Margaret Kowalski had spent twenty-three years building a reputation as someone who showed up. As a
city council member in a mid-sized Midwestern city, she was known for her presence at community
forums, her willingness to visit neighborhoods others avoided, her capacity to absorb difficult
conversations without flinching. That steadiness had come from somewhere. In 2019, at age fifty-
five, she had spent eight months working as a relief coordinator for an international humanitarian
organization in Syria. She had wanted to do something that mattered, something that used her
organizational skills in a place where they could save lives. She had been there for the water
sanitation projects, the coordination of medical supplies to field clinics, the logistics of keeping
people alive in a city that was systematically being destroyed.

On a Tuesday morning in April, Margaret was in a compound courtyard supervising the loading of a
delivery truck when an airstrike hit a building three blocks away. The sound was not like anything
she had prepared herself for—not an abstraction, but a physical force that seemed to come from
inside her own chest. The blast wave knocked her backward. In the seconds after, there was the sound
of her own breathing, ragged and panicked, and then the breathing of others around her, all of them
gasping in unison like a single wounded animal. She remembered the image of dust rising in a column,
the way it looked almost beautiful before she understood what it meant. She remembered a colleague's
face, his mouth open. She remembered the weight of her own body on the ground.

She had not been injured. She finished her deployment. She came home. For the first three months,
she functioned well enough—returned to her council work, attended meetings, made decisions. But by
the autumn, something had shifted. Unwanted thoughts would arrive without warning: images of that
dust column, the sensation of being knocked backward, the sound of collective breathing. They came
when she was alone in her apartment, when she was sitting in council chambers, when she was falling
asleep. She had come to believe that if she allowed herself to really think about what happened, to
let her mind settle on those images and that sound, she would not be able to handle it—that
something inside her would break in a way she could not repair. So she did not think about it. She
pushed the thoughts away when they came, which meant they came more often.

The worst triggers were sounds. When a colleague shut a heavy door too quickly in the hallway
outside her office, Margaret's body seized. When she heard a truck backfiring on the street below,
she found herself standing without remembering that she had stood. The sound of heavy breathing—her
own, or anyone's during exertion—sent her into a state of dread she could not name. She began
avoiding the gym where she had exercised for years. She stopped attending community events where
crowds might create unpredictable noise. She declined invitations to restaurants with open kitchens.
Her calendar, once packed with evening commitments, became increasingly empty.

What frightened her most was that she had lost access to something essential in herself. Before
Syria, Margaret had felt things deeply—joy at her nieces' achievements, satisfaction in her work,
even a kind of contentment in solitude. Now those feelings seemed permanently sealed away. When her
sister called with news that she was getting married, Margaret had felt nothing but a flat, distant
awareness that she should feel happy. It was as if the person who could feel those things had been
left behind in that courtyard, and what remained was a functional shell that could attend meetings
and make decisions but could not actually live.

By late 2023, nearly five years after the event, Margaret's isolation had become difficult to hide.
She had declined reelection to the council. She told people it was time for new voices, but the
truth was that she could no longer manage the unpredictability of public spaces, the sudden sounds,
the crowds. She spent most evenings alone in her apartment. The world felt dangerous in a way she
could not fully explain—not just the places where conflict happened, but everywhere, all the time.
She had come to believe that safety was an illusion, that catastrophe was always moments away.

It was her sister, during a difficult phone call in which Margaret could not explain why she had
missed yet another family dinner, who finally named what was happening. "You're not okay," her
sister said. "You haven't been okay for a long time." Margaret, sitting in the darkness of her
living room, realized that her sister was right. She had spent five years trying not to think about
Syria, and in doing so, she had lost her life. She called her doctor the next morning. She said she
needed help. She said she was afraid she would never feel like herself again, and that perhaps she
needed to find out whether that fear was true or whether there was still some possibility of return.
