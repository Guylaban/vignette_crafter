# Kofi's Vigil

Kofi Mensah had worked the checkout at SaveMart for seventeen years without incident. At seventy-
one, he had built a quiet life with his wife Margaret in their home in Honolulu, a man who knew his
regulars, who could run a register in his sleep, who had learned long ago that a steady job and
steady presence was how you made a life that held. But that had ended on a Tuesday afternoon in
March when a man came through his line with a child—a boy, maybe seven—whose arm bore the shape of
fingers that did not belong to him. Kofi had seen the bruise and said nothing. He had rung up the
groceries. He had taken the cash. He had watched the man and the boy leave the store.

Two days later, the boy came in alone. Kofi recognized him because the bruise had spread, a dark map
across his small shoulder. The child bought milk and cereal and left. That evening, Kofi told
Margaret what he had seen, and Margaret had told him to call someone—the police, the store manager,
someone. But Kofi had hesitated. He was not certain enough. He did not want to make trouble. He had
not actually seen anything happen. By the time he finally called the police department on Friday,
the boy had not come in again.

The officers who came to the store took his statement. They found the boy three days later in the
emergency room of Kapiolani Medical Center with a fractured collarbone and internal bleeding. He had
fallen down the stairs, the man—the boy's grandfather—had told the hospital. But the injuries did
not match a fall. By then, the damage was done in a way that could not be undone. The grandfather
was arrested. The boy was taken into state custody. And Kofi was left with the knowledge that he had
seen the warning and had waited too long to speak it.

In the weeks that followed, Kofi found himself unable to settle. The store, which had been a refuge
of routine, became a place of dread. Babies cried in the aisles, and Kofi's body would flood with a
sensation of falling, as though the ground beneath him had given way. The sound went directly to his
chest, to some place where alarm lived now. Doors opening—the automatic doors at the entrance,
customers pushing through the stockroom door—made him flinch as though something dangerous was
entering. He would find himself watching the faces of fathers and grandfathers in his line,
scrutinizing them for signs of cruelty, for the particular hardness in the eyes that he now believed
he should have recognized before.

What troubled Kofi most was that he could not hold the memory together. When he tried to tell
Margaret what had happened, the sequence would slip away from him. He remembered the bruise, but not
the man's face clearly. He remembered calling the police, but not what day it had been. He
remembered the boy in the hospital gown—or did he? He had never actually seen him there; he had only
heard from an officer what had been found. The gaps frightened him. He felt as though he were
remembering something that had happened to someone else, and he could not quite retrieve the shape
of his own failure to act.

At night, he lay beside Margaret and felt nothing. This was perhaps the worst part—not the fear or
the watchfulness, but the deadness that had settled into his chest. He had loved his work, had taken
pride in the small courtesies of the job. Now he moved through the register with the hollow
efficiency of a man going through motions. He had come to believe that the institutions meant to
protect children—the police, the hospitals, the social workers—had known what was happening and had
done too little, too late. And the grandfather, as far as Kofi knew, would eventually be released,
would live out his life, would face no real reckoning for what he had done.

Kofi had finally asked his doctor for a referral to a therapist, not because he believed anything
could be fixed, but because Margaret had asked him to, and because he could not continue to work in
a state of constant threat. He sat in the waiting room now, seventy-one years old, a man who had
tried to do the right thing and could not shake the feeling that he had failed.
