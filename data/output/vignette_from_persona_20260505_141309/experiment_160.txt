# Marcus Chen

Marcus Chen had been a jazz guitarist of modest but genuine reputation for thirty years when
everything fractured in the space of eight months. The stalking had begun in late 2022, after a
performance at a small venue in Portland where he'd played a three-night residency. A woman in the
audience—someone he did not remember meeting—had become convinced of a connection between them. She
appeared at his next two gigs, then at his apartment building. She called repeatedly. When Marcus
blocked her number, she found others to call from. She sent letters. She waited in the parking lot
of the music school where he taught two evenings a week. The police were called. A restraining order
was filed. By spring 2023, the immediate threat had receded—she had moved away, the calls had
stopped—but something in Marcus had not recovered.

The night she was arrested after violating the restraining order, Marcus had been closing up the
music school. He heard footsteps behind him in the hallway, turned, and saw her face. His memory of
what happened next was fragmentary and unreliable. He remembered her voice, remembered her hand
reaching toward him, remembered the sound of his own breathing becoming rapid and strange. But there
were gaps—whole minutes he could not account for, details that refused to cohere. When he tried to
reconstruct the sequence, pieces would surface unbidden and then vanish: the texture of the wall
he'd backed against, the specific words she'd shouted, the exact configuration of the fluorescent
lights overhead. These fragments arrived without context or warning, sometimes while he was
teaching, sometimes at three in the morning. When they came, they felt entirely present—not like
memory but like the event itself occurring in his body in real time, his heart rate spiking, sweat
breaking across his palms, the hallway materializing around him with absolute sensory completeness
before dissolving again.

In the months after, Marcus had begun to withdraw. He stopped accepting new students. He turned down
performance opportunities. The idea of being in any space where he could not control the exits,
where unfamiliar faces surrounded him, had become intolerable. He could not assess danger in unknown
places—could not predict who might be there, what they might want, whether the threat had truly
ended or merely paused. He had come to watch people closely, cataloging their movements, their
expressions, searching for the particular intensity of gaze that preceded harm. At the coffee shop
where he bought his morning espresso, he positioned himself at a corner table where he could see the
door. At the grocery store, he moved quickly through familiar aisles and left if the crowd felt too
dense. His ex-wife had stopped inviting him to family dinners; his presence had become a liability,
his scanning of the room and his sudden absences into private distress too disruptive for the
gathering.

Night was worse. Darkness seemed to erase the boundaries between past and present, between the
threat that had passed and the threat that might return. He lay awake listening to sounds in the
building, to cars passing outside, to the settling of the walls themselves. He had installed three
new locks on his apartment door. He kept his phone charged and within reach. Sleep came in
fragments, and when it did, the dreams were not dreams but the hallway again, her face, the
unretrievable middle of the event that his mind refused to yield.

What had broken him most deeply was not the stalking itself but what came after—the realization that
he had been unable to stop it, that the police had been slow, that the institution meant to protect
him had failed him until the moment of crisis. He had come to believe he was weak, fundamentally
unable to defend himself, and that the world was a place where such weakness was discovered and
exploited. He felt nothing anymore—not the satisfaction of a good performance, not affection for his
remaining friends, not even fear, really, but rather a profound deadness, as though the part of him
that had been alive before the hallway had simply ceased to exist.

At fifty, Marcus had finally called a therapist. He had not performed in six months. He was afraid
that if he did not do something, he would disappear entirely.
