# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was fifty-one when the diagnosis came through—stage three pancreatic cancer, delivered in a
hospital consultation room on a Tuesday afternoon in March. He had gone in for routine imaging after
weeks of vague abdominal discomfort, the kind of thing a busy school principal might easily dismiss.
The radiologist's tone changed mid-scan. Within days, he was meeting oncologists, signing consent
forms for chemotherapy, learning words like "resection" and "palliative care." The hospital itself
became a blur of fluorescent corridors and beeping machines, but what stayed with him most
viscerally was the smell—that sharp, chemical antiseptic that seemed to coat his throat and clothes,
mingling with the faint metallic odor of his own fear. During the first infusion, a nurse had opened
a bottle of alcohol-based hand sanitizer nearby, and the combination of scents had made him nauseous
in a way that had nothing to do with the chemotherapy itself.

The treatment was brutal. Eighteen months of cycles, hair loss, weight loss, the slow erosion of the
man he had been. His wife, Patricia, had stood beside him through it all—the hospital visits, the
nights when he couldn't sleep, the moments when he was certain he was dying. She had held his hand
during procedures, her own fear hidden behind a careful smile. Then, just when his oncologist had
begun using the word "remission," Patricia had a stroke. She was sixty-three. She died in the same
hospital where Marcus had spent so much time, and he had been there when it happened—in a room that
smelled of antiseptic and alcohol wipes, watching her slip away.

That was three years ago. The cancer had not returned. Marcus's body, improbably, had held. But
something fundamental had fractured in him that could not be scanned or treated with chemotherapy.

At first, the avoidance was practical. He couldn't face his annual physical—the thought of sitting
in an examination room, of a doctor's hands on his chest, of that particular quality of clinical
concern in a physician's voice, made his chest tighten unbearably. He skipped it. Then the next
year. When his blood pressure spiked and his internist's office called to schedule follow-up, Marcus
found himself unable to return the call. Instead, he rehearsed what he might say if he did call
back, running through explanations and deflections in his head until the moment passed and it felt
easier simply not to engage. He had come to believe that if he didn't go to the doctor, if he didn't
allow anyone to examine him, then his body couldn't betray him again. The logic was not conscious,
but it was absolute.

The isolation crept in more slowly. A colleague invited him to dinner; Marcus spent two days
beforehand planning what to say, how to explain his recent distance, whether he could manage to seem
normal. The weight of the performance exhausted him before he even arrived. He cancelled. Friends
from the school stopped calling as often. His daughter, who lived in Portland, began sending emails
instead of suggesting visits. Marcus told himself this was better—fewer opportunities for people to
see what he had become, fewer chances to perform the role of a functioning widower and cancer
survivor. The truth he could not quite articulate was that he no longer recognized himself in the
mirror. The man looking back was cautious, diminished, unreliable. He had survived, but the person
who had survived felt like an imposter wearing his face.

What brought Marcus finally to seek help was a moment of unexpected clarity. He had been in his
office at the school when the aroma of hand sanitizer drifted in from the hallway—a student had used
it near his door. The smell hit him, and suddenly he was not in his office but in the hospital, and
his body was rigid in the chair, shoulders hunched, hands gripping the armrests exactly as they had
gripped the sides of the examination table during his last oncology appointment. The memory came
without images—no visual recall of the room or the machines—but the emotion was total: the certainty
that his body was about to fail him, that he was unsafe even in his own skin. A student knocked, and
Marcus could not answer. He sat frozen for twenty minutes after the child left, terrified and
ashamed in equal measure.

That night, alone in the house he and Patricia had shared, Marcus made a phone call. He knew he
could not continue like this, cut off from everyone, unable to trust his own body or his own
judgment about what was safe. He was afraid of what treatment might require of him, afraid of
hospitals and examinations and the vulnerability they represented. But he was more afraid of
disappearing entirely into this smaller, isolated version of himself. He needed help to reclaim some
version of the man he had been, or at least to understand what had happened to him and why his own
body had become the enemy.
