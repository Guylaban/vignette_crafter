# Rajesh's Reckoning

Rajesh Mehta was thirty-two when the bomb went off at the downtown transit station. He had been
waiting on the platform for the northbound train, reviewing notes for a therapy session scheduled

The blast threw him backward into a concrete pillar. He remembered the sound before the pain: a
crack that seemed to split the world open, followed by screaming that didn't feel human. When he
regained consciousness in the hospital, his left arm was bandaged and his hearing in his right ear
was diminished, but he was alive. Seventeen others were not.

In the months after, Rajesh had returned to his practice with what he believed was resilience. His
wife, Priya, was relieved to have him back at work. His colleagues at the clinic remarked on his
composure. But something fundamental had shifted in how he moved through the world. He had come to
believe, with a certainty that felt absolute, that he could not prevent terrible things from
happening—not to himself, not to anyone. The bomb had taught him that control was an illusion, that
safety was a story people told themselves. The man he had been before October—the one who believed
in the efficacy of talk therapy, in the possibility of change through insight and effort—felt like
someone else entirely, someone naïve and foolish.

The nighttime became unbearable first. Rajesh would lie awake beside Priya, unable to sleep, his
mind cycling through scenarios of catastrophe. When exhaustion finally pulled him under, he would
jolt awake in the dark, his body flooded with adrenaline, certain that something was coming. He
began leaving lights on throughout the house. When Priya asked him to turn them off, he couldn't
explain the panic that rose in his throat at the prospect of darkness. He had started drinking
bourbon most nights—just enough to blunt the edge, to make sleep possible. It had become a ritual,
something he depended on.

Loud noises sent him reeling. A car backfiring on the street outside his office caused him to drop a
pen and grip the armrest of his chair so hard his knuckles whitened. His patients noticed. One had
asked if he was all right. The question had mortified him. He was supposed to be the stable presence
in the room, the one who held space for others' suffering. Instead, he was barely holding himself
together.

By his mid-thirties, Rajesh had begun avoiding anything that felt remotely unpredictable or
stressful. He turned down invitations to social events, canceled standing plans with friends,
stopped attending the community temple where he and Priya had been married. He told himself these
things were unnecessary, that they were just sources of potential threat. His world had contracted
to a narrow corridor: home, the clinic, the drive between them. Even at work, he had begun to limit
his caseload, referring away patients with trauma histories because their stories triggered a
vertigo he couldn't manage. He sat through sessions feeling distant from his own profession, as if
watching himself perform the role of psychologist from behind glass.

The alcohol had escalated. A glass of bourbon became two, then a third. Priya had found him asleep
on the couch at nine in the evening more than once, an empty bottle on the side table. She had asked
him, gently at first and then with visible frustration, what was happening to him. He couldn't
answer. How could he tell her that the person she had married—the thoughtful, engaged man who had
wanted to help people heal—had been replaced by someone hollow and afraid, someone who spent half
his day in a dissociative fog, drifting into fantasy scenarios where he had somehow changed that
October morning, where he had taken a different train, where the bomb had never been built?

It was Priya who finally insisted he seek help. She had found him researching substances on his
laptop at three in the morning, his speech slurred, his pupils dilated. She had wept, not from anger

choreography around his avoidance. Rajesh had resisted at first. How could he, a psychologist, admit
to needing therapy? But the weight of his own unrecognizability had become too much. He was no
longer certain who he was or what he was capable of. He was terrified of the darkness, of loud
noises, of the possibility that at any moment, without warning, everything could shatter again. And

The blast threw him backward into a concrete pillar. He remembered the sound before the pain: a
crack that seemed to split the world open, followed by screaming that didn't feel human. When he
regained consciousness in the hospital, his left arm was bandaged and his hearing in his right ear
was diminished, but he was alive. Seventeen others were not. Now, at thirty-seven, Rajesh sat in the
waiting room of another therapist's office—a role reversal that felt like a confession of
failure—and tried to remember what it had been like to believe that understanding yourself could
change your life.

In the months after the bombing, Rajesh had returned to his practice at the community mental health
clinic with what he believed was resilience. His wife, Priya, was relieved to have him back at work.
His colleagues remarked on his composure. But something fundamental had shifted in how he moved
through the world. He had come to believe, with a certainty that felt absolute, that he could not
prevent terrible things from happening—not to himself, not to anyone. The bomb had taught him that
control was an illusion, that safety was a story people told themselves. The man he had been before
October—the one who believed in the efficacy of talk therapy, in the possibility of change through
insight and effort—felt like someone else entirely, someone naïve and foolish. What frightened him
most was the randomness of it all. He had done nothing to deserve being on that platform at that
moment. He had not chosen poorly or failed to prepare. A bomb had simply existed in the world, and
his body had been in its path. There was no logic to survival, no moral framework that made sense of
why he had walked away when others had not. The world was fundamentally unsafe, and everyone who
believed otherwise was simply waiting for their own reckoning.

The nighttime became unbearable first. Rajesh would lie awake beside Priya, unable to sleep, his
mind cycling through scenarios of catastrophe. When exhaustion finally pulled him under, he would
jolt awake in the dark, his body flooded with adrenaline, certain that something was coming. He
began leaving lights on throughout the house. When Priya asked him to turn them off, he couldn't
explain the panic that rose in his throat at the prospect of darkness. He had started drinking
bourbon most nights—just enough to blunt the edge, to make sleep possible. It had become a ritual,
something he depended on. Loud noises sent him reeling. A car backfiring on the street outside his
office caused him to drop a pen and grip the armrest of his chair so hard his knuckles whitened. His
patients noticed. One had asked if he was all right. The question had mortified him. He was supposed
to be the stable presence in the room, the one who held space for others' suffering. Instead, he was
barely holding himself together. His startle response had become so pronounced that even the
notification sound on his phone could send his heart racing, his palms slick with sweat. The worst
part was that he couldn't control it. His body had learned something his mind couldn't unlearn: that
danger was everywhere, that the next sound could be the one that changed everything, that vigilance
was the only honest response to a world that could detonate without warning. On difficult nights,
when the bourbon wasn't enough and sleep remained impossible, Rajesh would find himself drifting
into a fantasy where the bomb had never gone off, where he was still the person he used to be—the
confident therapist, the man who believed in healing. He would construct elaborate scenarios in his
mind, imagining himself back in that Tuesday morning before the blast, imagining different choices,
different platforms, different outcomes. These daydreams would pull him away from his surroundings
for hours at a time, leaving him disconnected from Priya even when she sat across from him at the
dinner table. He would lose track of what she was saying, would nod absently while his mind
retreated into these safer versions of his life, versions where he still had agency, where terrible
things didn't simply happen to you without reason or warning.

By thirty-seven, Rajesh had begun avoiding anything that felt remotely unpredictable or stressful.
He turned down invitations to social events, canceled standing plans with friends, stopped attending
the community temple where he and Priya had been married. He told himself these things were
unnecessary, that they were just sources of potential threat. His world had contracted to a narrow
corridor: home, the clinic, the drive between them. Even at work, he had begun to limit his
caseload, referring away patients with trauma histories because their stories triggered a vertigo he
couldn't manage. He sat through sessions feeling distant from his own profession, as if watching
himself perform the role of psychologist from behind glass. The consequences had become undeniable.
His supervisor had gently suggested he consider a leave of absence; the clinic director had
expressed concern about his declining patient load and the impact on the department's capacity.
Priya had stopped inviting her sister over for dinner, had stopped mentioning gatherings with her
family, had learned to move through their home as though navigating around his invisible boundaries.
She had also stopped asking him to come to bed at a reasonable hour. Instead, she would find him on
the couch at nine in the evening, an empty glass of bourbon on the side table, his breathing shallow
and even, sometimes lost in one of his private fantasies, sometimes simply numb. The alcohol had
escalated. A glass became two, then a third. Some nights he would wake on the couch with no memory
of how he had gotten there, his mouth dry, his thoughts fragmented and confused. Other nights he
didn't wake at all—Priya would simply cover him with a blanket and retreat to their bedroom alone.
He had become unrecognizable to himself, a hollow version of the man who once sat with suffering
people and believed he could help them find their way back to themselves.

The turning point came when Priya found him at three in the morning, researching substances on his
laptop, his speech slurred and his pupils dilated in a way that frightened her. She had wept, not
from anger but from grief—grief for the man she had married, for the marriage that was slowly
becoming a careful choreography around his avoidance. Rajesh had resisted the idea of seeking help
at first. How could he, a psychologist, admit to needing therapy? But something in Priya's face that
night—a kind of resignation, as if she were grieving someone who was still alive—had broken through
his resistance. Now, at thirty-seven, five years after the bomb had taught him that the world was
fundamentally unsafe, he sat in a therapist's waiting room and tried to explain to himself why he
had come. He was terrified of the darkness, of loud noises, of sudden sounds, of the possibility
that at any moment, without warning, everything could shatter again. He was afraid of losing himself
entirely in those fantasies of the life he used to have, afraid of waking up one day and realizing
that the man he had become was not temporary but permanent. And he was more afraid still of the
person he was becoming in his attempts to outrun that terror—the hollow man, the absent husband, the
psychologist who no longer believed in the possibility of healing, least of all for himself.
