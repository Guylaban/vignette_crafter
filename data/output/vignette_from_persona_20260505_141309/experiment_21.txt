# Kahu's Vigil

Kahu was sixty-three when he first came to the clinic, though his weathered face and the careful way
he moved suggested someone older. He had spent four decades in construction—framing houses, pouring
foundations, the kind of work that kept a man's hands rough and his back perpetually sore. But it
was not the physical toll of the job that had finally driven him to seek help. It was the way his
body had begun to betray him, responding to the world as though it remained locked in a moment from
fifteen years ago that he could not, despite everything, seem to leave behind.

The relationship with Marcus had lasted seven years. They had met through mutual friends in their
church community, and for the first few years, Kahu had felt something he thought had closed off
permanently after his wife died—a sense of companionship, of being known. Marcus had been charming,
attentive in the beginning, and Kahu, lonely and aging, had let his guard down completely. The
violence had started small, a shove during an argument about money, a slap when Kahu had questioned
Marcus's whereabouts. By the time it escalated to the night that changed everything, Kahu had
already begun to believe that he deserved it, that his weakness—his inability to simply comply, to
stop provoking—was the problem.

That final night, Marcus had come home drunk and enraged over something trivial. The argument had
escalated quickly. Kahu remembered the feel of Marcus's fists connecting with his ribs, his face,
the specific terror of being pinned against the kitchen counter, unable to escape, unable to make it
stop. He remembered the exact angle of Marcus's shoulder as he drew back for another punch, the
particular shape of his hands as they gripped Kahu's throat. When the police finally arrived—a
neighbor had called—Kahu had been too ashamed to press charges. He had told himself it was over,
that he could move past it. But his body had other ideas.

Now, at sixty-three, Kahu found himself imprisoned by a vigilance so complete it had begun to
dismantle his life. On construction sites, he positioned himself always near the exit of whatever
building he was working in, his attention divided between the task at hand and the nearest way out.
In meetings with his supervisor, his eyes traced the perimeter of the room, cataloging escape routes
with the same precision he once applied to measuring load-bearing walls. When someone raised their
voice in frustration—a common occurrence in construction—Kahu's entire body would jolt as though he
had been struck. He had nearly fallen from scaffolding twice in the past year, startled by a truck
horn or a colleague's shout.

The nights were perhaps worse. He would wake at two or three in the morning, his heart hammering,
and perform a silent circuit of his small house to verify that he was alone, that the doors were
locked, that nothing had changed while he slept. This had become routine, something he did without
thinking, though it left him perpetually exhausted. He had come to believe that he could not trust
his own judgment, that his weakness—the essential flaw in his character that had allowed him to stay
with Marcus, that had made him unable to fight back effectively—meant he could not rely on himself
to recognize danger until it was too late. The thought that he was simply a weak person had
calcified into certainty.

To manage the anxiety, Kahu had developed other habits. He spent money compulsively, ordering things
online late at night when the hypervigilance became unbearable—tools he did not need, clothes he
would never wear—anything to interrupt the cycle of dread and checking. He avoided any situation
that might involve conflict, which had cost him friendships and opportunities at work. When his
supervisor had suggested him for a promotion that would have required managing younger workers, Kahu
had declined, certain that any disagreement would spiral into something dangerous.

The numbness was perhaps the cruelest symptom. He moved through his days feeling hollowed out,
disconnected from the work he had once loved, from people who cared about him, from any sense of
future. He had not felt joy or even simple contentment in so long that he had begun to wonder if he
ever would again. It was this deadness, combined with the fear that he was losing his job because of
his increasingly erratic behavior and his inability to be present with colleagues, that had finally
brought him to the clinic. He sat in the waiting room, positioned so he could see the door, and
admitted to himself that he could not continue like this. He did not know if he could be fixed, but
he knew that the man he had become—vigilant, numb, ashamed—was not the man he wanted to be.
