# Clinical Vignette: Rajesh

Rajesh was seventy-nine years old when he first came to the clinic, moving slowly down the hallway
with a cane, his weathered hands trembling slightly as he lowered himself into the chair. He had
been a warehouse worker for forty-three years, a job he'd taken up after returning from Vietnam in
1971. That war felt both impossibly distant and vivid in equal measure—a thing that had happened to
someone else, and yet something that still woke him at three in the morning with his heart hammering
against his ribs.

The combat itself had occurred over five decades ago. Rajesh had been stationed near the Cambodian
border, part of a reconnaissance unit. On a humid afternoon in June 1970, his patrol had come under
sudden mortar fire while moving through dense jungle. The sound had been apocalyptic—not just loud,
but total, a noise that seemed to obliterate thought itself. Three men in his unit had been killed.
Rajesh had survived, but the moment had calcified inside him: the smell of cordite and earth, the
weight of a dying soldier's head against his leg, the absolute certainty that he was about to die.

For decades, he had managed. He'd married, worked, raised a daughter. But in the past three years,
since his retirement from the warehouse and the loss of the structure that job had given him, the
symptoms had intensified dramatically. Loud noises now sent him into a state of acute panic. A car
backfiring on the street outside his apartment made him flinch violently, his body rigid for several
minutes afterward. He had stopped going to the grocery store because the beeping of the checkout
scanners made him feel as though the walls were closing in. His ex-wife, whom he'd divorced fifteen
years ago, had told him before their separation that he had become "unreachable"—that he would
disappear into himself for hours after an unexpected noise, and she couldn't reach him.

What frightened Rajesh most was the sense that he was losing control of his own mind. He had come to
believe that loud sounds were genuinely dangerous, that his body's reaction to them—the sweating,
the trembling, the overwhelming urge to run and hide—was an accurate reading of real threat. When he
heard a noise he couldn't immediately identify, his mind would race through possibilities: was it
gunfire? An explosion? Even as the rational part of him knew this was unlikely, the fear felt
absolute and undeniable.

He had stopped leaving his apartment except for necessary errands, and even then only during quiet
times of day. He avoided the senior center where he'd once played cards, because the noise of
multiple conversations and the occasional dropped dish triggered the panic. His daughter, who lived
two hours away, had begun to worry that he was becoming a hermit. She'd called him last month to say
that his grandson had asked why Grandpa never came to visit anymore.

Sleep was fragmented and unreliable. He would lie awake listening to the ambient sounds of his
building—pipes creaking, neighbors moving above him—and interpret each one as a potential threat.
His dreams, when they came, were vivid and distressing, often returning him to that jungle, though
the details shifted: sometimes he was searching for the bodies of his fallen comrades; sometimes he
was the one who was dying.

It was his daughter who had finally insisted he seek help. She had found him sitting in his darkened
bedroom on a Tuesday afternoon, unable to explain why he'd stopped answering the phone. "Dad," she'd
said, "you can't live like this." And something in her voice—a mixture of love and desperation—had
broken through the fog enough for him to make the call.

Now, sitting in the clinic, Rajesh spoke quietly about the war, about the sounds that haunted him,
about the life that seemed to be shrinking around him. What he wanted, he said, was to feel safe
again. What he feared most was that fifty years of carrying this alone had made it too late to
change.

potential threat. His dreams, when they came, were vivid and distressing, often returning him to
that jungle, though the details shifted: sometimes he was searching for the bodies of his fallen
comrades; sometimes he was the one who was dying, gasping for air as blood filled his lungs. He
would wake gasping, his sheets soaked through, unable to fall back asleep for hours.

During the day, his mind felt foggy and slow—he struggled to concentrate on anything, and his memory
seemed to slip away like water through his fingers. He couldn't remember if he'd taken his blood
pressure medication, couldn't follow the plot of a television show, couldn't hold a conversation
without losing the thread of what someone had just said to him. His thinking had become rigid in
other ways too; he found himself stuck on worst-case scenarios, unable to imagine any future in
which things improved. He had also developed a persistent sense of dread about the future, a
conviction that something terrible was going to happen and he would be powerless to stop it. These
lapses and this sense of impending doom frightened him almost as much as the loud noises did. He had
stopped leaving his apartment except for necessary errands, and even then only during quiet times of
day. His daughter, who lived two hours away, had begun to worry that he was becoming a hermit. She'd
called him last month to say that his grandson had asked why Grandpa never came to visit anymore. It
was his daughter who had finally insisted he seek help. She had found him sitting in his darkened
bedroom on a Tuesday afternoon, unable to explain why he'd stopped answering the phone. "Dad," she'd
said, "you can't live like this." And something in her voice—a mixture of love and desperation—had
broken through the fog enough for him to make the call. Now, sitting in the clinic, Rajesh spoke
quietly about the war, about the sounds that haunted him, about the life that seemed to be shrinking
around him. What he wanted, he said, was to feel safe again, to trust his own mind, to be present
with his family. What he feared most was that fifty years of carrying this alone had made it too
late to change.
