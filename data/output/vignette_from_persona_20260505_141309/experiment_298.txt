# Mariana's Reckoning

Mariana was thirty-seven when she first came to the therapist's office, though she moved through the
lobby of the Grandview Hotel—where she had managed operations for the past eight years—as though she
were much older. Her posture was correct, her appearance immaculate; she had learned long ago that
this was the safest way to move through a space. But there was a brittleness to her, a quality of
being perpetually braced.

The assault had happened four years earlier, on a humid August evening during a private event at the
hotel. Mariana had stayed late to oversee the ballroom setup for a corporate gala. The event
coordinator, a man named Derek whom she had worked alongside dozens of times, had offered to help
her move the final stack of linens. She had not hesitated. In the storage room on the second floor,
with the door half-closed, he had pushed her against the shelving unit, his hand covering her mouth.
The attack was swift and violent. When it was over, he had simply left, closing the door behind him
as though nothing had occurred. Mariana had sat on the concrete floor for what felt like hours,
though it was probably only minutes, unable to move or call for help.

What struck her most now, in the therapist's office, was that she could not construct a coherent
account of those minutes. She knew the sequence of actions—she could point to the facts like objects
on a table—but when she tried to speak about it, even to herself in the dark of her bedroom, the
words scattered. She would begin to describe what happened and find herself stuck, her throat
closing, unable to locate language for the violation itself. The shame that accompanied any attempt
to recall was so intense that she had learned simply not to try. It was easier to push the memory
away entirely, to keep it sealed off, than to face the burning sense of degradation that rose in her
whenever she approached it.

What had crystallized in the years since was a conviction that felt immutable: there was something
fundamentally wrong with her. Not because of what Derek had done, but because of who she was, the
kind of woman who had allowed herself to be alone with him, who had trusted him, who had somehow
invited this through her own deficiency of judgment or character. She had come to believe that the
assault was not something that had happened to her, but something she had brought upon herself
through her own flawed nature. This belief had calcified into certainty.

The hotel had become a landscape of avoidance. Derek no longer worked there—she had reported him,
and he had been terminated—but other men who resembled him, who had his build or his coloring or
something in his manner, would trigger a cascade of physical responses. Her body would flood with
adrenaline; her chest would tighten; she would feel as though she were in immediate danger even as
the rational part of her mind insisted she was safe. She had begun to avoid the storage rooms
altogether, delegating inventory tasks to her assistant. She avoided certain areas of the hotel
where she might encounter men who looked vaguely like Derek. And because these avoidances felt
insufficient, she had gradually extended her hours, arriving earlier and leaving later, filling
every moment with work—scheduling, problem-solving, calls to vendors, inspections of rooms. The work
was endless and that was the point: as long as she was moving, managing, directing her attention
outward, she was not alone with the thoughts that came when she stopped.

Her partner, Carlos, had begun to notice. He had noticed her leaving before dawn and returning after
he was asleep. He had noticed her pulling away from him physically, the way her body tensed when he
moved close. He had asked her repeatedly what was wrong, and each time she had deflected, insisting
everything was fine, she was simply busy with the hotel. But he had stopped asking. The distance
between them had become its own kind of presence.

What had finally brought Mariana to seek help was not the avoidance or the work, but a moment of
clarity that had terrified her more than anything else. She had been lying awake at three in the
morning, her mind cycling through plans for how she might find Derek, how she might make him
understand what he had taken from her, how she might engineer some form of reckoning. The fantasy
had become vivid and persistent, a place her mind went when she was exhausted. She had realized, in
that moment, that she was spending more energy on revenge than on her own life, and that the person
being destroyed was not Derek but herself. She was afraid of what she was becoming. She was afraid
that if she continued down this path, there would be nothing left of her at all.
