# Rajesh

Rajesh was fifty-eight when the trawler went down. He had been working the Gulf waters for thirty-
four years—since he was a boy of twenty-four, newly married to Priya, with calloused hands and a
reputation for reading weather and tide the way other men read newspapers. The morning of the
accident was unremarkable: gray sky, moderate chop, the diesel engine turning over as it always did.
He was alone on the boat, which was not unusual. He had sent his deckhand ashore the night before
because the man's daughter was sick. Rajesh remembered thinking, briefly, that he was grateful to be
alone—no one to worry about, no one to manage.

The other vessel appeared through the fog without warning. Rajesh heard the engine first, a low
thrumming that seemed to come from everywhere at once. He was below deck checking the nets when the
collision happened. The impact threw him forward into the metal frame of the hold. He felt ribs
crack—a sound and sensation he cannot now describe, though he knows with absolute certainty it
happened. The boat began to fill. Water came in fast, black and cold, and Rajesh found himself
underwater in the dark, his lungs burning, his body no longer responding to the commands his mind
was sending. He surfaced twice. The other boat was already gone. The Coast Guard found him clinging
to a piece of hull three hours later.

In the hospital, they told him his ribs would heal. His lungs would clear. He would be fine. But
Rajesh knew something had broken that no doctor could see. When he returned to his small house in
Biloxi—the same house he and Priya had bought together, where she had died of cancer five years
earlier—he could not sleep. He would lie in bed at three in the morning, listening to the silence of
the house, and his body would seize with the certainty that something terrible was about to happen.
The silence itself felt dangerous, as though it were a warning he did not know how to read. He began
sleeping with the television on, voices filling the darkness, but even then he would jolt awake, his
heart racing, his breath shallow, convinced that he had drowned.

The fear of drowning spread into other fears. Within weeks, Rajesh stopped going to the market. The
thought of standing in line, making small talk with the cashier or the other customers, filled him
with a dread he could not articulate. These strangers seemed to him unpredictable, unknowable—not
what they seemed. What if one of them was dangerous? What if they were waiting for something? He
began ordering groceries online, but even that felt risky. He would sit in his living room and scan
the windows, watching the delivery driver approach, his shoulders tense, his jaw clenched. He felt
on guard even inside his own home, even with the doors locked and the television playing.

Three months after the accident, his nephew called to tell him that his cousin's wife had died. It
was an ordinary death—she was ninety-two—but when Rajesh heard the word "lost," something in him
fractured. He could not attend the funeral. The idea of sitting in a room where people were talking
about death, about loss, about people disappearing from their lives, felt unbearable. He told his
nephew he was unwell. He did not explain that he was terrified.

By the time Rajesh came to his first appointment, he had not been on a boat in eight months. He had
not spoken to anyone outside his home in weeks. He knew this was not sustainable. He knew, on some
level, that he was not the man he had been. When he tried to describe what had happened in the
water, the words simply did not exist. He opened his mouth and felt the suffocation all over
again—not a memory of it, but the thing itself, happening in the present moment, happening now. What
frightened him most was not that he might drown again, but that his own body had betrayed him in the
water and might do so again at any moment. He could not trust it. He could not trust anything.
