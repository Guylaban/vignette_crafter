# Michael's Vigilance

Michael Chen was fifty-two years old when he walked into his school's main office on an ordinary
Tuesday morning in September and found himself unable to remember the specific sequence of punches
that had put him in the hospital six months earlier. He could recall the parking lot at the shopping
center near his home, could recall the figure approaching him as he loaded groceries into his trunk,
could recall the feeling of his ribs giving way—but when he tried to sit down and reconstruct the
event deliberately, to put the moments in order, the details scattered like startled birds. It
frustrated him, this gap, though he had come to suspect it was a mercy.

The assault itself had been brief and brutal. A man Michael had never seen before had targeted him
with what felt like calculated violence, throwing him against the car, striking his face and body
with methodical force. Michael had not fought back effectively. He remembered the cold asphalt of
the parking lot, remembered his own helplessness, remembered thinking with absolute clarity that he
might die there. Then it was over. The man fled. A stranger called an ambulance.

In the weeks that followed, Michael returned to his job as principal of Lincoln High School, a
position he had held for eight years with quiet competence. But something had fundamentally shifted.
Now, when he entered his office each morning, his eyes moved automatically to the windows, to the
layout of the room, to the exits. He had come to believe—not consciously at first, but with growing
certainty—that violence could find him anywhere. His office had two doors, one to the main hallway
and one to the administrative suite. He positioned his desk so he could see both. When parents came
for conferences, he found his attention dividing between their words and the space behind them, the
proximity of the door, whether he could move quickly if necessary.

Worse were the memories themselves. They came without warning and with overwhelming vividness.
Michael would be reviewing a budget report or signing off on a discipline form, and suddenly he was
there again—not thinking about being there, but inhabiting that moment. His body would respond as if
the threat were present. His shoulders would tense into the defensive posture he'd held in the
parking lot, his chest would tighten around the memory of impact, and his breathing would become
shallow. Once, during a staff meeting, his hands began to shake so violently that he had to excuse
himself. He told his assistant he was coming down with something.

The memories came like a film, the same sequence playing over and over. He saw the figure's
approach. He felt the first blow. He experienced again that moment of understanding—the realization
that he was being attacked, that his body was no match for this violence. When the memory released
him, sometimes seconds later and sometimes minutes, he would be drenched in sweat, his heart racing,
uncertain whether he was still in his office or back in that parking lot.

He began to notice things he had never noticed before. Walking through the hallway, he saw the metal
flagpole in the courtyard and thought about how it could be used as a weapon. In the cafeteria, he
observed the heavy serving utensils, the potential threat in ordinary objects. He scanned every room
he entered for exits—a habit so automatic now that he was barely aware he was doing it. When a
student slammed a locker door near his office, he flinched so violently that his secretary looked up
in alarm.

His wife, Patricia, had begun to notice. She had asked him why he needed to sit facing the door at
restaurants, why he had rearranged their bedroom furniture. He had not told her about the parking
lot replay, about waking at three in the morning with his body positioned exactly as it had been
during the assault, muscles locked in that old defensive crouch. He had not told her that he was
becoming afraid of his own school, the place he had built his professional identity around.

It was Patricia who finally insisted he see someone. She had found him one evening sitting in the
dark in their living room, unable to explain why he felt unsafe in his own home. Michael had
resisted, telling himself that time would heal this, that he simply needed to move forward. But the
film kept playing, his body kept remembering, and the exits kept calling his attention. He made the
appointment because his marriage felt as fragile as his sense of safety, and because he could no
longer pretend that what had happened in that parking lot was something he could simply endure
alone.
