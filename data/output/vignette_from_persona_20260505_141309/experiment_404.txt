# Margaret's Story

Margaret Chen was sixty-nine years old when she finally made the appointment, though the assault
that fractured her life had occurred forty-three years earlier. She had been working the early shift
at her family's vegetable farm outside Portland, Oregon, loading produce into the cooler at dawn
when a man she did not know came through the unlocked side door. She had been alone—her husband
still asleep in the house, her grown children not yet arrived for the day's work. What happened in
those twenty minutes had never fully left her body, though she had spent four decades trying to
convince herself it was behind her.

For the first thirty years, Margaret had managed. She worked. She raised her children. She attended
church on Sundays and smiled at neighbors. But around the time she turned sixty, something shifted.
The intrusive memory—not a blurry recollection but a vivid, sudden flash of his face, his hands, the
smell of the cooler—began arriving without warning. She might be washing dishes or sitting in the
garden, and suddenly she was back there, her heart hammering, her breath shallow. The memory came
most insistently when she was alone, which meant she began, very gradually, to stop being alone.

She stopped taking the bus to the farmers' market, a trip she had made every Saturday for decades.
The enclosed space, the strangers pressing close, the particular smell of diesel and unwashed
bodies—something about riding public transport had become unbearable. She couldn't articulate why at
first. She told her husband the market was too crowded now, that her knees made standing difficult.
But the truth was that on the bus, sitting among people she couldn't see clearly, she felt exposed
and trapped simultaneously. Her eyes would dart from face to face, scanning for threat. When someone
shifted or coughed nearby, her entire nervous system would ignite.

Certain conversations had become landmines. If someone mentioned an assault, a break-in, anything
touching on violation or safety, Margaret would excuse herself. Her daughter once began telling a
story about a friend whose home had been burglarized, and Margaret stood abruptly from the dinner
table, claiming a sudden headache. Her daughter had looked hurt. Margaret had not explained. She
could not explain, because to speak about why these topics terrified her felt like opening a door
she had nailed shut decades ago.

The drinking had started gradually, too. A glass of wine in the evening to quiet the racing
thoughts. Then two. Then a small bourbon before bed to ensure she could sleep without the nightmares
that came when she let her guard down. She had never been a drinker before, but alcohol softened the
sharp edges of her hypervigilance. It made the world feel less dangerous for a few hours. Her
husband had noticed, had asked gently if something was wrong, but Margaret had deflected. What was
she going to tell him? That she was falling apart over something that happened when she was twenty-
six?

What she had come to believe about herself was that she could no longer trust her own judgment. She
rehearsed worst-case scenarios obsessively, running through them like a grim meditation—if she went
to the market alone, someone would follow her home; if she attended her granddaughter's school
event, something terrible would happen because she was there; if she let her guard down for even a
moment, she would be hurt again. By mentally rehearsing these catastrophes, she felt she was
preparing herself, staying vigilant, staying safe. But the rehearsals never ended. There was always
another scenario, another way the world could betray her.

She had withdrawn from people steadily. Her book club had stopped calling after she declined three
consecutive months. Her sister had given up inviting her to family gatherings. Margaret told herself
it was kinder this way—that she was protecting others from her darkness, that she was too damaged
now to be good company. The isolation felt like both punishment and protection.

The only places where Margaret felt even remotely safe were her bedroom and her kitchen. She had
rearranged her bedroom so she could see the door from her bed. In the kitchen, her back was against
a wall. Everywhere else in the world felt like a stage where something catastrophic was waiting to
happen. She checked behind herself constantly—in parking lots, in the hallway, even in her own home.
Her husband would find her standing at windows, peering out at the driveway. She had become a
stranger in her own life.

It was her doctor who finally said the words that made something crack open inside her. At a routine
checkup, when Margaret mentioned her sleep problems and her drinking, the doctor had asked directly:
"Has something happened to you? Something traumatic?" Margaret had sat in that small examination
room, her hands shaking, and for the first time in forty-three years, she had said it aloud. "Yes. A
long time ago."

Now, sitting in the therapist's office, Margaret was both terrified and desperate. She was afraid
that naming what happened would make it real, would make it consume what little life she had left.
But she was also exhausted—exhausted by the constant vigilance, the isolation, the way her own mind
had become a cage. What she hoped for, though she barely dared to speak it, was that there might be
a way back to the world, to her husband, to her granddaughter's life. She didn't know if she could
trust herself to find that way. But she was willing to try.
