# Carmen's Vigil

Carmen Valdez was seventy-nine years old and had spent the last fifty-three years flying fighter
jets for the United States military—first as an active-duty pilot, then as a test pilot, finally as
a consultant to younger aviators. She had navigated hostile airspace, made split-second decisions at
supersonic speeds, and brought damaged aircraft safely to ground. Her hands had been steady. Her
mind had been sharp. On the morning of March 14th of the previous year, all of that precision and
training had meant nothing.

She had been driving home from the grocery store in her neighborhood in San Antonio, a place she
knew intimately, where she had lived for thirty years with her late husband Miguel. The street was
crowded—a Saturday afternoon, people on the sidewalks, cars parked bumper to bumper. She was turning
onto her own block when she noticed the police vehicle behind her, then another ahead. Within
seconds, officers were at her window, their bodies rigid, their hands near their belts. They had
confused her silver sedan with a vehicle involved in a robbery three blocks away. Carmen, a woman
with a lifetime of authority and respect, found herself on the pavement, hands behind her head,
while officers shouted commands she could barely parse through the adrenaline surge. One officer's
knee pressed into her back. Another's hand gripped her arm so tightly that bruises bloomed there for
two weeks afterward.

The worst part was not the physical contact itself, though that had terrified her. It was the
smell—the sharp, chemical stench of the officer's sweat mixed with gun oil and the acrid residue of
something burned. That smell had embedded itself somewhere deep in her nervous system. It returned
without warning: while she stood in the produce section of her regular grocery store, while she sat
in her own living room, while she lay in bed at three in the morning.

In the weeks that followed, Carmen's world began to contract. She stopped going to the grocery store
on weekends, then stopped going at all—she ordered her groceries online now, though the delivery
drivers sometimes came in pairs, and she found herself studying their uniforms, their posture, their
proximity to her door. She had owned a closet full of navy blazers and gray slacks, the uniform of a
professional woman who had commanded respect in a male-dominated field. She donated most of them.
The sight of her own reflection in similar clothes triggered a cascade of physical sensations: her
heart would race, her breathing would shallow, her hands would begin to shake. She had come to
believe, without quite articulating it to herself, that looking like an authority figure made her a
target, even though she knew intellectually that this made no sense.

The sirens were the worst. They came without warning, and when they did, Carmen's body responded as
though she were back on that street, on that pavement. Her hands would grip whatever was nearest.
Her eyes would dart to windows and doors. She had begun staying awake until well past midnight,
sometimes until two or three in the morning, when the city grew quiet. Sleep felt dangerous—a state
in which she could not monitor, could not scan, could not prepare. She would sit in her living room
with all the lights on, her eyes moving systematically across the room, cataloging potential
threats: the heavy bookend on the shelf, the lamp that could be a weapon, the kitchen knives visible
through the doorway.

Her daughter Rosa had noticed the changes first. Carmen had always been precise, controlled, a woman
who kept her home and her schedule with military exactitude. Now Rosa found her mother's house in
disarray, curtains drawn even on bright mornings, her mother's appearance neglected in a way that
shocked her. When Rosa suggested they go to lunch together at a restaurant downtown, Carmen had
refused so adamantly, her voice so sharp with fear, that Rosa had backed away. They had not had
lunch together in eight months.

It was Rosa who finally insisted that Carmen see someone. "Mami," she had said gently, "something
happened to you that day, and you haven't recovered. You're disappearing." Carmen had resisted—she
had survived combat training, had survived the loss of her husband, had survived a lifetime of being
one of the only women in rooms full of men. But the truth was that she could no longer relax, not
truly, not even when she sat alone in her locked house with all the doors checked and rechecked. Her
body remained in a state of readiness that exhausted her. The fragments of that afternoon kept
surfacing—the cold metal of the cruiser door, the officer's voice, the smell—arriving without
context or warning, then vanishing again, leaving her gasping.

What frightened Carmen most now was not the memory itself, but the possibility that this version of
herself—hypervigilant, isolated, unable to trust her own safety even in the place she had made a
home—was permanent. She had come to see the psychologist because she was afraid that the woman she
had been, the pilot who had trusted her instincts and her training, was gone for good.
