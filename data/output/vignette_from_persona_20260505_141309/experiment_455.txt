# Kofi's Distance

Kofi was seventy-seven years old when he first came to the clinic, referred by his wife, Ama, who
had finally insisted after forty-three years of marriage that something needed to change. He had
been a software engineer for most of his adult life—a career that had suited him well, one built on
logic and control, on systems that behaved predictably if you understood the rules. But for the past
six months, he had stopped going into the office. His company, patient at first, had begun to press.
His manager's emails asking for status updates felt like commands, and Kofi found himself unable to
respond, unable to sit at his desk even when working from home. The cursor on his screen would
blink, waiting, and Kofi would get up and walk to another room, any room, to keep his mind moving,
occupied with something—anything—that was not the feeling of being told what to do.

Kofi had been fourteen when the soldiers came to his village in Ghana. He did not speak of it often,
and when he did, the words came flat, stripped of the affect that should have accompanied them. He
could see it—he could see the trucks arriving in the early morning, the dust they kicked up, the way
the soldiers' boots sounded on the packed earth. He could see the officer's face, the rifle held
across his body, the gesture that meant *you, come here*. He could see himself standing in a line
with other boys and men. But when he recalled these moments, they arrived as disconnected
photographs in his mind, each one vivid and precise but unmoored from the next, and none of them
seemed to touch him emotionally. He described this to the clinician with a kind of bewilderment: *I
know what happened. I can see it clearly. But it feels like I am watching someone else's life.*

The specific moments remained with him as snapshots. The command. The march. The camp. The forced
labor. The hunger. Each image distinct and terrible, yet when he tried to feel the terror or the
pain or the desperation, there was only a gray distance between himself and the boy he had been.
This numbness had protected him for decades, perhaps, but it had also meant that the moments never
truly resolved; they simply existed in his mind as frozen instants, waiting.

By his late sixties, Kofi had built a life that kept the triggers at bay. He worked remotely when
possible. He interacted with colleagues through email and code reviews, not meetings. He and Ama had
developed a quiet routine: he managed the household finances, she managed the social calendar, and
he attended her events only when absolutely necessary. He had come to believe that being alone was
safer, that other people—their unpredictability, their voices, their presence—carried a subtle
threat he could not quite name. When he began to feel detached or unreal, as if the room around him
were becoming distant and dreamlike, he would stand and move, would turn on music or a podcast,
would fill his mind with noise and activity. Sitting still meant the snapshots could surface.

By the time Ama insisted he seek help, Kofi had not left their home in five months except for brief,
necessary trips to the grocery store, which he now did at dawn when few people were present. His
professional reputation, built over decades, was beginning to unravel. His manager had sent a formal
email—a command, really, though it was phrased as a request—that he either return to the office or
consider medical leave. Reading it, Kofi felt the detachment descend like a fog. He was seventy-
seven, and he realized that the life he had constructed to contain his past had finally become too
small.

What frightened him most was not the memories themselves—those were numb, almost abstract—but the
feeling that if he stopped moving, if he allowed himself to be still or to be with others, something
catastrophic would happen. He could not articulate what that something was. He came to the clinic
because Ama had made it clear that his isolation was costing him his marriage, his career, and the
possibility of the quiet, connected life they had both hoped to have in their final years together.
