# Roimata's Vigil

Roimata Parata was fifty-three years old when the delivery truck hit him. He had been a financial
analyst for twenty-eight years by then, a Samoan-New Zealand immigrant who had built a steady,
predictable life in Portland—a career managing portfolios, a marriage of thirty years to his wife
Aroha, a reputation at his firm for precision and calm judgment. The accident happened on a rain-
slicked Tuesday in October as he crossed the parking lot of a grocery store, his arms full of bags.
The truck's driver had misjudged the turn. Roimata heard the engine sound first—a heavy,
accelerating growl—before he felt the impact that threw him across the asphalt. His ribs fractured.
His left leg broke in two places. He lay there smelling the hot rubber of the tires, the acrid
diesel fumes, his own blood mixing with rain and wet concrete. The driver's door opened with a
metallic screech. Someone screamed. For what felt like hours but was probably minutes, Roimata
remained conscious, waiting to know if he would die, listening to the chaos of voices and sirens
that seemed to come from inside his own chest.

The physical recovery took fourteen months. The psychological one never really began, because
Roimata never quite accepted that what happened to him was over. By the time he returned to work, he
had already developed a habit of checking the fire exits in every room before he sat down—a quick
visual scan that had become automatic, something he did without thinking, the way other men might
adjust their glasses. At the office, he positioned his desk so that his back was never fully to the
door. His colleagues assumed he was simply being cautious; they did not notice that his eyes were
always moving, always cataloging the space around him, always aware of where the nearest exit lay.

The smells were worse than he had expected. Diesel fuel, which he encountered occasionally in
parking lots or near delivery bays, would stop him mid-breath. His body would flood with adrenaline
before his mind had even registered what he was smelling—his heart rate spiking, his palms going
cold, his leg muscles tensing as though preparing to run or fight. The smell of hot rubber came
later, triggered by worn tires or the friction of a car braking hard nearby. Aroha noticed that he
had begun avoiding certain routes to the grocery store, certain parking lots altogether. When she
asked him why, he could not quite articulate it. It was not rational. He knew, intellectually, that
the probability of being hit again was vanishingly small. But his body did not believe what his mind
knew. His body reacted first, always first, flooding him with certainty of danger before conscious
thought could intervene.

The auditory triggers were perhaps the most insidious. Car sounds—the acceleration of an engine, the
screech of tires, even the ordinary rumble of traffic—could transport him instantly back to that
parking lot. The memory would arrive complete and overwhelming, not as something he was remembering
but as something that was happening right now, in the present tense. He would hear the truck engine
again, that terrible growl, and the memory would carry not just the visual image but the actual
sounds, as though his ears were hearing them afresh. At night, he began waking in the dark, his body
rigid with vigilance, needing to verify that he was safe, that Aroha was beside him, that the
bedroom door was still locked.

Darkness had become a problem too. He could not enter a dark room without turning on every light.
The parking garage at his office building made him nauseated. He had started arriving at work
earlier and earlier, parking in the daylit areas near the entrance, accepting the longer walk rather
than risk the shadowed levels below. Aroha had gently suggested therapy six months ago. He had
refused. If he thought about the event, really thought about it, he was certain he would not be able
to handle it—the memory would consume him entirely. It was safer to stay vigilant, to keep his guard
up, to monitor the environment constantly. You never knew what could happen next. The world had
shown him that much.

He is now sixty-three. Last month, he had a panic attack at his desk when a colleague's car
backfired in the parking lot below. He left work at two in the afternoon and did not return for a
week. Aroha finally insisted. She told him she was frightened of losing him, not to the truck, but
to the life he had stopped living. This week, for the first time, Roimata made an appointment with a
therapist. He is terrified of what it will mean to stop being vigilant, to stop believing that
constant guard is the only thing keeping him alive.
