# Clinical Vignette: Michael Chen

Michael Chen, seventy-three, sits in the therapy office with his back pressed firmly against the
wall, his shoulders angled so that he can see the entire room and the door. He has done this
automatically for so long that he no longer notices himself doing it. The journalist who once moved
through rooms with the easy confidence of someone accustomed to being the observer rather than the
observed now orients his body as though every space might contain a threat he needs to monitor.

Thirty-four months ago, Michael was in the Astoria Hotel in Portland covering a labor dispute for
the *Oregonian*. He was in the third-floor conference room when the explosion occurred—a device
planted by someone with a grudge, though the full motive was never entirely clear. The blast came
without warning. Michael remembers the flash, the sound that seemed to come from inside his own
body, the taste of copper and something chemical, something burning that he has never been able to
identify precisely but which he tastes now when he thinks about that moment. He remembers the weight
of debris. He remembers the silence afterward, which was somehow worse than the noise. He was pulled
from the rubble by firefighters. Three people died in the conference room. Michael was not supposed
to survive.

For the first weeks after, Michael functioned on adrenaline and the strange clarity that comes from
being alive when others are not. He gave statements to authorities. He returned to his apartment in
Northeast Portland. But something had fractured in him that could not be repaired by simple passage
of time. The memory of those moments had taken on a quality of unreality that disturbed him more
than the fear itself. When he tried to describe it to his ex-wife during one of their stilted phone
calls, he found himself saying, "It doesn't feel like it really happened to me. It feels like I'm
remembering a movie I watched." But it had happened. He knew this. Yet his mind could not seem to
integrate the knowledge that he had been there, that his body had been crushed beneath concrete and
steel, that he had tasted that chemical taste while darkness pressed down.

The memory replayed constantly. In the shower. While reading the newspaper. At three in the morning.
It was like a film that his mind kept rewinding and playing again—the flash, the pressure, the
taste, the silence. When the memory came, it did not feel like remembering. It felt like the
explosion was happening again, right now, in the present moment. His heart would race. His breathing
would become shallow. His body would freeze completely, as though any movement might trigger another
collapse. He would sit motionless for minutes, unable to move or speak, waiting for the threat to
pass.

Michael began to notice things. The weight of a letter opener on his desk. The angle of a picture
frame. The distance to the nearest exit in any room he entered. He would scan environments
automatically, cataloguing potential weapons, potential dangers. His apartment became a fortress of
small rituals. He moved his chair so his back was always to the wall. He began to listen obsessively
for sounds—footsteps in the hallway, the elevator arriving, someone's key in a lock. He was certain
that if he was not vigilant, if he allowed his attention to lapse for even a moment, something
terrible would happen again.

The triggers were specific and unpredictable. Photographs—particularly any image from the hotel, any
news coverage from that day—would send him spiraling. Unpredictable situations, moments when he
could not anticipate what would come next, would activate a cascading sense of dread. And certain
tastes, particularly anything metallic or chemical, would transport him instantly back to the
rubble.

Over the months, Michael developed strategies to manage the intolerable feeling that he was losing
himself. He could not relax even in his locked apartment, even knowing rationally that he was safe.
So he spent money. He would order things online—books he would not read, tools he would not use,
clothes he would not wear—simply for the momentary distraction, the brief sense of agency that came
from clicking "purchase." His credit card debt climbed. His apartment filled with unopened packages.
But the spending worked, in a way. It interrupted the loop. It gave his mind something else to focus
on.

He stopped attending journalism conferences. He turned down assignments. He could not bear the
unpredictability of travel, the exposure of public spaces. After forty-two years at the *Oregonian*,
he took early retirement. His editor called it a loss. Michael called it survival.

What frightened him most was the conviction that had settled into his bones: if he allowed himself
to think deeply about the explosion, to really sit with it, he would not be able to handle it. He
would break. And beyond that fear was something darker—the belief that he had already broken, that
the capacity to feel anything normal, anything whole, had been taken from him in that moment.
Nothing in his life felt meaningful anymore. His work, which had once been the organizing principle
of his identity, seemed hollow. His relationships had withered. He was seventy-three years old and
he was living in a world that had become a series of threats to be managed rather than a place to be
inhabited.

It was his daughter who finally insisted he come. She had noticed the unopened packages, the tremor
in his hands, the way he positioned himself at the edge of family dinners. She had noticed that he
was disappearing. Michael came to therapy not because he believed it would help, but because his
daughter asked him to, and because some part of him—a part he could barely access beneath the
vigilance and the fear—wondered if there might be a way to live that did not feel like this.
