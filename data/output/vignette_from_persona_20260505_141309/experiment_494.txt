Marcus was forty-one when he walked into the clinic, though the way he carried himself—shoulders
rounded, eyes that seemed to look past rather than at you—made him appear older. He had come because
his ex-wife had finally stopped answering his calls, and his supervisor had given him a written
warning about missing work. Both developments felt inevitable to Marcus, like dominoes he had
watched falling but could not catch. What he wanted to understand, though he couldn't quite
articulate it, was why the ground beneath his life had become so unstable.

Seven years earlier, Marcus had been working on a residential renovation crew in Trenton when the
accident happened. It was a Tuesday in late September, the kind of clear morning that felt like
nothing bad could occur. He and three other men were framing out the second floor of a colonial-
style home. Marcus was on the ground, cutting lumber, when he heard the shout—a sound of surprise
rather than pain at first. He looked up to see his friend and crew chief, David, falling backward
off the scaffolding. The image burned itself into Marcus's mind in fragments: David's arms
windmilling, the terrible geometry of his body striking the concrete foundation, the specific smell
of dust and blood and something else Marcus couldn't name but could taste on his tongue—copper mixed
with something acidic, something he later realized was his own fear. The sound David made when he
landed was not a scream but a wet, final silence. Marcus had stood there, hands still gripping the
saw, unable to move, unable to stop what had already stopped.

David died three days later in the hospital. The investigation found that someone had failed to
secure the scaffolding properly. Marcus had checked it that morning—he was always careful—but not
thoroughly enough. That was the thought that had lived in his chest ever since: he could have caught
it. He could have stopped it. But he hadn't, because he was not the kind of person who stopped bad
things. The world, he had come to understand, was not a place where competence or vigilance
mattered. Bad things happened, and you stood there helpless, and then you lived with it.

For the first two years after David's death, Marcus had drunk heavily. When the drinking stopped
working—when it no longer dulled the sensory flashes that arrived without warning—he had found other
ways to absent himself. He began gambling, first online, then at the casino in Atlantic City. The
rush of placing a bet, the possibility of a win, the total absorption of his attention—these things
worked better than alcohol. They gave him permission to not be present in his body, to not feel the
weight of his own inadequacy. He would drive to Atlantic City on his days off and lose money he
didn't have, and in those hours at the table, he was not the man who had failed to save his friend.

At work, Marcus had become someone his crew barely recognized. He had always been steady, patient
with younger workers, someone who could defuse tension with a joke. Now he moved through job sites
in a kind of controlled fury that he never allowed himself to express. When a subcontractor made a
mistake, or when someone on his crew cut a corner, Marcus would feel the rage rise in his throat
like bile, and he would swallow it down. He would excuse himself, walk to his truck, sit in the cab
with his hands gripping the steering wheel until his knuckles went white. To let that anger out felt
dangerous to him—if he started, he believed, he would not be able to stop. And if he lost control,
he might hurt someone, and then he would be the kind of man who broke things, the kind of man who
made things worse. So he kept it sealed inside, a pressure that never released.

The memory of David's fall came without warning and with complete sensory vividness. A smell—dust
after rain, or sometimes the particular copper-iron scent of raw meat at a grocery store—would
transport Marcus instantly back to that moment. He could taste it again, that acidic-metallic
compound that coated his mouth. He would see the image: David's body, the angle of his fall, the
terrible stillness. These were not thoughts that Marcus could reason with or dismiss. They were
pictures that played without his permission, and they came with a certainty that he was powerless to
prevent the next catastrophe, that he was fundamentally inadequate to the task of keeping people
safe.

When these memories arrived, Marcus would sometimes deliberately try to leave his body, to
dissociate, to create distance between himself and the sensation. He had learned to do this—to
mentally step outside himself, to watch himself from a remove, to blank out the sensory details. It
worked sometimes. Other times, he would end up at the casino instead, or sitting in his truck for
hours, unable to account for the time.

His ex-wife, Jennifer, had tried for three years to reach him. She had suggested therapy,
medication, anything. But Marcus had come to believe that there was nothing to fix, nothing to
understand—only a truth he had learned: that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that he was
inadequate to protect anything or anyone, and that the best he could do was try not to hurt anyone
else while he waited for the next inevitable disaster. When Jennifer finally left, it had felt like
confirmation rather than surprise.

Now, sitting in the clinic, Marcus was not sure what he wanted. He was certain that talking about
what happened would not change the fact of it. He was certain that he could not stop bad things from
happening. But something had shifted. His supervisor's warning had been the final weight on a scale
that had been tipping for years. He had come because he had nowhere else to go, and because
somewhere beneath the conviction of his own helplessness, there was a small, terrified part of him
that wondered if he was wrong.
