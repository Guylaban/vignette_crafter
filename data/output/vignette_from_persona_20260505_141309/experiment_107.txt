# Karim's Reckoning

Karim was forty when the earthquake came. He had been in his apartment in Beirut for the month-long
visit home—a trip his wife Sarah had encouraged after two years of nothing but video calls with his
aging parents. He was standing in his mother's kitchen on a Tuesday morning, drinking coffee and
listening to her complaints about his sister's divorce, when the floor began to move beneath him.
The sensation was not violent at first, but undulating, as if the building itself had become liquid.
Then came the sound—a deep, grinding roar that seemed to emanate from the earth itself, a noise he
could feel in his teeth and spine. The walls cracked. Plaster fell. His mother screamed. He grabbed
her arm and pulled her toward the doorframe, but the kitchen cabinet beside them collapsed, and in
that moment of noise and dust and the terrible wrongness of the world shifting, he felt something
give way inside him—not physically, but something deeper, a certainty about safety that would never
fully return.

His mother survived with a broken wrist. Karim had a laceration on his shoulder from the falling
cabinet and minor injuries that required no hospital admission, though the thought of entering one
afterward made his chest tighten. He returned to Seattle two weeks later, and for the first month he
told himself and Sarah that he was fine, that he was simply grateful. He forced himself to think
only positive thoughts—his mother was alive, he had been spared, there was no point in dwelling on
what could have been. But the positive thoughts felt hollow, like words he was reciting rather than
believing. Underneath them lay something darker: a bitter conviction that someone else—someone more
capable, more aware, more deserving—would never have put themselves in such a precarious position.
They would have left Beirut earlier. They would have recognized the warning signs. They would have
been smarter.

The months that followed unfolded in ways Karim did not fully understand at the time. He began
working from home permanently, citing productivity concerns to his manager at the tech firm, though
the truth was more complicated. Open office spaces now felt dangerous—he needed to know where the
exits were before he sat down, and even then, the sudden movement of colleagues passing his desk
sent his body into a state of alarm that embarrassed him. When his wife tried to embrace him from
behind one evening while he was working, he flinched violently, his heart hammering as though
genuine threat had materialized. She had meant only affection; he had reacted as though she were an
assailant.

The memories themselves were fragmented and overwhelming. He could not place them in sequence—one
moment he was in the kitchen, the next he was on the street, then back inside, then somewhere else
entirely. When they came, they came with total sensory vividness: he could hear the roar again, feel
the dust coating his throat, smell the concrete and fear. It was not like remembering something that
had happened; it was like being there again, completely, with no distance between then and now. He
had begun to notice that certain sounds—a heavy truck passing, construction noise from the building
next door—could trigger these episodes. He would be at his desk, and suddenly the ambient sound
would shift and he would be back in his mother's kitchen, gasping.

What frightened him most was the conviction that had settled into his bones: nothing good could
happen to him anymore. He used to be happy—he and Sarah had planned to start a family, had talked
about buying a house. Now the thought of bringing a child into a world this fragile, this
unpredictable, seemed like cruelty. He was always miserable, he told himself, and he deserved to be.
He ruminated sometimes about the building's negligent construction, about the officials who had
failed to enforce safety codes, fantasizing about ways to hold them accountable, though he knew
these thoughts led nowhere and only deepened his despair.

Sarah had finally insisted he see someone. She said she barely recognized him anymore, that he had
become withdrawn and angry in ways that frightened her. Karim had resisted for months, but after he
snapped at her during a routine medical appointment reminder—the thought of a physical examination
triggering a visceral panic that he could not explain—he had relented. He was here now, sitting in a
therapist's office, uncertain whether anything could actually change the fact that the world had
shown him its true nature: unpredictable, violent, and indifferent to his survival.
