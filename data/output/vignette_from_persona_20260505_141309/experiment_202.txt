# Marcus

Marcus was twenty-six when two men cornered him outside a convenience store three blocks from his
apartment in New London. It was late—just after midnight on a Thursday in November—and he had run
out for coffee he didn't really need, a habit born of insomnia that had plagued him since his first
year with the Coast Guard. He remembered the cold, the particular quality of the sodium lights
overhead, the way one man's voice had a rasp to it when he said *give us your wallet*. He remembered
the fist that came at his face, the taste of copper, the sensation of being pressed against the
rough brick wall of the storefront. He remembered the second man's hands on his pockets, the
violation of it, the absolute certainty that something worse was about to happen. And then—a gap.
Then sirens. Then an officer asking him questions he couldn't quite answer, and the realization that
the men had run, that he was alive, that it was over.

Now, three years later, Marcus cannot narrate the event cleanly. When his therapist, or his
girlfriend Keisha, or the Coast Guard counselor has asked him to describe what happened, he finds
himself stalled. The words don't come. He knows intellectually what occurred—the mugging, the
violence, the aftermath—but when he tries to speak it aloud, something severs. He will say "I was
hit" and then fall silent, frustrated, as though the sentence itself is a foreign object in his
mouth. It is easier, sometimes, to say nothing at all.

What troubles him more is the fragmentation. The memory does not exist as a coherent sequence.
Instead, Marcus experiences it as disconnected shards: the rasp of that voice, isolated and vivid.
The pressure of the wall against his spine, a sensation without context. The sodium light reflecting
off something metallic—a ring, a knife, he cannot determine which—that appears and disappears in his
mind without narrative thread. These pieces do not assemble into a story. They float, discrete and
terrifying, refusing to cohere into something that has a beginning, middle, and end. When he tries
to arrange them, they slip past each other like cards he cannot shuffle properly.

More disorienting still is the emotional flatness that sometimes accompanies these fragmented
recollections. On certain mornings, usually when he wakes before dawn, the memory surfaces unbidden.
He finds himself thinking about the man's voice, the wall, the light—and feels nothing. No fear, no
anger, no grief. The events replay as though they happened to someone else entirely, a story he
overheard rather than experienced. This numbness is, in its own way, more disturbing than the
fragmentation. It suggests that some part of him has simply... disconnected. That he has become a
witness to his own life rather than a participant in it.

Marcus is thirty-two now. He has remained in the Coast Guard, though his commanding officer has
noticed a change—a flatness in his demeanor during briefings, a reluctance to engage in the
camaraderie that once came naturally. Keisha has noticed it too. She has asked him repeatedly to
talk about what happened, to let her in, but the words will not come. He sits beside her on the
couch and opens his mouth and feels the absence of language, the way the event resists articulation.
She has stopped asking.

It was a conversation with a fellow officer, one who had served in combat overseas, that finally
prompted him to seek help. The officer had mentioned PTSD, had named something Marcus had been
living with but not acknowledging. Marcus realized that three years of this—the fragmented images,
the emotional deadness, the inability to speak—was not something he could continue carrying alone.
What frightened him most was not the memory itself, but the possibility that he would spend the rest
of his life unable to fully own what had happened to him, unable to integrate it into a coherent
sense of self.
