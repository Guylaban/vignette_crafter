# Clinical Vignette: Kofi

Kofi was fifty-two when he walked into the office, though his bearing made him seem older. He had
been a plumber for twenty-eight years—the kind of man who knew the bones of buildings, who could
diagnose a problem by sound alone, who took pride in the steadiness of his hands and the reliability
of his reputation. That identity had anchored him through a long marriage to Ama, through raising
two children in a modest neighborhood outside Atlanta, through the small satisfactions of a trade
well-practiced. Three years ago, that identity began to collapse.

The assault happened on a Thursday evening in October. Kofi had been called to a residential job in
a part of town he did not know well—a basement pipe replacement that would take two hours, maybe
three. He was alone, as he usually was. The homeowner had left him the keys and said he would be
back by nine. Around seven-thirty, as Kofi was finishing up, a man came down the basement stairs
with a knife. Kofi never saw his face clearly. What he remembers, what returns to him in fragments
of image that he cannot quite string into sentences, is the blade catching the fluorescent light,
the man's arm moving, the shock of pain across his ribs, the sound of his own voice—a sound he did
not recognize—crying out. He was stabbed four times. A neighbor heard him calling for help and the
man fled. Kofi spent two weeks in the hospital and another six weeks recovering at home.

The police investigation went nowhere. No arrest was made. The case file grew cold. Kofi came to
believe that what had happened to him did not matter, that the world had decided his suffering was
not worth the effort to address. This belief took root and spread like an infection through
everything he thought about himself and his circumstances.

Within weeks of returning to work, Kofi found he could no longer move freely through his day. He
began taking jobs only in areas he knew, in neighborhoods where he had worked before, where he could
control the variables. His clients started to notice he was declining jobs, becoming selective in
ways he never had been. His supervisor asked if everything was alright. Kofi said yes, but what he
meant was that he could not manage the feeling of exposure, the sense that danger lived in
unfamiliar spaces, that his body was no longer his to trust in the world. He came to feel safe only
at home, in his basement workshop, and in the few commercial buildings where he had worked for
years. Anywhere else, his chest would tighten.

The startle responses came without warning. A coworker moving quickly past him on a job site sent
him into a state of acute fear that lasted hours. The sound of someone running up stairs behind him
made his vision narrow. He became hypervigilant to movement in his peripheral vision, interpreting
every sudden gesture as a threat. Ama learned not to touch him from behind. His children, when they
visited, stayed quiet.

Sleep became a battlefield. Kofi would lie awake until two or three in the morning, his mind cycling
through images he could not articulate—the blade, the stairwell, the man's silhouette—and a crushing
sense of shame that he could not explain. He had been helpless. He had been hurt in his own body, in
a space where he was working, doing the thing that defined him, and no one had been held
accountable. When he finally slept, the images followed him. He woke in a cold sweat, often with the
conviction that something terrible was about to happen.

What frightened him most was not the memory itself but what the memory suggested about him: that he
was no longer the man he had been, that he could not trust his own judgment anymore, that he had
somehow failed in a way he could not articulate or forgive. He had been strong. He had been capable.
Now, at fifty-two, he felt like a man diminished, confined to a shrinking world, unable to do the
work that had always meant something to him. His income had dropped by nearly forty percent. Ama had
stopped asking when he would feel better.

It was Ama who finally insisted he come in. She said he was disappearing, that the man she married
was becoming a ghost in his own house. Kofi came because he had run out of other options, because
the guilt and the rage and the trapped feeling had begun to feel like they might consume him
entirely.
