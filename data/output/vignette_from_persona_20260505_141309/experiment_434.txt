# Clinical Vignette: Katya

Katya was thirty-one when she came to the clinic, referred by her EAP coordinator after she had
called in sick four times in two weeks. She sat in the waiting room with her back to the wall, her
eyes moving across the doorway and windows in a steady, practiced rhythm. When the therapist invited
her into the office, Katya chose the chair that faced both the door and the window, declining the
more comfortable one that faced only the wall. She was thin, her uniform still creased from the
ambulance station, and her hands trembled slightly as she folded them on her lap.

The incident had occurred eight months earlier, during what should have been a routine call. Katya
and her partner had responded to a welfare check in a downtown neighborhood. A woman had been
screaming inside an apartment; police had arrived first. When Katya and her partner entered, a
uniformed officer—one she would later learn had a history of excessive force complaints—had
misidentified Katya's partner as an intruder. The officer had tackled him. Katya had moved forward
to help, to explain they were paramedics, and the officer had turned on her. His hand had come down
hard on her shoulder, spinning her around. His grip had been violent, proprietary. She remembered
the feeling of her arm being wrenched behind her back, the shock of being treated as a threat, the
absolute certainty in that moment that she might be hurt worse. The officer had released her only
when his supervisor arrived and clarified the situation. No charges were filed. No formal complaint
was made. The officer had simply left the scene.

In the weeks that followed, Katya had returned to work. But something had fractured. The first time
a colleague had reached over to tap her shoulder during a call, she had flinched so violently that
she had nearly dropped an IV bag. The second time, at the station kitchen, when someone had patted
her back in greeting, she had spun around with her fists raised. Her partner had backed away,
confused. Katya had apologized, but the shame had mingled with something worse: the knowledge that
she could not trust her own body anymore, that touch—even gentle, familiar touch—now announced
itself as danger.

Within two months, Katya had begun to avoid situations where she might encounter police. She
requested reassignment away from certain districts. She stopped going to the joint training sessions
where paramedics and officers coordinated. When she had to work alongside police at a scene, her
stomach would clench and roil, nausea rising in her throat until she had to excuse herself. She told
herself it was just the smell of the scene, the stress of the call, anything but the uniform itself.
But the truth was simpler and more terrifying: anything that reminded her of that officer—the
particular shade of blue, the weight of authority, the casual way some officers moved through space
as though they owned it—sent her into a state of hyperalert vigilance that left her exhausted.

What had begun as avoidance of police had metastasized. She found herself unable to be around anyone
in authority. She had left a medical conference early because the keynote speaker had been
introduced by a hospital administrator. She had stopped attending her divorce mediation sessions,
claiming scheduling conflicts, when in truth the mediator's formal tone and position of power had
made her feel small and trapped. The anger that might have been a natural response to all of
this—anger at the officer, at the system that had protected him, at her own fractured nervous
system—had nowhere to go. She had learned, years ago, that anger was dangerous, that it led to
consequences. So she swallowed it, pushed it down, kept her face neutral and her voice steady. But
the effort of suppression was like holding her breath underwater.

When Katya tried to describe the incident to the therapist, the narrative fell apart. She would
start with the call, then jump to the officer's hand on her shoulder, then loop back to the moment
before, unable to sequence the events in any coherent order. The memory did not feel like something
that had happened eight months ago. It felt immediate, present-tense, as though the officer's grip
was still on her arm, as though she were still in that apartment, still trapped in the moment of
being mistaken for a threat.

She had come to the clinic because last week, during a routine transport, a family member had
reached out to steady her as she was loading the stretcher, and Katya had struck him—not hard, but
hard enough that he had filed a complaint. The station commander had suggested she seek help. Katya
sat across from the therapist now, her eyes still scanning the room, and said quietly, "I don't know
how to be touched anymore. I don't know how to be around people who have power. And I'm terrified
that if I let myself feel angry about what happened, I'll lose everything."
