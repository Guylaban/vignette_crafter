# Margaret's Burden

Margaret Chen had been seventy-three years old for six months when the nursing home collapsed. She
had gone to visit her husband Robert on a Tuesday afternoon in March, bringing him the lemon bars he
loved, parking in the familiar spot near the east entrance of Riverside Gardens. The building had
been constructed in 1987, and the structural engineer's report would later confirm that a
combination of deferred maintenance and a structural failure in the north wall had created an
invisible catastrophe waiting to happen. Margaret had been in Robert's room on the second floor when
she heard the sound—not a crash at first, but a deep, terrible groan, as though the building itself
was crying out in pain. The floor tilted. A window shattered. She remembered the exact angle of the
afternoon light through the dust, the way Robert's wheelchair tipped, the smell of his cologne
mixing with plaster and something acrid she would later learn was electrical fire. She had tried to
hold him steady, had felt his hand grip hers with a strength she didn't know he still possessed, and
then the wall beside them gave way and he was gone—pulled downward into the collapse—and she was
falling too, landing hard on her left side, her hip screaming, unable to move, unable to reach him,
listening to the sound of concrete settling and people screaming and the terrible, terrible silence
underneath it all.

Robert died in the hospital three days later. Margaret survived with a fractured pelvis and a
fractured mind.

In the months that followed, certain things had begun to trigger her in ways she could not control.
When her daughter wept during a visit, Margaret would find herself rigid, her throat closing, her
own tears coming in a flood she couldn't stop—not because she was sad in that moment, but because
crying had become the sound of the building falling, the sound of Robert slipping away. When her
granddaughter hugged her, Margaret's body would seize. The pressure of another person's arms around
her would send her back to that room, to the moment of impact, her left side suddenly remembering
the exact position it had been in when the wall came down. Worst of all was the smell of alcohol—her
son-in-law's cologne, a hand sanitizer someone used in the kitchen—because the electrical fire had
carried that sharp, burning note, and it meant danger was present, it meant the world was about to
collapse again.

Margaret had stopped reading the news after the first week. A story about a building fire in Seattle
sent her into a panic that lasted six hours. She had found that if she kept her mind
occupied—painting, television, organizing the same cupboard three times a day—she could sometimes
push the memories back. But they came anyway, especially at night. She would lie awake listening for
sounds, certain that someone was entering her home, that danger was coming. The photographic quality
of her memories was almost unbearable: she could see every detail of that afternoon with perfect
clarity, could replay it frame by frame, and with each remembering came a crushing weight of guilt.
Why had she gone that day? Why hadn't she insisted Robert move to a different facility? Why had she
survived when he hadn't?

What frightened Margaret most was the certainty that had settled into her bones: that she would
never feel anything normal again, that the world she had believed in—a world where you could visit
your husband safely, where buildings stood, where love protected you—was a lie. She had come to
believe that her life was no longer worth the effort of living it. She had not said this to her
daughter, but she thought it every morning when she woke.

It was her daughter who finally insisted she see someone. Margaret had agreed only because she was
tired of fighting, tired of the vigilance, tired of the way her own body had become a stranger to
her. She sat in the therapist's office with her hands folded in her lap, wondering if anyone could
possibly understand what it meant to have watched your entire world—the person in it, the safety of
it, the meaning of it—simply cease to exist.
