# Marco's Story

Marco Delgado was thirty-seven years old when he first came to the clinic, though the exhaustion in
his face made him appear older. He had been working for the Archdiocese's Social Ministry for nine
years, coordinating community outreach and counseling services in the South Phoenix parishes. It was
work that mattered to him—helping families in crisis, walking alongside people through their darkest
moments—but lately he had begun to wonder if he could continue.

Four years earlier, Marco had ended his marriage to Daniel after discovering that the man he had
loved and trusted had been systematically using violence against him. The relationship had
deteriorated over two years, but the night that changed everything happened on a Tuesday in October.
Daniel had come home drunk, angry about something Marco had said to a friend. What followed was a
blur of rage: Daniel had pinned Marco against the kitchen counter, one forearm pressed hard across
his chest, holding him there while he shouted, while his breath reeked of whiskey and beer, while
Marco felt the full weight of a man's body trapping him in place. Marco had frozen completely,
unable to move, unable to fight back, his mind going blank except for the sound of the door to the
apartment slamming open and closed—Daniel kicking it repeatedly as he yelled—and the overwhelming
smell of alcohol mixed with his own fear.

The marriage had ended not long after. But the night had not.

In the months that followed, Marco found himself unable to be in the apartment they had shared. He
moved into a small house on the east side, but certain spaces triggered the same paralysis he had
felt that night. He stopped going to his favorite restaurant downtown because the route took him
past the neighborhood where they had lived together. He told himself it was practical—why torture
himself?—but the truth was more complicated. The thought of driving down those streets made his
heart race. He had come to believe that being near that place meant being vulnerable again, meant
that something terrible could happen. It was safer to avoid it entirely.

The door slamming haunted him in ways he could not fully explain. Any door closing abruptly—at the
office, at church, even at a neighbor's house—sent a jolt through his nervous system. His body would
tense as if bracing for impact. He had begun to notice that loud noises in general made him jump,
that unexpected movement in his peripheral vision caused him to react as though he were in immediate
danger. A colleague had walked up behind him in the hallway last month, and Marco had spun around
with his fists clenched before he even recognized who it was. The embarrassment was acute, but the
fear underneath it was more powerful.

At night, the vigilance intensified. Marco would lie awake, acutely aware of every sound in the
house. He had installed new locks on all the windows and doors, and he found himself checking them
repeatedly before bed—sometimes five, six, seven times—even though he knew intellectually that they
were secure. The darkness made him feel exposed in a way daylight did not. He had begun sleeping
with the bedroom light on, which meant he was perpetually exhausted.

The memories themselves were crystalline and overwhelming. When something triggered a
recollection—the smell of beer on someone's breath at a parish event, or the particular timbre of a
man's raised voice—the sensory details would flood back with startling clarity. He would hear the
slamming door in his head as vividly as if it were happening in real time. And with those memories
came a grief so profound it would stop him mid-conversation, mid-breath. He would excuse himself to
the bathroom and sit on the tile floor, overwhelmed by a sadness he could not name or control.

He had learned to manage his emotions by controlling them—by not letting them show, by maintaining a
professional demeanor at work, by keeping conversations at a distance. But the effort was
exhausting, and it was beginning to cost him. His supervisor had asked if everything was all right;
Marco had noticed that he had become quieter in team meetings, less present with the families he
served. His best friend had stopped calling as often, perhaps sensing the wall Marco had built.

It was his director at the ministry who finally suggested he seek help. She had noticed that Marco
had turned down the promotion he had been offered, that he seemed to be withdrawing from the very
work that had once animated him. Marco had resisted at first, but the accumulation of small
losses—the relationships he was not fully present for, the neighborhoods he could not enter, the
nights he could not sleep—had finally become too much. He sat in the waiting room of the clinic on a
Tuesday afternoon, the same day of the week as the night that had fractured his life, and wondered
if anyone could help him find his way back to himself.
