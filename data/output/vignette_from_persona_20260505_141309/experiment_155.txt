# Amara's Burden

Amara was eighty-two when she walked into the clinic on a Tuesday morning, her daughter's hand
steady at her elbow. She wore a long cardigan despite the warm weather, buttoned to her neck, and a
headscarf pulled low. She had been a pastor for forty-three years—first in Lagos, then in Atlanta
for the last two decades—but she had not preached in six months. The congregation had stopped asking
why.

The refugee camp at the border of Guinea and Liberia in 1991 was where the woman sitting across from
me now had nearly ceased to exist. Amara had been forty-nine, a young minister then, when militia
fighters swept through the settlement at dawn. She remembered the sound of boots on hard earth, the
shouts in a language she did not speak but understood perfectly. A soldier had grabbed her wrist,
twisted it until she thought the bone would snap. Another had pressed the barrel of a rifle against
her ribs—she could feel its coldness still, could hear the click of the safety being released. They
had taken the food rations. They had taken three women from the tent beside hers. Amara had been
left behind, and for three decades she had carried the inexplicable fact of her survival like a
stone in her chest, never understanding why she had been spared when others were not.

She had made a life. She had emigrated, married Samuel, raised two children in America, built a
congregation of two hundred souls. But the memory had never truly left her; it had only waited. Five
years ago, when her grandson came home from school and slammed a door loudly, something in Amara's
body had fractured. Sudden movements now sent her into a state of rigid alertness—her hands would
clench, her breath would catch, and she would feel as though danger were seconds away. At the
grocery store, she found herself watching other shoppers with an intensity that exhausted her,
tracking their hands, their intentions, the angle of their shoulders. When she rode the bus to visit
her sister, she sat near the window but never at the window, and she could not stop turning to look
behind her, certain that someone was following, that this time the danger would finish what it had
started all those years ago.

The memories came without warning. She would be standing in the pulpit preparing her sermon, and
suddenly she would hear the shouts, the metallic sound of the rifle being cocked, her own voice
begging in a language she had tried to forget. The vividness of these moments terrified her—the
sounds were so real that she sometimes looked around the empty church, confused about whether she
was truly safe. What frightened her most was not the memory itself but the certainty that if she
allowed herself to think about it deliberately, to speak it aloud to anyone, she would shatter
entirely. She had come to believe that the only way to survive was to keep it locked away, untouched
by words.

Samuel had asked her to see someone, a doctor, a counselor. She had refused. Talking about it would
only make it worse, she was certain. And besides, what good would it do? What had happened to her
was a violation so profound that no amount of talking could undo it. No one had ever been held
accountable. The soldiers had never been found. The women from the tent beside hers had never been
found. The injustice of it—that she had been left with this burden while the world moved on—was a
kind of slow poison. She had begun to feel as though she were not quite real anymore, as though the
trauma had hollowed her out and left behind only a shell that looked like Amara but felt like
nothing at all.

She had stopped riding the bus. She had started wearing clothes that made her invisible—dark,
shapeless layers even in summer. She had withdrawn from her congregation, claiming illness. Her
daughter had noticed the weight loss, the way her mother's hands shook at dinner. When Amara's
grandson asked why Grandmother didn't come to his soccer games anymore, Samuel had finally insisted.
He had made the appointment himself and driven her here.

Now, at eighty-two, Amara sat with her hands folded, looking at the floor. She had come because her
husband had asked it of her, but she was afraid. She was afraid that speaking the truth of what had
happened would prove that it was real, that it had truly happened to her, that she truly existed in
a world where such things were possible. She was afraid that she was too broken to be fixed, and
afraid too that she might discover she was not broken at all—that she was simply a woman who had
been treated like an object, and no amount of help could change that fundamental fact.
