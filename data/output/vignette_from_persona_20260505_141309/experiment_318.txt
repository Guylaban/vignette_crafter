# Margaret's Vigil

Margaret had spent forty-three years in front of cameras—first as a runway model in her twenties and
thirties, then as the face of luxury skincare lines well into her fifties. At sixty-six, she'd
transitioned into mentoring younger models and doing occasional editorial work, a graceful descent
that had felt natural until the afternoon that split her life into before and after. She had been
driving to a shoot in Tribeca, her silver Mercedes moving through light traffic on the FDR Drive,
when she'd glanced down at her phone for no more than two seconds to silence an incoming call. The
moment stretched and compressed simultaneously: the car ahead braking without warning, her hands
jerking the wheel, the sickening crunch of metal, and then the sight of the young cyclist crumpled
against her passenger door, his bicycle folded like paper beneath the wheels of the car behind her.

The cyclist, a twenty-two-year-old messenger named Devon, had suffered a shattered femur, a
collapsed lung, and a traumatic brain injury that left him in intensive care for six weeks. Margaret
had been cited for failure to maintain safe distance, though not for distracted driving—the police
report was ambiguous on that point, and her lawyer had advised her not to volunteer information. But
Margaret knew. She knew exactly what she had done and what she had failed to do. She replayed it
constantly: if she had not reached for the phone, if she had checked her mirrors more carefully, if
she had simply left five minutes earlier or later. Other drivers made the same mistake every day and
walked away unscathed. Other people were more careful, more present, more worthy of the privilege of
driving a car.

In the months after the accident, Margaret found herself unable to sit still. Her apartment on the
Upper West Side, which had been her sanctuary during her marriage to Robert and remained so after
his death three years earlier, became a place where she could not settle. She would begin to read on
the sofa and within minutes feel her chest tighten, her shoulders rising toward her ears. Her
muscles seemed to have forgotten how to release, as though her body had decided that relaxation was
a luxury she no longer deserved. She stopped accepting editorial assignments because she couldn't
concentrate on the technical demands of a shoot—the photographer's instructions would dissolve the
moment they left his mouth, and she'd find herself staring blankly at the camera, her mind somewhere
on the FDR Drive. After the third time a client remarked that she seemed distracted, Margaret
withdrew from the work entirely. The loss of purpose compounded the loss of peace; she had defined
herself through her presence, her focus, her ability to command a room, and all of that had
evaporated.

What frightened her most was the permanence of it. She had come to believe that she was the kind of
person who caused irreversible harm, that some essential carelessness ran through her that other,
more conscientious people simply did not possess. She visited Devon once in the hospital, an
excruciating encounter in which his mother had been coldly polite and Devon himself had been
sedated. Margaret had stood at the foot of his bed and understood with absolute clarity that no
apology, no financial settlement, no amount of remorse would undo what her moment of inattention had
set in motion. The humiliation was not social—no one blamed her publicly—but internal and corrosive.
She felt permanently marked by her own failure.

At night, Margaret would lie awake with her shoulders locked, her jaw clenched, her mind cycling
through scenarios she could not change. She had begun to avoid driving altogether, taking taxis or
staying home. Her friends had stopped calling as frequently; she'd declined so many invitations that
the invitations had simply ceased. Her body had become a sentry that never stood down, alert to
dangers she could not name and could not prevent. When her internist suggested that perhaps she
should speak to someone, Margaret finally made an appointment. She was hoping, against what felt
like all evidence, that someone could teach her to forgive herself. She was terrified that no one
could.
