# Clinical Vignette: Ama

Ama was forty-three when she first called the clinic, her voice steady but with an undertone of
exhaustion that suggested she had rehearsed this conversation many times in her head before dialing.
She worked in IT support for a mid-sized financial services firm in Atlanta, a role that had suited
her for nearly fifteen years—until the past year, when something had shifted in ways she could no
longer manage alone. She was married to David, a high school teacher, and they had no children. She
had come to believe, over decades, that she was fundamentally flawed in ways that made her
vulnerable to harm, and that belief had calcified into something that now governed how she moved
through the world.

The event that had set this trajectory occurred when Ama was seven years old, in rural Ghana, where
she lived with her maternal grandmother after her mother's death. Her grandmother's compound sat at
the edge of a wooded area, a place of red earth and dense vegetation where Ama and other children
played in the afternoons. One day, when Ama was alone near the tree line gathering firewood, her
grandmother's partner—a man who had lived in the compound for two years—appeared and pulled her into
a small storage shed built into the forest edge. He locked the door from the inside. What happened
in that locked, airless space, in the dark, lasted perhaps thirty minutes, though Ama had never been
certain of time. What she was certain of was that she had done something to cause it. She was
certain because her grandmother, when she learned what had happened, had wept and asked why Ama had
gone to the shed alone, why she had not stayed closer to the house, why she had made herself an easy
target. The man left the compound within a week. No one spoke of it again.

Ama had emigrated to the United States at age nineteen to study engineering, and she had built a
life that felt, on the surface, successful and stable. She married David seven years ago. They had a
comfortable home in a tree-lined neighborhood. She had friends, a career, a routine. But over the
past eighteen months, that life had begun to narrow in ways she could not fully explain to
anyone—not even to David, who had noticed her increasing withdrawal but attributed it to work
stress.

The narrowing had begun after a required all-hands retreat at her company, held at a resort in the
North Georgia mountains. The setting—dense forest, hiking trails, cabins—had triggered something
visceral that she had not anticipated. Walking through the wooded grounds on the first evening,
surrounded by trees and the sound of insects, Ama had felt her body flood with a terror so sudden
and complete that she had to excuse herself and sit in her hotel room for three hours, her heart
hammering, her skin cold with sweat. She told David she had a migraine. That night, she could not
sleep. Over the following weeks, the thought of being in any natural environment—even the small
wooded park near their home where she and David sometimes walked—filled her with a dread so powerful
that she began to find reasons to stay indoors. She started working from home more frequently,
citing deadlines and the need for focus, though the truth was that the drive to the office, which
took her past green spaces, had become almost unbearable.

Then, in the office itself, she began to notice something else. Her body felt as though it were
constantly braced for an attack. In meetings, she would find her jaw clenched, her shoulders rigid,
her hands gripping the edge of the table. When a colleague raised their voice during a technical
discussion, Ama's entire nervous system would spike—her heart would race, her breath would
shallow—as though the raised voice were a genuine threat. She would later realize, in the quiet of
her car in the parking lot, that nothing dangerous had actually occurred. But in the moment, the
fear was absolute and involuntary.

Concentration had become nearly impossible. In her IT role, she needed to focus on code, on system
architecture, on troubleshooting problems that required sustained attention. But her mind would
fracture at unpredictable moments. She would find herself staring at her screen without
comprehension, her thoughts scattered, her ability to hold a logical sequence in mind simply gone.
She had missed deadlines. She had made errors that required colleagues to redo her work. Her manager
had begun to express concern. David had noticed too—he would find her at the kitchen table in the
evening, a work laptop open before her, but her eyes distant, her body tense, as though she were
waiting for something terrible.

The worst trigger, though, was being in locked or confined spaces. Her office had a small supply
room where she sometimes needed to retrieve equipment. The first time she had gone in there after
the retreat, closing the door behind her, she had experienced what she later understood was a panic
attack—a sensation of the walls closing in, of being trapped, of needing to escape so urgently that
she had wrenched the door open and stumbled out, gasping. Now she asked colleagues to retrieve items
for her, offering vague explanations about a bad back or a scheduling conflict. She had started to
avoid certain meeting rooms that felt too small. She had even begun to feel anxious in the bathroom
at work if the door was locked, a realization that had frightened her because it meant the fear was
spreading, colonizing spaces that should have been safe.

What had finally driven her to call the clinic was a conversation with David three weeks ago. She
had been sitting on the couch, her body tight with the constant vigilance that had become her
default state, and he had gently asked her if she wanted to talk about what was happening. He had
said, with evident concern, that he barely recognized her anymore—that she seemed to be
disappearing, becoming smaller, more afraid. She had tried to explain, but the words had felt
inadequate. How could she tell him that she believed, in some deep and unshakeable part of herself,
that she was the kind of person bad things happened to? That there was something about her,
something intrinsic and unchangeable, that had made her vulnerable then and made her vulnerable
still? That she had spent thirty-six years trying to outrun that belief, and it had finally caught
up with her?

Ama had looked at David's face and seen the pain there—the pain of loving someone who was
disappearing—and she had realized that her attempt to manage this alone was no longer working. She
was not just afraid anymore. She was becoming someone she did not recognize, someone whose fear was
now eroding the life she had so carefully constructed.
