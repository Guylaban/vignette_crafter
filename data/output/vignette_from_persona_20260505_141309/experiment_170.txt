# Rosa's Story

Rosa was seventy-eight years old when she came to the clinic, her silver hair pulled back neatly,
her hands folded in her lap with the kind of stillness that suggested long practice at remaining
composed. She had been a librarian for forty-three years, and for most of those decades, she had
kept the chaos of her past neatly shelved, accessible only to herself. But in recent months,
something had begun to unravel.

The trauma that shaped Rosa's life had occurred in 1980, when she was twenty-four. She had been
living in San Salvador with her young husband, Miguel, and their infant daughter, Lucía. The
violence had come without warning one March afternoon—soldiers entering the neighborhood, screaming,
the sound of gunfire that seemed to come from everywhere at once. Rosa had hidden in a closet with
Lucía while Miguel stood in the next room. She heard him cry out. She heard the smell before she
heard anything else: the acrid, metallic scent of gunpowder mixed with something else, something she
could never quite name but that lived in her nostrils still. When the soldiers left, Miguel was
gone. They never found him. Within a year, Rosa and Lucía had fled to the United States with nothing
but documentation and a suitcase.

For decades, the work of the library had saved her. The quiet order of the stacks, the precision of
cataloging, the way books could be arranged and rearranged into perfect systems—this had given her a
structure that her life otherwise lacked. She had raised Lucía alone, worked her way up from shelver
to head librarian, married a kind man named Carlos when she was fifty-two. She had learned to push
the memory away, or so she had believed. But pushing away and surviving are not the same thing.

What had changed was subtle at first. Rosa found herself unable to concentrate during her work,
something that had never happened before. A patron would ask her to help locate a book, and she
would lose the thread of the conversation midway through, her mind suddenly elsewhere, alert to
sounds she couldn't quite identify. She became hypervigilant in ways that embarrassed her. When the
library's new security system was installed two years ago—a simple electronic door that beeped when
patrons left—Rosa had begun to dread her shifts. The beep sounded too much like something her mind
had decided meant danger. She started requesting only evening hours when fewer people came through,
when the library was quieter, when she could control the environment. Her supervisor had noticed,
had gently asked if everything was all right. Rosa said she simply preferred the peace of evening
work.

But the peace never came. Instead, she found herself going over and over the events of that day,
trying to understand why it had happened to her, why Miguel had been taken and she had been left.
She blamed herself for hiding, for not trying to stop them, for surviving when he had not. She would
be in the middle of cooking dinner, and the guilt would rise in her throat, thick and suffocating.
Why had she hidden? Why had she let him face them alone? These questions had no answers, but she
asked them anyway, compulsively, as if enough repetition might eventually yield some resolution that
made sense.

The only places where Rosa felt genuinely safe had become very few: her home, specifically the
bedroom she and Carlos shared, and the library itself during those quiet evening hours when she was
alone. Anywhere else—the grocery store, the park, even Carlos's family gatherings—set her on edge.
She found herself working harder and harder to stay busy, filling her days with tasks and projects,
anything to keep her mind from the rumination. But the busyness was itself a kind of trap: she was
exhausted, and the exhaustion made the memories worse.

What finally brought her to the clinic was something small and devastating. Last month, her
granddaughter had asked her to help with a school project about family history. Rosa had sat down to
tell the girl about where she came from, and she had found that she could not do it. The words
simply would not come. She could feel the memory—the smell, the terror, the specific texture of the
closet door against her back—but she could not translate any of it into language. She had sat there
in silence while her granddaughter waited, and she had felt the weight of all those decades of
survival suddenly become unbearable. She was seventy-eight years old. She had been carrying this
alone for so long. She came to the clinic because she was afraid that she would die without ever
being able to tell her story, and because the weight of the untold truth was finally too much to
bear alone.
