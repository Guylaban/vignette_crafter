# Amara

Amara's mother collapsed during a routine clinic visit on a Tuesday afternoon in March. Amara was
not there. She was in the field that day, conducting soil samples in a wetland restoration site two
hours north of the city, her hands in the earth, her mind focused on pH levels and microbial counts.
The call came at 3:47 p.m. from a hospital number she didn't recognize. Her mother had suffered a
massive stroke. By the time Amara arrived at the hospital, her mother was gone.

In the weeks that followed, Amara found herself replaying the morning before she left for work. Her
mother had asked her to stay home that day—nothing specific, just a feeling, she'd said. Amara
remembered this now with a weight that felt unbearable. She had been irritable that morning, eager
to get to the site, and she had dismissed her mother's request with the kind of casual indifference
that adult children sometimes show. If she had stayed. If she had listened. If she had been there,
perhaps she would have noticed something—a slur in her mother's speech, a weakness on one
side—something that would have changed everything. Amara had come to believe with absolute certainty
that her mother's death was a direct consequence of her own carelessness, her own failure to be
present. She was the kind of person who prioritized work over family, who couldn't be bothered to
listen to her mother's intuition. That kind of person deserved what had happened.

The rumination was relentless. Amara would lie awake at night—when she could sleep at all, which was
rarely—constructing elaborate scenarios in which she had made different choices. She picked
arguments with her colleagues about nothing, manufactured small dramas around lab protocols and data
entry, anything to occupy the space in her mind that would otherwise fill with the thought of her
mother alone in that clinic room. She stopped watching television, stopped scrolling through news
feeds. Any story about sudden death, about medical emergencies, about families torn apart by
circumstance, triggered a response so visceral that her body would move before her mind could catch
up—her heart racing, her hands shaking, a sensation of drowning even though she was sitting safely
at her desk.

When something took longer than expected—a delayed email response, a slow traffic light, a colleague
running late to a meeting—Amara's mind immediately spiraled into catastrophe. Something bad was
happening. Someone was hurt. She had learned not to trust the ordinary rhythms of the day.

The memory itself was strange and fragmented. Amara could recall certain details with photographic
precision: the way the hospital waiting room smelled, the color of the nurse's scrubs, the exact
phrasing of the words "I'm so sorry." But the larger narrative of her mother's death—the sequence of
events, the medical explanation, the finality of it—seemed to slip away whenever she tried to grasp
it fully. Her mind would push the memory away before she could complete it, as though some part of
her could not bear the full weight of what had happened. And when she tried to explain it to
anyone—to friends, to her supervisor, to the few people who had asked how she was managing—the words
simply would not come. She knew what had happened. She lived inside it every moment. But describing
it aloud felt impossible, as though the event existed in a space beyond language.

Most disturbing was the quality of the memory itself. When it surfaced unbidden, it did not feel
like something that had happened three months ago. It felt like it was happening now. She was in the
hospital again, receiving that news again, her mother was dying again, and there was nothing she
could do to stop it. The past and present had collapsed into a single, ongoing moment.

Amara had taken a leave of absence from her position at the environmental consulting firm. She had
stopped returning calls from friends. She had convinced herself that someone else—someone more
attentive, more worthy, more present—would never have let this happen. The injustice of it consumed
her: that her mother had died in a clinic, that no one had been held responsible, that the world had
simply moved on. Amara remained stuck, unable to move forward, unable to move backward, her body and
mind locked in a state of perpetual emergency. It was only when her sister, frightened by Amara's
increasing isolation and the raw desperation in her voice during their last phone call, insisted she
speak to someone that Amara finally made an appointment. She didn't know if therapy could change
anything. But she was terrified of what would happen if nothing did.
