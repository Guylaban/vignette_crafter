# Marcus

Marcus was fifty-five when he finally walked into a therapist's office, though the beating that
shaped his life had occurred more than half a century earlier. He was seven years old when his
stepfather—a man whose name he had learned to associate with the sound of a belt being
unbuckled—came home one evening in a rage. Marcus had left a toy on the kitchen floor. The
punishment that followed was not a single blow but a sustained assault, the kind that leaves a child
unable to construct a coherent narrative of what happened, only fragments: the kitchen tiles against
his cheek, the smell of his stepfather's cologne mixed with alcohol, a voice saying things he had
learned to forget. For decades, when Marcus tried to recall that evening clearly, the details
scattered like startled birds. He could remember the toy—a plastic car—but not what came after in
any sequence that made sense. Sometimes he could access the memory as though watching a stranger in
a film: detached, numb, untethered from the terror that had actually lived in his body that night.

The decades after had taught Marcus to interpret the world through the lens of that violence. He had
come to believe, with the certainty of someone who had learned this lesson early, that people could
not be trusted. His marriage had collapsed partly because of this: his ex-wife had complained that
he could not be close to her, that he flinched at her touch, that he seemed to expect betrayal from
her even when she had given him no reason. When she left, Marcus had spent months in a spiral of
rumination, replaying the marriage's failures, cataloging his own mistakes and inadequacies
alongside a seething inventory of her perceived wrongs. He dwelled on both with equal intensity,
unable to stop the loop.

His work as a fashion designer had once been a refuge. The precision required, the ability to
control every element of a garment's construction, had felt like a counterweight to the chaos of his
inner life. But over the past two years, his concentration had fractured. He found himself avoiding
certain times of day—particularly early evenings, when his stepfather used to return from
work—because he knew they would trigger nightmares. He had begun scheduling his design work only in
the mornings and afternoons, which meant missing client meetings and collaborative sessions. His
reputation, once stellar, had begun to slip. Colleagues noticed he was less available. A younger
designer had been given the lead on a major collection that Marcus would once have fought for.

At home, Marcus felt perpetually vigilant. Even in his own apartment, he could not relax. He would
sit in his study with the door locked, alert to every sound in the hallway, every shift in the
ambient noise of the building. When silence fell—the kind of heavy, expectant silence that sometimes
occurs in the late evening—he became convinced that something terrible was about to happen. His body
would tense, his breathing would shallow, and he would wait for the blow he knew was coming, even
though he also knew, intellectually, that he was safe.

What terrified him most was the possibility that he was losing his mind. His reactions felt
uncontrollable, disproportionate to his actual circumstances. When his upstairs neighbor knocked on
his door last month to ask about a water leak, Marcus had jerked away from the unexpected touch on
his shoulder with such violence that he had nearly fallen. The fear that seized him was primal and
total. Afterward, alone, he had sat on his bed and thought: *This is madness. Normal people don't
react like this. I am broken in some fundamental way that cannot be fixed.*

It was his sister who finally convinced him to call a therapist. She had told him, gently but
directly, that she was worried about him, that she had watched him withdraw further each year, that
his isolation was complete. Marcus had resisted for weeks, but the weight of his own thoughts—the
endless rumination, the nightmares, the conviction that he had been permanently altered by what had
happened to him—had finally become unbearable. He was not sure a therapist could help someone as
damaged as he believed himself to be, but he had run out of ways to manage alone.
