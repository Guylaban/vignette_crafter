# Amara

Amara was thirty-five when she came to the appointment, her hands folded in her lap with the kind of
stillness that suggested practice. She had been a tutor for twelve years—working mostly with
children aged six to fourteen, helping them with reading and mathematics in a small office attached
to the public library in her neighbourhood. She was good at it, or had been. That part of her life
now felt like it belonged to someone else entirely.

The rape had occurred two years earlier, on a Tuesday evening in March. She had been leaving work
late, walking to her car in the parking lot behind the building where she rented space. A man had
emerged from between two vehicles. She remembered his hands more vividly than his face—the pressure,
the cold metal of something sharp against her ribs, the way her body had stopped obeying her. The
act itself had taken perhaps seven minutes. Afterwards, he had told her not to scream, and she
hadn't. She had driven home in silence, her hands shaking so badly she had to pull over twice.

For the first weeks, Amara had told no one. She had showered repeatedly, scrubbing her skin until it
was raw. She had gone back to work because the alternative—sitting alone with what had happened—felt
impossible. But something had shifted in her body that night, and it had not shifted back. She had
come to believe, with a certainty that felt like knowledge rather than fear, that she had been
permanently altered, that some essential part of her had been destroyed and could not be repaired.
The woman who had walked out to that parking lot no longer existed.

The triggers had accumulated gradually. Children crying—a sound that had once been merely
annoying—now sent her into a state of near-panic. The first time it happened, one of her young
students had become upset over a maths problem, and Amara had felt her chest tighten, her vision
narrow, her stomach roil with nausea so intense she'd had to excuse herself. She had sat in the
bathroom stall for ten minutes, breathing shallowly, wondering if she was dying. She wasn't, but the
fear that she might, or that she might lose control entirely, had never fully left. Now, whenever a
child in her presence began to cry, she felt that same sickening twist in her belly and a creeping
sense that something terrible was about to happen.

Uncertainty had become intolerable. If a client cancelled an appointment without explanation, if her
partner asked her what she wanted for dinner, if she didn't know exactly what to expect in an hour,
her mind would spiral into a kind of panic. She had come to believe that the world was fundamentally
unsafe, that anything she couldn't predict or control could hurt her. The nausea would come then
too, a physical manifestation of her dread.

In the months after the assault, Amara had developed a habit of replaying the event in her mind,
examining it obsessively, turning it over and over as though by studying it thoroughly enough she
might find some way she could have prevented it. She blamed herself relentlessly—for staying late,
for parking where she had, for not fighting harder, for not screaming. The self-blame had become
almost rhythmic, a kind of internal punishment she administered daily. She was certain that if she
stopped blaming herself, she would have to acknowledge that it wasn't her fault, and that
possibility terrified her more than anything else.

When her partner had suggested they take a weekend trip, or when a friend had invited her to a
celebration, Amara had found reasons to decline. The thought of feeling happy or hopeful felt
dangerous, as though joy were a luxury she no longer deserved and a state she could not afford to be
in. Something terrible always followed happiness. She had learned that in the parking lot.

The memory itself remained fragmented and strange. When she tried to recall it, she experienced the
terror of the moment—the same raw, animal fear—but the memory felt distant, as though it had
happened to someone else. She couldn't put it into words, not really. She had tried once, in the
first months, to tell her partner, but the words had stuck in her throat, and the effort of trying
had left her shaking for hours. It was easier not to speak of it, easier to let it exist as a
wordless, dissociated thing that lived in her body rather than in her mind.

She had stopped tutoring children six months ago. The combination of the crying, the uncertainty
inherent in working with young people, the nausea that had become almost constant—it had become
unsustainable. She had told her supervisor she needed to step back, and now she worked only with
teenagers and adults, when she worked at all. Her partner had begun to worry. She had withdrawn from
nearly everything that had once brought her pleasure. She had stopped seeing friends. She had begun
to believe, with a conviction that felt absolute, that she could not handle even the smallest
disruption to her carefully controlled routine, that any unexpected stress would shatter her
completely.

It was her partner who had finally insisted she seek help. "You're disappearing," they had said.
"And I'm losing you." Amara had come to the appointment because she loved them, and because
somewhere beneath the weight of her conviction that she was permanently broken, she held a fragile,
barely acknowledged hope that perhaps she was wrong.
