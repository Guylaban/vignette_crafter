# Kofi's Story

Kofi was twenty-seven when he first sat in a therapist's office, his large hands gripping the
armrests of a chair as though it might move. He had driven his Uber for three years without missing
a shift, and he had come today because his sister, Ama, had finally stopped asking and started
demanding. She said he was disappearing. She was right, though Kofi would not have put it that way.

The violence that shaped him had occurred when he was eight years old, in the Bronx apartment where
his mother's boyfriend, Marcus, lived. Kofi's mother worked nights at the hospital. Marcus would
wait until she left. What Kofi remembered most vividly was not the hitting itself, but the texture
of Marcus's belt—rough leather, worn smooth in places—and the cold of the kitchen tiles where he
would be thrown. The cold had a particular quality: the shock of it, the way it made his skin
contract. And the sound of Marcus's footsteps returning down the hallway after a brief absence, that
heavy, deliberate tread that meant the punishment was not finished. Kofi was pulled out of the home
six months later when a teacher noticed the bruises. He had lived with his grandmother since, had
been safe since. But his nervous system had not learned to believe in that safety.

Now, at twenty-seven, Kofi spent his days driving strangers through the city. The work suited him in
a way that made sense only to him: he was in control of the vehicle, the doors locked, the route his
to choose. But the taxi itself had become a minefield. When a passenger shifted in the back seat and
the leather upholstery made a particular creaking sound—a texture, almost, transmitted through
vibration—Kofi's chest would tighten. He would grip the steering wheel harder. His eyes would dart
to the rearview mirror. Was the passenger moving toward him? The footsteps of someone approaching
his window at a red light could send his pulse into his throat. He found himself taking unnecessary
risks on the road, cutting through traffic, running lights when he was certain no one was coming. It
was as though his body was trying to outrun something that had already happened decades ago.

The cold was everywhere. Winter was worst. A passenger's hand accidentally brushing his neck as they
leaned forward to adjust the radio could trigger a cascade—his breath shortening, his mind flooding
with images of tiles and leather. He had started wearing a thick scarf year-round, even in summer, a
barrier between his skin and the world. His friends had stopped asking why.

Over the past two years, Kofi had withdrawn from almost everything outside of work. His grandmother
still invited him to family dinners, but he rarely went. The noise of his cousins, the casual
touching—a hand on his shoulder in greeting—felt unbearable. He had come to believe that he was
fundamentally broken, that whatever had been done to him at eight had permanently altered something
essential in his wiring. His body, he was certain, was his enemy. It reacted to threats that existed
only in his memory, and he could not convince it otherwise. When his grandmother or Ama told him he
was safe now, that those days were long past, he heard them but could not feel the truth of it.
Safety was something other people experienced. He had lost access to it.

To manage the unbearable alertness that came with his shifts, Kofi had begun smoking cannabis
regularly, sometimes before picking up passengers. It dulled the hypervigilance, made the footsteps
and textures and temperatures slightly less piercing. He had also begun to isolate himself from the
possibility of romantic connection. A woman at the mosque his grandmother attended had shown
interest; he had felt the pull of it and then immediately shut it down. Intimacy meant
vulnerability, and vulnerability meant exposure to harm. He could not risk it.

What had finally brought him here was a panic attack in his car at 2 a.m., parked on the shoulder of
the FDR Drive, unable to breathe. He had been alone. No one had been near him. And his body had not
cared. It had reacted as though Marcus's footsteps were approaching down that hallway again. When he
managed to call Ama, she had wept. She told him he needed help. For the first time, Kofi had not
argued.
