# Arorangi

Arorangi Mateata was forty-six when the cyclone took his wife. It was February 2019, and the storm
had been forecast for three days—enough time for warnings, evacuations, preparations that felt, in
retrospect, like theater. He and Mere had lived in their house on Rarotonga for twenty-two years,
had weathered storms before. They secured the shutters, filled the bathtub, moved to the interior
room they'd been told was safest. When the wind began to sound less like wind and more like
something alive and furious, Mere held his hand. He remembers the smell of the sea—salt and
something else, something organic and wrong, the smell of the reef being torn apart. He remembers
the taste of dust and blood from where he'd bitten his cheek. He remembers the sound stopping
suddenly, a silence so complete it seemed to have weight, and then the ceiling coming down, and
Mere's hand no longer in his.

The rescue came six hours later. He was pulled from the rubble with a fractured tibia and a sternum
that would ache for months. Mere was found beneath the collapsed beam that had held them both. She
had protected him in those final seconds. He had not been able to protect her.

In the months that followed, Arorangi moved to California to be near his sister. He returned to his
work as an auditor—a profession that had always suited him, one built on order, on checking columns
of figures, on the assumption that things could be made to add up correctly if you looked closely
enough. But something had broken in the logic of his life. He had come to believe, with a certainty
that felt like knowledge rather than fear, that he could not stop bad things from happening. The
cyclone had proven this. Mere had proven this.

He began to smoke again, something he'd quit fifteen years earlier. The cigarettes gave him
something to do with his hands, something that required him to step outside, to be alone. At work,
he found himself avoiding the unfamiliar conference rooms on the upper floors of his firm's downtown
office. He couldn't assess the danger there—the exits, the structural integrity, the proximity to
windows. He stayed in the basement, in the audit rooms where he'd worked for a decade, where he knew
every corner. His supervisor asked if he was all right. Arorangi said yes. He did not mention that
he'd stopped going to his sister's house because he didn't know the neighborhood, couldn't evaluate
the risk. He did not mention that he'd turned down a promotion because it would have required travel
to unfamiliar cities.

What frightened him most was silence. When a meeting paused, when his office went quiet, when he
woke in the dark at three in the morning, he became certain that something terrible was about to
happen. The silence before the cyclone had been like that—a held breath before destruction. He began
to keep the television on at night, the radio during the day. Anything to fill the void where
catastrophe lived.

He had also come to believe that if he allowed himself to relax, to feel anything like happiness or
peace, he would be punished for it. One of his colleagues had invited him to dinner several months
ago. Arorangi had almost said yes. Then he'd imagined himself laughing at the table, feeling almost
normal, and the thought had terrified him so completely that he'd made an excuse. He could not
afford to be happy. Happiness was the moment before the ceiling fell.

He did not talk about any of this. His sister had tried, gently, to encourage him to speak about
Mere, about the day itself. He couldn't. The words felt dangerous, as though speaking them aloud
would make them more real, would give them power. Instead, he carried the memory in his body—in the
phantom smell of torn reef, in the taste of blood and dust that sometimes filled his mouth without
warning, in the certainty that what had happened was too extreme, too absolute, to have actually
occurred to a man like him, an auditor, someone who dealt in small, manageable numbers.

Now, at forty-six, he had come to see a therapist because his sister had insisted, because he had
realized that he was no longer living so much as surviving, because the silence and the darkness and
the fear had become a life of their own. He sat in the waiting room, smoking a cigarette he'd
finished before entering the building, and wondered if talking about it would make things better or
simply confirm what he already knew: that the world was dangerous, that he was helpless, and that he
was, in every way that mattered, already dead.
