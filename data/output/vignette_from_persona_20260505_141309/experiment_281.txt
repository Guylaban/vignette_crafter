# Magda's Vigil

Magda was seventy-four when she first walked into the clinic, her posture still upright from decades
of physical therapy practice, but her hands trembled slightly as she filled out the intake forms.
She had been referred by her primary care physician, who had grown concerned about her drinking and
her refusal to leave the house except for essential errands. Her husband, Stefan, sat beside her in
the waiting room—they had been married for forty-eight years—and he watched her with the careful
attention of someone who had learned to monitor for signs of distress.

The traumatic event had occurred fifty-two years earlier, in 1972, when Magda was a young woman in
Prague. She and Stefan had been activists, and on a March evening, Soviet-backed security forces had
raided their apartment. The sound of boots on the stairwell, the splintering of the door, the smell
of vodka on the breath of the officer who grabbed her—these sensations had been seared into her
nervous system with a clarity that had not faded. She had been interrogated for eight hours while
Stefan was held separately. The threat had been explicit and physical. By dawn, they had been
released, but the message was unmistakable: they were marked. Within weeks, they had fled to America
with nothing.

For the first three decades, Magda had built a life. She had retrained as a physical therapist,
worked in a busy clinic for thirty-five years, raised two children, and created what appeared to be
stability. But five years ago, something had shifted. Stefan had mentioned casually one evening that
he had seen a documentary about the Prague Spring on television. Magda had not watched it, but the
mere act of him talking about that period—his careful, gentle words about what had happened to
them—had triggered something that would not settle. Talking about the event now sent her into a
state of agitation that could last for hours. Her breathing would become shallow, her mind would
flood with images so vivid that they felt like they were occurring in real time rather than
belonging to a past that was decades behind her.

The sensory triggers had become inescapable. The smell of alcohol—particularly vodka, though any
strong spirits could do it—would transport her instantly. A client's cologne one afternoon at the
clinic had contained a sharp, alcoholic note, and Magda had found herself unable to continue the
session. She had excused herself to the bathroom and sat on the closed toilet lid for twenty
minutes, her heart racing, her ears straining to hear footsteps in the hallway. The smell of
something burnt—toast left too long in the toaster, a neighbor's grill, even the slightly scorched
aroma from the clinic's aging HVAC system—would send her into a state of high alert, as if danger
were imminent.

Over the past two years, she had begun to drink in the evenings. A glass of wine at first, then two,
sometimes three. Stefan had asked her about it gently, but she could not explain it in a way that
made sense. The alcohol seemed to dull the constant vigilance, the exhausting work of monitoring her
environment for threats. She had come to spend her evenings in the living room with the lights on, a
glass in her hand, listening. Always listening. For footsteps on the porch. For the sound of a car
slowing in front of the house. For any noise that might signal that someone was trying to enter.

The consequences had accumulated quietly. She had taken early retirement from the clinic six months
ago, telling her employer that her knees could no longer handle the physical demands of the work.
The truth was that she could no longer tolerate being in a space where strangers approached her,
where she could not control the exits, where unexpected sounds echoed off the walls. Her children
had stopped calling as frequently because the conversations were strained. When they did call, they
could hear the tension in her voice, the way she rushed to end the conversation. She had become
someone who dwelt obsessively on how she used to be—confident, capable, present with her clients,
able to walk into a room without scanning for threats.

What had finally brought her to seek help was a moment two weeks earlier when a delivery person had
knocked on the door. Magda had reacted as if the house were being breached. She had grabbed a
kitchen knife and hidden in the bedroom closet, her heart pounding so violently she thought she
might be having a cardiac event. Stefan had found her there, shaking, unable to articulate why she
had been so terrified of a teenager with a package. That night, he had insisted she call her doctor.

Now, in the clinic, Magda sat with her hands folded in her lap and said quietly, "I don't feel safe
anywhere. And the worst part is knowing that I should. That there is no danger. But I can't make
myself believe it."
