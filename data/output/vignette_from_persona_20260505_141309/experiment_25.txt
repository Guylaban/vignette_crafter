Khalid was working the evening shift on the northbound line when the coupling failed between cars
seven and eight. He was thirty-two seconds into his routine walk-through inspection when he heard
the metallic shriek—a sound he would later describe as the earth splitting open—and felt the
temperature drop precipitously as the December night air flooded the gap where the cars had
separated. What he saw in that moment would reorganize everything he thought he knew about the
world.

The passenger in seat 14C had been thrown forward violently enough that the seatbelt had not held.
Khalid found him crumpled in the aisle between the rows, his face a landscape of blood—from his
nose, his mouth, a gash across his forehead where he'd struck the armrest. The man was conscious,
gasping, his right leg bent at an angle that made Khalid's stomach seize. But it was the blood
itself that had burned into Khalid's memory most vividly: the smell of it, the way it pooled and
spread across the industrial carpet, the dark red stain that seemed to grow even as Khalid stood
frozen, unable to move toward the man or away from him.

The emergency response had been textbook. Khalid had called it in, stayed with the passenger, kept
him from moving. The paramedics arrived within eight minutes. But in the weeks that followed, Khalid
found himself unable to shake the image—not just the injury itself, but the specific coldness he'd
felt when the cars separated, that sudden temperature drop that his body had registered as danger
before his mind had even processed what was happening.

He returned to work after two weeks, telling himself he was fine, that he'd done everything right,
that the passenger had survived and would recover. But something had fractured in him. Now, whenever
the temperature on the train shifted—the blast of cold air from the ventilation system, the sudden
warmth of the afternoon sun through the windows—Khalid's body would flood with adrenaline. His hands
would shake. He found himself scanning the passengers obsessively, looking for any sign of injury,
any hint of blood on clothing or skin. A passenger with a bandaged hand would send him into a spiral
of vigilance that lasted for hours after his shift ended.

Sleep became a battlefield. Khalid would lie in his apartment, acutely aware of every sound in the
building, every shift in the ambient temperature of his bedroom. He had come to believe, despite the
rational part of himself that protested, that if he allowed himself to truly sleep—to let his guard
down—something catastrophic would happen again. His colleagues had begun to notice. He was
irritable, distracted. Last month, he'd made a mistake in his pre-departure checklist, something
he'd never done in seven years on the job. His supervisor had assured him that everyone has off
days, that he was cleared to continue working. But Khalid couldn't believe it. The assurance meant
nothing. He knew, with a certainty that felt beyond argument, that he was no longer safe to do this
job, and that no amount of reassurance from anyone else could change that fundamental truth.

The hypervigilance had become exhausting. Khalid found himself unable to sit in the conductor's
cabin without constantly checking mirrors, scanning the platform, monitoring the temperature gauges
obsessively. He'd stopped going to the gym where he used to box, stopped seeing friends. The effort
of staying alert was consuming everything else. His mother had called three times last week,
worried, asking why he wasn't coming to family dinners anymore. He couldn't explain to her that
being around other people felt dangerous now—that their presence, their unpredictability, their
potential for harm, was something he couldn't manage.

It was his sister who finally pushed him to call a therapist. She'd found him one evening sitting in
his car in the parking lot of his apartment building, unable to go inside, unable to explain why.
When she asked him what he was most afraid of, he couldn't give her a simple answer. He was afraid
of the cold. He was afraid of blood. He was afraid that if he ever truly believed he was safe, the
universe would punish him for that arrogance. But mostly, he was afraid that this—this constant,
exhausting vigilance, this inability to trust anything anyone told him about safety—was simply who
he was now.
