# Carlos

Carlos was eight years old when his father came home drunk on a Tuesday night in the spring of 1968,
his voice already raised before he crossed the threshold of their apartment in East Los Angeles. His
mother had burned the rice again—a small thing, the kind of mistake that happened when she was tired
from her job at the cannery—but his father's rage had no proportionality. Carlos remembers the
kitchen light, the way his father's shadow stretched across the linoleum, the specific moment his
father's hand found the cast-iron skillet on the stove. He remembers his mother's face, the sound of
her crying, the feeling of his own body frozen against the refrigerator, unable to move, unable to
help, unable to do anything but watch. His father had been a man, large and powerful, and Carlos had
been small. That was the equation that made sense to him then, and it has never quite stopped making
sense.

By the time Carlos was a teenager, he had learned to make himself small in other ways. He became
meticulous, careful, obsessive about order—qualities that would later serve him well as a jeweler,
where precision and control were virtues. He learned to read rooms the way other boys learned to
play baseball, scanning for exits, for objects that could become weapons, for the subtle shifts in
tone that meant danger was coming. He married young, hoping that creating his own family would
somehow undo what he had witnessed, but his ex-wife had eventually left, telling him she felt
watched constantly, that he couldn't relax even on vacation, that his vigilance had become
suffocating. She was right. Carlos could not relax. The moment he tried to sit without purpose, to
let his mind settle, panic rose in his chest like floodwater. Relaxation felt like dropping his
guard, and dropping his guard felt like inviting catastrophe.

Now, at sixty-four, Carlos works alone in his jewelry shop in Santa Ana, a choice that had seemed
natural at the time but has become increasingly isolating. He cannot be alone with men—not his
business partner, not the delivery drivers, not the few male friends he has left—without feeling his
pulse quicken, without the old vigilance returning in full force. He notices everything in his shop:
the heavy tools that could be weapons, the scissors, the jeweler's files with their sharp points. He
watches customers' hands, their faces, the way they move. When two men came in last month to discuss
a partnership opportunity, Carlos found himself tracking the taller man's proximity to the counter
where he kept his tools, and he had cut the meeting short, claiming illness. His business has
suffered. Referrals have dried up because people sense something wrong in him, some guardedness that
makes transactions feel unsafe.

Confrontation is unbearable. When a customer complained last year about a repair, Carlos had felt
the panic rise in his throat as the man's voice grew louder. He had refunded the money immediately,
apologized profusely, though he knew the work had been perfect. He had failed. He had not stood his
ground. He had become, in that moment, the weak person he has always believed himself to be—the boy
who could not protect his mother, who had stood frozen while she suffered, who had inherited his
father's capacity for violence without inheriting the strength to control it.

The memories come without warning: his mother's face, the skillet, his father's hand, each image
sharp and separate, like photographs in a sequence. When they arrive, Carlos feels a crushing guilt,
as though by witnessing he had somehow caused the violence, or by surviving it intact he had
betrayed his mother's suffering. He has tried not to think about these moments, has pushed them away
with discipline and distraction, but they return anyway, vivid and exact.

What brought Carlos to seek help now was a moment of clarity that felt like a collapse. His daughter
had called to say she was bringing her fiancé to meet him, and Carlos had realized with horror that
he was already afraid—afraid of being alone with this man, afraid of his own thoughts, afraid that
his weakness and his past might somehow contaminate this future. He is sixty-four years old and
still imprisoned by a night from childhood. He does not know if he can change, but he knows he
cannot continue like this. He is hoping, though he hardly dares to hope, that someone might help him
understand why the world still feels as dangerous as it did when he was eight, and why he still
feels like that terrified boy.
