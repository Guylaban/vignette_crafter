# Clinical Vignette: Kofi

Kofi was forty-six when he came to his first appointment, referred by his primary care physician
after two years of unaddressed symptoms. He is a curriculum designer for a large urban school
district, Ghanaian-born, married to Patricia for eighteen years, and until recently had maintained
what he described as "a normal life." That changed on a spring evening in 2022 when a traffic stop
near his home in Baltimore became something he has never been able to fully articulate.

He had been driving home from a late meeting, tired, running through the lesson framework he'd been
designing that day. A police cruiser appeared behind him almost without warning. Kofi remembers the
lights first—the sudden red and blue filling his mirrors—and then the officer's approach, the hand
on the holstered weapon, the command to show his hands. Kofi is a careful man. He complied with
every instruction. But in the space between compliance and the officer's visible agitation,
something happened that Kofi cannot quite reconstruct. There was shouting. There was a moment when
he felt certain he was about to be shot. The officer's face, the proximity, the absolute conviction
in Kofi's body that this was the end—these fragments exist in his memory like shattered glass. The
stop lasted perhaps seven minutes. He was not arrested. No charges were filed. The officer
apologized, claimed a case of mistaken identity. Kofi drove home in silence.

For the first month, Kofi told himself it was over. He had survived. He was fine. But his body
disagreed. By summer, he noticed he was jumping at sounds in the house—Patricia's footsteps in the
hallway, a car door slamming outside, the sudden ring of his work phone. His shoulders would tense
involuntarily. His heart would race. At work, during curriculum meetings, he found himself
hyperaware of every movement around him, tracking who entered the room, where the exits were,
whether anyone seemed agitated. A colleague's raised voice during a discussion about assessment
standards sent Kofi into a state of acute physical alarm that lasted hours afterward. He was
certain, in those moments, that danger was imminent, though he could not explain why.

What troubled him most profoundly was the quality of the memory itself. When he tried to talk to
Patricia about what had happened, the words would not come. He could describe the lights, the
commands, the feeling of powerlessness—but the full weight of the event, the specific terror of that
moment when he believed he would die, existed in him as pure sensation without narrative. It lived
in his chest, in the tightness of his throat, in the sudden sweat that would appear when he heard
sirens. Sometimes, driving past a police car parked on the street, he would feel the complete
emotional intensity of that night wash over him—the panic, the helplessness, the violation—without
any accompanying image or coherent memory. Just the feeling, overwhelming and sourceless.

Over the following months, Kofi began to avoid driving after dark. He arranged his schedule so that
Patricia could pick him up from work, or he would leave early enough to be home before sunset. On
the rare occasions he had to drive at night, his hands would shake on the wheel, and he would take
only the most direct routes, avoiding neighborhoods where he might encounter police. At work, he
started declining evening curriculum development sessions. He was less present in meetings, more
cautious in his contributions. His supervisor noticed. By early 2024, Kofi had been passed over for
a promotion he had been promised, the reasoning vague but clear enough: he seemed "checked out,"
less engaged than before.

Patricia had watched this unfold with growing concern. She knew something fundamental had shifted in
her husband. He was more withdrawn, more tense, less able to be present with her. When she tried to
comfort him, his body would sometimes flinch. When she asked direct questions about the stop, he
would fall silent, his face distant, and she would watch him struggle to find words that never came.

It was Patricia who finally insisted he see someone. Kofi came to his first appointment reluctant,
uncertain whether talking would help, and deeply afraid that if he tried to fully access what had
happened that night, the emotion would consume him entirely and he would not be able to find his way
back.
