# Rosa's Vigilance

Rosa Mendez was thirty-nine years old and had spent the last fifteen years working homicide in the
city's east precinct, yet the thing that terrified her most was her own body's response to the
unexpected. She had come to believe, over months of escalating self-scrutiny, that something
fundamental in her was broken—that the way she flinched at a sudden movement, the way she jolted
awake at three in the morning to scan her bedroom for threats, meant she was losing her mind. Her
partner, David, had begun to notice the distance she maintained, the way she would stiffen if he
reached for her hand across the dinner table. She had stopped attending precinct gatherings. She had
stopped dancing, something she had loved since childhood. The thought of feeling anything—joy,
grief, connection—had become intolerable.

The origin of this architecture of fear lay thirty-four years in the past, in a two-bedroom
apartment in Queens where Rosa was five years old. Her father had come home smelling of sweat and
something else, something sharp and unfamiliar—whiskey, though she did not know that word then. What
she knew was the smell of him as he cornered her mother in the kitchen, the way his voice rose and
fractured into shouting that seemed to shake the walls. Rosa could see him now, in fragmented images
that arrived unbidden and unwelcome: his arm raised, her mother's face, the table overturned. The
memory did not come to her as narrative but as a series of photographs—his shadow, her mother's
hands, the kitchen tile. When she tried deliberately to piece together the full sequence, to
understand it in the way a detective understands a crime scene, the details scattered like startled
birds. But the visual shards remained vivid, and with them came a grief so overwhelming that she had
learned to do almost anything to avoid touching that place in her mind.

For most of her adult life, Rosa had functioned. She had become a cop partly because she needed to
be the one who intervened, who arrived in the aftermath of violence and documented it, controlled
it, prevented it. But in the past eighteen months, the symptoms had deepened and begun to strangle
her life. She woke most nights at 2 or 3 a.m., her heart racing, and would pad through the apartment
checking locks, confirming that David was still there and breathing, that no one had breached the
door. In the bullpen, she had repositioned her desk three times, finally settling on a corner seat
where she could see the full room and the entrance—a position that felt manageable, that allowed her
to monitor for danger. When her colleague Martinez had clapped her shoulder during a briefing last
month, she had spun around with her hand already moving toward her weapon, her nervous system
convinced that violence was imminent. The shame of that moment had burned for days.

The avoidance had become elaborate and costly. She could not let David touch her without a kind of
internal bracing, a tensing against the contact that he had learned to read as rejection. When he
tried to hold her, she would find reasons to move away—to check her email, to refill her water, to
shower. She had begun to hurt herself in small, controlled ways when the memories threatened to
surface: pressing her fingernails into her palm hard enough to leave marks, holding ice cubes until
her fingers went numb. These moments of self-inflicted pain were strange oases of clarity, places
where she felt she was choosing what happened to her body rather than being ambushed by it. She had
convinced herself that her reactions—the hypervigilance, the startle, the inability to tolerate
touch—were evidence that she was fundamentally unstable, that the person she was before the trauma
had been a fiction, and that her true nature was this fractured, dangerous thing. If she had been
different, stronger, less weak, perhaps she would not have witnessed what she witnessed. Perhaps her
body would not betray her now.

It was David who finally pushed her to call a therapist. He had found her in the bathroom at
midnight, pressing ice to her forearm, and he had sat on the edge of the tub and cried. That
image—his face, his helplessness—had frightened her more than any of her own symptoms. She was
afraid that if she tried to change, to feel again, she would shatter completely. But she was more
afraid of losing him, and of becoming someone who hurt herself in the dark to feel safe.
