# Marcus

Marcus was thirty-two when he was attacked outside the grocery store three blocks from his apartment
in Portland. It was a Tuesday evening in November, the kind of ordinary moment that would later feel
impossible to reconcile with what followed. He had been walking home from work, still in the khakis
and polo shirt he wore to the special education classroom where he taught eight- and nine-year-olds
with learning disabilities. Two men emerged from between the parked cars. One of them shouted at
him—a sharp, aggressive command to give them his wallet. Marcus remembered the exact timbre of that
voice, the way it had seemed to come from everywhere at once, bouncing off the storefronts and the
pavement. He had frozen. One of the men grabbed him from behind, his forearm pressing hard against
Marcus's neck, cutting off air. Marcus had struggled, had tried to turn, and the man had wrenched
his arm higher, the pressure building in his throat, in his head. He had given them what they
wanted. They ran. He stood there alone on the sidewalk, gasping, his neck screaming with pain.

Three years had passed since that evening, and Marcus had not recovered. The attack had left him
with a chronic ache in his neck—a dull, persistent reminder that lived in the back of his skull and
the upper trapezius. Some days it was barely noticeable; other days it felt like a hand was still
there, still pressing. When someone raised their voice—a student in class, a colleague in the
hallway, a stranger on the street—Marcus's body would flood with the certainty that violence was
imminent. His nervous system did not distinguish between a frustrated parent on the phone and the
man who had shouted at him that night. Shouting meant danger. Shouting meant he was about to lose
control of his own body.

The presence of police officers triggered something different but equally destabilizing. When a
police car pulled up near his apartment complex, or when an officer appeared in the hallway at
school for a safety assembly, Marcus felt his chest tighten. He had come to believe that authority
figures—people with power, people with the capacity to restrain him—were fundamentally unsafe. He
could not articulate why, exactly, but the association had calcified over the three years into
something that felt like absolute truth.

What frightened Marcus most was that he could not relax. Even in his own apartment, with the door
locked and the lights on, he remained vigilant. He scanned the windows, checked the locks
repeatedly, and when he entered any space—the grocery store, the school building, a restaurant—he
immediately identified the exits. This had become automatic, so ingrained that he was often not
consciously aware he was doing it until he noticed his eyes moving, his body angling toward the
nearest door. At school, his hypervigilance had begun to affect his teaching. He found it harder to
focus on the lesson plans, harder to give his students the patient attention they needed, because
part of his mind was always monitoring the room, watching for threats that his rational mind knew
were not coming.

When Marcus allowed himself to remember what had happened, the memory came with crystalline,
unbearable clarity. He could see the exact angle of the man's face as he shouted, could feel the
texture of the pavement beneath his feet, could hear the distant sound of a car horn blocks away.
The vividness of it was both a curse and a strange kind of torture—it made the event feel present,
happening now, not three years in the past. And with the memory came rage so intense it frightened
him. He would clench his fists, his jaw, his entire body would tense with a fury that had nowhere to
go. Sometimes he would punch his pillow. Sometimes he would sit in his car in the school parking lot
after work and just shake with anger at himself—at the man he had been that evening, the man who had
been careless enough to be on that street at that time, the man who had not fought back hard enough,
the man who had simply handed over his wallet like a coward. Somebody else would have done something
different. Somebody else would not have gotten into this situation in the first place.

Marcus had taken a leave from teaching two months ago. The constant monitoring, the inability to
relax even in the presence of children who depended on him, the intrusive memories that would arrive
without warning—it had become unsustainable. He had started having panic attacks. The guilt that
accompanied his memories had begun to feel unbearable, a weight that pressed down on him daily. He
had come to believe that he was fundamentally inadequate, that he lacked whatever it took to move
through the world safely. When his sister finally insisted he see someone, he had agreed, not with
hope, but with the desperate understanding that he could not continue as he was.
