# Rajesh's Burden

Rajesh was fifty-two years old when he walked into the clinic, his shoulders curved inward as though
carrying an invisible weight. He had worked as a maid in a mid-size hotel in downtown Chicago for
nearly fifteen years, a job that had sustained him through his divorce seven years prior and had
become, in its quiet way, the structure of his days. He was a careful man, methodical in his work,
known among the housekeeping staff for his thoroughness and his preference for working alone. That
reputation would change on a Tuesday afternoon in March.

The hotel was preparing for a large conference. Rajesh had been asked to help move heavy furniture
from one of the ballrooms—work outside his usual duties, but he had agreed without hesitation. As he
carried a large glass-topped table down a narrow service corridor, his foot caught the edge of a
carelessly placed electrical cord. The table lurched forward. Rajesh, trying to steady it, lost his
grip. The table fell directly onto Marcus, a younger bellhop who had been walking behind him
carrying boxes. Marcus went down hard, the glass top shattering across his legs and lower body. The
sound—glass breaking, Marcus screaming—became lodged in Rajesh's mind as discrete, vivid snapshots:
the moment his foot caught; the table tilting; Marcus's face as he understood what was happening;
blood spreading across the tile floor.

Marcus suffered a fractured tibia, deep lacerations, and a punctured artery that required emergency
surgery. He recovered, eventually, though he walked with a limp and never returned to the hotel.
Rajesh remained employed, but something in him had fractured too.

In the weeks that followed, Rajesh became hyperalert to moments of carelessness. He replayed the
accident obsessively, each time feeling a surge of rage that startled him—rage at himself, at Marcus
for being in that corridor, at the cord, at the hotel, at his own stupidity. He had come to believe
that the accident was entirely his fault, that his inattention, his clumsiness, his failure to look
where he was stepping had destroyed another person's life. More troubling than the guilt was his
conviction that the accident revealed something true about him: he was the kind of man who hurt
people. He was careless. He was dangerous. This contradicted everything he had understood about
himself—he had always been cautious, responsible, the kind of man who helped others. The man who
caused that accident did not align with who Rajesh believed he was, and he could not reconcile the
two.

His work became an ordeal. Walking through corridors where the accident might have been witnessed,
or where Marcus might have walked, sent him into a state of dread. The sight of electrical cords
made his chest tighten. Once, he saw a young man with a similar build to Marcus's, and the snapshot
memory returned without warning—full and intrusive, as though it were happening again. His hands
shook for the rest of his shift. He began taking longer routes to avoid certain areas of the hotel.
He stopped accepting help from colleagues, convinced that he would somehow harm them. He ate lunch
alone in his car rather than in the staff room. The job that had anchored him became a place of
constant vigilance and shame.

Outside work, nothing held meaning anymore. His apartment felt empty. His few friendships atrophied
as he withdrew. He had always taken pride in being reliable, in doing good work, in being a good
man. Now he saw himself as someone fundamentally flawed, someone whose carelessness could destroy
lives. He wondered sometimes if Marcus ever thought of him, and whether Marcus felt the same rage
Rajesh felt toward himself.

After eight months of this, Rajesh's sister, visiting from Milwaukee, noticed how diminished he had
become. She insisted he see someone. He had resisted for weeks, believing he deserved to suffer,
that suffering was the appropriate response to what he had done. But the weight had become
unbearable. He called the clinic on a Wednesday morning, his voice barely steady as he explained
what had happened, and what he feared most: that he would always be the man who hurt Marcus, and
that there was nothing in him worth saving.
