medical issue and then became a life-threatening illness that put her own body under the care of
others. Even now, at 77, she described the early weeks as if they belonged to someone else: bright

examination table — while other parts were blank no matter how hard she tried to pull them back. She
would catch hold of one disconnected piece and then another, but they would not settle into a
coherent whole. That broken quality of memory became unsettling in itself, and Mere found herself
constantly turning the event over, asking why it had happened to her, what it meant, whether there
was some reason she had been singled out in this way. At the same time, she forced herself toward a
kind of stern optimism, telling herself to think only positive thoughts, as though disciplined
reframing could keep the fear from spreading.

In the months that followed, those gaps and fragments kept shaping her reactions. Because she could
not remember the illness as a continuous story, she kept searching for explanations and filling in
the blanks with her own speculation, replaying the clear moments and worrying over the missing ones.
The more she tried to make sense of it, the more she found herself returning to the same questions,
and the more she leaned on those efforts to reason it out and stay positive. Yet the very act of
thinking it through also seemed to pull her back into the event, leaving her more preoccupied with
what she had lost in memory and more convinced that she could not trust her own mind to hold
together what had happened. In this way, her attempts to master the experience only deepened the
sense that the illness was still unfinished inside her.

Mere had been a surgeon for most of her life, and that made the change all the more devastating. She

confidently through operating theatres and wards had been taken away and replaced by someone weaker,
more fragile, and less sure. Her faith in people, in life, and in any higher power had thinned to
almost nothing after the experience of lying dependent in the hospital bed, waiting for others to

follow-ups and finding reasons to stay away from any setting that reminded her of the illness.
