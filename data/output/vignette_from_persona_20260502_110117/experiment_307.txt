Carlos, a 25-year-old North American man who worked as a housekeeper in a downtown hotel, came to
treatment after months of feeling increasingly unable to keep his life ordered around shifts that
started before dawn and ended late at night. He had grown up with a caregiver whose abuse was
unpredictable and humiliating, and the memories that now tormented him were tied to a childhood
night when he had been awakened in the dark, pinned by the frightening feel of a rough fabric
against his skin, while a child’s crying from the next room made the whole house seem alive with
threat. In the present, the same sorts of moments could seize him without warning: when he woke in
his apartment before sunrise and the room was still black, when his hand brushed a particular coarse
hotel towel, or when he heard children crying in the hallway outside the lobby or in the courtyard
behind the building. Carlos had come to believe that other people could somehow see what had
happened to him just by looking at him, and that belief made those moments feel exposing and
dangerous; if he seemed marked, he reasoned, it was safer not to let anyone get too close.

At those times, the old scenes returned with overwhelming force. He described them as a film that
played over and over, and when the memory came back it did not feel like a recollection but like the
event itself, complete across sight, sound, touch, and the sickening sensation in his body. The
feeling would hit so hard that he had to stop whatever he was doing, sometimes standing frozen in a
supply closet or at the end of a hallway until he could catch his breath. Those episodes often
arrived after a trigger, but they also made the triggers sharper: once the memory took over,
nighttime felt more menacing, the texture of certain linens became intolerable, and a child crying
nearby could instantly pull him back into the old fear. He became superalert in the hotel, scanning
corridors and listening for sounds that reminded him of the past, and the more he watched for
danger, the more he noticed the very cues that set him off.

Carlos’s attempts to stay safe began to organize his life around avoidance. When the dark of night
or waking in darkness brought on the memories, he would drop responsibilities he knew mattered,
volunteering to switch shifts, skipping errands, or putting off bills and calls so he would not have
to move alone through the building before daylight. In the evenings he would drift off into a world
of his own, staring at his phone or losing himself in imagined scenes until hours passed, and he
made a point of never being alone with another person, preferring to stay near coworkers,
houseguests, or anyone else in sight. Those strategies brought brief relief, but they also kept the
traumatic material close. The more he organized his days around escape and safety, the more time he
spent quietly turning the event over in his mind, and the memory stayed primed to return with the
same unbearable vividness. He noticed too that these habits seemed to confirm the worst things he
believed about himself: that he was not really a person to others, but something damaged and
exposed, someone who had to hide because if people got a clear look they would know.

Over time, the fear of harm hardened into a general rule about people. Carlos came to feel that you
could never know who would hurt you, and that conviction kept him watching everyone around him with
suspicion. In the hotel, a child crying in a hallway could tighten his chest and make his skin
crawl; in his apartment, a dark room at 3 a.m. could have him wide awake and listening for
footsteps; a rough towel in housekeeping could stop him cold long enough for the memory to rush
back. Each episode made him pull further away, and each act of withdrawing seemed to leave him more
convinced that danger was everywhere and that he was somehow already singled out. By the time he
sought help, he was exhausted by the constant guard he kept over himself, troubled that his work was
suffering because he avoided tasks and lingered in distraction, and frightened that the cycle would
never loosen. What finally brought him in was the realization that he could not keep building his
life around darkness, textures, and crying children without losing more of it, and he hoped, more
than anything, to feel like a person again rather than an object waiting for harm.
