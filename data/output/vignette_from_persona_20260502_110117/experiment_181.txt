Omar was a 40-year-old married paramedic who had always prided himself on staying composed when
other people were panicking. The trauma that unsettled that confidence was not something that
happened to him, but something he witnessed up close: during a late-night call, he arrived to find a
young victim with severe visible injuries, blood already soaking through clothing, the corridor
around the stretcher carrying that sharp hospital-and-antiseptic smell even before they reached the
emergency bay. Omar had worked scenes like that before, but this one had lodged itself in a
different way because the victim’s face, the sound of their breathing, and the frantic pace of the
handoff seemed to hold everything in a single unbearable moment. He later found that even the
smallest reminders of victims — a face in distress, a body on a stretcher, the sight of wounds on
someone in public — could pull him straight back into that night. When that happened, he would
immediately try hard not to think about it, cutting off the image as soon as it surfaced and
throwing himself into work or tasks to keep his mind occupied. At home he stayed busy with chores
long after they needed doing, and at work he found reasons to stay moving rather than sit with
anyone else’s distress.

The problem was that the more he forced the memory away, the more it seemed to return in fragments
with the force of the original scene. Omar described that when it came back, it was not like
remembering a story but like being dropped back into the ambulance and hospital bay all over again,
with every sense lit up at once; the smell, the visual details, even the tension in his body felt
completely real. There were parts of the event he simply could not account for, gaps he kept trying
to fill but never could, and those missing pieces only deepened the terror each time he tried to
review what had happened. He became convinced that something in him had broken permanently, that he
had changed for the worse and could no longer rely on himself the way he once had. What haunted him
most was the belief that he should have protected the victim better and that he had failed, a
thought that made him scrutinize every decision afterward. That self-blame made him more alert to
anything that resembled the original scene: visible injuries on others, reminders of victims, and
the hospital smell all took on an immediate charge, and he would feel compelled to pull back, avoid
going to work or study-related training sessions, and keep himself occupied so he would not have to
face the images.

Over time, those habits began to narrow his life. Omar started arranging his days around not being
confronted with the things that set him off, and even outside the hospital he chose seats based on
how safe or protected he felt, usually with a wall at his back and a clear view of the room. If
someone touched him unexpectedly, he reacted with alarm, and the reaction embarrassed him enough
that he became even more vigilant about who stood behind him or approached too quickly. He noticed
his concentration slipping during charting and routine conversations, partly because he was watching
the room and partly because his mind kept drifting back to the same sensory flash of the call. The
more he avoided, the less chance he had to settle the memory, and the less settled it became, the
more often it intruded; the more often it intruded, the more certain he felt that he was damaged,
unsafe, and responsible. That sense of being permanently altered fed the belief that he needed to
stay busy and away from anything that might stir the event, yet the very act of skipping workdays,
suppressing thoughts, and piling on extra tasks kept the trauma vivid and close, as if he were
always bracing for the next reminder. Even now, a glimpse of injuries on a stranger or the
antiseptic smell when he passed through the hospital corridors could set off the full sequence:
alarm in his body, the surge of the memory, and the old conviction that he had failed and could not
trust himself to do better.

He finally sought help after noticing that he was becoming increasingly withdrawn from his wife and
more strained at work, where the job that once steadied him now felt like the most dangerous place
to be. He was not asking because he wanted to stop caring; he was afraid that if this continued, the
terror would keep taking over every part of his life, and he would end up proving to himself that he
really was no longer the man he had been.
