in schools and later as a curriculum designer, but the memories of her military service had never
quite arranged themselves into a story she could tell. During a deployment in Oceania’s tense
coastal operations, she had been caught one afternoon in a situation where younger men with weapons
controlled the road, the radios, and the pace of everyone’s breathing. Helicopters passed overhead
while she waited with her unit, listening for instructions that came too slowly, and the scene broke
apart in her mind into flashes: dust, rotor noise, a hand gripping her sleeve, someone shouting in a
language she could not answer. Even now, Malia said the memory felt so extreme that it seemed hard
to believe it had happened to her at all. She knew what happened, but when she tried to explain it,
the words would not come; what remained were disconnected pieces that refused to fit together.

At first those fragments would surface without warning when she was doing something ordinary, and
the memory itself became the most reliable doorway back into terror. A sound in the distance, a
sudden image, or the heavy beat of a helicopter overhead could drop her straight back into that
broken afternoon. When that happened, she was flooded not only by the scene but by the thought that
she had been unable to make sense of it then and still could not now. The suddenness of those
recollections made her feel that she could not trust herself to judge what was happening or to do
the right thing if danger came again. She began to believe that she was set apart from other people
because they could speak about difficult things in orderly sentences, while she carried something
wordless and strange that made her feel isolated even in company.

The same helicopter sound that pulled up the memory also sharpened her sense that the world could
turn on her in an instant. Malia became especially uneasy anywhere she could not control who stood
above her or what authority might decide her fate, and she linked that feeling back to the days when
power had belonged to others on the road. Being in a situation where someone had power over her no
longer felt merely uncomfortable; it felt dangerous, proof that she would once again be stuck
waiting and watching. That appraisal deepened her conviction that she had to be on guard all the
time. She checked exits automatically, noticed uniforms, and measured the room before she took a
seat, especially in meetings at the school district where she worked. If a man raised his voice in a
conference room or an aircraft passed low near the office windows, she would feel the old alarm rise
and the thought would come that she could not trust herself to do the right thing if pressure
mounted.

To keep that fear from overwhelming her, Malia shrank her life. She avoided going out unless she
absolutely had to, and when she did, she chose places where she could stay near the exit. She sat
with her back to walls, declined invitations that might trap her too long, and left family
gatherings early, telling herself that having a clear path out was just practical. She also tried
not to allow herself to feel any strong emotion, because she had learned that once fear or grief
started, it seemed to pull the broken memories after it. Yet those efforts carried their own costs.
By keeping herself tightly controlled and distant, she became more isolated, and the loneliness
confirmed her suspicion that she was different from others and that no one would really understand
what lived in her mind. The more she withheld feeling, the less she could make sense of the event;
the memories stayed in fragments, and the silence around them made them seem even more unreal and
harder to put into words.

That same guardedness made her more alert to danger outside the home as well. Because she rarely
went out, every trip felt consequential, and a helicopter sound over the parking lot or the sight of
a powerful, impatient person in a store could ignite the same surge of vigilance she had been trying
to avoid. She began to move through daily life as if each outing might become another version of
that roadblock, and she would hurry back inside before her fear had time to grow. Over months, the
pattern hardened: the memories surfaced, the sense of unreality and loss of words followed, the
threat felt immediate again, and she retreated further. By the time she sought help, Malia was still
working, but she arranged her schedule around avoiding crowds, avoided strong emotion as much as
possible, and kept leaving herself near the door. What finally brought her in was the fear that the
circle was tightening and that she would keep becoming smaller if she did not speak now. She said
she wanted to understand what had happened to her before her life became permanently organized
around exits, alarms, and the sense that she could never quite trust herself again.
