when the bombing happened, and the fact that it occurred during an ordinary workday made it hard for

shouting, the sharpness of commands, and the sight of people moving with a kind of violent,
uncontained panic that he had never imagined on a public bus. He survived, but he came away
convinced that the life he had known had been destroyed in a single morning. Since then, Arthur has
spoken about the world as if it were fundamentally treacherous, saying with grim certainty that
people are not what they seem and that danger can appear anywhere, even in a routine ride across
town.

Over time, those beliefs began to shape everything he noticed. When a colleague raised her voice in
the emergency department, or when a driver barked instructions at passengers, Arthur felt the same
cold tightening he had felt in the bus moments before the blast. Violence in the form of sudden
aggression or even the hint of it pulled him back into the conviction that catastrophe could erupt
without warning. He began to connect each such moment to the larger story he told himself about the
attack: if a city bus, a workplace corridor, or a crowded intersection could become lethal in an
instant, then the world truly was dangerous and people could not be trusted. Those appraisals were
not abstract for him; they were immediate and personal, and they seemed to confirm themselves every
time he was confronted with forcefulness, authority, or the sight of conflict in public.

inside his own home, listening for the sounds that might mean someone was entering, checking the
lock more than once, and remaining on guard long after the doors were shut. He described himself as
irritable and quick to anger, a man who could no longer tolerate minor frustrations without feeling
as if he had to defend himself. He was certain that this constant alertness was the only thing
keeping him safe. Yet it also kept reinforcing the sense that his life had been altered beyond
repair: if he had to monitor every creak in the house and every stranger’s expression, then the
trauma had not simply happened to him, it had taken over his future. That sense of a destroyed life
fed the vigilance further, leaving him more watchful, more suspicious, and more convinced that even
familiar places could no longer be taken for granted.

fraught because it placed him back in the position of a captive passenger, unable to control who sat
nearby or where the vehicle might stop. Being given orders or instructions, even in benign settings,
could bring a jolt of anger and dread, as if authority itself carried the threat of another unseen
disaster. Violence in the news, at work, or on the street had the same effect. Each time one of
these reminders appeared, it seemed to Arthur to confirm his belief that the world was dangerous and
that people were not what they seemed, and that confirmation made him even more guarded at home and
in public. He found himself scanning faces, listening for sudden changes in tone, and mentally
rehearsing what he would do if something went wrong. The more he monitored, the more certain he
became that danger was everywhere; the more certain he became, the more sharply he reacted to the
next reminder.

By the time he came for help, Arthur’s life had narrowed in ways his wife had noticed before he
would admit them himself. He still worked as a doctor, but he had begun avoiding buses and arranging
his schedule around private transport whenever possible, and even then he remained tense on the way
to the hospital. At home he was tired from listening for sounds that might mean someone entering,
and he hated how easily he became angry with people he loved. What finally brought him to treatment
was the fear that this watchfulness was becoming permanent, that he would spend the rest of his life
trapped inside the ruins of what had happened to him. More than anything, Arthur hoped to find a way
to feel safe enough to stop guarding every door and every passing face, but he was afraid that the
bombing had changed him so completely that he no longer knew how.
