Martin, a 65-year-old prosecutor, came to treatment after months of feeling as though ordinary life
had become freighted with danger. He described the original incident with a lawyer’s precision:
years earlier, while he was in a confrontation with police, the officers’ sudden force, their
authority, and the shouting around him left him feeling trapped, humiliated, and physically unsafe.
What had stayed with him most was not only the pain and fear of that moment, but the sense that
people in power could turn on him without warning and that other people nearby might be too chaotic,
too misreading, or too willing to join in. Since then, he had found that certain moments in daily
life seemed to pull him straight back into that state. When he heard incomprehensible chatter in a
hallway or crowded restaurant, he would tense and feel immediately on guard, as if he could not tell
who was saying what or what anyone intended. He was especially unsettled by police presence, even
the sight of a patrol car in the neighborhood or uniformed officers entering the courthouse where he
worked. He also noticed a strong reaction to people misbehaving—raised voices, disrespect, or rule-
breaking at work or in public—because it felt to him like the first step in a situation getting out
of control.

Those moments fed a persistent sense that danger could emerge from any direction. Martin said he had
become superalert and watchful, constantly scanning the environment for signs of threat and watching
other people closely for signs that they might harm him. At the courthouse he would position himself
so he could see the exits and the faces of people passing by; in waiting rooms he kept his eyes
moving from one person to the next, reading expressions, checking hands, trying to decide who looked
“off.” He slept badly because his mind would not settle enough to let him drift off, and when he did
fall asleep he woke easily and struggled to stay asleep, already braced for something to happen. The
more he felt this alertness rising, the more he tried to manage it in ways that gave only brief
relief. He put on loud music or the TV at home to drown out nearby sounds, especially when the house
was quiet and every noise felt magnified. He kept asking his wife and adult son to reassure him that
he was okay, that nothing was wrong, and that he was overreacting. Even then, he would continue to
check the surroundings, glancing toward windows, doors, and the street, as if one more sweep might
finally tell him he was safe.

Over time, those strategies began to shape the very experiences that set him off. The loud music and
television made it harder to hear what people around him were saying clearly, so the broken
fragments of conversation at work or in public became even more difficult for him to interpret and
more likely to leave him uneasy. The constant scanning and watching other people kept his attention
fixed on every tense expression, every abrupt movement, every small breach of etiquette, which made
misbehaving or rowdy behavior stand out even more sharply and reinforced his conviction that trouble
was near. Seeking reassurance helped only for a moment; once the words faded, he was left needing to
check again, and the repeated checking kept his body in a state of readiness that made the whole
world feel threatening. At home, his wife noticed that he could not fully settle into dinner or
conversation, and at work he began lingering at the edges of meetings and court corridors, less
because he wanted to be distant than because he felt he had to keep himself positioned to see what
others might do next.

By the time he presented for help, Martin had become discouraged by how small his life had grown
around vigilance. He was not only frightened by police or by the sound of confusing chatter; he was
frightened by how quickly his own mind moved toward danger, how quickly a crowded room or a
disrespectful exchange could become a threat in his body, and how much energy he spent trying to
outrun that feeling. What finally pushed him to seek help was a stretch of nights in which sleep
became so fragmented that he was exhausted at work and increasingly irritable at home, along with
the realization that his constant monitoring was no longer protecting him from fear but keeping him
inside it. He hoped treatment might help him feel less on guard and more present with his wife and
family. At the same time, he remained afraid that if he stopped scanning or stopped asking for
reassurance, he would miss the moment when something bad was about to happen.
