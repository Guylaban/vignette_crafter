Ravi, a 41-year-old South Asian graphic designer, came to treatment after nearly a year of living as
though ordinary life had become a series of hidden ambushes. The trauma that still organized his
days began on an evening walk home from the train station, when he was stopped by police officers
responding to a report of a suspicious man in the area. He had trusted the uniformed officer who
asked to see his identification, relieved to comply and eager to be cooperative, but the encounter
turned abruptly when he was searched, pushed against a wall, and left humiliated and confused as
bystanders watched. What stayed with him most was not only the physical force but the smell of
something burnt from a nearby alley—an oily, acrid odor that seemed to attach itself to the scene.
Since then, the smell of burnt food, burnt toast, or even a scorched electrical cord could drag him
back to that night with startling force, and the sight of police, security guards, or any person in
uniform had the same effect. When those cues appeared, he would feel the event return in fragments
rather than as a coherent story: he knew what happened, but when he tried to describe it, the words
would not come, and the account would slip away before he could finish.

Ravi had come to believe that people could not be trusted, that the world was a dangerous place, and
that he had been betrayed by someone he had once believed was there to protect him. Those beliefs
shaped the meaning of the memory every time it surfaced. He did not remember the encounter as a
single bad night; he remembered it as proof that authority could turn on him without warning, and
that being reasonable or compliant offered no protection. The more he returned to that
interpretation, the more the memory filled with shame and self-reproach. He would remember the
officer’s face, the shouting, the moment his hands were forced behind him, and then be overwhelmed
by intense guilt, as if he should have done something different, said the right thing, or recognized
the danger sooner. He found himself unable to find words for it, and when he tried to tell his wife
or a colleague, he lost the thread halfway through and stopped, frustrated and embarrassed by his
own silence.

In the months that followed, Ravi began rehearsing conversations before almost any interaction. He
would stand in the kitchen before answering the phone, practicing what to say if a neighbor asked a
question, or if someone in the lobby stopped him, or if a parking attendant challenged him. At work
as a graphic designer, even a routine email from a client could send him into careful mental
scripting before he replied, as though the wrong phrase might expose him to someone dangerous. That
effort to stay prepared kept his attention fixed on what might go wrong, and he became increasingly
vigilant for dangerous people wherever he went, scanning faces, hands, badges, and body language in
the office building, on the train, and outside the grocery store. Because he was constantly
anticipating threat, the presence of uniforms and the smell of burnt food became even more charged;
he noticed them immediately, and each sight or odor seemed to pull the memory back into the present
before he had time to think.

His attempts to manage the fear gradually hardened the beliefs that had begun the cycle. The more
Ravi rehearsed and controlled his speech, the more convinced he became that people were
untrustworthy and that the safest assumption was danger. Avoiding future plans followed naturally
from that outlook. He stopped agreeing to trips, deferred family outings, and would not commit to
projects at work that extended beyond the week, telling himself that planning ahead only set him up
for disappointment or exposure. Even his marriage narrowed around his caution; his wife noticed how
often he asked what would happen next and how little he would say about weekends or holidays. Yet
despite all this preparation, he still felt ambushed by the cues he could not fully control. A burnt
smell drifting from a neighboring apartment, a police car passing his building, a uniformed
maintenance worker in the lobby—each could bring the scene back and leave him shaken, mute, and
ashamed, which in turn sent him back into more rehearsing and more scanning.

By the time he sought help, Ravi was exhausted by the effort of keeping himself ready for danger and
by the loneliness of carrying a memory he could not explain. What finally brought him in was a
recent evening when he smelled something burnt in the hallway of his apartment building and saw two
police officers speaking with a tenant downstairs; he froze, could not speak when his wife asked
what was wrong, and spent the rest of the night pacing and checking the locks. He said he was most
afraid that he would spend the rest of his life expecting betrayal from every authority figure and
every unfamiliar face, but he was also hoping to find a way to speak about what happened without
losing the thread, and to stop living as if the next burnt smell or uniform meant the same thing
would happen again.
