Marisol Tran was 38, single, and had built her life around the steady rhythm of welding work: the
hiss of the torch, the bright glare of the shop lights, the predictable weight of steel in her
hands. Months before she came to treatment, that order broke in an instant when she accidentally
caused a serious injury to a coworker during a shift. She had been working on a large frame in the
shop, focused on the joint in front of her, when the error happened and another person was hurt
badly enough that the memory still returned to her with a sick, metallic taste in her mouth. What
stayed with Marisol was not a clean story but fragments: a flash of movement, the sound of alarm,
the taste she had had in her mouth during the event, and then gaps where the rest should have been.
She felt as though she could only remember pieces of what happened, and the missing parts made the
whole scene feel more horrifying, as if something unforgivable might be hidden in what she could not
fully retrieve. Even when the memory came back, it came with intense guilt, and she would sit
rigidly trying to make sense of what she had done wrong.

In the weeks that followed, Marisol began to avoid thinking about the incident because she was
afraid that once she started, the missing pieces would rush in and confirm her worst fears. At first
she tried to push it away during the day, but her mind kept circling back to what she had done
wrong, and she found herself dwelling on every step, every decision, every moment she might have
prevented it. Because the memory felt incomplete and contaminated by guilt, she started avoiding
other people too, especially coworkers who might ask about it or remind her of the aftermath. At
work she became quieter, kept to her station, and took pains not to linger near the group during
breaks. Outside of work she stopped answering invitations from friends and declined family
gatherings, telling herself that being around people would only risk someone bringing up the
accident or seeing the shame she believed was written all over her. The more she tried not to think
about it, the more her mind seemed to return to it in jagged flashes, and those fragments made her
pull back further from everyone around her.

The memory itself had begun to feel unstable for Marisol because the threat around her was keeping
it alive. She slept poorly, lying awake for hours and waking often, and on those nights the same
broken images drifted in with startling force. When that happened, the taste from the event seemed
to come back as if it were present again, and the incomplete picture of the accident felt even more
unbearable in the dark. She would wake tense and alert, her body reacting strongly to anything that
reminded her of the shop, whether it was the smell of hot metal, the buzz of a machine, or even a
scrape of sound that made her shoulders jerk. By morning she was exhausted and had trouble
concentrating, rereading instructions at work and losing track of what she was doing. The fatigue
and poor focus fed her fear that she could make another mistake, and that fear made the memories
crowd in again, especially when she was alone with too much time to think. As the weeks became
months, Marisol’s life narrowed around that loop: the more the memory surfaced with guilt and
missing pieces, the more she avoided people and avoided thinking, and the more her sleep,
concentration, and physical startle reactions deteriorated.

At the welding shop, these reactions became harder to hide. A sudden clang from a dropped tool or
the flare of light from another station could bring a violent jolt through her body, and she would
have to clamp down on her breathing so no one would notice. She began taking extra precautions,
arriving early to work in a nearly empty shop and leaving as soon as her tasks were done. Her
supervisor noticed she was less engaged, and Marisol feared that others could see not just her
distraction but the guilt she carried. She had come to believe that if she let herself remember too
much, she would be overwhelmed by what she had done and perhaps learn that the missing parts were
even worse than she imagined. So she kept herself busy, kept herself away from people, and kept
trying not to think, even though each effort seemed to circle her back to the same scene.

What finally brought Marisol to seek help was not a single breakdown but the growing sense that she
could no longer sleep, focus, or stay away from the memory without her whole life shrinking around
it. She was most afraid that the fragments in her mind would never come together enough to give her
any relief, and that the guilt would remain as sharp as the taste that returned with the memory. At
the same time, she was hoping that if she could understand why the event kept intruding and why she
kept pulling away from everyone, she might finally be able to work without feeling that one mistake
had defined the rest of her life.
