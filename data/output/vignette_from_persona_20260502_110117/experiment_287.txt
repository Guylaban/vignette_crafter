Marcos, a 64-year-old divorced jeweler from a Latin American family, came to treatment after years
of feeling as if his nerves had never fully settled since childhood. As a boy, he had repeatedly
witnessed his father shouting at and frightening his mother in their home, with confrontations in
the kitchen or hallway leaving him frozen nearby, watching for what might happen next. What stayed
with him was not a full story but a series of sharp, vivid pictures: his father’s face, his mother’s
posture, the angle of a hand, the sound of raised voices cutting through the room. When he remembers
those moments now, he feels intense guilt, as if he should have done something, and he experiences
them almost like isolated snapshots that return with unnerving clarity.

In the present, the most ordinary things can pull him back toward that old fear. Shouting,
especially a man’s voice raised in conflict, makes him tense immediately, and even being alone with
men can set him on edge. At his jewelry shop, a customer arguing over a price or two men entering
together can change the whole feel of the day. Marcos becomes hyperalert, scanning their hands,
their faces, and the room itself for anything that could be used to hurt him; he notices scissors,
tools, heavy display objects, even the edge of the glass case, as potential weapons. He has come to
believe that he cannot truly trust safety even when others reassure him, and that belief makes the
environment feel unstable and dangerous. He watches people closely for signs they might turn
aggressive, and he takes any rise in tension as evidence that trouble is near. Those moments also
feed his self-concept: he thinks he has failed in his most important role in life, that he has been
weak when he should have protected others, and that he has lost his connection to the community and
culture that once gave him a sense of belonging.

After particularly triggering encounters, Marcos tries to manage himself by shutting down anything
that might intensify the fear. He avoids relaxing because it feels dangerous to let his guard down,
as if ease would leave him exposed. He constantly monitors his own thoughts for signs that he is
“going crazy,” and he works hard not to think about the childhood scenes at all. Yet the more he
tries to keep them away, the more the images press back in. A shouted exchange in the shop, or even
a tense conversation in the parking lot outside, can bring the memory back in fragments, and the
very act of pushing it away makes it return with greater force. He notices that after these efforts
he feels more keyed up, more suspicious of others, and more convinced that something is wrong with
him. When he catches himself scanning a stranger’s waistband, avoiding stillness, or refusing to sit
back and relax after closing the store, he takes that as proof that he is weak and damaged. He tells
himself that if he were stronger, he would not need to live this way, and that thought deepens his
sense of having failed.

Over time, the cycle has narrowed his life. He is more likely to be triggered by conflict because he
is already watching for it, and what he notices in others confirms his expectation that danger can
appear anywhere. The old scenes of his father shouting and his mother’s fear remain so clear that
they seem to organize the present; each vivid image reinforces his conviction that he must stay on
guard, and that vigilance is the only thing keeping him safe. But constant vigilance also keeps the
memories close, because he never fully lets his mind settle, and never quite escapes the feeling
that he is back in those rooms again. He has become quieter with family, less involved in community
events, and more likely to keep interactions brief at work so he can avoid confrontation. That
withdrawal, in turn, has left him feeling more isolated and more unlike the man he expected himself
to be.

Marcos finally sought help after realizing that the shop, which had once felt like his livelihood
and a source of pride, had become a place where he spent the day braced for threat, unable to relax
even after closing. He is afraid that if this continues, he will remain trapped in suspicion and
guilt, never able to feel safe with men, with conflict, or with his own mind. At the same time, he
still hopes that treatment might help him stop living as if the past is happening now and let him
recover some sense of strength and connection.
