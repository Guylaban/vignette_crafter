Leah, a 58-year-old divorced rabbi of Oceania background, came to treatment describing life since a
roadside accident that had left her with a sense that her world had split into “before” and “after.”
The crash had happened years earlier on a narrow stretch of highway at dusk, when another vehicle
crossed the center line and struck her car hard enough that she still remembered the violent impact,
the grinding metal, and the acrid burnt smell that filled the cabin as smoke curled in from under
the hood. In the weeks after, she began waking from nightmares with her heart racing, disoriented by
the residue of the dream and certain, for a few seconds, that the danger was back in the room with
her. Those mornings became especially hard if she caught even a whiff of burnt toast, an overheated
appliance, or smoke from a neighbor’s grill; the smell would snap her back into the crash, and she
would immediately try to think only positive thoughts, force a smile, or hide her face in her hands
until the feeling passed. She ate excessively in the evenings to manage the distress, telling
herself that if she could fill herself physically she might not feel so unsteady inside.

Over time, these moments of re-experiencing seemed to confirm her fear that she had never truly
recovered. Leah had come to believe that her body kept reacting as if she were still in danger even
when she was safe, and that thought left her feeling isolated and set apart from others, as though
no one around her could understand how quickly her system could be seized by alarm. She spoke about
becoming unrecognisable to herself, especially because she had once seen herself as calm, steady,
and in control. Now she felt like a stranger who startled when someone touched her unexpectedly,
even in ordinary settings like greeting congregants after services or brushing past coworkers in the
synagogue hallway. That sense of being changed beyond recognition fed her irritability; she noticed
herself snapping at people, reacting with angry outbursts when startled, and then feeling ashamed
that she could not seem to regulate herself. Her fear that her body was unreliable made the past
feel dangerously close, and that closeness made the nightmare residue, the burnt smell, and even
stories of trauma-related events especially potent.

As Leah’s alarm and anger intensified, she began taking too many risks in ways she later described
as almost defiant, as if she could prove something by ignoring her own caution. She drove when she
was exhausted, stayed too late in poor weather, and pushed herself into situations that could have
put her in harm’s way. Yet these efforts did not restore confidence; instead, they seemed to deepen
her conviction that she had become someone she did not recognize and could not trust. When the body
sensations surged or she found herself on edge, she would reach again for the same coping
habits—overeating after long days, forcing positive thoughts, hiding her face—only to wake again the
next morning from a dream with the crash still clinging to her. The more she tried to shut down
distress in these ways, the more she noticed that familiar cues returned with renewed force: a burnt
smell in the kitchen, a mention of an accident in the news, a sudden touch on the arm at synagogue,
or even a nightmare that left her breathless before dawn. These moments made her feel further apart
from other people, and that isolation, in turn, seemed to leave her even more vulnerable to reacting
sharply, scanning for danger, and bracing for the next jolt.

By the time she sought help, Leah was exhausted by the cycle and frightened by how much it had
narrowed her life and work. She remained committed to her role as rabbi, but she had begun avoiding
situations that might expose her to unexpected contact or distressing reminders, and she worried
that congregants could see the strain in her face before she could compose herself. What finally
brought her in was a particularly vivid nightmare followed the next morning by the smell of
something burning in her kitchen, which sent her into a burst of anger at herself and a rush to
leave the room. She sat down with the clear fear that if this continued, she would keep becoming
more isolated and more unlike the person she had once been. What she most hoped for was to feel safe
in her own body again; what she most feared was that her life would keep shrinking around every
reminder of the crash.
