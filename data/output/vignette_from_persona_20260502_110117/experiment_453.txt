Malik, a 22-year-old school principal in a small district office on the edge of a busy African
immigrant neighborhood, came to treatment after months of feeling as though the past was pressing
into the present from every direction. He had grown up with a caregiver who beat him as a child,
often in abrupt bursts that left no warning and no explanation, and he now described his world as
being organized around the fear of the next unpredictable blow. What made the memories especially
potent was not just the abuse itself but how they returned: old photos could stop him cold, and
waking from a nightmare would leave him disoriented and on edge, as if he had been yanked back into
an old room he could not quite see. In those moments, the event did not come back to him as a smooth
story; it arrived in disconnected pieces that did not fit together, and because he could not
deliberately pull the memory into a clear sequence when he tried, he was left with fragments that
felt more real and vivid than ordinary memories, sharp enough to make him distrust what he was
seeing around him.

The more Malik found himself caught by those fragments, the more he came to believe that something
had been permanently altered in him. He would sit at his desk in the school office, looking over
disciplinary reports or fielding sudden interruptions from staff, and the smallest unpredictability
in the day could pull him into the same conclusion: he could not stop bad things from happening to
him. A student arriving in tears without warning, a parent demanding to speak immediately, even a
change in the schedule he had not anticipated could make his chest tighten with the old sense of
helplessness. Because the memories came back in broken pieces, he struggled to place them safely in
the past, and that uncertainty fed his fear that he had changed for the worse. He had come to
believe that if he were pushed enough, his own anger might become dangerous; he worried that he
would not be able to control it and would do something terrible. That fear did not stay abstract.
When the memories felt especially vivid, he became more watchful for anything that might set him
off, and he found himself bracing against ordinary moments, scanning faces and voices for signs that
another blow, humiliation, or loss of control might be coming.

As the months went by, those fears shaped what Malik allowed himself to encounter. Old photographs
were no longer casual reminders of family or childhood; he avoided looking through albums, and he
would ask his partner to put away pictures from family gatherings if they surfaced on a phone
screen. When his own mind felt unstable, he interpreted the old photos as evidence that the injured
child was still present inside him, unchanged in the worst way, and that made the memories feel even
more vivid and threatening. The nightmares had the same effect. He dreaded waking suddenly in the
night because the residue of the dream made him feel as if something bad was about to happen, and by
morning he was already prepared for danger. In the school building, unpredictable situations became
increasingly hard to tolerate, so he began stepping back from hallway conflicts and delegating more
than he used to, not because he lacked competence but because he feared what his body and mind might
do under pressure. Yet every attempt to steady himself seemed to circle back into the same
experience: the more he tried to keep the memory in order, the more it broke apart; the more it
broke apart, the more convinced he became that he was damaged and at risk; and the more convinced he
became, the more tightly he avoided the things that might remind him of it.

By the time he sought help, Malik was exhausted by how much space the fear had taken up in his work
and his relationship. He cared deeply about his partner and wanted to stay present, but he was
frightened that his irritability and constant anticipation of danger would spill into the people he
loved or into the school community that relied on him. What finally brought him in was a night of
waking from a nightmare and finding himself unable to settle, then spending the next day moving
through the office with the old familiar sense that he could not stop bad things from happening. He
hopes to understand why the memories remain so raw and why old photos, unexpected disruptions, and
the shock of waking from dreams can still make him feel as though he is back in the hands of that
childhood violence, and he is most afraid that the version of himself shaped by it will stay
permanently in control.
