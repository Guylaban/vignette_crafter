days on foot, checking trails, speaking with visitors, and knowing the land by smell, sound, and the
angle of light through the trees. Years earlier, while working as a civilian guide in a war zone in

through the area. He remembered smoke rising among the trees, the sudden flash of fire in dry brush,
shouted orders from armed men in military uniforms, and the sickening pause when a group he had been
helping was struck and he survived while others did not. What stayed with him most was not only the
violence itself but the way he had acted to keep moving and stay alive, actions he later judged as
shameful and against the kind of man he believed himself to be.

In the months after that event, Musa found that even small reminders pulled him back into the same
dread. A supervisor’s criticism at work could hit him like danger, because being corrected now felt
less like feedback than the first sign that something terrible was about to happen. If a fire pit
was lit at a campground or he saw flames from a controlled burn, his body tightened immediately, and
if he walked in a forest that resembled the place where the attack happened, the whole day could
collapse into alarm. Those moments left him unable to relax even when he knew he was in a safe
place, and he began sleeping badly, lying awake listening for sounds he could not trust. When
something at work took longer than expected, he assumed something bad was happening on the trail or
that a crisis had begun out of sight. The same sights and situations also drove him deeper into
himself: he cut off contact with friends and family, stopped answering calls, and avoided any
setting where he might see military or police uniforms or weapons, because those images seemed to
belong to the world that had swallowed the people he could not save.

Musa had come to believe that surviving meant something was wrong with him. He felt guilty for being
alive when others did not make it out, and that guilt carried a harsh sense that he had violated his
own values in the way he had tried to preserve himself. He described feeling isolated and set apart
from other people, as if his life had split off from theirs at the moment of the attack. When he was
criticized, or when he found himself back among trees and brush that looked too much like the
original place, those appraisals surged again: he was certain that he had failed the dead, that he
no longer belonged among ordinary people, and that the man he had been before the war was gone. The
more he judged himself in this way, the more on edge he became, and the more he braced for danger.

That constant strain shaped the way he moved through his days. He drank alcohol and took medication
or drugs to blunt the tension and force himself through evenings when his thoughts would not settle.
But the short-lived relief left him more withdrawn and more vulnerable to the very reminders he was
trying to outrun. After drinking, he often woke with his heart racing and his sleep even more
broken, and the fatigue made him quicker to see threat in delays, criticism, or a patch of woodland
that resembled the site of the attack. Because he had isolated so completely, he spent less time
with people who might have helped challenge these fears; instead, the silence around him made his
guilt and sense of separation grow stronger, which in turn made the world feel more dangerous. At
the park, he started taking different routes to avoid areas where visitors lit fires or where ranger
vehicles and uniforms would remind him of armed men, and the effort of avoiding those cues kept the
event close in his mind. The more he arranged his life around not seeing, not hearing, and not being
reminded, the more ordinary surroundings seemed loaded with threat, and the more easily his thoughts
turned back to what had happened and what he believed it said about him.

By the time Musa sought help, he was exhausted by sleeping poorly, unable to relax even in his own
home, and increasingly afraid that his life had narrowed to a series of cautions and withdrawals. He
came because he could no longer carry the guilt and the constant sense that something bad was about
to happen, and because he feared that if this continued he would lose the last pieces of himself
that still felt loyal to who he believed he was. What he hoped for, though he said it carefully, was
to sleep again, to walk the trails without feeling hunted by fire, criticism, and trees, and to find
a way to be around people without feeling cut off from them.
