Evan, a 28-year-old European American financial analyst, had been doing well by ordinary measures
before his son’s illness, working long hours during the week and returning home each evening to a
small apartment he shared with his wife. The crisis began when his toddler suddenly became gravely
unwell, spiking a frightening fever and being rushed through emergency rooms and inpatient tests
before doctors could stabilize him. Evan remembers the hours as a chain of fluorescent hallways,
clipped voices, and the crushing sense that his child might die while he stood there unable to fix
it. Since that period, certain moments have become loaded with alarm: a breaking-news alert about a
pediatric illness, a stretch of cold rain outside the office windows, or a colleague’s child
appearing in the break room can all make his body tighten before he has even had time to think. In
those moments he reacts as if something terrible is about to happen, scanning exits in restaurants
and conference rooms, choosing seats nearest the door, and lying awake at night needing to stay
alert because he cannot trust that he will be ready if danger comes again.

What Evan finds most disturbing is that the fear does not stay on the surface. The same weather that
once coincided with the hospital calls now seems to carry the illness back into the present for him,
and the sight of children in public makes his attention snap to the possibility of loss. He has come
to believe that his mind has changed in a way he cannot recognize, that he no longer knows himself,
and that the man he has become is unfamiliar even to him. He often says that he should have
protected others and failed, a belief that takes on particular force when he sees news about sick
children or hears parents talking in the grocery store. Those reminders make him feel newly
implicated, as if the world is asking him again whether he will be ready next time, and he cannot
bear that question. At work, after an email about a pediatric charity event or a news clip on
someone’s screen, he will find himself stepping away, checking the hall, and trying to calm the
surge of dread by planning how he could get out quickly if something happened.

His memory of the illness remains strikingly vivid and detailed, but it comes to him mostly in
pictures: the small body under hospital blankets, the red glow of monitor lights, his wife’s hand
gripping the chair arm, the doctor’s mouth moving while he waited for the verdict. At the same time,
he finds himself pushing the memory away before he can fully recall it, stopping short whenever the
images start to assemble into a complete scene. He has learned that if he lets himself follow the
memory all the way through, he is flooded with the same shame and sense of failure, and that makes
the images come back even more sharply. The pictures then seem to confirm what he already fears
about himself—that he was not enough, that he should have done more—and that conviction keeps the
memory close even as he tries to shut it down.

Those memories, in turn, have made the present feel dangerous in a way he cannot shake. When he
notices the first dark clouds before a storm, or hears a news segment about a child’s
hospitalization, he feels the old hospital terror rush back and immediately starts scanning for ways
to leave, as if every room might become another waiting room. Children present the hardest test of
all: at family gatherings he stands rigidly near the door, watching the children play and feeling
the same need to escape and prepare that he felt in the hospital. The more he notices these cues,
the more he believes they are proof that he has changed into someone unstable and failed, which
makes him want to keep the memory at bay and stay away from anything that could bring it forward
again. Over weeks, that pattern has narrowed his life; he avoids staying late at the office when a
storm is rolling in, turns off the television when medical stories appear, and has begun declining
invitations where friends’ children will be present, leaving his wife to explain his absences.

By the time he sought help, Evan was sleeping poorly because he needed to stay alert, checking the
apartment locks and mentally rehearsing how to get out of buildings before he could relax in them.
He was most afraid that he would remain permanently unrecognizable to himself and that his constant
sense of danger would keep him from being the father and husband he wants to be. What brought him in
was the realization that he was no longer simply remembering the illness; he was organizing his days
around it, and he feared that if he did not get help he would keep living as though his son’s near-
death were happening all over again.
