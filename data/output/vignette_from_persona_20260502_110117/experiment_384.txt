hours had ended. After the rape, which had happened on an evening outreach visit when a man she had

to speak about it, she would stop and say she knew what happened but could not find the words for
it. What remained most vivid was not explanation but a series of pictures, and with each picture
came an immediate wave of guilt. She felt as if she had done something wrong simply by having been
there, and that shame spread outward until she came to believe she had lost something fundamental in
herself. She said she had once been happy and open, but now she felt miserable almost all the time,
and she no longer trusted people, life, or even God to protect what was good.

Those beliefs shaped the way she moved through her days. Marisol had come to feel that she deserved

relaxing because rest felt dangerous to her, almost like lowering her guard in a place where harm
could return. When she remembered the assault, the same painful images reappeared, and rather than
soothing her they pushed her further into punishment thinking and rigid self-control. She would skip
meals after a disturbing memory or after a hard shift at the ministry, telling herself she had no
right to comfort. The more she tried to manage herself through deprivation and strain, the less
words she found for the trauma, and the more it remained in those frozen snapshots that hovered at
the edges of her mind.

At work, the world began to feel newly menacing. Marisol noticed that unusual silence in a hallway,
a church office, or even her own home made her brace for something terrible. If a delivery was late
or a colleague took longer than expected to answer a call, she immediately assumed something bad was
happening. She could not relax even when she was sitting in a safe place, because her body seemed to
know danger before her mind could disprove it. That heightened watchfulness drew strength from the
images that kept intruding on her and from the shame she carried; because the assault still lived in
brief, vivid scenes, she stayed on guard for the next wrong moment, and because she believed people
were no longer trustworthy and that her body had been violated in a way that made her less whole,
every silence or delay seemed to confirm that the world was not safe. In turn, this constant
scanning kept her tense enough that she turned again to strict routines and self-denial, using
control over eating and rest to steady herself for a few moments.

But the habits she adopted also kept the trauma close. By refusing to slow down, she gave herself no
settled time in which the fragments might be spoken through, and the story remained trapped as
images rather than a coherent narrative. The more she used control and punishment to hold herself

danger was still near. Weeks became months of this tightening pattern. She became harder to be with
at home, less present with her husband, and more depleted at work, where she started avoiding any
quiet room or unexpected pause in the day. Her fear was no longer limited to the original assault;

By the time she sought help, Marisol was exhausted by living this way and frightened by how small

feel safe enough to relax, to eat normally, and to remember what happened without the images and
guilt swallowing her whole. At the same time, she was most afraid that if she let herself soften
even slightly, the danger would rush back in.
