Amina had lived in the United States for decades, but at 63 she still carried herself as if her body
expected interruption. She worked as a financial analyst, a job that kept her at a desk in a quiet
office tower downtown, yet even there she found herself scanning for exits, listening for footsteps
in the hall, and bracing when a supervisor stopped by unexpectedly. The original fear belonged to
her years as a refugee, when she was forced out of her home and made to follow orders from men with
rifles and uniforms she could not question. She rarely spoke about the journey in full, but she
could still describe one stretch of countryside in sensory detail: the dry heat, the scrubby trees,
the narrow road, and the moment police arrived at a checkpoint and began barking instructions that
left no room for refusal. What lodged in her most was not only the danger, but the humiliation of
being made to do things against her will, as though her own judgment had been erased. Since then,
the sight of police uniforms, a request delivered in a commanding tone, or any situation that felt
like being forced into compliance would jolt her back into that same powerless state. Even a visit
to a park near her apartment could unsettle her if the path curved into a wooded area that reminded
her of the landscape around the checkpoint, where she had once had to keep walking while frightened
she would be separated from the people she was with.

In the years after resettlement, Amina learned to protect herself by shrinking her world. She came
to believe that disagreement was dangerous, that if she challenged anyone—an employer, a landlord, a
neighbor, even a clerk at the store—things might escalate into the kind of confrontation she could
not control. So she avoided conflict with almost mathematical care: she said yes when she wanted to
say no, let small injustices pass, and rehearsed polite answers in her head before every phone call.
She also stayed away from authority figures whenever possible, choosing side streets if she saw
police cars, leaving buildings if a uniformed guard was stationed at the door, and asking a cousin
to handle any errand that might require speaking to officials. Those habits had their own cost. At
work, she let a younger colleague take credit for a mistake rather than risk a tense conversation,
and at home she was often alone because she had no spouse and had trained herself not to burden
friends with problems she thought should be handled privately. Yet what kept the pattern alive was
her constant worry that something terrible might happen to the people she cared about if she were
not vigilant enough. She phoned relatives repeatedly, imagined accidents when they did not answer
quickly, and read every delayed text as a sign that harm had already begun. That obsessive fear made
her even more determined to keep the peace and keep authority at a distance, because any hint of
challenge or exposure felt like inviting danger into the lives of the people she loved.

The trouble was that her protective habits narrowed the very world that set them in motion. When she
skipped a family gathering because she heard there would be police present outside the venue, or
turned down a walk with friends because the trail ran through a natural area that looked too much
like the place of her old ordeal, she felt brief relief but then more uncertainty, more time to
imagine the worst happening elsewhere. The less she engaged, the more her mind filled the empty
space with catastrophic images of harm to her siblings, nieces, and old friends. If a nephew was
late returning a call, she was certain something dreadful had happened; if she heard sirens near the
office, she became preoccupied with whether someone she loved was in danger. That worry fed back
into her caution, making any possible disagreement or encounter with authority feel even less worth
the risk. The cycle had become a daily routine: monitor, avoid, reassure herself, then monitor
again. She noticed it most at work, where a routine compliance review or a visit from security could
send her into silence, her attention fixed on the doorway, while in the evenings she sat at home
replaying every slight family conversation for signs that she had failed to keep everyone safe. By
the time she sought help, her distress had become hard to manage. Her sleep was poor, her
concentration at work was slipping, and she was exhausted by the need to stay on guard against
uniforms, commands, arguments, and the terrible possibility that if she let down her vigilance,
someone she loved would pay for it. What finally brought Amina to treatment was the realization that
her world had become so small she could no longer tolerate living inside it, and what she most hoped
for was the chance to feel safe enough to stop fearing both authority and the harm she imagined
waiting just beyond the next call or conversation.
