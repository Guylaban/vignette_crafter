treatment after years of living as though danger might be waiting in any ordinary room. Her distress
traced back to the period when her former partner repeatedly assaulted her in their home, often in

did not describe the trauma as one single scene so much as a set of broken flashes: the glint of a
blade on the counter, the sound of a door slamming, her own body bracing before she had time to
understand what was happening. Even now, when she tried to remember, the event came back in
disconnected pieces that would not join together, and at times it felt to her like looking at
photographs laid out on a table—separate images of fear, impact, and shock rather than a coherent
story. What frightened Mei most was that remembering did not feel like remembering at all; the same
terror rushed back into her body, and she had come to believe that she had permanently changed for
the worse. In her mind, the violence did not belong to the past in a settled way. It proved
something about the world and about her own place in it: that you can never know who will harm you,
and that she was now permanently an outsider, no longer someone who moved through life with the ease
other people seemed to have.

That belief made her revisit the memories again and again, trying to make sense of what had happened
and why she had stayed so long, but each attempt only seemed to pull up the same jagged fragments
and the same sense of humiliation. The more she turned the memories over, the more fixed her
conviction became that she was damaged in a lasting way. She noticed that she was not the same
person she had been before the abuse, and every failed effort to organize the experience into a
tolerable narrative seemed to confirm her fear that the change was permanent. In turn, that sense of
being altered and set apart made the memory feel even more charged, as if she were standing outside
her own life looking in at a frightening scene she could never fully leave behind.

In response, Mei developed a relentless habit of scanning. At home and especially when she left for
tutoring sessions, she checked doorways, reflections in shop windows, and the spaces behind her in
mirrors. She watched people’s hands, watched for sudden movements, and noticed potential weapons or
dangerous objects in any environment—a kitchen knife on a student’s counter, a metal ruler on a
desk, a heavy mug left near the edge of a table. As a tutor, she found herself doing this in
clients’ homes and in the quiet common room where she sometimes met students, taking in exits before
she could even settle into conversation. Her body often reacted to danger before she had had time to
think, a tightening in her chest or a jolt of muscle tension that came the moment she saw someone
step too close or heard a voice rise. These habits were meant to keep her safe, and for a while they
seemed to make sense to her. Yet the more she checked, the more everywhere felt dangerous. Each scan
seemed to reinforce her sense that threat could appear anywhere, and the world gradually began to
organize itself around potential harm. She became superalert and watchful, always on guard, and the
constant vigilance made it harder for her to relax enough to disbelieve what she feared.

The memory itself kept feeding this circle. Certain sights or moments—a flash of metal in a sink, a
man standing too close in a narrow hallway, the angle of a mirror catching movement behind her—would
drag her back into those disconnected fragments and make the terror feel immediate again. Afterward
she would scan more intensely, checking and rechecking her surroundings, because the memories seemed
to prove that danger was still close. Over time, the checking narrowed her life. She avoided
lingering in unfamiliar rooms, sat facing entrances when she could, and found herself less willing
to take on tutoring work in homes she did not know. The habits drained her concentration, strained
her marriage with her husband, who did not understand why she appeared so tense over ordinary
things, and left her exhausted by the effort of staying alert. By the time she sought help, Mei was
no longer only afraid of what had happened; she was afraid of how completely her mind and body still
expected it to happen again. She wanted relief from the terror that rushed back with every fragment
of memory, and she hoped to reclaim a sense of safety that would let her live and work without
constantly searching the world for the next sign of harm.
