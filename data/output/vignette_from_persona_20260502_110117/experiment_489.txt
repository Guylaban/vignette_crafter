timelines straight, remembered everyone’s deadlines, and moved through meetings with quiet
authority. Since surviving a life-threatening illness two years earlier, she had come to feel that
her body no longer belonged to her in the way it once had. The illness had begun with a sudden
collapse at home, followed by ambulance lights, a tangle of drips and needles, and days in which she
was being examined, scanned, and handled by strangers while she was too frightened and too weak to
make sense of what was happening. Even now, when the memory returned, it did not feel like something
recalled from the past; it arrived all at once, across her senses, with the plastic smell of the
ward, the pressure of the bed beneath her, the cold in her arm where the line had been inserted, and
the same helpless position of her body as when she had feared she might die. She could not put the
experience into words easily, and when family asked what it had been like, she would trail off,
ashamed of what had happened to her body and unable to explain the way it had seemed to betray her.

That sense of bodily betrayal sat at the centre of how Mereana made sense of everything that
followed. She was certain that her life had been destroyed by the trauma, and the belief hardened
each time she noticed fatigue, a twinge, or any ordinary bodily sensation that might once have
passed without comment. What frightened her most was the thought that her body was unreliable, that
it could fail without warning again, and that she would not be able to stop it. Because of that,
certain conversation topics became loaded with danger. At work, if colleagues in a planning meeting
started discussing hospital admissions, test results, or someone’s surgery, she felt her attention
snap away from the spreadsheet in front of her and toward the possibility that illness could be
spoken of too casually, as if what happened to her might happen again in the middle of an ordinary
day. Her concentration would break, and the room could feel suddenly too close. In those moments she
noticed herself scanning for signs of threat and becoming irritable, snapping more quickly than she
used to with staff or, at home, with her husband when he tried to reassure her. The same thing
happened when medical equipment came into view, even in harmless settings: a drip line on
television, a packet of syringes in a pharmacy display, or the sight of needles during a flu clinic
downstairs from her office. Her body would tense before she had time to think, and if someone
touched her unexpectedly she reacted with alarm, jerking away as though the touch itself might be
the first step toward being taken over again.

As the months passed, those reactions fed the memories back in. Mereana found that once she became
startled or agitated, the ward scenes returned with greater force, as if her mind was pulled
straight back into the bed and the monitors and the helpless waiting. Then the memory itself
intensified her threat response: she would feel on edge, short-tempered, and unable to stay with
what she was doing, and that state in turn made the old images easier to trigger again. In the same
way, the more she believed her body was damaged and untrustworthy, the more she noticed anything
that might confirm it. A routine medical examination became almost unbearable because the
examination table, the clinician’s gloves, and the expectation of being looked at all seemed to
promise a repeat of the original ordeal. Her shame about what had happened to her body also
sharpened these moments. She had not expected to feel embarrassed by surviving, but she did; she
felt exposed and diminished, and that feeling made her withdraw from conversations that might lead
to questions about her health. She stopped lingering after meetings, avoided lunches when people
talked about their own appointments, and found excuses to leave the office early if a colleague
described a procedure or illness story in too much detail. The less she spoke, the less able she
became to put the trauma into words, and the more the memory remained a vivid, wordless body
experience that could be reawakened by a stray sentence or the sight of a needle.

By the time she sought help, Mereana had become tired of living on alert. She had noticed that the
irritability was affecting her marriage and her work, and that her concentration was slipping at the
very tasks she once managed with ease. She came in saying she wanted to understand why, after all
this time, a brief conversation about a medical procedure or an unexpected touch could still make
her feel as if she were back in the hospital bed, helpless and ashamed. What she hoped for was some
way to trust her body again, or at least to stop feeling that the illness had taken over the rest of
her life; what she feared was that the memory would keep returning with the same force, and that
every reminder would continue to pull her into the same alarm, anger, and despair.
