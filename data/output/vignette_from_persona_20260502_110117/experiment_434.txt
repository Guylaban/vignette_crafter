assault that had occurred on duty and left her feeling as though her own body and mind had become
unreliable witnesses. During a late-night call, she and her partner were met by police at the scene
of a disturbance, and in the confusion of uniforms, shouted commands, and hands grabbing at her
while she was trying to move a patient, one officer turned his aggression toward her. What happened
next never settled into a clean sequence for her. When she tried to tell the story, she lost the
thread halfway through and could not finish, circling back to fragments—a shove, the sting of being
grabbed before she was ready, the metallic nausea that rose in her throat, a flash of a face in
uniform—without being able to place them in order. She said the memory did not feel like something
in the past at all; it came back with the force of something still happening, and that sense of
immediacy made even ordinary reminders feel dangerous. A sudden touch from behind in the ambulance
bay, a hand on her shoulder from a coworker, or the smell of stale sweat and adrenaline on a
stressful shift could pull her back into that night so quickly that she would go pale and nauseated
before she had time to think.

What frightened Marisa most was not only the memory itself but the feeling that it could erupt
without warning into the present. Because the event remained so disorganized in her mind, she was
left with a constant sense that danger might be hiding in the next moment, especially when she was
around anyone in authority or in uniform. She began to watch people before they reached her,
stiffening whenever someone moved too close, and she reacted with clear alarm when touched
unexpectedly, even by a familiar colleague. At work she started choosing her seat obsessively,
always looking for the place that felt most protected—against a wall, near an exit, never with her
back exposed. She found herself scanning the station, the hospital corridors, and the back of the
ambulance for signs of threat, her attention pulled to badges, posture, and the tone of a voice
before she could focus on anything else. The more vividly the memory broke through, the more
convinced she became that she had to stay on guard, and that vigilance began to spill into almost
every part of her day.

Marisa coped by trying to keep herself tightly controlled. She had come to believe that if she
allowed herself to feel anger about what had happened, it would spill out and make her vulnerable or
unprofessional, so she never allowed herself to feel or express it. She avoided anything that
reminded her of the person who had harmed her, including assignments that brought her near police
vehicles or scenes involving officers, and she tried to keep distance from authority figures
whenever she could. These efforts brought some short-lived relief, but they also kept the event
sealed off in a way that made it harder to sort out. When she tried to speak about it, she became
even more tangled, as though holding herself back from anger and from the offending reminders
stripped her of words as well as emotion. The more she pushed the material away, the more blurred
and incomplete it became, and the more she doubted her own account of what had happened.

Over time, the cycle narrowed her world. Stress on shift became a trigger in itself, because tense
calls, raised voices, and urgent decisions made her body feel ready for another sudden violation.
Nausea would rise before she could name why, and that physical warning would send her back into
scanning and seating herself in the safest possible place, while also making her work around
officers and other authority figures feel increasingly intolerable. She avoided some scenes
altogether when she could, traded tasks with coworkers, and kept conversations brief, especially if
they touched on the assault. At home, after her divorce, the apartment grew quieter and smaller
around her habits of vigilance, and she noticed that her whole day seemed organized around not being
caught off guard. By the time she sought help, Marisa was exhausted by how much effort it took to
keep herself contained and alert. She said she was finally coming in because she could not keep
living as if every touch, every uniform, and every surge of stress meant she was about to be
attacked again, and what she most hoped for was to be able to remember what happened without losing
herself in it.
