steady eye for color, fabric, and texture, but after the night of the police violence that brought

incident in fragments: a heated street confrontation near her home, officers arriving in uniform,
hands on her arms, the sharp tug of a rough cuff against her wrist, the press of bodies, the
flashing lights, and then blank spaces she could not fill no matter how hard she tried. When she
attempted to tell her daughter what had happened, she would stop mid-sentence because she knew what
happened but could not find the words to hold it together. The memory came back as disconnected
pieces that did not fit: the texture of the sleeve against her skin, the sight of dark uniforms, a
burst of shouting, the feeling of being pinned, and then nothing. From the beginning, she had come
to believe that if she could not even make sense of what had happened, she must be too fragile to
cope with anything at all. The more she stared at those broken pieces, the more certain she became
that nothing good could happen to her anymore, that her body had turned against her and would react
before she had time to think. That conviction made the event feel ongoing rather than past, as if
she were still trapped in it.

In the weeks that followed, anything that resembled those moments pulled her back into the same
fear. She lived in a busy apartment building where police occasionally came through the lobby, and
the sight of a uniform in the entrance made her shoulders tighten before she could stop herself. A
particular coarse fabric, the kind used in heavy work shirts and some police gear, could set off a
wave of dread if she touched it while shopping for upholstery samples in her studio. At first she
tried to push through it because she was still taking small design jobs, but each encounter left her
feeling humiliated by how quickly her body responded, her heart racing and her hands going cold. She
took those reactions as proof that she could not deal with even the slightest upset. What frightened
her most was not only the immediate surge of fear, but the meaning she gave it: if a uniform or
rough texture could still shake her so badly, then she must be living in a world where danger could
return at any moment. That belief made the police presence itself more potent, and even a passing
officer outside her building or on the sidewalk could leave her bracing, watching, and unable to
settle.

As her sense of being unable to cope grew, her memory of the event became even harder to gather into
words. When she tried to explain it, she found herself circling the same few images and then losing
the thread. She would say that there were “pieces” she knew were real but could not connect, and the
more distressed she felt, the more the gaps seemed to widen. This left her more dependent on the
fragments that were easiest to recall: the uniform, the grip, the texture, the feeling of being
overpowered. Those scraps in turn sharpened her alarm when she encountered anything similar in daily
life. At her design office, she began avoiding certain fabric swatches and asked colleagues to
handle deliveries if there was any chance a security officer or police presence might be involved.

the train to client meetings, declined evening appointments, and let promising projects slip away
because she could not bear the thought of an unexpected encounter. Friends noticed she was quieter

the same state of alarm, and that reaction only confirmed the grim belief that nothing good could
happen to her anymore.

By the time she sought help, Anjali was exhausted by the constant vigilance and by the shame of
living as if the event were still unfolding. She could no longer ignore how often the broken
memories, the fears about her body, and the sight of police uniforms were feeding one another. A

helplessness would send her mind back to the same ungraspable fragments, which then made the

hoped for relief from the body she no longer trusted, and she feared that if this continued she
would shrink her life down until there was almost nothing left of the work and independence she had
