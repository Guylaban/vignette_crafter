Mai had been driving home from her shift at the salon one rainy evening, tired and thinking only
about getting off the road before dark, when the car in front of her suddenly braked hard and
swerved. In the confusion of the wet intersection, she struck another vehicle. No one had told her,
in those first seconds, just how badly the other driver was hurt, but she saw enough to know the
crash was serious: the twisted angle of the car, the motionless figure, the shouting, the flashing
lights. Afterward, what stayed with her were not words but pictures. When she tried to remember the
scene, it came back in sharp images — the impact, the windshield spidering, the other car crumpling
— and those images often arrived without warning as unwanted thoughts when she was at work, standing
at the sink washing colour from a client’s hair or reaching for clips. Loud noises had become
especially potent because they snapped her back to that intersection: a dropped dryer, a slammed
station door, even a backfiring car outside could pull up the crash in an instant. In those moments
she felt her body react before she could think, her heart hammering and her chest tightening, and
she would scan for exits as soon as she entered any room or building, measuring the distance to
doors in the salon, in shops, in restaurants, anywhere she might need to get away quickly. She had
come to believe she could not relax even when she knew she was in a safe place, because safety had
once failed so abruptly.

The memory itself was not steady. Sometimes Mai could talk through what happened in a flat, almost
numb way, as if she were describing someone else’s life, but if she lingered too long on the
details, overwhelming grief broke through. She would picture the other car and feel the full weight
of what she had caused, then the thought would turn immediately into the conviction that she should
have protected others and failed. That sense of failure sat alongside a deeper fear that she could
no longer trust her own body, that the same hands that now carefully sectioned hair or mixed colour
could also make one catastrophic mistake. She also found it hard to rely on other people; even her
boyfriend, who wanted to reassure her, could not undo the feeling that if she let her guard down, no
one would catch the consequences for her. Her memory of the event and her appraisal of it fed each
other: the more those pictures returned, the more certain she became that she had been careless and
dangerous, and the more her grief settled into shame.

Over the weeks and months that followed, Mai’s life began to narrow around those beliefs and the
constant sense that something terrible could happen again. Because she was so on edge, she avoided
driving altogether, and after a while she even stopped accepting rides as a passenger. She arranged
her days to keep away from anniversaries connected to the crash, making excuses to miss the date and
planning extra-long shifts around it if she could. At the salon, she became noticeably more
compliant, saying yes to extra tasks, laughing off clients’ complaints, and apologizing quickly if
anyone sounded displeased, as if being easy to please might keep trouble from finding her. But those
strategies did not really quiet her mind. Not driving meant she was no longer being confronted by
the road and the kind of sudden images and noise that came with it, yet it also left her more
convinced that the world outside was dangerous and that she was incapable of handling it. The more
she organized her life to avoid reminders, the more the crash stood out in her mind as something
still unfinished, still waiting to break in through any unexpected sound or stray thought. She
started noticing triggers everywhere: the squeal of brakes outside the salon, a car image on a phone
screen, even the sound of two vehicles passing too closely in the street. Each time, she felt the
same surge of alarm and the same need to get away, and each time the old conviction returned that
she was unfit to trust herself, unworthy of trust from others, and forever one mistake away from
harming someone again.

By the time she sought help, Mai was exhausted by the constant vigilance and by how much of her life
had been built around fear. She came in because she no longer wanted to be trapped by the crash
every time a door slammed, a date approached, or a car passed outside the salon window. What she
feared most was that the images, the grief, and the certainty of her own failure would never loosen
their grip; what she hoped for was a way to be in a room, on a street, or eventually back in a car
without feeling that disaster was already waiting there.
