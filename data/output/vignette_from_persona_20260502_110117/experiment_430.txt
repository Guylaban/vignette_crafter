Ravi, a 50-year-old South Asian animator, came to therapy after a year in which sleep, work, and
family life had narrowed around the memory of his son’s medical crisis. The ordeal began when his
child developed a sudden, life-threatening illness and was rushed into the hospital, where Ravi
spent long nights sitting beside a monitor, watching nurses move quickly through procedures he did
not understand and breathing in the sharp antiseptic smell that now seemed to him inseparable from
danger. What frightened him most was not only how close his son came to dying, but how helpless he
felt while strangers performed examinations and interventions that determined whether his child
lived. Since then, the most ordinary reminders of that period had taken on a force he could not
shake: an unfamiliar bed when traveling for animation work, the silence of sleeping alone when his
wife was away, the sterile odor of a clinic corridor, or the sight of a medical exam room door left
half-open could pull him back into the same dread.

In the months after his son recovered, Ravi found that these moments did not simply unsettle him;
they pushed him into a pattern of mental strain he described as trying to stay one step ahead of
catastrophe. If he had to sleep alone or in a hotel room for a work trip, he would lie awake
rehearsing every possible emergency, turning over worst-case scenarios in his mind until he felt
vaguely prepared, as if imagining the disaster in advance might make it less shocking. When a family
member mentioned a checkup or when he had to accompany his child to a follow-up visit, the medical
procedures themselves became difficult to bear, and afterward he would spend hours thinking about
how powerless he had been and even ruminating about revenge against the illness, as though there
were someone to punish for what had nearly happened. At the same time, he repeatedly asked his wife,
his siblings, and even colleagues to reassure him that he was okay, that his son was okay, that
nothing hidden was about to go wrong. These habits gave him momentary relief, but they also kept the
hospital nights alive in his mind, because each round of reassurance or mental rehearsal pulled him
back to the same fearful scenes and made the old triggers feel even closer.

Over time, Ravi began organizing his days around avoiding anything that might set that process off
again. He declined assignments that would require overnight travel, because sleeping alone in an
unfamiliar place felt too similar to those nights in the hospital when he had sat awake waiting for
news. He became increasingly tense around clinics and would hurry his family through hallways that
smelled of antiseptic, sometimes turning his face away as if that could keep the memories from
landing. Even at home, the cycle continued: a single conversation about a doctor’s appointment could
send him back into worst-case rehearsal, which left him needing more reassurance and more certainty
than his family could give. The more he tried to prepare himself by mentally replaying threats or
asking others to calm him, the more he seemed to notice potential dangers everywhere, and the more
any reminder of the hospital threatened to seize his attention.

His wife had started to notice how much of his emotional energy was being consumed by these loops.
Ravi, once easygoing and absorbed in his drawings, now checked and rechecked plans, avoided being
alone at night when he could, and carried a low-level fear that something disastrous could return
without warning. He remained especially vulnerable to the combination of unfamiliar sleeping
arrangements and medical reminders, because both carried him back to the helplessness he felt when
his son was critically ill. After another business trip in which he barely slept in a hotel room and
spent the night mentally running through emergencies, he finally sought help, saying he was
exhausted by living as if he had to brace for the next hospital call at all times. What he most
feared was being caught unprepared if his son became ill again; what he most hoped for was to sleep
without scanning for danger, and to sit in a doctor’s office or a strange room without feeling that
the old crisis was about to begin again.
