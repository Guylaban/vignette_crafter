months of feeling as though his body no longer belonged to him. The problem began the previous
winter, when what started as a “bad flu” quickly became a life-threatening respiratory illness that
landed him in the hospital on oxygen, frightened and exhausted, watching nurses come and go with
drips, blood draws, and hurried examinations he could not escape. What stayed with him most was the
breathlessness: the awful sense that he was suffocating while trying to stay still and cooperate
with the staff. Even now, the memory came back in flashes whenever he thought about the hospital
bed, the mask, or the sight of a needle taped to an arm. Those moments carried such force because
they were tied to the feeling that his breathing had failed him and that the people trying to help
were also the people asking him to submit to more procedures.

After discharge, Daniel did not feel relieved for long. He described his body as constantly tense,
“like it’s waiting for the next hit,” and he was acutely aware of every twinge in his chest or
change in his breathing. Strong fear, horror, anger, and a sharp sense of shame appeared with little
warning, especially when he noticed himself becoming short of breath walking up stairs or when his
heart raced for no obvious reason. He was certain that these reactions meant something was still
seriously wrong, and the intensity of the feelings made him feel even less able to trust his body.
In response, he started to organize his life around keeping control in any way he could. He
restricted his eating strictly, telling himself that if he could master food intake and keep his
weight steady, at least he would not be adding “one more variable” to a body that had already
betrayed him. He also stopped exercising, even though he had once gone to the gym several times a
week, because getting his breathing up at all felt dangerously close to the suffocation he
remembered. And he began avoiding medical cues with determination: he would not drive past the
hospital where he had been admitted, refused to let himself watch medical scenes on television, and
made excuses to skip routine physical examinations. At work, where he spent long hours at a desk
maintaining databases, he arranged his day so that he would not have to accompany colleagues to the
clinic or handle any paperwork involving health services, and he became wary of the ambulance
station near his office building because even seeing the vehicles parked there could unsettle him.

Those efforts gave Daniel a temporary sense of control, but they narrowed his world and fed back
into the same fear he was trying to master. Because he was cutting down food and avoiding exercise,
his body felt weaker and more unfamiliar; ordinary exertion made him notice his breathing more,
which was enough to send him back into alarm. A short walk from the parking lot to his office could
leave him aware of a tight chest or a faster pulse, and once that happened he would monitor himself
obsessively, convinced he needed to catch any sign of trouble early. The more he scanned for danger,
the more often he found sensations that seemed to confirm it. He began to interpret these bodily
changes as evidence that he was fragile and at risk, which deepened the fear, shame, and anger he
already carried. In turn, those strong negative emotions kept his body feeling wound up and ready to
react, and he would notice it in the tautness of his shoulders, the clench in his stomach, and the
jolt of physical reactivity whenever he was unexpectedly reminded of the illness.

Over the months, Daniel’s avoidance became part of the structure of his days. He skipped workouts he
had once enjoyed, declined invitations that might involve walking or outdoor activity, and planned
routes that kept him far from hospitals and clinics. At the office, if a coworker mentioned a
medical appointment or if a delivery person rolled a cart with supplies through the lobby, he felt a
brief spike of distress and tried to distract himself by burying himself in code. Yet even that
strategy backfired, because the more he kept himself from the places and activities that stirred his
fear, the less confident he felt in his own capacity to tolerate those sensations. He had come to
believe that staying away was the only reason he was still functioning, but it also left him
increasingly preoccupied with what his body might do next. By the time he sought help, the divorce
had already been strained by his withdrawal and irritability, and he described himself as tired of
living “like a hostage to my breathing.” What brought him in now was the realization that he could
no longer keep shrinking his life around the illness. He was afraid that one strong sensation of
breathlessness, one glimpse of a needle or hospital bed, would send him spiraling again, but he was
also hoping to regain enough trust in his body to eat normally, move again, and stop living as if
danger were still waiting in every inhale.
