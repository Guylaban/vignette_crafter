chest tighten as if the scene had reached up and taken hold of him again. He had come to believe
that if the images were so clear, so insistently available, then the danger must still be near
enough to matter.

In the months afterward, Daniel began shaping his life around that certainty. He stopped seeing
friends as often, telling himself he was busy, though the deeper reason was that social meals, bars,
and casual gatherings carried the risk of an alcohol smell that could abruptly drag him back to the
night of the disaster. At restaurants or work functions, he would choose a seat with his back
against the wall so he could see the room, and once inside any space he found himself constantly
scanning doorways, reflections, and the faces of strangers for signs that something was about to go
wrong. These habits made him feel momentarily safer, yet they also kept the event close at hand;
every careful survey of the room seemed to confirm that he was right to stay guarded, and the more
he arranged his body and attention around escape routes, the more the memory sharpened. He found
that the frightening event was most likely to surge into mind when he was not wanting it to,
especially after a poor night’s sleep or after a nightmare that left him waking with the scene still
clinging to him. In those half-dazed mornings, the images came back with a force that made him
replay them, and each replay seemed to deepen their clarity.

feel as if it required more vigilance, and the more he watched and listened, the more his mind
stayed tuned to danger. The same scanning that helped him keep watch also fed the memory; each time

prove that the danger might return at any moment.

finally sought help when the nights became harder to bear—when he woke from nightmares already on

his mind would keep forcing him back into it.
