wetlands restoration team, came to treatment months after her younger brother, Daniel, died suddenly
in a car crash on his way home from work. The call came late at night from her mother, but by the
time Maya reached the hospital Daniel was already gone, and the image that stayed with her was not a
full scene but fragments: the bright corridor, the nurse’s mouth moving, her mother folding in on
herself in the waiting room. In the weeks that followed, Maya could not make the loss feel finished.
When she tried to tell people about it, she said she knew what had happened but could not find the
words, and the memory would cut in so sharply that it felt as though it were happening again rather
than something over. If a message from her mother was delayed, or if her supervisor took longer than
expected to reply, she felt a surge of alarm in her chest and assumed something terrible was
unfolding. At those moments her body reacted before her mind could catch up: her heart would pound,
her stomach would clench, and she would sit frozen, certain that another call would bring bad news.

What made the death especially hard for Maya was what she came to believe about it. She was haunted
by the thought that somebody else would have stopped it, that a different person would have made
Daniel leave earlier, insisted on a safer route, or somehow prevented the crash. She also turned the
blame inward, convinced that the event had happened to her because of the sort of person she was —
too distracted, too controlling, never protective enough. The injustice of it felt unbearable to
her, and she repeated to herself that what had happened was deeply unfair and that nothing had been
done about it. Those beliefs fed each other: because she saw herself as somehow at fault and
imagined someone else could have prevented it, she became angry, ashamed, and restless, and she
started picking arguments with friends and creating drama in small ways just to get away from the

articles about fatal accidents, and she changed the channel whenever a news story sounded similar,
fearing that one image might pull her back into the hospital corridor.
