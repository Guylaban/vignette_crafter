how quickly her mind could be pulled back into that scene. She would be standing in her tailoring

though the men were just outside the room.

That sense of danger had become the organizing fact of her days. Ayesha said she scanned exits as
soon as she entered any room or building, not because she wanted to, but because she felt she needed
to know where she could run if something went wrong. In her shop, she positioned herself near the
doorway and noticed every movement in the street outside. In clinic waiting rooms, she sat rigidly,
eyes moving from one door to another, counting people, memorizing paths, watching for anyone who
might come in behind her. She was superalert and watchful on guard, and the effort of keeping
herself prepared left her exhausted by evening. Even at home with her husband, she listened for
unexpected sounds and slept lightly, as if her body had learned that rest made her vulnerable.

Over time, this constant sense of threat shaped what she allowed herself to do. Ayesha had come to
believe that if she let her guard down, she would be caught unprepared again. That belief pushed her
toward a narrow, protective routine: she avoided emotional stress, especially arguments, frightening
stories, or anything that stirred strong feelings, because she feared that once her emotions rose,
the memories would become harder to hold back. She also avoided relaxing because it felt dangerous
to her, almost like inviting something bad to happen while she was not paying attention. If her
husband suggested that she sit with tea on the balcony after dinner, she would refuse and instead
keep busy folding clothes or checking locks, telling herself that staying active helped her stay
safe. The calmer she tried to become, the more exposed she felt, so she worked to keep herself tense
and occupied.

That same fear had kept her from seeking medical care and psychological help for a long time. After
the torture, she had not gone to a doctor for the lingering pain in her body, and later she delayed
coming for therapy because she worried that talking about what happened would make the images more
vivid. She said she did not trust the relief that other people offered, because the moments when she
felt briefly at ease had often been the moments when a flashback arrived without warning. In her
mind, symptoms and danger had become linked: a wave of memory, a rush of fear, then the need to pull
back from people, from emotion, from stillness itself. Her husband noticed that she had grown more
distant and that even small social gatherings left her depleted, but Ayesha preferred to stay on the
edge of the room rather than risk feeling overwhelmed.

appointments made her hyperaware and scanning. She had begun leaving the shop earlier than before,
not wanting to be there after dark when the street outside felt less predictable. She was distressed

this state, unable to trust her own body or let herself rest. She hoped treatment might help her
feel safe enough to stop watching every doorway, but she was equally afraid that if she lowered her
guard, the memories would return with the same force as before.
