upper racks, and sent everyone ducking for cover as sirens, shouting, and the smell of smoke flooded
the loading bay. Mariana remembered the instant the noise hit and the floor seemed to jump beneath
her, but she also knew there were stretches of that night she could not recover no matter how hard
she tried. She could picture fragments — a torn poster, someone screaming in Spanish, her own hands
gripping the edge of a pallet — yet large pieces remained missing. What she did remember came with a
rush of rage so sharp it startled her; she would think of the people who caused it, and her body
would tighten as if it still needed to fight them. Even now, she could not find the words to explain
the event clearly, and when asked about it she often went quiet, not because she did not know what
happened, but because the memory felt too broken and too hot to say out loud.

Afterward, Mariana began to treat the world as a dangerous place that could turn violent without
warning. She became convinced she had to be on guard all the time, and that belief shaped almost
everything she did. At work she kept her head down, avoided eye contact with coworkers and
supervisors, and stood where she could see both the warehouse doors and the rows of forklifts. When
a pallet jack clanged or a coworker came up behind her quickly, she felt immediately on edge, her
heart jumping before she could even think. Night was especially difficult; after dark the warehouse
lights and long aisles made her feel exposed, and on her commute home she would scan parking lots,
bus stops, and storefront reflections for anything out of place. She had come to believe that her
own body could not be trusted either, because it reacted before she could reason with it —
flinching, tensing, and going rigid in response to ordinary movements — and this only deepened her
sense that danger was everywhere.

Those reactions did not stay separate from the memory of the bombing; they fed it and were fed by
it. The more Mariana felt the world closing in around her, the more she reached for ways to get
through the day without fully being there. She found herself deliberately trying to leave her body
during stressful moments, blanking out while stacking boxes or standing in line at the store, as
though disconnecting might keep her safe. The more she checked her surroundings and stayed braced
for sudden movement, the more the warehouse, the street, and her apartment building seemed to
confirm that threat was constant. That constant scanning also left her less anchored when something
did happen — a shout across the loading dock, a cart rolling too fast, a door slamming — and the
shock would throw her back into pieces of the original event: the noise, the confusion, the missing
time. In those moments she would feel the old rage flare up again, but still be unable to put the
experience into words, only into body memory and fragments.

Over the months, these habits hardened into a way of living that made her feel briefly safer but
increasingly trapped. By avoiding eye contact and staying mentally far away, Mariana also found
herself more isolated from coworkers who had once tried to include her in breaks or conversation,
and she began to tell herself that no one could really understand what she had lived through.

was unsafe and her body unreliable. The more she withdrew and monitored, the more alert and
frightened she felt; the more frightened she felt, the more she withdrew. By the time she sought
help, she was exhausted by the constant strain of night vigilance, jumpiness, and the effort of
holding herself together at work. What finally brought Mariana in was the realization that she was
no longer simply remembering the bombing — she was organizing her entire life around preventing it
from happening again — and she was afraid she would stay locked in that state forever unless she
could learn how to feel safe again without needing to disappear from herself.
