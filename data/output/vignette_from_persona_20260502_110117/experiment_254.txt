came to the clinic with his wife after years of trying to keep his distress private. He described
the terrorist attack that had shattered his sense of safety as happening in a crowded civic building
during a public event, when the normal sounds of conversation, movement, and anticipation were
suddenly broken by chaos and violence. In his telling, the memory remained painfully exact: the
layout of the room, the faces, the sounds, the immediate fear on people’s faces. He said the scene
could return to him “like a film,” and that he could be walking through the quiet aisles of the
library or sitting at home and, without warning, feel the whole thing come back in full detail. What
startled him most was how often it arrived tied to ordinary moments that should have been harmless.
A remark on the radio, a discussion about current affairs, or even hearing people speak carelessly
about cruelty or disrespect could pull him straight back into that moment, because what disturbed
him most was not only the danger but the moral violation of it all. People “misbehaving,” as he put
it, had become intolerable to him, as if disorder and selfishness were the first signs that the
world could again tip into catastrophe.

Over time, Abdul’s vivid and replaying memory began shaping the way he moved through daily life. He
started avoiding things that might make him feel briefly hopeful or glad, because happiness felt to
him almost reckless, like lowering his guard. He found himself drifting off into a world of his own
in the middle of conversations, letting his mind slip away from the present whenever the
surroundings felt emotionally loaded or too open. At the library, he would take longer routes
through the building, choose quieter tasks, and keep his attention fixed on books and catalogues
rather than on people. Crowds became especially difficult. Shopping centres, community events, and
even larger gatherings connected with his church were places he began to pass up, because the
density of people, the noise, and the sense that anything could happen at once made him feel
cornered. His wife noticed that on the anniversary of the attack he became more withdrawn and
irritable, sometimes refusing even planned family outings. Those dates brought the memory back more
sharply, and the stronger the memory grew, the more he pulled away from anything that might stir
feelings, conversation, or company. He had come to believe that if he allowed himself pleasure, or
if he stayed too long among people and noise, he would be less prepared for another terrible breach.

Yet the very habits that seemed to keep him safe had also made the memory more dominant. When Abdul
shut down hopeful moments and retreated into his own private world, the attack returned with greater
force and less interruption, as if his mind had fewer places to go than back to the scene itself.
The film-like replay became easier to trigger in part because he was constantly scanning for the
kinds of things that might set it off: rude behaviour, heated topics, anniversaries, crowded spaces.
That watching made the world feel full of danger, and because he was so alert for it, small moments
could suddenly ignite the full memory without warning. A stranger speaking sharply in the
supermarket, a news discussion at the library about violence, or someone behaving thoughtlessly in a
queue could all seize him in an instant. Once that happened, the old scene was vivid again, and he
would avoid the rest of the day, withdraw from conversation, or escape into reverie just to get
through it. In that way, the memory and the avoidance had begun to feed each other, each making the
other harder to escape.

By the time he sought help, Abdul’s world had narrowed considerably. He was still going to work less
reliably than before, and his wife had begun to worry about how much of his day he spent
disconnected and how often he refused invitations that might have brought him comfort. He did not
come because he thought himself weak; he came because the pattern was tightening, and he feared that
the anniversary, the wrong conversation, or an ordinary encounter with people behaving badly would
once again pull him helplessly into the same full and detailed horror. What he most hoped for was
some way to live without bracing himself against every hint of joy or danger, and what frightened
him most was that the memory would keep claiming his present until there was little of it left.
