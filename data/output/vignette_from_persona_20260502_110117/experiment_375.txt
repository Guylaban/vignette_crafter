Marisol, a 19-year-old Latina train conductor, sought help after months of feeling as though her
body and mind never fully left an old childhood scene behind. She grew up in a home where her
father’s violence toward her mother was heard before it was understood, and the moments that still
returned were tied to sensory fragments she could not shake: the darkness of a hallway after the
lights were cut off, a pounding heart she noticed in her own chest when she hid nearby, and the
sudden crash of loud noises that had once meant furniture slammed, a door hit the wall, or voices
rose too quickly. As an adult, those same cues still carried the force of danger. When she passed
into dark spaces at work — the unlit platform edge before dawn, the narrow maintenance corridor
behind the trains — her mind went back with startling immediacy, and the memory came not as a story
from long ago but as something happening again right then. She would feel the same sick knot of
confusion about the order of events, unable to put the scenes where they belonged, and she sometimes
reported tasting the metallic bite of what she had tasted during the original violence, as if her
body recognized it before she could name it.

What frightened Marisol most was not only the memory itself but what it seemed to prove about her.
When the recollection surged up in the dark or after a loud clang in the rail yard, she would
immediately think that she could not stop bad things from happening to her, and that she should have
protected others and failed. She had come to believe, too, that the event happened because of the
way she acted as a child — that if she had been quieter, braver, or faster, the violence might have
gone differently. Those appraisals and the memory fed one another: the more she blamed herself and
felt helpless, the more vividly the scene returned, as if her mind kept re-entering it to search for
the moment she had gotten it wrong. Even now, when a train door slammed or a shouted instruction
echoed unexpectedly across the platform, the memory could snap back with the same immediate quality,
and her thought that she had somehow caused or failed to prevent it made the images feel even more
present, less like something over and done with.

Living with that sense of threat changed how Marisol moved through her days. She found it harder to
feel safe at night than during the day, and after evening shifts she would repeatedly check the
locks on her apartment door and windows, listening for any loose latch or unfamiliar sound. At work,
she stayed on high alert whenever she heard something sudden or loud, her attention narrowing to the
sound itself and to what it might mean. Those moments of vigilance did not calm her for long;
instead, they often pulled the old scenes back into her mind, and once the memories returned, she
would throw herself into coping by staying busy. She worked hard at keeping occupied with extra
tasks, taking on more errands, polishing routine chores, and filling every pause so there was less
room to think. At the same time, she began to restrict food, telling herself she was managing better
if she did not eat much, though the habit left her more drained and irritable. The more tired and on
edge she became, the more vivid the memories felt, and the more convinced she was that something
terrible could happen again at any moment.

Marisol also noticed that these strategies had begun to shape the very cues that unsettled her.
Because she was constantly scanning and staying busy, she became more attuned to her own racing
heart, and each pounding pulse could itself set off alarm, making her feel that danger was already
near. After a difficult shift, she would lie awake in the dark, replaying what might have happened
if she had done things differently as a child, and the counterfactual thinking did not bring relief;
it deepened her self-blame and sharpened her sense that she had failed. The more she tried not to
feel the memory by keeping in motion or eating less, the more she seemed to encounter the world as
threatening — a hallway in shadow, an abrupt bang from the tracks, a door that sounded heavier than
it should — each one quickly becoming another trigger. Over time, this left a concrete mark on her
life: she became less open with coworkers, avoided lingering after shifts, and felt increasingly
isolated, all while insisting to herself that constant vigilance was the only reason she was still
functioning.

What finally brought Marisol to treatment was the realization that she was no longer simply “coping”
but living in a narrow, exhausting loop. She was afraid that if she kept going this way she would
either miss something dangerous or never stop feeling as though she were back in that childhood
home, listening for blows in the dark. At the same time, she hoped that if she could understand why
loud noises, darkness, and her own racing heart still carried so much power, she might finally feel
safe enough to sleep, work, and eat without bracing for the worst.
