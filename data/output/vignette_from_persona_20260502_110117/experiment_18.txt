been helping neighbors usher children toward a basement shelter when a blast struck nearby; in the
chaos, she lost sight of an older woman who had been leaning on her arm only moments before. Asha
remembered the woman’s face, the sudden crush of bodies, and her own frozen certainty that she
should have done more. From that day, she came to believe that she had failed to protect others, and
that belief hardened into a broader judgment that she was weak, the kind of person who could not be
relied on when it mattered. Even after the war ended, she carried herself as though the danger had
followed her home. She no longer felt like the counselor she had once been, the person who could
move easily among students and parents, and instead described herself as no longer capable of doing
the things that had defined her.

Those beliefs shaped the way she lived. Asha began avoiding news reports about events similar to
what had happened to her, because even a brief image of bombed streets or crowded shelters would
leave her tense for hours, replaying the morning she could not undo. She also stopped using public
transport, telling herself she could not trust being trapped among strangers, unable to control what
might happen or how she would react if panic struck. In the same vein, she started wearing
concealing clothing, choosing loose layers and muted colors so she would not stand out or be
noticed. At work as a school counselor, where she spent her days moving between classrooms,

if being overlooked might keep her safe. These efforts made sense to her: if she looked less
noticeable, read less about violence, and stayed out of crowded buses and trains, perhaps she could
keep the world at a distance and protect herself from being overwhelmed again.
