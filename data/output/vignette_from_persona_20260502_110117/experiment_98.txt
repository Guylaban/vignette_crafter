hard enough to leave him stunned; what stayed with Jamal most was not a neat story he could tell,
but flashes of the men’s faces, the glint of the knife, his own hands dropping the bills, and the
sound of his wife’s voice in his head later asking how it had happened so fast. He said he knew what
happened, but when he tried to explain it, the words would not come; it was all “just pictures,” as
if the scene kept arriving in fragments he could not arrange into language. Those pictures replayed
like a film over and over, especially at night, and the more they returned, the more he found
himself scanning the street for people acting suspiciously and tensing whenever he caught alcohol on
someone’s breath or coat.

In the weeks that followed, Jamal noticed that criticism and correction began to sting in a new way.

vulnerable again. Those moments sent him back to the alley in his mind: the sudden threat, the

loudly near the storefront, every whiff of liquor at the end of the day pulled him back into the
