nurse, came home to his wife, and tried to keep up the steady routines that had always made him feel
useful and in control. That order cracked on an evening walk after a late shift, when two men

yanked at his bag and shoved him hard against the wall. Somchai remembered the smell of exhaust, the
harsh fluorescent light, and the shock of his shoulder and ribs striking the bricks; afterward, what
stayed with him was not a full story but a sequence of vivid pictures, almost like photographs, each
frame fixed and bright in his mind. He could see the angle of the men’s faces, the hand on his
jacket, the pavement under his shoes, and when the memory came back it brought a sharp, bodily ache
in the same places that had been bruised that night. From the beginning he was left with the sense
that there was something about him that had made it happen, that nothing good could happen to him
anymore, and that he had no future that felt secure.

day around what felt safest, choosing where to sit in restaurants, in staff rooms, and even on the
bus based on which seat gave him the best view of the door or the most protection from being
approached. At work on the ward he kept his mind occupied all the time so he would not drift back to
the mugging, but the effort of constant distraction made him tired and irritable. He checked mirrors
repeatedly, and as he left the hospital at night he scanned doorways, parked cars, and the edges of
the car park, still expecting danger to be waiting just out of sight. Because he believed something
in him had drawn the attack, he became more watchful in a way that made the world seem even less
forgiving; every shadow confirmed the feeling that he was vulnerable and that his future had already
narrowed.

scanning of the ward corridors and parking areas, and his habit of looking behind him whenever he

began to cue the same images of the wall, the hands, and the force of the shove. At night he slept
badly, waking again and again, and each broken stretch of sleep left the pictures of the assault

searched for signs of threat, the more the memory returned in those sharp visual flashes,
accompanied by the same physical pain in his shoulder and ribs. Somchai came to read these
intrusions as proof that he was damaged and that his life had already been reduced to waiting for
the next attack.

tense and vigilant, and afterward he would judge himself for being unable to put the event behind
him. That judgement fed the thought that there was something intrinsic in him that had brought this

might catch him unprepared from behind.

mind busy during a shift, but these habits had narrowed his days and strained his sense of himself

again, and the dawning dread that his children and wife were beginning to see him as permanently
changed. He hoped, with some uncertainty, that treatment might help him stop living as though the
attack were still happening, but he was most afraid that the future he had imagined had already been
taken from him.
