Amina had always thought of herself as sturdy, the kind of woman who could crawl under a sink in a
cramped apartment, haul a toolbox up three flights of stairs, and keep working until the job was
done. That certainty was shattered two years ago, when a sudden life-threatening illness sent her to
the hospital with severe breathlessness and terrifying dizziness; she remembers the antiseptic smell
in the corridor, the blur of fluorescent lights, and the panicked thought that her own body was
failing her in front of strangers. Even now, a whiff of hospital cleaner in a clinic lobby or the
first wave of lightheadedness after bending too long can pull her straight back into that fear. When
her chest feels tight on a hot afternoon or she notices herself short of breath climbing stairs at
work, she does not read it as fatigue anymore; she takes it as proof that something dangerous is
starting again, and she has come to believe she has to be especially careful because you never know
what can happen next. What frightens her most is not only the memory of being sick, but the sense
that she can no longer trust her own body to warn her in time.

After she came home, Amina’s world narrowed. She began avoiding hospitals, ambulances, and physical
examinations, even when she knew she was overdue for follow-up care. She would rather sit with pain,
push through dizziness, or dismiss breathlessness than walk into a clinic and smell antiseptic
again. The same fear kept her from seeking medical or psychological help; she told herself she could
not count on anyone to explain what was happening, and that if she let herself depend on others she
might be abandoned, misunderstood, or sent back into the same panic. She also started avoiding
physical stress. As a plumber, that meant refusing heavier jobs, asking to be assigned only the
easier callouts when she could, and turning down work that required long climbs, crouching in tight
spaces, or lifting equipment that made her heart race. At first she saw this as sensible caution, a
way to keep disaster from catching her off guard, but the narrowing of her life only fed the belief
that danger was everywhere and that she alone had to stay on guard.

The more Amina cut herself off from the places and situations that stirred those sensations, the
more convinced she became that her body truly was fragile. Each avoided appointment left her without
reassurance, and each time she backed away from a demanding job she felt momentarily safer but then
more uneasy, as if she had confirmed that even ordinary exertion was something she could not
survive. She watched herself closely for any hint of dizziness or breathlessness, and every small
bodily shift seemed to reinforce her certainty that she was unreliable from the inside out. That
distrust of her own body made her even more determined to manage everything alone, because relying
on other people felt risky and exposing. In practice, though, the strategy isolated her: she stopped
calling colleagues for help with difficult repairs, fell behind on the more physically demanding
contracts, and began losing income because the jobs she once handled without hesitation now felt
like threats. At home, the fear carried over into ordinary tasks; she moved more slowly, rested more
often, and organized her days around avoiding anything that might push her body too far.

By the time she finally came in for help, Amina was exhausted by how much effort it took to keep
herself safe. She had spent months pretending she was simply busy or careful, but the worry had
become constant and the shrinking of her work life was no longer sustainable. What brought her to
treatment was the dawning fear that if she kept going like this, she would end up unable to work at
all, yet she was still most afraid that a doctor would find something serious or that trusting
anyone again would leave her vulnerable in the same way her body had once betrayed her.
