child while trying to brace for blows. She found that she could not put the experience into words,
even when she tried to explain it in therapy, and what came with the memory most sharply was rage—an
intense, hot fury at what had been done to her and at how helpless she had once been.

That same memory made ordinary moments at her current job hard to manage. Leila worked with
vulnerable families in community offices and church basements, but she had begun avoiding certain
meetings and training days because walking into a room full of people, unsure of where the doors
were, made her feel pinned in place. At work she made a habit of choosing a chair nearest the exit
and of never allowing herself to be alone with another person, even a colleague or client she knew
well. She told herself this was simply prudence, yet it was the old memory of being trapped with an
abusive caregiver that made enclosed, uncertain situations feel intolerable. The more her body
seemed to re-enter those childhood positions during recall, the more determined she became to keep
herself physically uncornered in the present.

Leila also lived with a hair-trigger sense of danger that shaped how she moved through her days. She
described becoming irritable over small things, snapping at her adult daughter on the phone or
feeling a surge of anger when someone approached her too quickly in the ministry office. She often
reacted as if something harmful was about to happen even when nothing was wrong, and she frequently

following her. Those false alarms were not random to her; they felt like her body’s way of refusing
to be caught off guard again. At the same time, the state of constant alert made the old memory more
accessible. A slammed door, footsteps behind her, or the feeling of being watched could bring back
not a narrative but the same bodily constriction and fury, as if the past were breaking through in
fragments she could not quite speak.

always be another person nearby, but this left her feeling tense and exposed in a different way, as
though she could never fully relax. The repeated scanning, the constant readiness to bolt, and the
refusal to be alone all seemed to keep danger at bay, yet they also kept the memory alive in her

aware of her own guarded posture, and that awareness brought back the same trapped feeling and the
same furious shame she associated with childhood.

By the time she sought help, Leila was exhausted by how much of her life had been organized around

alone, on guard, and unable to trust even her own moments of safety.
