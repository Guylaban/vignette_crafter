Daniel was 19, a European American college student who worked part-time as a counselor at a youth
drop-in centre, and he described his life as having split cleanly into before and after the illness.
The change began the winter he was rushed to hospital with a sudden, life-threatening infection that
left him attached to drips, surrounded by beeping monitors, and repeatedly examined by staff in blue
gloves under the sharp smell of antiseptic. He remembered the cold of the examination room, the
sight of needles and tubing in his arm, and the helplessness of being told what was happening to his
body while he felt it slipping out of his control. Even now, walking past a clinic entrance or
catching that hospital smell in a pharmacy or at work, he would feel the moment snap back with
alarming force. The equipment itself had become charged with danger for him: a needle, a drip stand,
the snap of a glove, even a nearby ambulance siren on the street outside the centre where he worked
could pull him straight back to that bed. Those cues did more than unsettle him; they made him
physically tense, his heart racing and his body jolting as if danger were right in front of him, and
they left him convinced that his body was not something he could trust anymore.

In the weeks after discharge, Daniel began arranging his life around those reactions. He avoided
hospitals and any medical examination whenever he could, found excuses not to look at therapy
material if it touched on the illness, and developed the habit of staying up until very late so he
would not have to face sleep and the thoughts that came with it. He told himself that if he kept
busy at the drop-in centre and never let his mind settle, he could keep the memories from surfacing.
But the more he tried to push away reminders, the more alert he became to them. He would scan
corridors for the smell of antiseptic, stiffen at the movement of a staff member passing behind him,
and react to any unexpected motion nearby as if he were about to be hurt. Sleep became unreliable
too, with trouble both falling asleep and staying asleep, and the fatigue made him even more jumpy
the next day. At work, if a young person mentioned blood tests or an ambulance, he felt the familiar
surge in his chest and would quickly change the subject or step out of the room, telling himself he
could not afford to be caught off guard.

As those reactions repeated, Daniel’s thoughts about himself and his future darkened. He had come to
believe that nothing in his life felt meaningful anymore, because everything he had assumed about
strength and health had been taken away so abruptly. He no longer trusted his own body; it seemed to
him that it kept reacting as if he were still in danger even when he was safe, and that made every
sensation feel suspicious. A skipped heartbeat, a wave of nausea, or a sudden flush of heat could
become proof, in his mind, that illness was returning. That belief made medical cues feel even more
potent. The sight of a needle in a tray, the press of a blood-pressure cuff, or the smell of a
hospital corridor did not merely remind him of what happened; they now seemed to announce that his
body was fragile and that he might be sliding back toward the same terrifying loss of control. In
turn, that sharpened his urge to stay away from anything medical, to avoid therapy content that
might bring the illness into focus, and to put off sleep until exhaustion finally took him under.

The more Daniel organized his days around avoiding those reminders, the more threatened he felt.
Staying up late left him depleted and more reactive to small movements and sudden noises; lack of
rest made his startle response worse, and the stronger his physical reactions became, the more
convinced he was that something was wrong with him. He noticed that even in ordinary settings—at the
youth centre, on public transport, or in a waiting room for his partner’s appointment—his body
seemed to brace for impact. That constant alertness made the original hospital cues seem even harder
to escape, because once he was already keyed up, the smell of antiseptic or the sight of medical
equipment could hit him with overwhelming force. He found himself narrowing his world further,
ducking out of conversations, avoiding anything that might touch on treatment or illness, and
feeling ashamed that as a counselor he could help others sit with distress yet could not bear his
own memories. By the time he came for help, Daniel was frightened by how small his life had become
and how certain he was that his body would betray him again. What brought him in was the fear that
he could not keep living in this tense, sleepless state, and what he hoped for—though he scarcely
trusted it was possible—was to feel safe in his own body again and to find some meaning in a life
that no longer felt like his.
