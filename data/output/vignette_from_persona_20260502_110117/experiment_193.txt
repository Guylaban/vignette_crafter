Marilyn, a 68-year-old North American woman, came to treatment more than a year after her husband’s
sudden death, describing herself as “still living in the day it happened.” The loss had been
unexpected and violent in its impact: one evening he had been laughing with her at the kitchen
table, and within hours he was gone following an acute medical emergency that left no time for
goodbyes or preparation. In the weeks after the death, Marilyn found herself returning again and
again to the same terrible thought that she should have protected him and failed, even though she
could not point to anything she might realistically have done differently. That belief settled over
her with a sense of deep isolation, as if she had been left outside a life that other people could
still enter. She said she no longer belonged anywhere, not at family gatherings, not with neighbors,
not even in her own home, because every room reminded her of what she had lost and of what she
believed she had not managed to prevent. From that place, she began working hard at keeping busy
with other things. At first it was practical—laundry, reorganizing drawers, sorting bills—but it
gradually became a way of outrunning the thought that she had failed. She filled her retired days
with chores, errands, television, and small projects because stillness gave her too much room to
feel the shame and grief that rose whenever she thought about the night he died.

Marilyn’s sense of being set apart did not stay as a belief alone; it also sharpened the way her
body reacted. She described feeling constantly tense, as if she were braced for some new blow, and
said she could not relax even in places she knew were safe, like her own den or bedroom. The more
she kept herself occupied and away from people, the more her body seemed to stay on guard, never
fully coming down from that state of readiness. She noticed tight shoulders, a clenched jaw, and a
startle that made ordinary sounds feel abrupt and threatening. The same bodily alertness fed back
into her mind. When her heart raced or her muscles tightened, she took it as evidence that danger
was still near and that she had not really gotten through what happened. That physical tension made
her more certain that something bad could happen again, and the certainty deepened her belief that
she was alone in carrying it. What frightened her most was not only the memory of her husband’s
death, but the sense that her body was acting as if it expected another catastrophe and that she
could not trust herself to feel safe.

Over the following months, Marilyn’s efforts to stay busy and keep people at a distance became part
of the way she managed the days, yet they also kept her nervous system wound tight. She avoided
other people whenever she could, and especially avoided seeing the friends and couples she had
socialized with before the event, because their ordinary conversations made her feel more
conspicuously widowed and out of place. Her retired life shrank around the house and a few necessary
errands, with only brief contact at the grocery store or pharmacy. Each time she turned down an
invitation or stayed home instead of going out, the relief was immediate, but it never lasted. The
quiet gave her more time to notice the tension in her body, and the tension brought back the same
negative feelings—fear, horror, anger, guilt, and shame—that had first attached themselves to her
husband’s death. Those feelings made her more convinced that she was isolated and had no place among
other people, so she withdrew further, and the pattern became harder to interrupt.

By the time she sought help, Marilyn said she was exhausted by the effort of holding herself
together. She was still functioning in a narrow way, but it felt increasingly brittle, as though she
were one jarring reminder or one unexpected social contact away from becoming overwhelmed. She
wanted to stop living in this guarded, solitary way, yet she was also afraid that if she let herself
slow down, the full weight of her belief that she had failed to protect her husband would catch up
with her. What brought her in finally was the growing realization that she was not simply grieving;
she was stuck in a life organized around being tense, busy, and apart. What she most hoped for was
to feel safe enough to rest, and what she most feared was that without help she would remain
permanently outside the world she once shared with him.
