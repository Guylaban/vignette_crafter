Amina was 78 and had spent most of her life working with clay, her hands steady at the wheel in the
small pottery space behind her home. Since her husband died, she had lived alone, and the assault
that brought her to treatment had shattered what had once felt like an ordinary, familiar world. One
evening, as she was locking up after working late, a man forced his way near the doorway and struck
her during a brief struggle. What stayed with her was not a full story she could tell smoothly, but
a set of hard, vivid pictures: the man’s arm, the corner of the doorway, the sudden look of the room
as if it had narrowed around her. Afterward, the memory forced its way into her mind at odd moments,
especially when she was alone in the pottery shed or moving through the house after dark, and when
it came she felt as though it were happening right now. She described it as images rather than
words, flashes that arrived without warning, and she was certain that the world was a dangerous
place. She had come to believe that if she thought about the event for too long, she would not be
able to handle it, and that her own body kept reacting as if she were still in danger even when she
was safe.

Those beliefs quickly shaped the way Amina moved through her days. She began checking the mirrors in
the house and in the workshop more than once, wanting to see who might be behind her, and she
repeatedly checked the locks, doors, and windows before sitting down to work or going to bed. At
night she woke to make sure she was safe, listening hard for sounds that might mean someone was
entering the house. Even in the pottery room, where she had once been able to settle into the rhythm
of the clay, she could not relax; part of her stayed braced, scanning, waiting. Her fear that the
world was dangerous and that she could not trust her own body made these routines feel necessary
rather than excessive, the only way she knew to keep herself from being caught off guard again.

The memories themselves kept feeding the same pattern. When one of the images slipped in while she
was shaping a pot or carrying water, she would stop what she was doing and try hard not to think
about the assault, pushing it away with enough force that she could sometimes finish the immediate
task, but not settle. The more the scenes returned in that raw, picture-like way, the more convinced
she became that something terrible could happen again without warning, and that her body was
betraying her by reacting with the same jolt of alarm in the safety of her own yard or workshop.
That conviction made her watch the house more closely, listen harder at night, and keep checking the
locks even after she had already checked them. She found herself arranging her days around these
precautions, but the routines never brought lasting relief; instead, each new round of checking
seemed to confirm for her that the danger was still present. If she was this unsettled, she
reasoned, then the world must truly be unsafe, and if she could not keep herself calm, then perhaps
the event had done more permanent damage than she wanted to admit.

Over weeks and months, these habits narrowed her life. Amina began leaving the pottery to her
granddaughter more often, and she stopped staying late to glaze pieces because the walk back to the
house at dusk made her feel exposed. She tried to suppress thoughts of the assault whenever
customers came by or neighbors stopped to talk, but the effort left her tense and distant. By the
time she sought help, she was exhausted by waking in the night, by listening for movement, by
checking and rechecking the doors before she allowed herself to sit down. What finally brought her
in was the realization that she was living as though danger were always just outside the next wall,
and her deepest fear was that she would never feel safe in her own home again; what she hoped for,
though she said it quietly, was to be able to work with clay and sleep through the night without
bracing for the attack to return.
