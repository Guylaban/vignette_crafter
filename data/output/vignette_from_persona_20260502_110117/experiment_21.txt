Mikaere, a 63-year-old widowed construction worker of Oceania heritage, came to treatment after
years of trying to keep himself moving and unnoticed. He described a past relationship in which his
former partner repeatedly physically abused him, with the worst assaults leaving him focused on the
parts of his body that had been used against him—forearms twisted behind him, his face turned by a
blow, the shape of a hand closing around his throat. Even now, he said, a conversation that touched
on what happened could pull him straight back into that time, and being around people connected to
the relationship brought the same tight, sick feeling in his chest. He had come to dread those
reminders because they made him feel stripped back to that frightened version of himself, and he was
certain they showed he was weak, that he could not rely on himself to stay safe. Since his wife died
and the house grew quieter, he found that the deadness he carried with him was almost worse than
fear: he described feeling “dead inside,” as if the only thing left was getting through the day
without anyone seeing how small he felt.

At work on construction sites, Mikaere kept himself near the exit of whichever building or half-
finished frame he was in. He scanned doorways and stairwells the moment he entered a room, and if a
voice rose in disagreement or a crew member started arguing, he would drift away before anyone
noticed. He was especially reactive to ordinary sounds on site—a dropped wrench, a nail gun
snapping, a truck backfiring from the street—because he would jump hard and feel his body go rigid
before he had even made sense of the noise. At night he often woke and checked the locks and the
hallway, needing to confirm that he was safe before he could lie back down. These habits were not,
to him, signs of caution so much as proof that he could not trust himself; the more he found himself
fleeing tension, the more he concluded that he was the kind of man who folded under pressure. That
belief, in turn, made him avoid any situation that might involve disagreement or confrontation, even
when a foreman shortchanged him on a task or a nephew asked him a hard question, because he feared
he would freeze, embarrass himself, or lose control.

The same routines that seemed to keep him calm also kept his memories alive. Seeing a clenched jaw,
a forearm raised to point, or a hand grabbing a tool in just the wrong way could bring back the
body-memory of being grabbed and hurt, and once that happened he would need to move closer to an
exit or leave the space altogether. The need to be near a way out had become a reflex, but it also
kept his mind organized around danger; every doorway, every person standing too close, every family
member of the former partner he had once known could become a signal that sent him back into
alertness. After years of arranging his days around escape routes and silence, Mikaere began to
think there must be something fundamentally wrong with him. If he had to plan his whole life around
avoiding conflict and bolting from rooms, then he must be weak, he told himself, and that conviction
deepened the numbness until he felt detached from friends, from his late wife’s memories, and from
his own body.

To steady himself, he spent money impulsively on things he did not need—tools he already owned,
takeaway meals he barely tasted, small purchases online at night after waking and checking the
doors. For a few minutes the buying distracted him, gave him a brief sense of control or comfort,
but the relief never lasted. Afterwards he would look at his bank balance and feel ashamed, which
only confirmed his belief that he could not rely on himself. Then the old fear returned more
sharply, and with it the need to watch for sounds, exits, and people who might corner him. The cycle
narrowed his life: he declined invitations, kept conversations short, and stayed near the edge of
gatherings, all in the name of preventing another loss of control.

What finally brought Mikaere in was a recent morning on site when a coworker jokingly grabbed his
forearm to get his attention, and he nearly struck out before realizing where he was. The flash of
panic, the shame that followed, and the recognition that he was now avoiding more and more of work
and family life frightened him enough to ask for help. What he most hoped for was to stop living as
if every room contained a threat; what he feared most was that the deadness, the startle, and the
shame would keep tightening until there was nothing of himself left to trust.
