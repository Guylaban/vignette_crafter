Marilyn had always thought of herself as steady, the sort of school counselor who could sit with a
frightened child, lower her voice, and make a hallway feel safe again. After her husband died, she
kept working part-time in the same public school in North America, partly because the routine
helped, partly because she did not know who she was without the familiar rhythm of students, bell
schedules, and lunch duty. The attack happened on an evening when she was closing up her small house
alone. A group of assailants broke in during a wider man-made disaster in the neighborhood, forcing
their way past the door, shouting over one another, and moving through the rooms in a chaos that
made her feel as if the world had been turned inside out. What she remembered most was the
helplessness of being in her own home and not being able to control who entered or what they might
do. Since then, ordinary sounds in the house have carried a dangerous charge for her: a car door
slamming outside, the thud of a pipe, the scrape of a neighbor’s bin, any noise that seems like
someone entering. She listens for it constantly, and when she hears something unexpected she jumps
hard enough to spill her tea or grip the edge of the counter. Even in a safe place, she says, her
body will not settle.

In the weeks that followed, Marilyn found herself telling the story over and over in her head, but
not in any way that brought relief. She ruminated about how to get even with the assailant and the
people responsible, rehearsing imagined confrontations and fantasies of the punishment she believed
they deserved. At the same time, she began to drink more in the evenings and occasionally used
medication in a way that was not how it had been prescribed, telling herself she only wanted a few
hours of sleep without dreaming. She also became careful about where she sat, always choosing places
with a clear line to the door and making sure she could stay close to the exit. At first these
habits seemed like ways to keep herself composed, but they left her more convinced that something
terrible could happen again at any moment. The exits, the extra drink, the repeated plotting of
revenge all kept her attention circling danger, and she started to notice how quickly her body went
on alert at the smallest sign of noise. The more she organized her days around escape and vigilance,
the less able she was to trust the ordinary quiet of her home or classroom.

Marilyn had also come to believe that what happened to her was deeply unfair and that nothing had
been done about it. The lack of accountability gnawed at her, and it fused with a feeling that she
no longer knew herself. She would look in the mirror and feel as if the woman staring back was a
stranger—someone who had once been happy, patient, and useful, but now seemed miserable all the
time. That sense of being wronged and changed made her scan constantly for proof that she was still
at risk. She checked locks twice, listened from the bedroom for footsteps in the hall, and could not
relax even when her daughter stayed over and assured her the house was secure. If a student’s locker
slammed at school, she startled. If a janitor rattled keys nearby, she flinched. Each jolt seemed to
confirm the same thought: the world was still unsafe, and she herself had been permanently altered
by what had been taken from her.

In turn, her vigilance fed back into the way she saw herself. Because she was always braced for
danger, she felt more exhausted, more unlike the woman she had been, and more certain that her life
had been stolen from her. The constant monitoring and sudden startles made it hard for her to enjoy
the small pleasures she once relied on—coffee on the porch, a book after supper, talking with
colleagues after school. She began declining invitations and leaving campus before the building
emptied, telling herself she preferred quiet, though in truth she wanted to avoid hallways and doors
that reminded her of the night of the attack. Her daughter noticed that Marilyn often sat near the
apartment entrance when visiting family, eyes fixed on the corridor, as if even there she needed a
quick path out.

By the time she came for help, Marilyn was exhausted by the circle of fear, anger, and misery that
had narrowed her life. She wanted, as she put it, to stop being so easily startled and to sleep
without reaching for a drink or a pill first. More than anything, she was afraid that if she kept
living this way she would disappear into bitterness and never feel like herself again.
