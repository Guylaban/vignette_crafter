Maurice, a 65-year-old widowed project manager of Oceanian background, came to treatment after more
than a year of living as if the illness that nearly took his youngest son might return at any
moment. The original crisis had begun in a hospital room, when his son’s breathing became shallow
and frantic, and nurses hurried in and out with monitors, masks, and rushed explanations that
Maurice struggled to follow. He remembered the awful stretch of waiting beside the bed, watching his
son’s chest rise unevenly, and the helplessness of hearing medical staff discuss procedures while he
stood there feeling he had already failed. Since then, anything tied to that period could pull him
straight back into it: a relative asking about the son’s treatment, the sight of an ambulance
outside the office tower where he worked, or his own brief breathlessness when he climbed stairs and
suddenly felt his chest tighten.

What frightened Maurice most was how quickly delay itself began to feel dangerous. If a doctor was
late, if a family member did not answer the phone, if test results were taking longer than expected,
he assumed something bad was happening. Those moments hit him physically; his heart would pound, his
stomach would drop, and he would feel the same surge of horror and guilt he had felt in the hospital
corridor. He had come to believe the illness unfolded the way it did because of the way he had
acted, that he should have protected his family better, and that he could not trust himself to do
the right thing when it mattered. Those thoughts made him watchful and brittle, scanning for signs
of trouble in other people’s faces and in his own body, especially any hint of breathlessness or any
situation that resembled a medical examination.

In the months after the crisis, Maurice tried hard not to think about what had happened. He kept his
mind occupied all the time, filling every hour with work emails, property reports, errands, and
television at night so there would be no quiet space for the hospital memory to intrude. He also
avoided hospitals, ambulances, and physical examinations whenever he could, even postponing routine
appointments and asking his wife’s sister to handle family visits to medical clinics. Yet the more
he worked to push the memory away, the more certain he became that he had missed something crucial.
When he found himself distracted or unable to remember the exact sequence of events, he interpreted
that as proof that he could not trust his own judgment, and that sense of self-doubt fed back into
his belief that he had failed to keep his son safe.

Those appraisals shaped where he went and what he could bear. Maurice now avoided the hospital
district near his office and took a different route home if he saw an ambulance parked by the curb.
At work, when a meeting in his project-management role ran over time or a contractor was late to
reply, he felt a rising alarm that something was wrong and would become tense, irritable, and unable
to settle. He would excuse himself, check his phone repeatedly, or bury himself in spreadsheets to
keep from thinking about the son’s illness. Even a routine physical examination at his GP’s clinic
felt intolerable because the room, the smell of disinfectant, and the clinical attention to his
breathing could make the old scene return in full force. The bodily reactions were so strong that
they made the event feel immediate again, and that immediacy confirmed his fear that danger could
return without warning.

Over time, Maurice’s attempts to avoid reminders and keep himself constantly occupied did not bring
relief so much as deepen his sense that he had done something wrong and could never fully trust his
reactions. The more he pushed away the memories, the more unfamiliar they felt, and the more he
wondered whether forgetting details meant he had been negligent at the worst possible time. He
finally sought help when his daughter noticed that he had started missing family gatherings because
he could not tolerate talk of doctors, and when he found himself sitting in his car outside a
routine appointment, unable to make himself go in. Maurice said he wanted to stop living as though
every delay signaled another catastrophe, but he was most afraid that if he let his guard down, he
would again fail to protect the people he loved.
