understood the illness as something routine. It began with abdominal pain that seemed stubborn but
ordinary, then a sudden deterioration that left her in the hospital hearing words she had never
expected to hear about her own body: life-threatening, emergency surgery, complications. What stayed

dependent on strangers while her body betrayed her. Afterward, she could not hold the experience in
mind as a finished event. When the memory returned, it did so with full force, complete and sensory,
as if the fluorescent lights, antiseptic smell, beeping monitors, and sharp edge of pain had all
crowded back in at once. At those moments Marina was overwhelmed by grief for the version of herself
she believed had been lost.

In the months that followed, Marina came to believe that her body had been permanently damaged by
what happened, and that she would never be able to feel normal emotions again. Those beliefs shaped
the way she moved through daily life. She watched her own body for signs of weakness, convinced that
every tightening in her chest or flutter of nausea meant danger was returning, and she could not
relax even in her own apartment or in the quiet of the kitchen at work. She also struggled with the
sense that the whole event was so extreme it hardly seemed possible that it had happened to her at
all; parts of it felt unreal, as if she were recalling a story told by someone else. Yet that
distance did not protect her. The more she thought about the hospital stay, the more the images and
bodily sensations broke through, and the more certain she became that her body remained an enemy
that could turn on her without warning. Those appraisals made her scanning and alarm more intense,
so that a small wave of tiredness or a passing ache could pull her back into fear almost before she
had time to think.

That alarm soon began to shape her behavior. Marina started numbing her feelings because any
sustained attention to what she had survived seemed to crack open grief she could not bear. She
avoided situations that might make her feel happy or hopeful, because hope felt dangerous to her
now; if she allowed herself to imagine being well again, she feared the fall would be unbearable if
symptoms returned. She also began staying up until very late, stretching the day out as long as
possible, partly because sleep meant vulnerability and partly because she dreaded lying still with
her thoughts. These habits brought short-term relief, but they also left her more exhausted and
disconnected, and that emotional flatness made it harder for her to feel anchored in the present.
The more she numbed herself and pushed away anything uplifting, the more convinced she became that
something essential in her had gone missing and that she was no longer capable of ordinary feeling.
That belief fed back into her withdrawal, making the world seem smaller and less safe.

The threat reactions never stayed quiet for long. In the kitchen at work, where the heat, metallic
smells, and sudden demands already kept everyone on edge, Marina would sometimes be seized by a jolt
of certainty that danger was happening again. Her body reacted before her mind could catch up; a
change in sensation, a momentary pain, or the sight of a tray of medical-looking tubing in a
television image could make her feel as if she were back in the hospital, trapped inside the same
helplessness. At such times the memory returned not as a thought but as an embodied reliving, and
the more vivid it became, the more she concluded that her body was still damaged and unreliable.
That conviction made the alarms feel justified. If her body could betray her once, she reasoned,
then every physical cue might be the first sign of catastrophe, and every flashback or wave of panic
only confirmed that she was still in danger. In turn, she numbed herself more deeply, avoided
anything that might brighten her mood, and stayed up late to postpone the quieter hours when the
images would rise.

What finally brought Marina to seek help was the growing sense that she was no longer managing her
life so much as arranging it around fear. Her partner had begun noticing how often she seemed
distant, how little joy she allowed herself, and how frequently she startled or went rigid at
ordinary sensations. Marina was afraid that she would remain trapped in this state indefinitely,
unable to trust her own body or her own emotions, and she was beginning to worry that the grief, the
sleeplessness, and the constant internal vigilance were costing her both her relationship and her
work. What she hoped for was simpler and more fragile: to feel safe in her own skin again, and to
believe that feeling alive would not necessarily mean being hurt all over again.
