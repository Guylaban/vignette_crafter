story, the sequence broke apart in his mind. The hardest part was that the memory did not feel like
something that had happened to him; it felt strangely detached, as if it belonged to another man, a
scene he could only watch from a distance. He would catch himself seeing flashes of the room, the
hands, the concrete floor, but the order never settled. It came back like a film that played over
and over, beginning and stopping in the same frightened fragments.

In the months after he was released, Minh found that certain moments pulled him straight back into
the old fear. If someone asked him about the future—at work, about next year’s deadlines, or even
something simple like where he might live after the divorce—he felt a surge of unease that was hard
to explain to others. Talking to strangers was worse. A casual question from a new client, a phone
call from an unfamiliar number, or standing beside an unknown man in the elevator could make his
body tense before he had time to think. In those moments, he became convinced that danger could come
from anywhere, and that you could never really know who would harm you. That belief hardened quickly
into the way he saw people now, as though any unfamiliar face might conceal the same intention as
the men who had taken him.

he was forced into brief contact with strangers, Minh found himself thinking that something inside
him had been permanently changed for the worse. He did not speak about it dramatically; instead, he

who he had been before the torture—more open, less watchful, someone who had once made plans without
hesitation—and could only conclude that that version of him was gone. At times he also found himself
angrily wondering why nobody had stopped it from happening, why someone else would not have found a
way to intervene. That thought did not bring relief. It seemed to deepen his sense that he had been
left undefended, and that what happened said something bleak about how little protection a person
could count on.

the lack of sequence made the whole thing feel even less like his own past and more like an invasion
that had no beginning or end. Sometimes he would stop mid-task at his desk because a scene flashed

in a quiet, almost embarrassed way, scanning the office lobby, the parking lot, and the sidewalk
