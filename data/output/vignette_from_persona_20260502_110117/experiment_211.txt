Sister Teresa, a 55-year-old nun, came to treatment with a quiet but unmistakable strain that had
been building for years since the day a fellow sister died unexpectedly during a ministry trip. The
event had happened in a small parish house several hours from her convent, at the end of a long
evening when the group had been sharing a meal and planning the next day’s work. Teresa had stepped
away briefly to answer a call from another room, and when she returned, she found the sister
collapsed and unresponsive. In the months that followed, Teresa kept returning to that moment with a
tormenting conviction that she had not been where she should have been. She had come to believe the
death happened because of the way she acted, and that she should have protected others and failed.
The thought that she could not rely on herself at a critical moment became part of how she
understood the loss, and it made ordinary decisions feel loaded with danger.

Those beliefs gradually shaped the way Teresa lived her days in the convent and at the parish work
she still did. She began avoiding the site of the event, even when church obligations would have
taken her back there, and she found excuses not to pass the room where it had happened. Anything
that reminded her of someone she had lost became hard to tolerate, including old photographs,
anniversary Masses, and even the sound of certain prayers that had been used at the funeral. She
also started performing mental rituals to prevent bad things from happening again, silently
repeating phrases in her mind whenever a sister left the building or when she herself had to walk
alone at dusk. These habits had the feel of a desperate private duty: if she could stay away from
the place, keep grief reminders at bay, and think the right words, perhaps she would never again be
the one who failed to keep someone safe.

instantly bring back the full memory without warning, and this often happened in the everyday life
of the convent. The smell of strong coffee from the parish kitchen, a particular hallway light, or
the sight of an empty chair at supper could catch her off guard and take her straight back to that
evening. When the memory came, it was vivid and detailed: she could see the pattern of the wallpaper
in the parish house, hear the scrape of a chair, and feel the sudden stillness that followed. Yet
parts of the event remained blank, which disturbed her almost as much as what she remembered
clearly. She would strain to reconstruct the sequence and become more convinced that something vital
had been her fault. The clearer the images became, the more she felt she had missed her chance to
prevent the death; the blank spaces, instead of easing her, made her distrust her own recollection
and herself.

Over time, the ways she tried to manage the loss seemed to keep the memory active. Avoiding the site
of the event meant that when she was unexpectedly near anything resembling it, the whole scene would
rush back with even greater force. Her mental rituals, though meant to steady her, kept her
attention fixed on the possibility of another catastrophe, and each silent repetition seemed to
confirm that danger was always close. The more she withdrew from reminders of the sister she had
lost, the more the loss itself stood out in her mind as unfinished and uncontained. Teresa found
herself thinking that she had not only failed once, but had also proven she could not trust her own
judgment now. That feeling deepened the sense that she should have protected others and did not
deserve confidence in her own actions, which in turn made her cling more tightly to avoidance and
ritual.

By the time she sought help, Teresa had become increasingly restricted in both her spiritual and
practical life. She was still married to her vocation, but her world had narrowed around the fear of
being caught off guard by another vivid memory or a reminder she could not bear. What finally
brought her in was the realization that she was no longer able to move through her convent duties
without planning around the loss, and that her mind had become as much a source of dread as the
place where the death occurred. She was afraid that if she stopped her rituals, something terrible
would happen again, yet she was also afraid that continuing this way would leave her forever
convinced that she had failed. What she wanted most was some way to remember her sister without
being seized by the scene, and some assurance that she could trust herself again.
