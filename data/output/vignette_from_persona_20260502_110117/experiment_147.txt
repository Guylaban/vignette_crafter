more certain that the event had permanently altered him and that safety was something other people
got, not him. The memory was especially liable to be pulled loose by military topics; if someone at
home mentioned the army, or if a news report showed uniforms, his mind jumped straight back to those
few terrible moments. Uniforms also set off the same reaction at work, where, as a park ranger, he
sometimes encountered law enforcement or search-and-rescue personnel in similar clothing. Being seen
in those moments felt exposing, and once he was self-conscious under someone’s gaze, he would think
again that he had been foolish, weak, and uniquely unfortunate, which only made the old images more
vivid and shameful. As the months passed, the memory itself started to drive the rest of the cycle.
Keeping the memory at bay through strict routines and silence only seemed to keep it alive; the less
he spoke about it and the more tightly he managed himself, the more intact and ready to return those
snapshots felt. A sudden return of the snapshot images could bring a wave of bodily pain and make
him feel as if the captivity were happening again, and once that threat feeling rose he became even
more certain that he could not trust safety. When he was jolted by a sound or an abrupt movement on
a trail, the room or forest seemed to tilt toward the past, and that sense of danger made the
fragments of memory sharper still. The memories, in turn, fed back into his negative beliefs: each
return seemed to prove that he remained isolated, permanently humiliated, and somehow at fault.
Because he felt that way, he redoubled his efforts to keep control, eating even more strictly,
deferring to others, and fantasizing about revenge in private. But these strategies brought their
own costs. Skipping meals left him shaky and more reactive to sudden movement, which made the world
feel even less safe. Going along with everyone else left him resentful and more aware of being
unseen. Staying in those patterns without ever testing whether he could manage differently left the
original threat feeling untouched, and in his body’s accounting that meant the danger must still be
real. Revenge fantasies kept his mind circling the captivity long after work, making the images
easier to summon and the humiliation harder to shake. And once he was already keyed up, even a
uniform at the trailhead, a military comment on television, or a stranger turning too quickly on the
path could trigger the same surge of dread and pull him back into the snapshots again. By the time
he sought help, Arjun was still functioning at work but only by keeping himself tightly managed and
on edge. His partner had noticed that he was distant at home, jumpy when someone moved unexpectedly,
and increasingly unwilling to go out unless he could control the setting. What finally brought him
in was the fear that he was becoming trapped in the same private loop every day: the images, the
humiliation, the rigid control, the constant scanning. He said he was most afraid that the captivity
had permanently changed him, but he was also hoping that therapy might help him feel safe enough to
stop living as if danger were always one step away. After weeks of skipping meals and deferring to
everyone else, he came to read those habits as proof that he could not manage on his own, which only
deepened the humiliation and made him more certain that the event had changed him for good.
