Mr. Anwar Rahman, an 80-year-old widowed South Eastern Asian man who had spent much of his working
life as a data scientist, came to treatment describing a fear that had narrowed his world since his
time as a relief worker in a war zone. He could still picture one night in particular: a crowded
shelter with broken lights, shouting outside, families pressed into corners, and his team unable to
move people to safety because armed men had blocked the only road out. The sense of having no
options in that moment had stayed with him, and now even ordinary encounters could pull him back to
it. If he saw people misbehaving in a store, arguing loudly on the street, or dismissing rules as if
they did not apply to them, he felt the old alarm rise immediately. The same happened when he woke
from a nightmare in a sweat, disoriented for a few seconds, still caught between sleep and the
present. In those moments, Mr. Rahman would begin scanning the room, checking exits and windows, and
mentally rehearsing what to say before speaking to anyone, as though careful preparation could keep
the world from turning dangerous.

He described the memory itself as having an unnerving quality that made it impossible to place in
the past. When he remembered the event, he did not experience it as something he had survived and
left behind; he felt as if it was happening right now, like a film playing over and over with no
break. The sounds, the pressure in his chest, and the feeling of being trapped returned with such
force that his body reacted before he could reason with it. These memory surges were often followed
by the same conclusion: if his reactions were this intense, perhaps he was going crazy. He had
become frightened by his own anger as well, worrying that one day he would not be able to control it
and would do something terrible. That fear of himself sat alongside a broader conviction that the
world was dangerous, a place where violence and humiliation could erupt without warning. The more
his mind returned to the war-zone scene, the more certain he became that danger was not over but
waiting.

Over the months that followed, Mr. Rahman’s attempts to manage that dread made his symptoms more
entrenched. He avoided news reports about events similar to what had happened to him, telling
himself that he could not afford to let those images in. Yet when he did encounter a headline or a
broadcast, even briefly, the familiar shelter scene flooded back with startling force, and his
memories became vivid again, as if the old film had been started from the beginning. The same was
true of encounters that echoed the original trap: a crowded hallway, a blocked doorway, or a
situation where he felt there were no choices. Those moments were potent because they carried the
same helplessness he had known in the war zone, and they immediately stirred the present-tense
memory of being trapped. After one such episode in the lobby of his apartment building, he spent the
rest of the day replaying the event in his mind and checking the surroundings every few minutes,
trying to prevent a repeat. But the more he rehearsed and monitored, the more tightly the
frightening scene seemed to cling to him, making the memory feel even less like something finished.

At work, where he still consulted part-time from home on data projects, Mr. Rahman found himself
rehearsing conversations before every call and every meeting, worrying that a careless word or a
sudden irritation would expose how unsettled he felt. He would spend long stretches scanning the
environment around his desk, listening for raised voices in the building and watching the hallway
through his door crack. Yet these efforts also kept the fear alive; each time he checked for danger,
he was reminded that danger might be near, and each rehearsal confirmed how unsteady he believed
himself to be. He came to the painful conclusion that his reactions meant he was losing his mind,
and that belief deepened his anger and shame. A nightmare followed by waking in the dark would leave
him tense and suspicious for the rest of the morning, and the next news bulletin or quarrel in the
neighborhood would reopen the same memory. Over time he withdrew from social contact, spoke less
with neighbors, and became increasingly isolated in his apartment, where the constant vigilance
exhausted him.

By the time he sought help, Mr. Rahman was no longer only frightened by what had happened overseas;
he was frightened by the way his mind kept bringing it back, by the anger he felt in his body, and
by the possibility that he might become someone dangerous himself. What finally brought him in was
his growing concern that the cycle was tightening around him: the nightmares, the scanning, the
avoidance, and the replaying were leaving him more tense, more irritable, and more convinced that he
could not trust either the world or his own reactions. He said he wanted relief, but what he feared
most was that this would keep happening for the rest of his life and that he would end up alone,
ruled by a danger that never truly stayed in the past.
