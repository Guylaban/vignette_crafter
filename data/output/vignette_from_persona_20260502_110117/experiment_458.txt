Nadia was a 30-year-old African American school counselor who came to treatment after months of
feeling as though she could not settle anywhere, not even at home with her husband. Her symptoms
traced back to a police encounter that had left her shaken in a way she had not expected. One
evening, driving home after work, she was pulled over for a minor traffic issue. The stop escalated
quickly: the officer’s tone sharpened, his movements felt abrupt and unpredictable, and when he
stepped close she caught the smell of alcohol on his breath. Nadia remembered being acutely aware of
how tired she already was that day, how hard it was to think straight, and how vulnerable that
exhaustion made her feel while she tried to answer carefully and keep her hands visible. She left
the interaction physically safe but deeply unsettled, and in the weeks that followed she began to
read the event as evidence that something was wrong with her. She told herself that if she had
handled things differently, if she had sounded less nervous or been more composed, it would not have
gone the way it did. That self-blame deepened into a broader sense that there was something
defective in her, and she came to feel isolated and set apart from other people, as if others moved
through the world with a confidence and ease she no longer possessed.

As Nadia’s view of herself worsened, she began shrinking her life in ways that made sense to her at
the time. She avoided situations that might make her feel strong emotions, especially anything that
might bring back the shame and panic of that night, and she also steered clear of anything that
might make her feel hopeful or happy, because those moments felt fragile and dangerous, as if
disappointment would only confirm what she already feared about herself. She pulled away from people
at school and outside of work, declining invitations from colleagues and keeping conversations brief
with friends, because being around others seemed to expose her vulnerability and invite questions
she did not want to answer. The more she withdrew, the more she noticed how alone she felt, and that
loneliness fed the belief that she was fundamentally different from everyone else. It also made the
world feel narrower and less predictable, so she became even more watchful. In her mind, the
distance she put between herself and other people was partly a way to protect herself from being
hurt again, but it also seemed to confirm that she did not belong.

That withdrawal had another cost: it made certain cues hit harder. At work, where she spent her days
in crowded hallways and meetings with students, sudden movements by others put her immediately on
guard. A student darting up too quickly from a chair, a door slamming in the counseling office, or a
loud noise in the corridor could jolt her back into the feeling that something bad was about to
happen. She noticed the smell of alcohol in a parent’s breath during an after-school conference and
felt her body tighten before she could think. Because she was so intent on avoiding strong feelings
and other people, she had fewer chances to learn that these moments were safe, and each new scare
reinforced how hard it was for her to settle. She became superalert and watchful in a way that
exhausted her, scanning faces and movements, and even in her own home she found she could not relax,
though she knew she was in a safe place. The constant effort to stay ahead of danger left her worn
down, and that tiredness itself became a trigger: on the days she felt very exhausted, her mind
seemed slower and her guard thinner, which made her even more vulnerable to sudden alarms. The
pattern fed on itself. The more she avoided emotional stress, avoided positive events, and avoided
people, the more isolated she felt and the more certain she became that something was wrong with
her; the more she felt defective and set apart, the more vigilant she became for threat. And the
more vigilant she was, the more every abrupt movement, loud sound, or whiff of alcohol seemed to
confirm that she had good reason to stay guarded.

By the time she sought help, Nadia was still functioning at work, but only by pushing herself
through each day with effort and dread. She had started saying no to lunches, birthday gatherings,
and even small celebrations at school, because hopefulness felt risky and emotions felt too big to
manage. What finally brought her in was realizing that her world had become organized around bracing
herself, and that she was missing the parts of life that had once mattered to her. She was most
afraid that if this continued, she would remain trapped in the belief that she was broken and alone,
but she was also quietly hoping that treatment might help her feel safe enough to stop watching
every movement and to live without constantly preparing for the next shock.
