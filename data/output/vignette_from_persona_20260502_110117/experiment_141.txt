Daniel Mercer had spent most of his adult life helping other people make sense of crises, so the
assault he experienced at 53 left him especially shaken by how quickly his own confidence in
judgment disappeared. He was a social worker in a busy county office in a North American city,
married, usually the person colleagues turned to when a situation felt confusing or unsafe. One
evening, after leaving a late meeting, he was sexually assaulted in a place he had assumed was
routine and ordinary rather than dangerous; the details still came back in fragments, and what he
could not get out of his head was not only what was done to him but the humiliating sense of being
overpowered and trapped. When he tries to remember it now, he feels the same surge of terror he felt
then, and alongside that terror comes an intense shame that makes the memory hard to speak aloud.
Daniel says he knows what happened, but when he tries to tell it, the words do not come; he finds
himself stopping short, circling around it, and going quiet.

In the weeks afterward, those memories began to shape his days. The more vividly the assault
returned, the more he found himself trying to push it out of his mind, only to end up thinking about
it even more. He started spending long stretches in rumination about how he might get even with the
man who hurt him, not because he had a clear plan, but because the fantasy gave him a brief feeling
of having some control over what had been taken from him. At the same time, he became wary of
enclosed spaces, especially the small interview rooms and windowless storage areas in his office,
where he could feel his chest tighten and his body brace as though he were back in that trapped,
helpless state. Daniel began choosing stairs over elevators and avoiding unfamiliar places
altogether, telling himself he could not assess the danger there, that it was safer to stay where he
knew every exit. Even delays felt loaded with threat; if a client arrived late or his wife was held
up on the way home, he immediately assumed something bad was happening.

That sense of danger did not stay abstract. Once he had begun moving through the world as if
something terrible might be just behind him, he noticed himself frequently looking over his
shoulder, checking behind him in parking lots and on the street, scanning for anyone who might be
following. A short walk from his office to the train, which he once made without thought, became a
tense ritual of looking back at every crossing and every storefront reflection. The more he
monitored for danger, the more real danger seemed to feel, and the more the memory of the assault
returned with its old terror and shame attached. Those returns were not calm recollections; they
came as flashes that made him feel exposed and contaminated, and each one renewed his urge to shut
the whole thing down by force. He pushed the images away, rehearsed revenge in his head, and avoided
places where he might feel cornered, yet these efforts only left him more keyed up, more alert for
threat, and quicker to interpret ordinary interruptions as signs that something bad was unfolding.

Over time, the cycle reached into work and home. Daniel began declining site visits to unfamiliar
neighborhoods, which made him less available to clients and more isolated from colleagues who knew
him as steady and dependable. He took longer routes to meetings, checked hallways before entering,
and sometimes sat with his back to a wall in the staff lounge. At home, his wife noticed him going
quiet when she asked how he was doing, and he would change the subject rather than try to find words
for the assault. What had once been a life organized around helping others now felt organized around
staying ahead of danger, and the vigilance itself kept the event alive for him: every delay, every
closed door, every glance over his shoulder seemed to confirm that the world could turn threatening
without warning. That, in turn, made the terror on recall stronger and the shame more crushing,
which sent him back to suppression, avoidance, and the private revenge fantasies that briefly
soothed him but never settled him.

Daniel finally sought help after a recent evening when he left the office late, found the elevator
out of service, and had to decide whether to walk down several dim flights enclosed by concrete
walls. He froze on the landing, suddenly convinced someone was behind him, then spent the drive home
checking mirrors and imagining what he would do if the assailant ever reappeared. He came to
treatment because he is tired of living as though he is still being hunted, and because the shame of
not being able to speak about what happened has begun to feel as painful as the memory itself. What
he most hopes is that he can recover a sense of safety without having to keep looking over his
shoulder all the time.
