Minh Tran was a 33-year-old married physical therapist who came to treatment after months of feeling
as though his life had narrowed around a single violent morning. He had been driving home after an
evening shift when another car ran a red light and struck his vehicle at high speed, spinning him
into the median and pinning him in place while traffic rushed past. In the hours that followed, he
was acutely aware of the force through his chest and back, the scrape of metal, and how helpless his
body felt in the seat. Since then, he had come to believe that his body had been permanently damaged
by what happened and that he could no longer trust his own body to protect him. He described feeling
especially unsettled by loud noises, because a slamming door or sudden bang could carry him straight
back to that moment in the wreck, and unwanted thoughts of the crash would arrive without warning,
as if his mind were replaying the scene against his will. Waking from nightmares left him with a
heavy residue of fear in the early morning, and those dreams made the accident feel close again, not
safely over.

Those experiences quickly fed into a way of seeing the world that felt newly dangerous. Minh had
become convinced that you can never know who will harm you, and after the crash he found himself
noticing weapons or dangerous objects in nearly any environment, scanning parking lots, sidewalks,
and even the therapy clinic lobby for anything that might be used against him. His attention was
pulled away from patients and toward doorways, hands, and objects on carts, and he noticed that he
was jumpy and easily startled whenever someone approached too quickly or a machine clanged in the
gym. The more he watched for threat, the more danger he seemed to find, and each fresh jolt
confirmed his sense that the world was less predictable than he had once assumed. At the same time,
those moments of vigilance also sharpened his dread about his own body: a sudden startle, a
tightness in his back, or a brief wave of dizziness could convince him again that he was still
broken from the accident and that his body was unreliable under pressure. When he was on alert, the
intrusive thoughts about the crash became more frequent, and the sound of a cart wheel hitting a
threshold or a patient dropping a weight could pull him back into the memory with startling force.

Minh tried to manage this growing alarm by cleaning and organising things repeatedly to feel in
control, straightening the home bathroom, rearranging supplies at work, and checking his
surroundings over and over before sessions began. He also mentally rehearsed worst-case scenarios to
feel prepared, imagining where he would move if a stranger came in angry, how he would protect his
wife, or what he would do if his back gave out in front of a patient. For a while, these routines
gave him a sense that he was doing something useful, but they also kept his mind fixed on danger and
made him even more watchful. The more he scanned for threats and ran through disasters, the more
readily he spotted possible weapons, the more easily he was startled, and the harder it became to
concentrate long enough to finish notes or stay fully present with patients. He found himself
rereading charts and losing track of conversations, then becoming more frustrated with himself and
more certain that his body and attention were no longer dependable. That sense of unreliability
pushed him back into the same habits of cleaning, checking, and rehearsing, because uncertainty felt
unbearable.

Over the following months, Minh’s life at home and at work became arranged around avoiding reminders
and staying prepared. In the clinic he chose treatment rooms near exits, and in the evenings he
would return home and methodically clean the kitchen counters or sort the garage tools before he
could sit down with his wife. He knew these habits made him feel briefly steadier, but they also
left him tense and exhausted, as if he were always bracing for another impact. The nightmares, the
unwanted crash thoughts, and the bursts of fear at loud noises had become common enough that he no
longer trusted ordinary routines to remain ordinary. What finally brought him to seek help was the
growing strain on his marriage and his fear that he was teaching himself to live as if danger were
everywhere. He was most afraid that one day his body would fail him again at work or on the road,
and most hoping that he might learn how to step out of that constant scanning long enough to feel
safe with his wife and patients again.
