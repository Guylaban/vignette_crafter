trusting systems to behave predictably. That expectation had been shattered the summer the wildfire
came through the edge of the neighborhood where he and his wife lived. He remembered the air first:
hot, dry, and carrying a sharp smell of smoke that seemed to arrive before the flames were visible.
From the kitchen window he had seen orange light licking up behind a line of houses, and when the
wind shifted, the smell thickened until it felt like it was in his throat. The weather that day had
been unnervingly similar to the conditions in which the fire had spread so quickly—still, hot, and
deceptively calm—and that sameness has stayed with him. Even now, when smoke drifts from a
neighbor’s grill or a brush fire is reported on the news, or when the afternoon turns dry and heavy
in the same way, Rafael feels the old alarm rise before he can stop it.

In the months after the evacuation, those reminders set off a familiar chain of reactions. The smell
of smoke, the sight of flames, or even a stretch of weather that felt too much like that day would
send him into long bouts of self-blame and prevention-focused rumination. He would replay the event
over and over, dwelling on what he had done wrong and what he should have noticed sooner, wondering
whether he had left too late, packed too slowly, or missed some sign that the fire was moving
closer. He could spend an evening silently working through alternate versions of the day, convinced
that if he thought hard enough he might find the one decision that would have prevented everything.
At times he tried to push the thoughts out of his mind altogether, forcing himself to turn the
television up or bury himself in routine tasks at the house, but the harder he tried to shut them
down, the more they seemed to gather force at the edges of his attention.

What made this pattern especially hard to break was how his body reacted whenever the memories were
stirred. The moment a trigger hit, Rafael would feel his chest tighten, his heart pounding hard
enough that he became acutely aware of it, and he would feel suddenly on edge, as if danger might be
nearby again. He described himself as jumpy and easily startled; a door closing in the next room,
the hiss of something heating on the stove, or a sudden movement in his peripheral vision could make
him flinch before he had time to think. Those bodily surges made the disaster feel immediate rather
than distant, and once he noticed them he became even more vigilant, scanning the air, the windows,
the neighborhood, and the weather forecast for anything that might suggest smoke or fire. He found
himself checking outside more often, watching the sky at dusk, and listening for news alerts,
because if his body was warning him, he believed he had to stay watchful.

That watchfulness, however, began to make the reminders more frequent. Rafael noticed that once he
was scanning for smoke or signs of fire, he started picking them up everywhere: the burnt smell from
a passing car, a faint haze from a backyard fire pit, the orange glow of a sunset that resembled
flames at the horizon. He had come to dread the summer heat and the thick, still afternoons because
they seemed to invite the same vigilance all over again. The more he monitored for danger, the more
cues he encountered, and each cue sent him back into the same pattern of trying to suppress the
images while mentally revisiting what he had done and how the disaster might have been avoided. At
home, this left him tense and irritable, and at work, even though he was no longer in the same
active role as before, he noticed he was less able to concentrate when the building’s heating system

walk slowly around the office, and try to collect himself before returning, determined not to let
the memories show.

Over time, the cycle narrowed his life. Rafael became more guarded in the house and around his
neighborhood, and his wife noticed that he seemed to be waiting for something bad whenever the
weather turned warm and still. He wanted to believe that keeping alert would keep him safe, but the
effort left him exhausted and more sensitive to every smell, every flash of light, every change in
the air. By the time he came to treatment, he was afraid that his mind would not stop replaying the
fire and that his body would keep reacting as if the next smoke smell meant they were in danger
again. He hoped for a way to feel ordinary weather, ordinary smells, and ordinary evenings without
immediately being pulled back to that day.
