Mereana, a 71-year-old Māori woman, had spent most of her adult life working as a police officer,
and the event she now returns to in fragments happened during one late shift that still feels, to
her, like it split life into before and after. She had been called to a serious road crash, and when
she arrived the scene was chaotic: twisted metal, people shouting, and one injured child’s mother
nearby, reeking of alcohol, trying to explain what had happened through tears and panic. The child
was alive but badly hurt, and Mereana was left with the unbearable certainty that something had gone
wrong under her watch. In the months that followed, she began to replay that night in a way that
made the images feel almost physical. Her memories were not like a story she could tell from
beginning to end; they came as vivid pictures, sharp and detailed, and sometimes with sounds
intruding in her head as if she were back there hearing the noise of the crash, the cries, and the
frantic voices around her.

What brought those memories back most strongly were ordinary moments that now felt contaminated by
the scene. Seeing cars hit in an accident, even briefly on the roadside or on television, could stop
her in her tracks. Talk about accidents was no easier; if someone at work started discussing a
collision, she would feel her body tense before she had fully registered what was being said. The
smell of alcohol was particularly hard to bear. In a supermarket aisle, at a family gathering, or
when a stranger passed by with alcohol on their breath, she would be pulled straight into the same
images and sounds from that night. Those reminders did not just upset her; they confirmed what she
had come to believe about herself. Each time a crash image, an accident discussion, or the smell of
alcohol set off the scene again, she was driven back to the thought that the event happened because
of the way she acted, that she should have protected others and failed, and that she could not stop
bad things from happening to her or to people she was meant to keep safe.

Over time, Mereana began to organize her life around avoiding those reminders. She was still
married, but at home she would change the channel if an accident came on the news and would leave
the room if family members started talking about road injuries. At work, the avoidance was harder to
hide. Although she remained employed as a police officer, she started maneuvering away from tasks
that might expose her to collision scenes or long conversations about crashes, and she noticed
herself becoming more guarded during shifts. The problem was that the very things she worked so hard
to avoid kept appearing in the life she had built: on patrol, at roadside incidents, in everyday
conversations with colleagues, and in the public spaces she used to move through without thinking.
Each encounter seemed to bring back the same detailed pictures and the same sounds, and each return
made her more certain that she had failed in some basic way. She began to feel that if she had acted
differently at that crash, she might have prevented the harm, and when she could not shake the
images, she took that as further proof that she had not done enough.

accident imagery or the smell of alcohol, the more firmly she settled into blame and helplessness.

sharp fragments, hearing the sounds inside her head and seeing the scene as clearly as if it were

person who failed when it mattered most.
