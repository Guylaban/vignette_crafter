can picture the scene and feel almost nothing, as if it happened to a stranger. He could not tell a
clear story of the event even to himself, only pieces that return in no reliable order.

In the weeks after the attack, Frank began to judge himself harshly for how he had handled it. He
became convinced that somebody else would not have gotten into that situation, that he had failed in
his most important role in life, and that he could not deal with even the slightest upset. Those
conclusions shaped what he did next: he started choosing seats with his back to the wall so he could
see the whole room, scanning for exits and for anyone who looked dangerous. At work, he would stand
where he could watch people enter the building, and in restaurants or church basements he was quick

might prevent another disaster, but the vigilance also kept him trapped in the belief that he was
weak and inadequate, always one step behind what a capable man should have managed.

guard. Sitting with his back to the wall became less a preference than a rule, a habit that followed

time he walks into a room.
