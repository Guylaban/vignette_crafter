David, a 56-year-old European American musician, came for help after years of feeling as though his
world had narrowed to a few “safe” rooms and routines. He had been married for decades, and his wife
had increasingly noticed how carefully he arranged his days around places he trusted: the home
studio, the kitchen table early in the morning, the back porch at dusk. Outside those small islands,
he felt on guard. Sudden movements from other people could put him immediately on edge, and if he
was in a crowd or anywhere he associated with authority—security staff, police, uniformed
personnel—his body tightened before he could think. He described an irritable, snappish temper that
had grown harder to hide, though he was ashamed of it and tried to keep it contained. What
frightened him most was that he no longer felt able to move through ordinary life without scanning
for danger, as if safety existed only in a very limited number of places.

The assault that changed him had happened after a late-night performance nearly a year earlier.
David had been leaving a small venue when a man he knew from the local music scene—someone he had
once socialized with at clubs and after gigs—cornered him in a back hallway. David remembered the
smell of a sharp, expensive cologne first, then the hard jolt of being shoved against the wall.
There was violence, a crushing sense of powerlessness, and blood when he bit his lip trying to fight
free. The rest of the event is not something he can tell in a clear sequence, even to himself. He
remembers some moments with painful clarity: the cologne, the glint of a ring, the pressure of a
hand, the taste of blood. Other parts are blank. When he tries to put it into words, he finds
himself pushing the memory away before he can fully recall it, because the fragments come with such
force that he feels he will be overwhelmed if he lets them gather into a whole story.

Since then, the brokenness of the memory has itself become part of what unsettles him. The more he
tries to hold it at bay, the more it returns in pieces, and the more his mind seems to fill in gaps
with alarm. He has come to fear that if he allowed himself to remember too much, he would be flooded
by rage, shame, or panic. In response, he has made a rule for himself never to allow himself to feel
or express anger. That rule has helped him stay functional in the short term, but it has also left
him feeling tense and bottled up, as if any sign of anger would make him dangerous or exposed. He
notices that this same guardedness now shapes what he avoids: he stays away from people in authority
or uniform, because their presence feels too much like the helplessness of that night, and he avoids
seeing people he socialized with before the event, especially anyone from the music circuit who
might remind him of the attacker or ask questions he cannot answer.

Specific reminders now seem to reach him through the body before reason has time to intervene.
Violence in movies, arguments in public, or even a raised voice can put him instantly on guard,
because his mind links any sign of aggression with what happened in that hallway. Blood is another
sharp trigger; the smallest sight of it can pull him back toward the assault and make him feel that
something terrible is about to happen again. The cologne is the most unsettling of all. If a
stranger passes too close in a store or backstage at a show wearing a similar scent, his chest
tightens and he becomes hyperalert, scanning the room for exits and for the nearest person who might
move abruptly. In those moments, his careful avoidance does not feel voluntary so much as necessary:
he has learned that keeping distance from those reminders is the only way to keep himself steady.

Yet that same narrowing of his life has fed the problem. Because he is constantly restricting where
he goes and who he sees, the memory never gets a chance to settle into any coherent shape, and each
fragment remains charged and unfinished. The more he suppresses anger and keeps to himself, the less
he can sort through what happened, and the less ordered the memory becomes. In turn, the unfinished
pieces keep his sense of threat alive, so that a smell, a splash of blood, or a sudden movement can
still feel as if it belongs to the assault. David finally sought therapy after his wife told him
that his vigilance, irritability, and shrinking world were becoming impossible to ignore, and after
he realized he had turned down performances simply to avoid venues where he might encounter old
acquaintances or anyone in a uniform. He is hoping to regain a wider life and to stop living as
though danger waits in every doorway, though he is most afraid that if he lets the memory in, he
will lose control of the anger he has spent years trying not to feel.
