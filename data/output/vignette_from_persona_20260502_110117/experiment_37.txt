Marisol Rivas had been proud of how self-possessed she was at fifty-three, a seasoned stock broker
in Miami who could manage volatile markets, difficult clients, and the pressure of being the person
others looked to when numbers turned ugly. The mugging happened one winter evening after she left
her office later than usual, crossing a nearly empty parking garage with her laptop bag tucked close
under her arm. Two men appeared from between the concrete pillars. One moved abruptly toward her
while the other angled behind her, and the suddenness of it—the quick step, the sharp reach for her
bag, the feeling that she had been cornered before she could even think—lodged in her body as horror
and fear. For months after, ordinary movements from strangers could send her instantly on guard, and
even the clatter of a dropped tray in a café or a car backfiring in the street made her jump in a
way that embarrassed her. She found herself constantly scanning doorways, mirrors, and parked cars,
taking in exits and faces before she was aware she was doing it, as though vigilance might keep her
one step ahead of danger.

What disturbed Marisol most was not only the robbery itself but how little she believed anyone had
truly protected her. Security had been “around,” the garage had cameras, and yet in the moment she
felt abandoned by the very systems that were supposed to make a professional building safe. She
became convinced that the world was a dangerous place in a way she had once considered exaggerated
or naive. At the same time, she felt emotionally hollow, as if some essential part of her had gone
dead inside. That deadness sat beside a wash of fear, anger, shame, and guilt she could not organize
or set aside. Because she believed danger could appear anywhere and that institutions had failed her
when it mattered, she began to move through her days with a careful, appeasing politeness she had
not needed before. She overexplained herself to doormen, smiled too quickly at men who came too
close in elevators, and became unnaturally compliant when she felt threatened, handing over her
purse or stepping aside before anyone had even asked. She told herself that if she was agreeable
enough, attentive enough, and quiet enough, she might prevent a repeat of that night.

The fear did not stay abstract. A sudden movement by a stranger in the subway station near her
office, a hand darting into a coat pocket, or even a colleague standing up too fast in a meeting
could make her heart pound and her shoulders lock. Those moments fed the same grim conclusion: she
lived in a world where danger could break through at any time. In response, Marisol doubled down on
habits that she believed would keep catastrophe away. She performed mental rituals before leaving
home—counting, repeating phrases under her breath, mentally checking that she had “covered” each
possible risk—because she was certain that if she did not keep her mind orderly, something bad would
happen again. The rituals offered brief relief, but they also reinforced the sense that danger was
everywhere and that she had to work constantly to stay ahead of it. Each time she tried to manage
her fear that way, the world seemed more perilous, and her thoughts returned to the same bleak
appraisal: if the people and institutions around her had failed once, they could fail again.

Over the following months, the pattern narrowed her life. At work, in the glass-walled brokerage
office where colleagues moved quickly behind her chair and clients arrived unpredictably, Marisol
found herself monitoring every sound and movement instead of following market data with her usual
focus. She avoided staying late, took longer routes to the parking structure, and began asking her
husband to pick her up whenever possible. Her husband noticed that she flinched when he came up
behind her in the kitchen and that she had grown quieter at home, as if the emotional numbness made
conversation effortful. She was ashamed of how dependent and accommodating she had become, yet the
more threatened she felt, the more she fell back into compliance and scanning, and the more certain
she became that vigilance was the only thing standing between her and another attack. That
certainty, in turn, deepened her belief that the world could not be trusted and that she had been
left to fend for herself.

By the time she sought help, Marisol was exhausted by living as though every corner might hide the
same threat. She came in after a week in which a sudden shout in the office lobby left her shaking
for an hour, and after realizing she had spent more time rehearsing safety in her head than
sleeping. What brought her to treatment was the fear that this way of living was shrinking her
marriage, her work, and the confident life she had built. She was most afraid that if she did not
find a way to feel safe again, she would remain deadened inside and forever braced for the next
sudden movement in a world she no longer trusted.
