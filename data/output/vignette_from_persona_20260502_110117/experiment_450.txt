Amina, a 25-year-old African woman working as a surgeon in the United States, came to treatment
months after a military trauma that she still struggled to describe. During a deployment, she had
been caught in the chaos of a sudden attack near a helicopter landing zone, with the sharp crack of
explosions overhead and the roar of aircraft chopping through the air while the ground and nearby
equipment vibrated with a particular rough texture she now found impossible to tolerate. What stayed
with her was not a coherent story she could tell, but flashes of terror: the smell of dust, the jolt
in her chest, the sense that death was arriving in the next second. When she tried to talk about it
afterward, the words seemed to fail her; she would stop mid-sentence, shake her head, and say she
could not put it into words. Even months later, remembering it would bring back the same terror she
had felt in the moment, not as a distant recollection but as if her body had been dropped back into
the scene.

From the weeks after the trauma onward, Amina’s life narrowed around the kinds of things that could
pull her back there without warning. A helicopter passing over the hospital roof, an aircraft sound
in the city, or the sudden bang of a dropped tray in the operating room could instantly flood her
with the memory, and the specific texture of certain surfaces—especially anything that reminded her
of the roughness she had felt during the attack—could set her on edge before she fully understood
why. She had come to believe that danger could appear anywhere and without notice, and that if she
did not stay ahead of it, she would be caught unprepared again. In response, she began planning
escape routes before entering every place, mentally mapping doors and hallways before stepping into
a clinic room or a restaurant. She constantly scanned her surroundings for signs of threat, her
attention pulled from conversations to windows, corridors, and the people near her. She always sat
with her back to the wall, including during lunch with colleagues at the hospital, so she could see
the room and no one could approach from behind. Those habits brought brief relief, but they also
made the triggers feel even more important, as if every sound or texture confirmed that vigilance
was necessary.

At work, the pattern became harder to hide. In the operating theater and in hallways outside the
wards, Amina would orient herself to exits before speaking to anyone, and even ordinary sounds could
shift her attention away from her patients. When a helicopter landed on the hospital helipad, she
froze long enough that a nurse had to repeat her name. Later, a sharp bang from a tray cart made her
shoulders jerk and her gaze sweep the room automatically. She found herself checking the environment
almost continuously, and because she was always measuring where to go if something happened, she
noticed every possible threat more intensely. That constant watchfulness made the helicopters,
explosions, and even certain textures more potent, because she encountered them already primed to
expect danger. The safer she tried to make each space, the more each reminder seemed to prove that
the danger was still close enough to matter.

Over time, Amina’s avoidance changed how she moved through the world. She turned down invitations if
she could not sit with her back to a wall, and she arrived early to places so she could choose the
most defensible seat. She noticed that her colleagues interpreted her behavior as reserve, but for
her it was an effort to keep terror from returning. What finally brought her to seek help was how
much energy the constant scanning and route-planning were taking from her work and how often she was
being pulled back into the same overwhelming memory by sounds she could not control. She was not
sure she would ever be able to speak the trauma clearly, but she was most afraid that the terror
would keep taking over her life in operating rooms, hallways, and crowded spaces unless she found a
way to face it without immediately feeling trapped again.
