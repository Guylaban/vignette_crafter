brother died suddenly and violently in an unexpected incident that tore through the family with no
warning. One day his brother was there, joking in the kitchen over coffee, and the next Carlos was
standing in a hospital corridor hearing words he could not take in, then later watching relatives
gather in stunned silence while his mother repeated his name as if repetition might bring him back.
After that, ordinary moments began to feel dangerous in a way he could not explain to his wife or
coworkers. When a news clip about a fatal accident came on in the break room, or when someone at
work casually talked about people losing loved ones, he would feel a strange detached, unreal
quality settle over him, as if he were standing a little outside his own body watching everything
through glass. Those moments did not just upset him; they made him feel that the loss was
everywhere, waiting for him in the next headline or conversation, and he would immediately shove the
feeling away by covering his face with his hands, leaving the room, or creating drama with whoever
was nearest so he would not have to sit with the grief. He came to avoid anything that reminded him
of someone he had lost, including the kind of music his brother liked and family gatherings where
his brother’s absence was obvious.

What Carlos told himself about these reactions made everything feel worse. He had become convinced
that he was no longer the happy person he used to be and that now he was always miserable, and he
carried a bleak sense that he had no future that could feel intact or worth wanting. Beneath that
was the harsher thought that he should have protected others and failed, a belief that settled on
him whenever he remembered how helpless he had felt at the hospital and how little he could do to
change what had happened. That judgment did not stay contained; it made every reminder of loss feel
like evidence against him. A conversation about someone losing a loved one, a condolence card, or a
news story about sudden death would immediately pull him back into the same conviction that he had
failed once already and could fail again. In those moments he would hide his face, leave the scene,
or pick a fight at home, not only to escape the pain but because the misery and shame seemed too big
to hold quietly.

Over the months, the habits began to tighten around him. At home with his wife, he would sometimes
start arguments over small things, almost as if he needed noise and conflict to outrun the stillness
that arrived whenever the house reminded him of his brother. At work in the prison, where death and
loss were sometimes discussed in the blunt language of staff rooms and corridors, he found himself
scanning news feeds on his phone with dread, only to regret it and react by snapping at a coworker
or stirring up unnecessary conflict to push the feeling away. Yet those outbursts left him more
ashamed and more certain that he had become a man who could not hold himself together. The more he
acted this way, the more he returned to the same thought that he was miserable and had no future,
and the more any mention of death, any story of a family member lost, seemed to strike straight at
that belief and set off the unreal, detached feeling again. He had learned to dread those moments
precisely because they carried him back into the proof, in his mind, that he had failed and that
loss would keep finding him.

By the time he sought help, Carlos was exhausted by the cycle and frightened by how quickly he could
shift from numbness to anger to shame. He wanted to stop hiding his face, stop provoking fights, and
stop turning away from anything that might remind him of the brother he lost, but he was also afraid
that if he let himself grieve, he would be swallowed by the certainty that he had no future and that
the man he used to be was gone for good.
