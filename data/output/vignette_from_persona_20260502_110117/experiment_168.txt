life before the assault as busy, practical, and largely centered on the rhythm of the kitchen. The
assault had occurred after a late shift, when she found herself alone with a man she had thought she
knew well enough to trust. What unsettled her most was not only the violence of the attack but how
quickly it had been tied to ordinary closeness: a conversation that turned personal, a sense of
familiarity, the false safety of being in a familiar space with someone who had seemed harmless. In
the months afterward, she found that certain conversation topics now made her body tense before she
could explain why, and emotional closeness felt dangerous in a way that was hard to justify to
herself. She had come to believe that people could not be trusted and that you could never know who
would harm you. That belief gathered force from the way the event lived in her mind: she could not
put it into words, only into disconnected pieces that would not settle into order, and when it came
back to her she felt her body in the same position as during the assault, as if the memory had not
ended but was still happening somewhere in her muscles.

As those fragments kept intruding, Marta’s understanding of other people darkened. She found herself
thinking again and again that she had been betrayed by someone she trusted, and that if it had
happened once it could happen again with anyone. The memory itself fed that conclusion, because what
she could not narrate cleanly felt to her like proof that the event was still unresolved and still
capable of returning at any moment. At the same time, her sense that the danger had not passed made
her scan her surroundings with increasing intensity; she became superalert and watchful, reacting to
unexpected movement nearby as if it were a threat. In ordinary situations she felt threatened by
strangers approaching, even in a supermarket or on the street near her apartment. That constant
sense of being on guard made the world seem more hostile, and it also pulled her back toward the
original themes of the assault: a man standing too close in the kitchen, a conversation that turned
private, the awareness of being trapped with someone she had thought she knew. Those moments now
felt saturated with danger, and she noticed that when men were around, especially when she was alone
with them, her mind went immediately to the possibility of harm.

Marta’s fear of harm then began to shape what she could bear to remember and what she could bring
herself to hear. When she felt watched or startled, the assault came back in disjointed flashes, and
the bodily sensation of being pinned or positioned as she had been during the attack made the
fragments sharper and less speakable. She would lose the thread of the event and be left only with a
bodily certainty that it had been real and still mattered. The more often this happened, the more
she felt confirmed in her belief that people cannot be trusted, and that anyone could turn on her
without warning. That conviction made her even more guarded in conversation, especially if someone
asked personal questions or tried to get emotionally close; she would pull back, change the subject,

teamwork and easy banter, she now preferred distance. She stayed busy at the pass or with prep
rather than risk prolonged exchange, and she noticed that a sudden movement behind her or a man
stepping into her workspace could make her flinch before she had time to think. Over weeks, the
pattern hardened: the more she watched for danger, the more often she found evidence of it, and the
more those encounters brought the assault to mind in fragments that strengthened her sense of
betrayal and threat.

By the time she sought help, Marta was exhausted by the way her world had narrowed around vigilance
and mistrust. She still went to work and managed her marriage, but she described feeling as though
she was always braced for the next approach, the next unexpected motion, the next conversation that
might turn too personal. What finally brought her in was the fear that she was becoming unable to
live around other people without feeling under attack. She hoped therapy might help her make sense
of the pieces of the assault that still lived in her body and mind, and she was most afraid that if
she could not do that, she would spend the rest of her life assuming that every stranger, every
close conversation, and every man alone with her could be the one who hurt her.
