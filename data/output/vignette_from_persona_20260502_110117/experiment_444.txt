Leela had spent much of her life making images of other people’s lives, yet the event that organized
her own had remained lodged in the background, half-formed and never safely put into words. As a
child in a South Asian household, she was repeatedly beaten by the caregiver who was meant to
protect her, and even now, at 70, divorced and living alone, she could still feel how small the
rooms were where it happened and how complete her isolation felt inside them. What has remained most
painful is not only the memory of the blows, but her conviction that no one who harmed her ever
faced consequences. She has come to believe that people like her are meant to carry things in
silence, that she no longer belongs anywhere, and that the world is simply not built for her to feel
safe in it.

That belief has shaped the way Leela has tried to manage herself. She spends long stretches thinking
about why the event happened to her, turning it over as if explanation might make it less unjust or
less personal. When the questions become too sharp, she covers her face with her hands, almost as
though shielding herself from being seen in the very act of remembering. She also tries hard to
control her emotions, keeping her expression composed even when her chest tightens or her throat
closes, because she fears that if she lets anything show it will spill over and become unbearable.
These efforts are not abstract habits for her; they are ways of trying to survive the thought that
the people responsible escaped without consequence while she was left carrying the mark of what they
did.

Yet the more Leela tries to hold herself together around these thoughts, the more the memory seems
to resist her. When she deliberately tries to remember what happened, she finds it difficult to
access the details in a straight line, as if the scenes are behind a locked door. What does break
through is not orderly recollection but the same terror she felt at the time, sudden and bodily,
along with an intense shame that rises so fast she wants to hide her face again. She has noticed
that the harder she presses herself to understand it or to explain it to others, the more the memory
slips into fragments and sensations, leaving her shaken without giving her the relief of a complete
story. In this way, her searching and her effort to contain herself seem to keep her caught in the
experience rather than freeing her from it.

Over the years, that pattern has fed her constant sense that she must stay on guard. Leela notices
doors, footsteps, changes in tone, and the presence of people around her with a vigilance that
leaves little room for ease. Working as a photographer has not protected her from this; if anything,
it has given her more moments in which she must remain alert while moving through crowded studios,
public events, and unfamiliar locations. She has found herself scanning faces before she can relax,
then feeling the familiar conviction that she does not belong anywhere, whether in a room full of
clients or even among people she knows. At times she retreats into stillness, speaking less, keeping
her face covered when memories surge, and leaving social gatherings early because she does not trust
herself not to be overwhelmed.

The consequences have become concrete in the life she lives now. Leela has withdrawn from
invitations and cut short work that once felt expansive, not because she has lost her eye for
images, but because she no longer trusts her own reactions in the presence of other people. She
remains preoccupied with what happened and with the injustice of it, and each time the memory is
stirred the shame and terror return with such force that she is left more convinced she must keep
watch. She finally sought help after noticing how exhausting it had become to spend every day
bracing for something she could not name, and how lonely her life had grown around that effort. What
she hopes for now is not to erase the past, but to stop feeling as though she is still trapped
inside it, and what she fears most is that, without help, she will spend the rest of her life
believing she belongs nowhere at all.
