Daniel Morales was thirty, married, and had spent the last decade working as a carpenter in and
around Raleigh, moving from framing houses to finish work with the kind of steady physical
competence he had always taken pride in. The traumatic event that still organized his days happened
two years earlier on a summer afternoon at a jobsite, when a coworker’s hand was pulled into a saw
and Daniel was the first to reach him. He remembered the suddenness of the blood, the raw tear of
skin and bone, and the metallic, sugary taste of the coffee he had been drinking when he bent over
the injured man. What stayed with him most was not a clear story he could tell from start to finish,
but a flood of sensation that arrived all at once: the sight of the wound, the pressure of trying to
hold the man still, the smell of wood dust and blood, and the taste in his mouth that seemed to
belong to that moment forever.

In the months after, Daniel found that ordinary contact could jolt him back into that scene. A hand
on his shoulder in a crowded aisle, a sudden grip from his wife when she was trying to get his
attention, even the brief squeeze of a handshake at work could make his chest tighten. The same
happened when he caught the taste of the coffee he had been drinking that day, or when he saw open
cuts on another worker’s forearm after a minor accident on a site. Those moments would not just
upset him; they would drag him immediately into the memory without warning, and he would feel as if
he were standing over his coworker again, helpless and nauseated. When that happened, he would pull
away from people, blank out on purpose, or eat far more than he wanted once he got home, using the
heaviness of a meal to smother the feelings he could not otherwise control. Yet the eating never
lasted as relief. He would finish feeling ashamed, and the shame only confirmed what he had already
come to believe: that his life had been destroyed by the trauma, that he had permanently changed for
the worse, and that he could not rely on himself when anything unexpected happened.

That belief had also changed the way he made sense of the memory itself. Daniel was certain there
was something wrong with him because the event never stayed in words; whenever he tried to describe
it, the details collapsed, and what came back was not a narrative but a complete sensory reliving,
vivid enough to feel the blood in his mouth and the weight of the injured hand all over again. He
had come to believe that because he could not put it into words, he must be permanently damaged. The
more he thought this, the more those sensations seemed to arrive on their own, triggered by the
slightest reminder: a gloved hand brushing his arm, the coppery taste of a bitten lip, the sight of
a fresh wound at the hardware store. Each reminder made him fear another wave of the same memory,
and the fear itself made him watch for danger everywhere. At work he began scanning coworkers’
hands, forearms, and tools; at home he avoided talking about the incident with his wife because
thinking about it even for a moment seemed to open the door to all of it.

Over time, his attempts to manage the distress only deepened his sense that he was failing. He would
deliberately try to leave his body, sitting in the truck after work and staring through himself, or
he would eat until he felt numb, telling himself that at least then he was not thinking about the
event. But each time he used those strategies, he woke up feeling more disconnected and more
convinced that something essential in him had been altered. That conviction made the world feel more
dangerous and the reminders more potent. He became more alert to touch, to the taste of coffee, to
any visible injury, and because he was watching so closely, he noticed them everywhere. In a
carpentry job, where hands are always in motion and minor cuts are common, he found it hard to stay
present when a coworker reached for a tool or when someone walked in from a site with a scraped
knuckle. At home his wife noticed he flinched when she touched him from behind, and he withdrew from
the easy physical closeness that had once been natural in their marriage.

By the time he sought help, Daniel was exhausted by the cycle and frightened by how quickly it could
seize him. He was not simply haunted by one memory; he was living around it, shaping his days to
avoid thinking, bracing for touch, scanning for injuries, and then hating himself for how easily he
was pulled back under. What finally brought him in was a stretch of evenings in which he found
himself eating in secret after work, blanking out in the truck, and then sitting at the kitchen
table unable to explain to his wife why he felt so broken. He came hoping to regain some trust in
himself and to stop feeling as though one summer afternoon had permanently taken his life away.
