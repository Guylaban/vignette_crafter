Omar, a 19-year-old Middle Eastern man who worked as an environmental scientist while newly married,
came to treatment after years of carrying a fear that felt older than his own adult life. He grew up
in a home where a caregiver’s anger could turn physical without warning, and what stayed with him
was not only the pain but the unpredictability of it: a sharp voice rising over a minor
disagreement, a hand coming down hard, the sense that his body had to anticipate danger before he
could even name it. By the time he was in college and then in his first job, he found himself
scanning every room before settling in. He always chose his seat with care, looking for a wall
behind him and a clear view of the door, because he felt unsafe unless his back was protected and he
could watch everyone’s face. In meetings, in restaurants, even in his own home, he watched for tiny
shifts in expression and posture, studying whether someone’s jaw tightened or shoulders squared, as
if threat might announce itself there first.

Those moments of scanning were not neutral habits for Omar; they were tied to the way certain cues
could suddenly make the past feel present. Arguments were especially potent. If voices rose in his
apartment, even between other people, he felt his body tense and his attention narrow, because
disagreement had once meant that a caregiver’s anger could become violence. Crying had a similarly
charged quality. Hearing someone sob, or seeing his wife upset, could bring an immediate rush of
dread, because distress in others had often preceded explosions at home. Unexpected touch was
another trigger that could jolt him hard. If a colleague tapped his shoulder without warning or his
wife touched him from behind, he would flinch and brace, his mind instantly searching for where the
contact might turn into something worse. These moments fed directly into his sense that danger was
always nearby, hidden in ordinary social cues and in the faces of people around him.

That expectation of danger shaped how Omar lived. He began avoiding any situation that might involve
disagreement or confrontation, even when a problem at work needed to be addressed. In staff meetings
for his environmental projects, he would stay quiet rather than challenge a plan, and at home he
tried to smooth over even small tensions with his wife rather than risk an argument. He also avoided
being alone in a room or building, and he avoided people more generally when he could manage it,
lingering near exits or filling his days with tasks that kept him from lingering in empty spaces. At
work, he chose projects that kept him outdoors with a team rather than in a quiet office, and he
made sure not to be the last person left in the building. These choices made sense to him: if he
stayed where he could see others and keep away from confrontation, he felt less exposed.

Yet the cost of living that way was that the world kept confirming his fears. Because Omar watched
faces and body language so closely, he often noticed tension where others might not have, and that
vigilance made ordinary interactions feel charged. A coworker’s clipped tone or a relative’s frown
could register as danger, which made him pull back, speak less, and avoid people even more. The more
he withdrew, the more unfamiliar and unsafe social contact felt, so that even brief conversations
could leave him on edge. His avoidance also fed the very moments he feared: when he tried to skirt
around disagreement, small misunderstandings lingered, people asked him why he seemed distant, and
those questions sometimes turned into the arguments he had been trying to prevent. At home, his wife
occasionally confronted him about shutting down, and the sight of her frustration or tears would
trigger the old alarm, making him retreat further instead of staying engaged. Over time, he felt
trapped in a pattern where his efforts to stay safe kept the threat alive in every room he entered.

By the time he sought help, Omar was exhausted by the constant effort of orienting himself toward
danger, by the way he calculated every seat, every doorway, every expression. He had begun to notice
that his marriage was becoming narrower around his avoidance, and that work had started to feel like
a series of managed escapes rather than a place where he could fully think. What brought him in was
a recent incident in which a colleague touched his shoulder from behind while asking a question, and
Omar jerked away so sharply that the room went silent; afterward, he spent the rest of the day
avoiding everyone and replaying the scene, ashamed of how frightened he had become. He came hoping
to stop feeling as if every argument, every tear, every unexpected touch could turn into something
dangerous, and he was most afraid that if this kept going, he would keep shrinking his life until
there was almost nowhere left he felt safe.
