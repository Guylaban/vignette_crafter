Leilani Ratu, a 35-year-old accountant from an Oceania background, came to therapy after months of
living as if the mugging were still poised to happen again. One evening outside her apartment
building, just after she had stepped off the bus and was walking with her work bag tucked under one
arm, she heard quick footsteps behind her on the pavement. Before she could turn, a man came up
close, grabbed her shoulder from behind, and shoved something hard against her side while demanding
her wallet. She saw his face for only a second under the streetlight, enough to fix the shape of his
eyes and the hood pulled low over his forehead. In the struggle he caught her wrist and yanked the
bag free. Later, she could still taste the cheap mint gum she had been chewing when he leaned in to
speak, a taste that would come back vividly whenever the memory returned. From the beginning, the
event seemed to her like something that should not have happened to her at all; it felt so extreme
that it was hard for her to believe it had actually happened to her, yet she also blamed herself for
being the kind of person who could be targeted. She told herself the world was dangerous, and that
the mugging happened because of the way she had acted—leaving work late, checking her phone, not
noticing him sooner.

Since then, ordinary moments have become charged with threat. Footsteps behind her in a hallway, on
the sidewalk, or echoing in the stairwell at her office make her body tighten before she can think,
and if someone approaches her too quickly or touches her unexpectedly, she flinches as though the
assault is beginning again. Those cues do not just scare her; they send her straight back to her
explanations for what happened, and she starts dwelling on what she has done wrong and how the event
could have been prevented. She finds herself thinking that it happened because of the sort of person
she is, as if there were something careless or vulnerable about her that invited danger. That same
belief feeds her sense that the world is unsafe, and even when her husband tells her she is in a
secure building or that the street outside is quiet, she cannot believe him. Delays have become
especially hard: if he is late answering his phone or a meeting runs longer than expected, she
assumes something bad is happening. She cannot relax even when she knows she is in a safe place,
because safety feels like a temporary story rather than a fact.

The memories themselves arrive with a physical force that makes her feel trapped in the original
position, shoulders raised and wrist pinned as though her body were still there on the pavement.
When those images surface, she can taste that mint gum again, and the scene comes with a bodily
reenactment so vivid that she feels her posture lock into the same angle it had under his hand. The
memory is unreal in one moment and bodily immediate in the next, which leaves her disoriented and
desperate to push it away. To do that, she often tries to leave her body deliberately or blank out,
and she ruminates over prevention for hours, replaying the route home, the streetlights, the bus
schedule, the angle of his approach, all in search of the mistake that might explain it. The more
she tries to blank out or think her way backward through every detail, the more the memory seems to
sharpen: the taste returns sooner, the shoulder grab feels closer, and her certainty that the world
is dangerous hardens. In turn, that threat makes her scan for footsteps, anticipate touch, and brace
against any delay, because her mind has learned to expect danger in the smallest interruption.

At work, Leilani has begun taking longer routes between meetings to avoid narrow corridors where
someone might come up behind her, and she lingers at her desk until the office quiets rather than
leaving with colleagues and risking a crowd. She catches herself monitoring the sound of footsteps
in the carpeted hallway outside the accounting suite, then spends the evening going over what she
should have done differently that night. Those efforts to control the fear have not brought relief;
instead, they have kept the memory active, made her more certain that she is to blame, and left her
even less able to trust that she is safe. What finally brought her in was the realization that she
had started avoiding the walk home altogether and asking her husband to meet her at the building
entrance, not because she believed he could protect her, but because she no longer felt able to
stand in the lobby and wait. She came hoping to stop living as if every set of footsteps might be
his, and fearing that if she could not, she would keep shrinking her world until it felt as
dangerous as the night of the mugging.
