in a quiet suburban neighborhood, first sought care months after a devastating event involving

person’s body in distress, details that now stayed with him with awful vividness. In the aftermath,
what unsettled him most were not only the images of what had happened, but the way the loss had
seemed to strip him of any sense of being a man who could be relied on. He had come to believe he
was weak, that he had failed to protect others when it mattered most, and that he now felt more like
an object carried by events than a person with any real agency.

That sense of weakness shaped the way Kaleo remembered the event. When he tried to explain it to his
adult children or to a coworker who asked why he seemed distant, he would lose the thread halfway
through a sentence and stop, embarrassed and angry with himself. He would begin with a clear fact,
then drift into silence as if the memory itself were breaking apart under the pressure of speaking.
The more he noticed that his story would not come out cleanly, the more certain he became that
something was wrong with him, that a stronger person would be able to tell it plainly. And when the
memory came on its own, it did not arrive as a calm recollection but with fragments of sound
attached to it — bits of what had been said in the frightening moment replaying in his head — along
with an overwhelming grief that hit him in the chest and left him feeling raw and exposed. Each time
that happened, it confirmed his belief that he had not protected the person he loved, and that he
was somehow less than he should have been.

Those appraisals also made ordinary reminders in his daily life land with unusual force. As a nanny,
Kaleo spent his days in homes where children scraped knees, complained about sore feet, or reached
for a parent with a cut finger, and specific body parts would seize his attention in a way he could
not control. Seeing a child’s hands, legs, or face in a moment of hurt could bring a flash of the
original event into the room with him, because those details had become tied to his conviction that
he had been powerless when another body needed him. He found himself watching more closely than he
used to, almost reflexively scanning for signs that someone might be about to lose a loved one or
need protecting in a way he might fail to provide. Conversations about families grieving, or even
casual talk about people losing someone they cared about, also set him on edge. What should have
sounded like ordinary sympathy instead felt like a direct summons to the guilt he already carried,
and he would become quiet, staring at the floor, trying not to picture the loved one he could not
save.

Over the following weeks and months, Kaleo began narrowing his life around these reactions. He
avoided talking about the event because once he started, the grief and broken memories rose so
quickly that he could not finish. He gave short, vague answers when friends or his ex-wife asked how
he was doing, and he became increasingly withdrawn, especially in settings where someone might bring
up bereavement or family loss. At work, he kept his composure for the children, but he noticed
himself bracing whenever a child cried or a parent mentioned an accident, and that constant
vigilance exhausted him. He was ashamed of how jumpy and fragile he had become. The more he noticed
these reactions, the more he judged himself for them, and the more he felt like a weak person who
had been reduced to moving through the world without dignity.

By the time he came for help, the pattern had begun to affect the life he had built after divorce.

people who might have noticed how badly he was struggling. What finally brought him in was the fear
that the memories were becoming easier to trigger, not harder — that a glimpse of a child’s injured
hand or a conversation about someone’s loss could pull him back into the same wave of grief and
self-blame. Kaleo said he wanted relief, but what he feared most was that he would stay trapped
feeling like an object defined by what he had not been able to do, and that he would never again be
able to speak about the event without losing his way.
