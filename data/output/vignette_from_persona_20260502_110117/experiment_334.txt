in order. She remembered the sudden crush of bodies, the sharp metallic smell in the air, shouting

to move. As a City Council Member, she had been expected to stay visible and composed, but in the
weeks afterward that sense of public responsibility became tangled with fear. She began to believe
that the world was a dangerous place and that she had to be especially careful because you never
know what can happen next. The belief did not stay abstract; it settled into her body and decisions.
Asha found herself on guard all the time, scanning crowds, watching people’s hands and faces, and
feeling that she had to identify escape routes before she could feel comfortable anywhere.

Her sense of threat was strongest at night. Even in her apartment, she would pause by the door, note
the window latches, and look down the hallway before she allowed herself to relax, though she rarely
did. Unfamiliar places were worse. When council work required her to attend community meetings in
neighborhoods she did not know well, she would refuse if she could, saying she needed the details in
advance, though what she meant was that she could not assess the danger. If she could not map a room
or a route out of it, she felt exposed and trapped in the same way she had felt during the disaster.
That sense of threat fed a darker conclusion: if danger could appear anywhere and at any moment,
then the world really was unsafe, and she could not afford to let her guard down even briefly.

Those appraisals shaped her days. Asha avoided physical stress because she had come to associate a

depleted. She also became relentless in the small rituals of protection that organized her movements
through the city. At work she sat where she could see entrances, kept her back away from open
spaces, and constantly scanned the environment for signs of threat. In meetings she watched for the
kind of person who might become dangerous, her attention drifting from the agenda to exits,
doorways, and anyone moving too quickly.

Over time, those habits narrowed her life and made the world feel even more threatening. Because she
was always checking, always braced, she noticed every unfamiliar sound, every raised voice, every
unexpected movement, and each one seemed to confirm that her caution was necessary. The more she
avoided unfamiliar places and physical strain, the less she felt she could tolerate them, and the
less she trusted herself to manage if something went wrong. She found herself sitting through
council sessions with her shoulders rigid, calculating the quickest way out before she could focus
on the discussion. At night, the apartment felt less like a refuge than a place where she had to
remain alert to prove she was safe. She slept lightly, waking at the slightest noise, and would lie
in the dark thinking that she could not afford to miss anything. The pattern made her increasingly
isolated; she stopped accepting invitations after hours, and friends noticed she was always the
first to leave.

By the time she sought help, Asha was exhausted by the effort of staying safe. She had continued to

her in was the fear that the vigilance itself was taking over her life, yet she remained most afraid
that if she stopped scanning, stopped planning every exit, or walked into the wrong place
unprepared, something terrible would happen and she would not be able to protect herself.
