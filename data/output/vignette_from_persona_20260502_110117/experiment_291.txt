before anything was said. As a boy, he learned to read the air for signs of what was coming, and

rising too fast, his own mind going blank. Sometimes he can picture it, but it feels numb and

him unsettled, and it feeds the private conclusion that he has permanently changed for the worse,
that whatever happened to him never really ended and that his reactions now mean something is deeply
wrong with him.

other people have done to him, replaying old slights and humiliations with a sharp, resentful edge,
and he also turns the blame inward, spending long stretches of time asking himself what he should
have done differently. Because he has come to believe people cannot be trusted, he watches everyone

makes the alarm feel even more ominous, as if the very fact that he is startled or uneasy is proof

brushes against him unexpectedly on the crowded subway, in a fitting room, or while pinning fabric

The memories and the threat feed each other in a way Calvin finds exhausting. When he is startled by

pieces, not as a coherent narrative but as flashes that carry the same sickening feeling of
helplessness. Those fragments do not settle things; they make the danger feel current, and the

he assumes the worst about his own mind.

habits also keep him from being alone with the images long enough for them to form. The problem is
