Mariana, a 26-year-old Latin American student, came to treatment after months in which everyday life
had narrowed around a single toxic exposure that still felt lodged in her body. The event happened
in a university science building where she had gone late in the evening to collect materials for a
class project; a chemical spill in an enclosed room left the air sharp and antiseptic, and she
remembered the sudden sting in her throat, the confusion of not knowing whether to run or hold
still, and the way the dark hallway outside seemed to swallow the sound of her own breathing. Since
then, darkness had become loaded for her in a very specific way: dark spaces, waking in the night,
and even the faint hospital or disinfectant smell in a clinic corridor could pull her back to that
room. When those reminders appeared, Mariana found herself avoiding the site of the event whenever
campus routes took her near the building, and she began trying to erase the memory of what happened
by forcing herself not to think about it, not to talk about it, and not to let the images settle
long enough to be named. At the same time, she would constantly scan the environment for signs of
threat, her eyes moving to exits, vents, and the faces of people around her as if one missed detail
could mean another exposure. She described a body that stayed tense and ready to react to danger,
and she was startled with alarm if someone touched her unexpectedly, especially in dim light or in a
hallway that smelled faintly clinical.

Mariana could tell what had happened, but she could not put it into words in a way that felt
coherent. When she tried to explain it, she would say, “I know it happened, but I can’t say it
right,” and the sequence of the night dissolved into fragments: the room, the smell, the pressure in
her chest, then a blank gap, then the memory of her hands shaking. At times, when the memory
returned, she felt physical pain in the same parts of her body as during the event, as if her throat
and chest were tightening again in real time. Because the pieces would not line up, she came to
believe something was wrong with her as a person, not just with what had happened to her. She became
convinced that her body had turned against her, that it kept reacting as if she were still in danger
even when she was safe, and that she could no longer trust her own body to know the difference.
Those appraisals made the darkness and the hospital-like smell even more potent; they confirmed her
fear that her body was unreliable and that any unfamiliar sensation might mean disaster. They also
made the memory harder to bear, because each failed attempt to organize it left her feeling
defective and ashamed, and the shame pushed her back toward erasing it again.

Over time, the vigilance itself began to feed the sense of threat. Mariana’s constant scanning kept
her on edge, and the more she watched for danger, the more every dark corner, every nighttime
awakening, and every antiseptic trace seemed to signal that something bad was close. She would brace
herself before entering her apartment after sunset, hesitate in unlit stairwells, and avoid staying
late on campus. Even ordinary touch could jolt her, and the bodily alarm that followed only
strengthened her belief that she was still vulnerable. When the memory surfaced during these
moments, it came back in disordered pieces, with the old pain in her body and the same difficulty
finding words, and that return of sensation reinforced her conviction that she was broken and that
her body could not be trusted. She withdrew from classmates and stopped going near the building
where the spill had occurred, which helped her feel briefly safer but also shrank her world and kept
her attention fixed on danger. Mariana noticed that the more she tried to avoid the reminders, the
more intense the reminders seemed when they did appear, and the more certain she became that the
event had permanently changed her.

By the time she sought help, Mariana was sleeping badly because nighttime and waking in the dark
felt intolerable, she was arranging her days around routes that would keep her away from the event
site, and she was exhausted by the effort of keeping the memory out of reach. She wanted relief from
the constant alarm in her body, but what brought her most urgently to treatment was the fear that
she would never again be able to trust her own reactions, or herself.
