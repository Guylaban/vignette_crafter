Yousef was a 30-year-old divorced tax inspector who had come to treatment after months of feeling as
though his life had narrowed to the distance between his apartment, his desk at work, and the phone
in his hand. The collision that changed things had happened on an evening drive home after work,
when a car coming through an intersection struck his vehicle hard enough to spin it sideways and
leave him trapped amid the smell of fuel, shattered glass, and a sharp alcohol odor from the other
driver. He had survived with injuries that healed, but the experience stayed in him as a series of
vivid fragments: the crumpled cars, the jolt of impact, the sound of metal, and the sense that
ordinary driving had become a place where death could arrive without warning. Even now, if he saw
cars hit by accident in a news clip or passed a similar model on the road, the scene would jump back
with such force that he felt pulled straight into it again. The smell of alcohol could do the same,
collapsing the present into that night, and for a moment he would feel as if danger were right
beside him. Those moments left him immediately on guard, scanning for what might come next, and his
body reacted before his mind could catch up; an unexpected horn, a door slam, even a dropped stack
of files in the office could make him startle in a way that embarrassed him and then kept him
watchful for the rest of the day.

After the crash, Yousef began to tell himself that he could not rely on himself anymore. He had once
seen himself as disciplined, practical, and competent — the sort of man who handled numbers,
deadlines, and responsibility without hesitation — but now he felt no longer capable of doing the
things that had defined him. He noticed that he was driving more tensely, second-guessing himself at
every turn, and each time he felt shaken he took it as proof that something in him had broken. He
was also certain that he would never be able to feel normal emotions again; if he felt fear, he read
it as weakness, and if he felt flat or distant, he read that too as damage. Those beliefs pushed him
away from situations that might stir them up. He stopped driving except when absolutely necessary
and refused to be a passenger in other people’s cars, even declining offers from relatives who
wanted to help. He began organizing his days around avoiding roads, traffic, and the possibility of
being trapped in a vehicle, and that avoidance spread into smaller habits too: he would spend long
hours on his phone, games, or the internet to keep his mind occupied, and he often restricted food
because appetite felt like another body signal he could not trust. The more he withdrew, the more he
noticed how little control he seemed to have, and that fed the belief that he was not the man he had
been.

At work, the consequences became harder to hide. As a tax inspector, Yousef used to spend long hours
visiting businesses, riding between sites, and moving confidently through the city; now he arranged
routes to avoid busy roads and felt his chest tighten whenever a similar car appeared beside him in
traffic. The office itself was not free of reminders: a report about a collision, a colleague
mentioning a drinking weekend, or the sudden honk of a delivery truck outside could send his
attention snapping outward, searching for threat. Because he was always scanning, he found it harder
to concentrate on case files, and because he had been spending so much time online to escape his
thoughts, he arrived tired and irritable, which only confirmed to him that he was failing. He
started to feel that his routines were proving his own inadequacy. What frightened him most was that
each time he withdrew into the phone, skipped meals, or avoided the road, he came away feeling even
less capable and even more on edge, and that heightened state made the world seem full of cues that
could drag him back into the crash. A similar car on the street, the odor of alcohol at a family
gathering, or the sudden bang of a closing door could set off the same chain again, and he would
tense, scan, and recoil, then escape further into distraction and restriction to get through the
day. By the time he sought help, Yousef was exhausted by the cycle and ashamed of how much his life
had shrunk. He said he wanted to drive his own life again, but he was most afraid that the crash had
permanently taken away the man he used to be.
