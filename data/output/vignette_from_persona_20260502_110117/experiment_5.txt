feeling as if the danger he had fled had followed him home. Before the trauma, he had volunteered as
a relief worker in a war zone, helping distribute supplies and move injured civilians through

men in military uniforms. He remembered the hard stare of a policeman leaning close, the way he felt
pinned under being watched, and the sense that any wrong movement could make the situation turn
violent in an instant. He had been trying to do something useful, something that mattered, and yet
he left with the conviction that he had been helpless and had failed in his most important role in
life. From that point on, uniforms, weapons, and the sight of authority figures carried the weight
of that day, and even ordinary moments of being observed by strangers in public began to pull him
back into the same fear.

In the months that followed, Minh began to organize his life around not encountering those
reminders. At university he started choosing empty stairwells, sitting where no one could easily see
him, and keeping his head down if a classmate from before the deployment waved at him across campus.
He would leave lecture early rather than pass a security guard in a dark jacket, and if he saw
police near the student union or a veteran in a military-style uniform on the street, he would cross
to the other side or turn back entirely. Even making eye contact felt dangerous; he said it was as
if the other person might see through him and notice how unsettled he was. These moments were not
just uncomfortable to him—they seemed to confirm what he had come to believe about himself. He was
certain his body had betrayed him, that it kept reacting as if he were still in danger even when he
was safe, and that meant he could not trust himself to stay steady in the present. The more he
noticed his heart racing or his muscles tightening at the sight of a uniform or a watchful face, the
more convinced he became that he was fundamentally unsafe and unable to stop bad things from
happening to him.

That conviction sharpened the reminders themselves. When Minh felt watched in a classroom, or
noticed a police officer standing near the campus entrance, his mind would flash back to the
compound, the weapons, the uniforms, the sensation of being trapped under another person’s gaze.
Because he believed he had failed when it mattered most, the same scenes seemed to return with even
greater force, as if the world were proving him right. He began to anticipate the next moment of
scrutiny before it arrived, scanning windows, doorways, and faces for signs that someone was
observing him. In that state, ordinary encounters became potent triggers: a professor at the front
of the room, a security guard outside the library, a student in a military-style jacket. Each one
seemed to announce that he was once again the man who could not protect anyone, and the fear of
being seen made him feel as though he were already exposed and powerless.

By the time he sought help, Minh’s world had shrunk considerably. He had fewer friends on campus

power over him: that perhaps he had never really left the war behind, and that he might never be
able to live as a student, a friend, or simply a young man who could look others in the eye without
feeling hunted.
