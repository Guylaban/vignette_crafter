Layla, a 31-year-old divorced Middle Eastern woman working as a social media manager, came to
therapy after months of living as though her own life had become unsafe from the inside out. The
event she kept returning to happened in a crowded parking lot outside a café, when she was backing
her car out and struck a pedestrian she had not seen in her rear-view mirror. In the panic of
hearing the impact and seeing the person crumple, she had frozen, then called for help while shaking
so badly she could barely hold her phone. The injured man survived, but he required serious medical
care, and Layla has never been able to experience that moment as something finished. Since then, she
has been startled by unexpected touch, jerking when a coworker taps her shoulder at the office or
when her ex-husband’s hand brushes past her in handoff conversations, and she often reacts to
ordinary moments as if danger is unfolding even when nothing is wrong. What frightened her most was
not only that she had caused terrible harm, but that she no longer trusted herself to behave safely;
she had come to believe she could not rely on herself to do the right thing, and that belief made
the world feel filled with hidden threats. At times she looked at herself and felt unrecognisable,
as if the woman she had been before the accident had been replaced by someone brittle, afraid, and
permanently changed for the worse.

Her memory of the accident has remained fragmented and intensely sensory. The smell of hot asphalt,
car exhaust, and the coffee she had spilled in her lap will return abruptly when she thinks about
the crash, and with those smells she can feel the room narrowing around her again. Sometimes she can
describe the sequence only in broken pieces, knowing what happened but being unable to find words
that make the scene feel real enough to speak aloud, and in those moments she goes strangely blank,
recalling the event as if it happened to a stranger. That numbness has not protected her; instead,
it has deepened her fear that she is cut off from the woman who caused the injury and from any
version of herself she can trust. Because she cannot fully narrate the memory, she finds herself
replaying it in silence, trying to decide where she failed and whether a different person would have
seen the pedestrian in time. The less she can put it into words, the more she doubts her own
judgment and the more convinced she becomes that she has permanently changed for the worse.

Over the following months, those doubts and that fragmented memory kept feeding her sense that
danger might appear without warning. At work in a busy digital marketing office, where colleagues
often come up behind her while she is editing posts or checking campaign analytics, she stays tense
in her chair and flinches at a hand on her arm, already braced for catastrophe. The smell of burnt
coffee from the office kitchen or the sharp chemical scent of the parking garage can pull her back
into the crash in a flash, and when that happens her body reacts before her mind catches up: her
heart races, she scans for what is wrong, and she feels the same surge of fear and horror she had on
the day of the accident. Those alarm reactions make her even more certain that something in her has
been damaged, because she experiences them as proof that she is no longer dependable. That
conviction in turn keeps the memory close and vivid, as though her mind is searching the scene again
and again for the moment she should have known better, and the more she searches, the more her body
stays on guard.

Layla began avoiding situations that reminded her of the accident, especially driving in crowded
areas, and her world shrank around routes she thinks she can manage. She has limited her social
life, partly because she is embarrassed by how easily she startles and partly because she does not
want anyone to see how much guilt and shame she carries. At times she wonders if her divorce was
hastened by the way she withdrew, becoming watchful and self-protective, unable to explain why a
casual touch or a familiar street could leave her overwhelmed. She finally sought help after another
near-miss in the office parking lot, when a coworker reached around her car to close the door and
she jerked away with such panic that she had to sit in the driver’s seat until she stopped shaking.
What brought her in was the fear that this narrow, frightened version of herself will become
permanent, and what she most hopes for is to trust her own mind and body again without feeling that
the accident is still happening every time someone comes too close.
