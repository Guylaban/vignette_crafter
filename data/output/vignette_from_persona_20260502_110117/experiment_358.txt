Martin Ellis was 67, a school principal in a midsized American suburb, a husband of forty years, and
by his own account a man who had once prided himself on being steady under pressure. That certainty
was shaken on a winter evening when a pickup truck ran a red light and T-boned his sedan, spinning
him into the median hard enough to make the airbags explode across his face and chest. In the
seconds after impact he heard metal shrieking, shouted fragments from people outside, and the sudden
slam of other cars braking nearby. The collision was life-threatening enough that, in the days
afterward, the ordinary sight of cars hit by accident on the roadside, a burst of shouting, or any
loud noise would seize him by the chest before he could reason with himself. Those moments did not
just startle him; they pulled him straight back into the crash, and he would feel the terrifying
scene coming over him again in flashes he could not shut off.

What Martin found most unsettling was that the memory would not stay in words. He could describe
that he had been hit, but the frightening event never settled into a tidy story he could tell his
wife or the therapist he finally saw. Instead it returned like a film, over and over, with the
crushed hood, the violent jolt, and the helplessness of knowing he could not stop what was
happening. At times, when he tried to think about it, he felt as if it were happening right now, not
years ago, and that reliving left him with the conviction that he had been permanently changed for
the worse. He also compared himself bitterly to other people he knew, certain somebody else would
not have gotten into such a situation, and that thought deepened his sense that he had become a
lesser version of himself.

From there, his days narrowed. Martin came to believe the world was a dangerous place and that
danger could appear without warning, so he began watching for signs of it everywhere. If a hallway
announcement at school lasted longer than expected, if a student was late to an appointment, or if a
phone call dragged on without an answer, he assumed something bad was happening. The same dread
spread into nights at home, where he could not relax even in his own living room, and sleep became
broken and shallow. When he felt the alarm rising, he forced himself to think only positive
thoughts, ate excessively to take the edge off, and told himself these were small disciplines that
should help. In practice, they only gave him a brief sense of control; by pushing away the fear and
filling himself with food, he became even more on guard for the next hint of trouble.

That vigilance did not calm him. Martin found himself scanning for shouting in the school building,
flinching at loud noises from dropped books or slamming doors, and dreading any scene that reminded
him of a crash. If he heard raised voices from the front office or saw a wreck on the morning
commute, the old images came back with more force, and the return of those images confirmed his
belief that the world was dangerous and that he was damaged. The more that happened, the more he
avoided going to work unless absolutely necessary, rearranging meetings and stepping away from the
building whenever he could. He was embarrassed by how often he checked the parking lot before
crossing it and how tense he felt moving through normal routines, but he also felt he had no better
option.

Over the following months, the cycle hardened. Each time Martin stayed home, skipped an unnecessary
commitment, or swallowed his fear with another round of positive self-talk and food, he told himself
he was managing. Yet the relief was short-lived, and the avoidance left him more isolated from the
school he had led for years. It also fed the very beliefs that haunted him: if he could not cope
with an ordinary workday, perhaps he really had been permanently altered; if a delayed text or a
loud hallway noise could set off panic, perhaps he was right that danger was everywhere. His
memories kept returning in vivid, wordless bursts, his body stayed keyed for the next threat, and
the more he lived around those reactions, the more brittle his world became.

By the time Martin sought help, his wife had become concerned that he was disappearing into
himself—sleeping poorly, eating too much in the evenings, and avoiding the school whenever possible.
He came in because he was tired of living as though every delay were an omen and every sudden noise
a warning, and because he feared he was losing the life he had built. What he hoped for, and what
frightened him most, was the possibility that he might never again feel safe enough to stand in a
classroom, hear a shout, or drive through an intersection without the crash rushing back.
