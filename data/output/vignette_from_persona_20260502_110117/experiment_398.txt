Minh Tran was 74 and had spent most of his working life as a welder, a man accustomed to heat,
sparks, and the steady focus of hands at work. When he finally agreed to come to therapy, it was
with his wife’s encouragement after years of saying he was “fine” and should not dwell on the past.
He described a civilian life in a war zone in which an ordinary day had turned catastrophic: he had
been outside near a road when vehicles approached, their engines loud and sudden, and then the air
changed around him as he realized he was exposed and unable to get to cover. In the chaos that
followed, he could still remember the raw sensation of not being able to breathe properly, as if his
chest had locked, and that breathlessness has remained one of the sharpest gateways back into that
day. Even now, a car passing too close to the house or idling outside the workshop can make his body
jerk with alarm. He also notices that any related media content—news footage, a documentary, a brief
clip on television—can pull him back before he has time to prepare, and he finds himself reaching
for old habits to shut it down.

Minh’s first response to those moments is to redirect his mind elsewhere. He will force himself to
think of something else, often the next repair job, a grocery list, or a memory from years before
the war, and he has become careful not to talk with others about the event or what happened
afterward. In his mind, speaking about it would only stir things up and make the images stronger.
That reluctance is tied to what he has come to believe about the event: that it was deeply unfair,
that the people responsible have faced no consequences, and that somehow he himself was at fault
because of the way he acted in those moments of danger. He keeps returning to that self-blame,
asking whether he should have moved sooner, hidden better, or understood the threat earlier. The
same beliefs make him wary of open or exposed spaces where he feels vulnerable, including the
parking lot outside his apartment building and the wide stretch between his truck and the welding
shop entrance, where he feels too visible and too easy to catch off guard.

The memory itself remains badly fragmented for him. He says he is only remembering parts of what
happened and the rest is missing; what he does have feels like a set of pieces without a full
picture. At times the memory feels like it belongs to someone else rather than to him, and he is
struck by how extreme it was, almost too hard to believe it actually happened to him. That estranged
quality makes him uneasy when the memory surfaces, because it seems both vivid and unreal at once.
When the fragments come, they are often enough to bring back the car sounds, the breathlessness, and
the sense of being out in the open, and he finds himself avoiding those reminders before they can
gather strength. He moves away from the street, shortens errands, and keeps to routes that let him
hug walls or stay near buildings. On workdays he prefers the places in the shop where he can see the
door and the yard, and he avoids tasks that would require him to stand in exposed spaces for long.

The more he tries to push the memory away, the more unfinished it remains in his mind. Because it
stays broken and uncertain, his mind returns to the same questions of blame and injustice, and those
thoughts keep his body on alert. He lies awake needing to stay vigilant, as if sleep would leave him
unprotected, and even when he is exhausted he has difficulty falling asleep. During the day he is
superalert and watchful, scanning cars as they pass and listening for changes in sound, and that
watchfulness has made concentrating harder, especially at work where a moment’s lapse with metal and
machinery could matter. On the days when he is most tense, the breathlessness itself becomes
frightening; if he feels short of breath walking from the lot or standing in the heat, it can bring
the memory closer again and sharpen his attention to every engine, every road noise, every open
space. In turn, the avoidance that follows—turning away from conversation, shunting thoughts aside,
leaving spaces quickly—has left him with only more isolated fragments, more unanswered anger, and a
stronger conviction that nothing has ever been made right.

By the time he sought help, Minh had become tired of living this way. He had noticed that his
marriage was being narrowed by what he would not discuss, and that his days at the welding shop were
shaped by constant scanning and a need to position himself for escape. He wanted relief from the
cycle of car sounds, the breathlessness, the unreal fragments of memory, and the unfairness he still
carried. What frightened him most was the possibility that this vigilance would never let up; what
he hoped for was simply to sleep without standing guard and to feel that the memory belonged to the
past rather than to every road he crossed.
