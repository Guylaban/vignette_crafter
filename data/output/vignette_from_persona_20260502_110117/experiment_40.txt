hospital, a role that had once given her a sense of purpose and calm competence. Her husband had
long since grown used to her long shifts and her habit of moving briskly from one task to the next,

changed after a military deployment many years earlier, when she was working in a conflict zone as

rush, shouted commands, boots on hard ground, and one soldier repeatedly standing in the doorway
while she tried to work. Aisha could still remember the unbearable feeling of being watched as she
bent over the patient, the sense that the soldier’s gaze pinned her in place and that any mistake
would bring consequences she could not manage. What frightened her most now was that the memory did
not stay in the past; it lived on in her body whenever she found herself alone with a man, when a
uniform came into view, or when someone seemed to be looking at her too closely.

After that experience, Aisha became constantly watchful. She found herself scanning faces, doorways,
and exits without meaning to, and she was irritated by how quickly her body reacted to sudden
movements from other people, as though she needed only a flash of motion to become immediately on
guard. Even in places she knew were safe, she could not truly relax. At home she sat facing the
room, not the wall, and in public she noticed her shoulders tightening whenever a man stood near her
or someone in uniform crossed her path. At work, the hospital corridors were filled with triggers
she could not avoid entirely: a security guard in a dark jacket, a maintenance worker stepping into
a doorway, a physician’s abrupt turn toward her at the nurses’ station. Those moments stirred the
same state of alertness she had known during the deployment, and she felt herself bracing before she
had time to think. She had come to believe that danger could appear without warning, and that if she
let her attention drift for even a moment, she might miss the one sign that would protect her.

To manage that sense of threat, Aisha threw herself into work and tasks with unusual intensity. She
volunteered for extra shifts, stayed late to finish charts, reorganised supply cupboards, and moved
from one chore to another until there was no quiet moment left for her thoughts to catch up with
her. She also made sure never to be alone with another person if she could help it, especially not
with men; she arranged to have nurses or clerks nearby, asked for doors to remain open, and chose
crowded hallways over empty ones. In the hospital and at home she was vigilant for dangerous people,
watching how a person walked, how quickly they turned, whether they wore a uniform, whether they
were too close. These habits gave her a short-lived sense of control, but they also kept the world
looking full of possible threats. Because she was always watching, she noticed more things that
frightened her: a man waiting outside an exam room, a uniformed officer at the entrance, a stranger
pausing behind her with no explanation. The more she organised her life around not being cornered or
surprised, the more often she found herself encountering exactly the cues that set her on edge, and
each new scare confirmed her sense that vigilance was necessary.

between her and another overwhelming moment. She came to treatment hoping to regain some ability to
relax in her own home and at work, and fearing that if she stopped scanning the world so carefully,
she would miss the next sign of danger.
