Mai, a 57-year-old shopowner from a South Eastern Asian family, described the start of her
difficulties as the afternoon her son nearly died in hospital. He had been admitted suddenly with a
life-threatening illness, and for days she watched him hooked to drips, needles taped into his arm,
his skin pale beneath the fluorescent lights while staff moved briskly around him. She remembered
the blood in the tubing and on the gauze, and how every medical procedure seemed to pull the same
terrible thought through her body: that she had not protected him. Even now, when she tried to tell
it, she could say only fragments. She knew what happened, but the details would not stay in words;
sometimes the full force of the fear and guilt rose in her chest without any clear image at all.
Already living on edge, she found that almost anything could tip into a warning — a raised voice
near the counter, a flash of white coat in the clinic window by her shop, even the smell of
disinfectant drifting from the medical building down the street. On the nights when she lay awake
feeling that same danger again, the memory came back hardest, as though the sense of threat pulled
her straight into the ward. When the threat felt strongest, the hospital scene seemed to arrive of
its own accord, as if her mind dragged her back there whenever she was most braced for danger. The
more on guard she became, the less able she was to keep the memory at bay. The more unsafe she felt,
the more her mind seemed to drag her back to the hospital scene so she could not relax. In those
moments, the hospital scene returned only in pieces — the drip stand, the needle tray, the smell of
antiseptic — and those fragments were enough to make her feel again that she had failed her son and
that the people who should have protected him had let him down. Once the memory had surfaced, she
began to notice medical-shaped things everywhere: the television news about hospitals, a customer’s
bandaged hand, the pharmacy queue near her shop, each one turning into a fresh reminder before she

more certain to her, as if her fear was proof that she had been right to doubt herself. Each
