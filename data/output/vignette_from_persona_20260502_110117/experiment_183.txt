Marisol Alvarez was 48, a single social ministry worker who spent her days moving between parish
offices, community kitchens, and apartment visits across North American city neighborhoods, places
that had once felt familiar and purposeful. Her accident happened two years earlier on a wet
afternoon when a delivery van ran a red light and struck the driver’s side of the small car she was
riding in with a colleague. The impact spun the vehicle into traffic, pinned her body hard against
the seatbelt, and left her trapped while strangers shouted and glass crackled around her. What
stayed with her most was the complete loss of control: being told to stay still, not to move, not to
touch the door, while her own body shook and refused to feel reliable. In the months afterward, she
found herself revisiting the scene in fragments whenever she saw a car crumpled at the curb or a
news image of a collision; even the sight of a dented fender could flood her with the same rush of
alarm, as if the crash were about to happen again. She came to believe that what happened to her was
deeply unfair and that nothing had been done about it, and that conviction made it harder to trust
reassurance from anyone. Friends and coworkers could tell her she was safe, but she could not
believe them; she was certain that bad things could still happen without warning and that she could
not stop them. From there she began to live on alert, scanning exits as soon as she entered a church
hall, clinic waiting room, or ministry office, and jumping at ordinary sounds such as a dropped pan
or a slammed car door.

Marisol’s sense that her body could betray her also changed the way she moved through daily life.
She had once prided herself on endurance, on pushing through long volunteer shifts and late
meetings, but now any feeling of being physically unwell — a wave of nausea, a flutter in her chest,
even ordinary tiredness — could carry her straight back to the accident. That fear made her avoid
physical stress in almost any form. She stopped taking stairs when she could, turned down
assignments that required hauling boxes of donated food, and began building her workday around the
assumption that exertion was dangerous. She also started avoiding relaxation because it felt unsafe
to let her guard down; lying still on the couch, closing her eyes, or sitting too long in silence
left her feeling exposed, as if danger would arrive while she was unprepared. The more she
structured her life around these rules, the smaller it became. She no longer drove to visits and
rarely accepted a ride from coworkers, even when the routes were short and familiar, and that
avoidance began to shape the practical and relational texture of her work. She missed outreach sites
on the far side of town, depended on others for transportation, and found herself declining
opportunities that would have meant sitting in a car or being a passenger. Yet the same efforts that
made her feel protected kept reminding her of the accident and of what she feared most: every time
she had to pass a parking lot, see a stalled vehicle, or wait near traffic, the memory of the crash
returned more sharply, and the conviction that her body was no longer trustworthy grew stronger.

By the time she sought help, Marisol’s days were organized around staying ahead of danger. She would
enter a room and immediately check where the doors were, where she might get out, and who was near
her, because she believed safety could vanish in an instant. Even then, she remained unable to
settle; one sharp noise could make her flinch, and the tension she carried kept her from resting.

that she was now living in a constant state of watchfulness. She came hoping for relief, but most of
all she feared that if she let her guard drop, even for a moment, she would be forced again into the
same helplessness she had never been able to forget.
