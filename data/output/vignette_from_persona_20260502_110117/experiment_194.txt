Elena had been working a late shift at the community counseling center when the accident happened,
the kind of evening she still could not bring herself to describe in a straight line. A client had
arrived in visible distress, and in the rush of moving through the waiting area Elena had acted on
instinct, turning too quickly and colliding with another visitor near the door. The fall, the
impact, the immediate cry of pain, the sight of paramedics kneeling on the linoleum, all seemed to
her afterward to come in shattered fragments rather than a continuous sequence. She remembered the
bright ambulance lights reflected in the glass, a shoe lying on its side, someone saying the word
fracture, and the sickening realization that the injury was serious. Even months later, when she
tried to tell the story, she could not make it into a clear account; it remained a series of
disconnected pieces that never quite fit together, and each attempt to recall it brought back an
overwhelming grief that felt almost physical.

In the first weeks after the incident, Elena found herself trying hard not to think about what had
happened, because any deliberate contact with the memory seemed to open the door to the same
unbearable wave of shame and sorrow. She stopped looking people in the eye at work, keeping her gaze
on charts, shoes, or the edge of her desk, as if direct eye contact might expose what she believed
was written plainly across her face. Hospitals, ambulances, and even routine physical examinations
became intolerable; she would tense before driving past the emergency entrance near her home, and
she began avoiding any setting that reminded her of the hospital corridor where she had watched the
injured person be lifted onto a stretcher. At home, her husband noticed that she would go quiet
whenever the day’s events threatened to drift toward that night, and she would steer the
conversation away with a practiced, strained calm.

Elena had come to believe that the accident happened because of the way she acted, and that she
should have protected others and failed. Those beliefs settled into something harsher over time: she
was certain that if she had been more careful, more attentive, more worthy of the role she held, no
one would have been hurt. That self-blame kept her on edge. She slept badly, lying awake long after
her husband had turned out the light, or waking in the dark with her heart already racing, convinced
she needed to scan the apartment, the street outside, or the condition of everyone around her. The
more she judged herself, the more vigilant she became, and the more vigilant she became, the more
every unexpected sound, every siren in the distance, every memory of a waiting room or gurney felt
like proof that danger was still close by.

Her efforts to push the event away only seemed to leave the memories more fragmented. When a
fragment surfaced—a hand gripping a metal chair, the sound of someone sobbing, the blur of a uniform
sleeve—she would hurry to suppress it, but that left her with even less confidence in what had
actually happened and less ability to place it in order. The gaps made her more watchful, because
not knowing exactly how things unfolded felt, to her, like a warning that something equally
catastrophic could happen again without her seeing it coming. That watchfulness then made her avoid
anything that might sharpen the memory further. She took longer routes to work to avoid the hospital
district, became careful not to meet clients’ eyes when they were upset, and began declining
meetings that involved medical referrals or physical-health discussions. Each avoidance bought her a
momentary easing of panic, yet it also kept the event unprocessed, leaving the pieces just as jagged
and the threat just as present. In that state, nothing felt meaningful anymore; she moved through
her counseling days with a dutiful flatness that frightened her almost as much as the memory itself.

By the time she sought help, Elena was exhausted by the cycle of sleeplessness, hypervigilance,
guilt, and retreat. What finally brought her in was the growing fear that she was losing the part of
herself that had once made her feel useful, and the distress of noticing how much of her work and
her marriage had narrowed around keeping the accident at bay. She hoped therapy might help her
understand what had happened without being overwhelmed by it, but what frightened her most was that
if she let herself remember fully, she would be forced to see herself as the person who caused the
injury and failed to prevent it.
