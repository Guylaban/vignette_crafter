At 60, Daniel, a European American warehouse worker living with his long-term partner, came to
treatment carrying a history that still felt lodged in his body more than three decades after his
military deployment. He described one night in particular, when an operation went wrong and men he
had trained beside were killed while he survived. What stayed with him was not a clean narrative but
fragments: the glare of headlights on dust, shouted orders, the smell of fuel, and the sight of
uniforms torn and darkened. Since then, he had come to believe that surviving when others did not
made him somehow stained, as if there were something fundamentally wrong with him. He spoke in the
language of a man who no longer knew who he was, saying that he did not recognize himself anymore
and that the world had become dangerous in a way he could never quite brace against.

Those beliefs shaped what Daniel could and could not allow himself to do. He avoided going anywhere
near the area where the event had happened, even decades later, and would not drive past the old
base or the road that passed close to it. At work in the warehouse, he also avoided wearing anything
that reminded him of the fatigues and heavy gear from that period; even certain work shirts, with
their stiff fabric and muted colors, made him feel exposed and uneasy, so he kept to the same plain
clothes he had worn for years. He tried not to think about what happened, because when the memory
came, it did not feel like a recollection at all but like being pulled back into the same terror he
had felt then. That fear made him more certain that the world was unsafe and that he had somehow
failed the men who did not make it out, which in turn deepened his shame and sense that he no longer
understood his own character.

Daniel’s memory of the event had become painfully disorganized over time. He could say what
happened, but he often found himself unable to find the words, stopping mid-sentence and shaking his
head as if language itself had gone missing. When he did manage to remember, the guilt hit him hard;
he would lower his eyes and say he should have done more, should have died with them, should have
been the one to stay behind. The memory was not softened by years passing. It remained vivid enough
to bring back the same terror, and that terror made it harder for him to organize the story at all,
leaving him even more stuck with the sense that the event must have meant something terrible about
him. He noticed that whenever he touched on the memory, the old guilt and fear flooded back
together, and he would quickly turn away from it, certain he was safer not giving it words.

Over the years, Daniel’s efforts to keep distance from these feelings became increasingly organizing
features of his life. He would reroute his driving to avoid any area that resembled the old base,
and he felt a rising panic if a conversation turned toward military service or if he caught himself
remembering too much. In the warehouse, a glimpse of similar clothing on a veteran coworker or a
pile of khaki work uniforms in the locker room could make him tense and leave the area. But the more
he avoided reminders, the more alone he felt with the belief that he had survived when others had
not for no good reason, and the more dangerous the world seemed in his mind. The less he could
tolerate remembering, the less able he was to put the event into words, and that silence fed the
sense that something was wrong with him at the core. His partner had noticed him becoming more
distant and watchful, as if he was always bracing for something he could not name, and Daniel
himself admitted that he had started to feel like a stranger to his own life.

He finally sought help after a recent workday when a delivery route unexpectedly took him close to
the old military site. He felt his chest tighten, his mind flood with the same terror, and for the
rest of the day he could barely function at the warehouse, needing to keep to himself and leave
early. What brought him in was not only the intensity of that reaction, but the fear that he was
running out of ways to keep it contained. He was most afraid that if he kept avoiding the places,
thoughts, and clothes tied to the trauma, his life would shrink even further around the guilt and
fear he already carried. He also hoped, tentatively, that if he could finally find words for what
happened, he might discover that he was more than the man he had come to believe himself to be.
