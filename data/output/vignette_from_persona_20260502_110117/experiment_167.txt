that had once given her a steady place among familiar faces. She spoke of the war years as a time
when ordinary life had been torn open around her, when she was a civilian caught in a zone where
fire could appear without warning and where the ground itself seemed unsafe. She did not describe
the event in a neat sequence. Instead, she said that parts of it were simply missing no matter how
hard she tried to reach them, and that what remained came back with an eerie force, more vivid than
any ordinary memory. At times it felt to her as though the memory belonged to someone else, not to
the woman she had become, and that strangeness only deepened her sense that something had gone
terribly wrong with her own body and mind. She was haunted by the belief that there was something
about her that had made the event happen, and by a lasting, almost physical humiliation that she
could not shake. As she told it, the shame settled in early and never really lifted.

moved from task to task, filling quiet moments with prayer recitations, sorting objects, listening
to the television without really hearing it, anything that might stop the memory from rising up. She
also began checking the locks, doors, and windows over and over, especially in the evening, as if
careful repetition could keep disaster from finding her again. Those habits were tied to the way she
now understood herself: if something in her had invited danger once, she reasoned, then vigilance
was the least she owed herself. But the more she tried to stay ahead of fear, the more the fear
seemed to confirm her sense that her body could not be trusted. When she felt a sudden startle, a
weakness in her legs, or the old uncertainty in her chest, she took it as proof that she was
unreliable and vulnerable, and she pushed herself into even more checking and mental busyness.

Certain cues made the past break through despite her efforts. The smell of soil after rain, the
earthy scent of a garden path, could pull her abruptly back toward the war years, because the memory
she carried was bound up with the outdoor ground where she had been crouching and trying to stay
hidden. Fire or flames had a similar power; even a small candle flame could make her tense and
withdraw, because it echoed the burning she had witnessed and the terror that had surrounded it.
Unexpected touch was just as difficult. When someone reached for her without warning, she recoiled
sharply, and that reaction was not simply fear of the contact itself. It was the immediate jolt of
being thrown back into the memory, which felt so real that she responded as though it were happening
again. Her body’s reflexes and the intrusive vividness of the event worked together, and afterward
she would redouble her efforts to occupy her mind and control her surroundings. The more she forced
herself not to feel, the more she noticed the very sensations and images she feared, and she came to
believe again that her body was failing her.

Over time, the strategies that had once felt protective began narrowing her life. Mali avoided
starting new friendships or relationships, even in the warm religious circles she had once moved
through with ease. She told herself she did not have the strength for attachment and did not want to
burden anyone with her fear, but underneath that was the conviction that her shame would be obvious
if she allowed anyone too close. Keeping to herself also meant fewer surprises, fewer chances of an
unprepared touch, fewer moments when someone might ask about the war and bring the memory nearer.
Yet the isolation fed her sense that she was permanently marked by what had happened. In the quiet,
the memory returned with its strange clarity, and because she kept herself so busy and so guarded,
she had fewer ordinary experiences to balance it against. The trauma memory remained more real than
the rest of her life, and that imbalance made the humiliation feel fresh rather than past.

By the time she came for help, Mali was sleeping poorly, checking doors in her home before bed, and

the event had left her permanently damaged. She hoped, in coming, that someone might help her make
sense of the memory that felt both hers and not hers, and she feared that without help she would
continue living as though danger were still waiting just beyond the door.
