Daniel, a 24-year-old QA engineer of Latin American background, had been driving home from work on
an ordinary Thursday evening when a multi-car crash unfolded at an intersection a block from his
apartment. He had pulled over, hearing the screech of tires and the impact in quick succession, and
for a few disorienting minutes he stood on the curb while people shouted, smoke drifted from a
crumpled car, and others tried to help before emergency services arrived. What stayed with him most
was not only the chaos but his conviction that he had been close enough to do more. In the days
afterward, he kept returning to the moment in fragments, replaying the scene and asking himself how
life would have been different if he had reacted faster or done something that might have protected
the people involved. He found himself imagining alternate versions of that night with a painful,
almost compulsive insistence, as if mental rehearsal could somehow correct what had happened.

From the beginning, the crash became a lens through which Daniel made sense of other experiences.
Hearing friends talk casually about accidents, road closures, or “that crash on the highway” made
his body tense and his mind snap back to the intersection outside his building. He noticed the same
thing when news stories or TV segments touched on similar events: he could feel himself beginning to
detach, as though the room were receding or he were watching everything from a distance, and that
unreal, drifting sensation told him he needed to shut it off immediately. On his phone he started
skipping headlines about collisions and avoiding programs or films that might show wrecks,
ambulances, or bystanders trapped in a crisis. Even brief talk about accidents at lunch in the
office kitchen could send him back into the scene, and once the images started, he was caught in the
same painstaking review of what might have been different if he had only done more.

As the weeks passed, Daniel’s view of the event hardened into a set of grim conclusions about
himself and the world. He came to believe, with a kind of ashamed certainty, that he should have
protected others and failed them. That belief made every replay feel morally charged, not just
upsetting. It also fed a broader fear that had taken hold since the crash: you could never really
know who might harm you, and danger could appear without warning in what had seemed like an ordinary
street on an ordinary night. From there his thoughts slid toward a bleaker forecast for his own
life, the sense that nothing good could happen to him anymore, because whatever had once made the
future feel open now seemed suspended under the shadow of that evening. These appraisals did not
stay abstract; they colored his reactions whenever something reminded him of the crash. A news clip,
a coworker’s comment about a fender-bender, or the sight of flashing lights through his apartment
window could all intensify his certainty that he had been exposed to a world in which safety was
fragile and his own actions had been insufficient.

Daniel’s attempts to manage the distress ended up narrowing his world. He began avoiding the police
presence near the crash site and took longer, less direct routes to work if he thought he might pass
patrol cars or see an accident scene. At his QA job, where he spent long hours in a quiet open
office, he became hyperalert to anything on colleagues’ screens or in conversation that might
involve a car wreck or emergency response, and he would excuse himself when the topic came up. After
work, he increasingly stayed away from television altogether, not wanting to risk stumbling onto a
film or report with similar content, and the silence in his apartment became easier than the chance
of being flooded by images. Yet the more he avoided, the more the event dominated his inner life. He
kept measuring the night against the life he might have had if it had not happened, and the
comparison only deepened his conviction that something had been permanently stolen from him. That
sense of loss and failure sharpened his urge to withdraw further, which kept the crash feeling close
and unfinished.

By the time Daniel sought help, he was exhausted by the loop of replaying, avoiding, and judging
himself. He said he was no longer sure how to sit through a normal evening without scanning for
reminders of the crash or feeling that strange detached sensation begin in his chest and head. What
finally brought him to treatment was noticing that his avoidance was spreading into work and
relationships: he was turning down invitations, leaving shared spaces when accident talk came up,
and feeling increasingly cut off from people who had once made life feel predictable. He hoped
therapy might help him stop living as if the crash were about to happen again at any moment, though
what frightened him most was that he might be right—that nothing good was ahead, and that he would
never fully trust the world, or himself, again.
