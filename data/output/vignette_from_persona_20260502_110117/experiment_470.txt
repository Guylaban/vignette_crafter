Aisha, a 32-year-old South Asian woman who worked as a chaplain at a large urban hospital, had once
been known among her colleagues for her calm presence on the wards and her ease with people from
many backgrounds. That changed after she was detained and tortured overseas while visiting family,
an episode she still struggled to describe in any settled way. What she could say remained painfully
fragmentary: she knew what had happened, yet when she tried to put it into words, the account broke
apart before it could become a coherent story. In supervision, she would start with a few factual
details and then lose the thread, staring down at her hands as if the next sentence had vanished.
She would sometimes say, with visible frustration, that she could not find the words, that the
frightening event seemed to live in her body more than in language. Because she could not tell the
story clearly, the memories stayed raw and unfinished, returning to her as sensations rather than as
a narrated past. The sound of sharp voices in a corridor or a door striking shut could pull her back
to the room where she had been held, and the rigid smell and feel of institutional spaces made her
shoulders tense before she understood why. Even the sight of military or police uniforms in the
hospital lobby could make her stomach drop, as if authority itself were about to close around her
again.

In the months after she returned to the United States and resumed work, Aisha began arranging her
life around the places and people that might bring the event back into focus. She avoided open
spaces, crossing parking lots quickly with her keys clenched in her fist and choosing narrow routes
along walls or through crowded hallways where she did not feel exposed. She also pulled away from
former friends and acquaintances, especially people she had socialised with before the torture,
because seeing them stirred up the same wordless pressure inside her; their easy questions about her
trip, her marriage, or why she had become so distant threatened to bring the memory rushing in
before she could control it. At times, when the images and bodily panic became unbearable, she hurt
herself physically to stop thinking about what had happened. She described it not as wanting to die,
but as needing something immediate and concrete to overwhelm the flash of fear in her mind. Those
moments left her more ashamed and more unable to speak, and after them she found herself even less
able to explain the event to anyone. The memory remained locked in sensation and fragments, and each
failed attempt to tell it seemed to make it harder to shape into words.

The same pattern played out in her daily work. As a chaplain, Aisha was expected to move freely
through the hospital, sit with distressed families, and enter open atriums, waiting areas, and
conference rooms that now felt dangerously exposed to her. She began taking indirect routes through
the building and lingered in smaller offices whenever possible, quietly declining assignments that
would place her in large open spaces. Colleagues noticed that she no longer joined them for coffee
or lunch and that she kept conversations brief, especially with those she had known before the
trauma. She feared that if she stood too long in a bright open lobby, or if she was unexpectedly
brushed against and held in a crowded lift, the event would return with full force. The more she
avoided those moments, the narrower her world became, and the more the experience seemed to resist
being put into language. She would think, with a kind of exhausted certainty, that if she could only
describe it properly, it might become bearable, but every time she tried, the words slipped away and
she was left with the same mute horror.

By the time she sought help, Aisha was exhausted by how tightly her life had shrunk around what she
could not say. She had been holding the fear at bay through avoidance, silence, and occasional self-
harm, but the cost was mounting: isolation from people she once cared about, increasing difficulty
functioning at work, and a growing dread of any place that felt open, public, or controlled by
authority. What finally brought her in was the realization that she was no longer only trying to
survive the memory; she was losing her ability to live alongside it. What frightened her most was
that it might stay forever beyond words, and what she hoped for, even more than relief, was some way
to tell the story without being overwhelmed by it.
