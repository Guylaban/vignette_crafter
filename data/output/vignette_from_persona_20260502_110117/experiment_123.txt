Arjun was a 32-year-old South Asian housekeeper who had come to treatment after months of carrying
around a frighteningly clear memory of a mugging that still felt, in his body, as if it had just
happened. He had been walking home after an evening shift, cutting through a quieter side street
because it was faster, when two men stepped out from between parked cars. One grabbed his bag while
the other pinned him against a wall and shoved a forearm hard across his chest. Arjun remembered the
sharp sting in his ribs, the pressure in his neck, the roughness of the wall at his back, and the
flash of one man’s face leaning in too close. Even now, he could describe the scene in unusually
exact detail, down to the color of the jacket the second man wore and the sound of his own breathing
turning thin and shallow, yet when he tried to tell it to his wife or to the clinician, he would
stall and say that he knew what happened but could not find the words to say it cleanly. The memory
seemed to come back all at once, vivid and complete, and with it came a familiar tightening in his
chest and a painful ache through the same parts of his body that had been struck during the robbery.

In the weeks afterward, Arjun found that what he believed about other people changed the way the
memory lived in him. He had come to believe that people were not what they seemed, that strangers
could look ordinary and still be ready to hurt him, and that the world was a dangerous place that
could turn on him without warning. Because of that belief, any reminder of being trapped near parked
cars, any man stepping too quickly from behind a vehicle, even the sight of a narrow street outside
the apartment building where he lived with his wife, could pull the scene back with startling force.
At those moments the memory did not feel like a story from the past; it arrived with the same bodily
pain and the same helplessness, as if his mind were warning him that danger was still close by. The
more clearly the scene returned, the more certain he became that trust itself was foolish. He
watched people more closely, read their faces for signs of threat, and each fresh surge of the
memory seemed to confirm that his earlier sense of safety had been naïve.

That sharpened vigilance gradually narrowed Arjun’s life. As a housekeeper, he moved through other
people’s homes and apartment corridors, but now he avoided taking late assignments and became uneasy
when he had to carry cleaning supplies alone in unfamiliar buildings or walk to his car after dark.
He would choose longer routes, keep his keys in his hand before leaving a building, and scan every
lobby or doorway before stepping through it. Yet the effort to stay ahead of danger kept feeding
back into the same fear. When someone crossed close behind him in a hallway, the old memory rose
with its vivid details intact; he could feel the old pain, the same pressure in his chest, and his
mind immediately returned to the conviction that no one could be trusted. That belief made the
memory harder to put into words, because the moment he tried to explain what happened, the scene
came back too fast and too complete, leaving him tense and frustrated, convinced that language
itself could not contain how unsafe the world felt. Over time he became more withdrawn with
coworkers and less willing to stop and chat with neighbors, not because he wanted to be distant, but
because distance seemed like the only reliable protection.

By the time Arjun sought help, the robbery had become more than an event he remembered; it had
become the lens through which he organized ordinary life. His wife noticed that he was tense every

himself felt exhausted by how often the memory intruded and how quickly it pushed him back into the
belief that danger was everywhere and trust was misplaced. What finally brought him to treatment was
the fear that he was shrinking his world too far to keep himself safe, while still never feeling
safe at all. He hoped for relief from the pain that came with the memory and for some way to
believe, even a little, that not every person outside their door was a threat.
