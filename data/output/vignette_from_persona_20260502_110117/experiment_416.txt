Eleanor had spent most of her life at her kitchen table in a quiet suburb outside Cleveland,
checking specifications for a small manufacturing firm from home even after retirement because she
liked the precision of QA work and the predictability of it. That preference for order made sense to
her, given how her childhood had begun. Her earliest memories were of a father who could turn from
calm to brutal without warning, and she learned to track the smallest shift in his stance, the set
of his shoulders, the sound of his boots in the hall. When he struck her, there was no preparation,
only the sickening certainty that she had nowhere to go. Decades later, the old scenes could still
arrive in her mind with a photographic sharpness that startled her: the pattern of the wallpaper,
the stain on the carpet, the shape of his hand. Yet when she tried to speak about it, the words felt
unavailable, as if the whole thing sat behind glass. She could say that it happened, but not build a
sentence sturdy enough to hold it.

As a girl she had told herself she must have done something wrong, and that belief never fully left
her. She carried a deep shame that she had not stopped it, that she had not shouted louder, run
faster, or somehow protected herself. She had come to believe that bad things could happen to her
and that she would be powerless to prevent them. She also carried the conviction that someone she
trusted had betrayed her in the most basic way imaginable. Those appraisals returned whenever she
was reminded of the abuse, and they colored even the way she remembered it: sometimes she could
replay the events, but with a blankness that made her feel as if they had happened to a stranger,
not to herself. At other times, the memory was so vivid it seemed to thrust her back into the same
helpless child’s body.

In recent years, certain moments would ignite all of it at once. Funerals, cemeteries, and grief-
related events were especially difficult, because they brought her face to face with loss, silence,
and the sense that life could be abruptly taken away. At her sister’s graveside service, she found
herself scanning the people around her and felt her body react before she could think. Sudden
movements by others—someone reaching for a tissue, a man stepping back too quickly, a chair
scraping—put her immediately on guard. She would go rigid, her chest tightening, eyes moving from
face to face, as if vigilance itself might keep danger away. Being alone with men had become
particularly intolerable. Even at work, when she was still doing contract QA reviews in a small
office suite, she would arrange to have a female colleague nearby if a male contractor needed to
speak with her. Uncertainty was just as potent. If a delivery was delayed, if a phone rang without
warning, if her husband was not where she expected him to be, her watchfulness sharpened and she
felt compelled to locate exits, listen for footsteps, and keep track of who was coming and going.

The more her body stayed on alert, the more certain Eleanor became that she could not trust the
world to be safe. That sense of constant danger fed directly back into her darkest beliefs: that she
was still helpless, still exposed, still the kind of person bad things happened to. Those thoughts,
in turn, made the triggers more pervasive. She began declining memorial services, avoiding
cemeteries, and steering clear of situations where she might be alone with men or caught off guard
by uncertainty. She preferred to stay close to home, where she could control the schedule and reduce
the chances of being startled. But the narrowing of her world did not bring relief for long. The
less she went out, the more fragile she felt when she did, and the faster her mind returned to the
old scenes of being trapped with her father. When those photographs of the past returned, they
revived the same accusations—why didn’t you stop it, why did you let it happen, how could you trust
him—and she would fall back into numbness or wordlessness, unable to explain the terror in a way
that felt sufficient.

What finally brought Eleanor to seek help was a recent memorial service for a neighbor, where she
had nearly left before the eulogy because the combination of the cemetery, the men standing in the
aisle, and the sudden opening and closing of umbrellas in the wind left her shaking and on edge. On
the drive home, she realized how much of her life had become organized around avoiding reminders and
bracing for harm. She was frightened that the world was shrinking around her, and frightened too
that if she did not get help, she would spend the rest of her life trapped between the old
photographs in her mind and the certainty that she could never keep herself safe.
