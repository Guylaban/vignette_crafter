Rafael had spent most of his life working as a welder, a man who understood heat, pressure, and the
necessity of keeping a steady hand even when metal hissed and sparked. Since his wife died, he lived
alone and kept to a narrow routine, but the panic that brought him to treatment began much earlier,
in the period after he was tortured during a time of political violence in his home country. He
described being taken to a confined room with armed men, nowhere to go and no way to control what
happened next. The memory he carried from that time was not a clean story with a beginning and an
end; when he tried to speak about it, he lost the thread, his account broke apart, and he would stop
mid-sentence as if he had stepped back into the room. What remained most vivid was the feeling that
it was happening right now, not decades ago, and the rush of rage that came every time he let
himself remember.

Certain moments still pulled him straight back. The slam of a metal door, the sudden crack of a
gunshot on television, or even a sharp noise from the street could make his body go rigid, because
those sounds seemed to announce that violence was near and that he had no options. A crowded doorway
at the grocery store or a repair bay where he could not easily leave could have the same effect. In
those moments, Rafael’s mind did not stay in the present; it folded into the old scene, and the
torture felt immediate again. That sense of being trapped made the old memory flare, and once it
did, the world around him narrowed to the possibility of harm. He became convinced that if he did
not stay alert, violence could arrive from anywhere, and that fear colored the places and people
around him as potentially dangerous.

As the years passed, Rafael tried to manage these surges in the only ways that seemed available to
him. When the memory surged, he detached himself from it, pushing it away as if it belonged to
someone else. He tried hard not to think about the torture at all, and when he felt threatened he
became overly compliant, quick to agree, quick to apologize, quick to smooth over tension before it
could grow. He had come to believe that you can never know who will harm you, and that belief kept
him watching for danger in everyday life. It also deepened his self-blame. He told himself that the
event happened because of the way he acted, and that he would never forgive himself for not doing
more to stop what happened. Those thoughts made the old fear feel justified, and they also made him
more likely to fall silent, to yield, and to keep his distance from anything that might stir up what
he called “that room.”

The cycle became especially visible at work, where the welding shop itself could turn into a place
of repeated reminders. A loud bang from a nearby machine, a burst of sparks, or the sudden clatter
of dropped steel could bring back the same trapped feeling that had followed him for years. After
those moments, Rafael would withdraw into himself, refuse to talk about what had happened, and focus
only on getting through the shift without drawing attention. He noticed that he started to avoid
certain tasks that required him to work in tighter spaces, and he would linger near exits when he
could. The more he tried not to think about the torture, the more the memories seemed to ambush him,
and the more he felt he had to watch for danger. That vigilance did not ease his fear; it kept the
world arranged around threat, and it made every sudden sound or constricted space feel like proof
that harm could return at any moment.

By the time he sought help, Rafael was exhausted by how often the past would seize him and by how
much of his life had shrunk around that fear. He wanted relief from the way the memory arrived as if
it were happening now, and from the anger and self-accusation that followed. What finally brought
him in was the sense that he could no longer keep living as though every unexpected noise or blocked
doorway meant violence was about to begin. He was most afraid that his body would keep reacting
before his mind could catch up, and most hoping that someone might help him believe, even a little,
that the danger was not always around the corner.
