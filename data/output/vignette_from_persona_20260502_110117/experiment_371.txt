Mateo was 22 when he came for help, a single HVAC technician who had built his life around early
starts, roof work, compressor repairs, and the hard physical routines that made sense to him. The
event that changed everything had happened on an ordinary job site in late summer, during a service
call in a cramped apartment complex where the hallway units were humming and the air outside was
heavy and still. While moving equipment, Mateo had made a mistake that resulted in another man being
seriously injured. He described the moment with a kind of stunned precision, as if replaying the
sequence might somehow produce a different ending: the sudden movement, the impact, the shock of
seeing the other person go down, and the smell that rose in the aftermath, metallic and sharp, which
he could still catch in his mind without warning. From that day on, the memory never felt like
something safely in the past. He said it felt alien, as though it belonged to somebody else’s life,
yet it would force its way into his mind when he was trying to work, drive, or fall asleep, and when
it came it often arrived with that same smell attached.

In the weeks after the injury, Mateo became obsessed with figuring out exactly where his judgment
had failed and how one small decision had snowballed into catastrophe. He went over the details
again and again, convinced he had to find the point at which he should have done more, stopped
sooner, noticed faster. What sat underneath that replaying was a set of harsh conclusions about
himself: that his body could not be trusted anymore, that the person he had become was
unrecognisable to him, and that he would never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what
happened. Those beliefs began to shape what he did next. He started avoiding anything he imagined
could turn stressful, cutting short conversations, declining extra work, and turning down jobs that
felt unpredictable. He would not look at dates linked to the incident and became uneasy as
anniversaries approached, as if the calendar itself were a warning. The more he tried to control his
life by narrowing it, the more certain he became that he could not rely on himself, because if he
had truly been competent, the injury would not have happened. His shrinking world fed the self-
condemnation, and the self-condemnation made his avoidance feel necessary.

That avoidance came with its own costs. Mateo had once been the kind of technician who could move
between noisy mechanical rooms, crowded stairwells, and unfamiliar buildings without much thought,
but now he only felt safe in a very limited number of places, mainly his bedroom, his truck, and a
corner of his mother’s kitchen where he knew exactly what every sound meant. At work, even the
everyday silence between machines could put him on edge; if a unit shut off and the hallway went
still, he would tense before he could stop himself, certain that something dangerous was about to
happen. When that happened, his body reacted strongly and immediately, with a pounding heart, a
tight chest, and a jolt of dread that seemed to confirm his fear that something was wrong with him.
Those physical surges made the old scene intrude more forcefully, and once the memory came in, it
brought the threat with it: the apartment hallway, the smell, the sense that disaster could break
open again at any moment. He would then pull back further, skipping calls he could have managed and
asking coworkers to take over situations that felt too exposed or too loud. Over time, his efforts
to stay safe made the world feel more dangerous, not less, because every restricted route and every
quiet room taught him that only a few places could contain the alarm.

By the time he entered treatment, Mateo had lost confidence in his own body and in the version of
himself who used to trust it. He was still showing up to work, but he was doing so with constant
monitoring, measuring his reactions, scanning for silence, and bracing for the next unwanted image.
He had not been able to bring himself to face the anniversary without dread, and what finally pushed
him to seek help was the fear that his life was getting smaller around the injury he caused. He
wanted to stop living as though every stressful moment might bring the whole scene back, and he was
most afraid that his guilt had already changed him into someone he could never become again.
