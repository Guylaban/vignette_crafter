her life had narrowed around a childhood memory she could not keep at a distance. She grew up in a
home where her father’s violence toward her mother could erupt without warning, and she described
one night in particular that still anchored everything else: the crash of a plate hitting the wall,
her mother’s sharp gasp, the sound of something heavy being thrown across the room, and the smell of
spilled beer and cigarette smoke hanging in the hallway while Marisol hid behind her bedroom door
and listened. Even now, when she thought of that night, the memory arrived more as a scene than a
recollection. It felt more vivid and real than ordinary moments in her present life, and sometimes
the odor of beer or stale smoke seemed to come back so strongly that she could almost taste the
panic in her mouth. Yet when the image started to sharpen, she found herself pushing it away before
she could fully follow it through, as if stopping at the edge of it might keep it from swallowing
her whole.

In the years since, certain reminders cut through her quickly. If someone threw an object across a
room, even casually, her body reacted before her mind could catch up. Related photos — especially
images of yelling couples, broken household items, or family pictures that carried a tense
atmosphere — could pull her back into that same dread, and related sounds, particularly slamming,
banging, or a man’s voice rising sharply in anger, made her feel flooded and exposed. Those moments
sent her straight into coping rules she had built around herself: she stopped making any plans for
the future because plans felt fragile, almost inviting disaster, and she began restricting her
eating with unusual strictness because controlling food was one area where she believed she could
still impose order. She also avoided anything that reminded her of the person who harmed her,
steering clear of certain neighborhoods, television scenes, and even family gatherings where
someone’s tone or posture resembled her father’s. It was not simply that those reminders upset her;
in her mind, they seemed to prove how quickly life could turn dangerous, and her attempt to manage
them only made the memory feel more available, more pressing, as if every effort to stay in control
kept the past close.

Marisol had come to believe she could not deal with even the slightest upset. She read her own
reactions as evidence of helplessness, certain that she could not stop bad things from happening to
her. Her irritation and angry outbursts at work reflected that fear as much as any mood shift; when
a coworker moved abruptly behind her or dropped a stack of reference sketches near her desk, she
could snap before she had time to think, then feel ashamed afterward. As an animator, she spent long
hours at a shared studio table, and the unpredictability of other people’s movement in that space
kept her on edge. She watched doorways, monitored footsteps, and scanned the room constantly, her
attention fixed on who might come up behind her or what might be thrown. That hypervigilance wore
her down, and the more she remained on guard, the more easily she startled to movement nearby, as if
every sudden gesture carried the possibility of harm. In that tense state, fragments of the old
scene returned more often: the plate, the smell, the sound of things breaking. What she tried to
keep out of mind came back with greater force the moment she was keyed up, and each return made her
feel even less able to cope.

Over time, the way she tried to protect herself hardened into a loop. She had avoided future plans
so thoroughly that she had turned down opportunities to take on larger projects, worried that
committing to anything beyond the next few days would only set her up to lose control. She ate with
such strictness that meals at the studio became another source of vigilance rather than comfort. And
because she kept arranging her life around avoiding perpetrator cues, her world grew smaller and
more dependent on scanning for danger, which only confirmed her sense that she was never truly safe.
She also held tightly to the belief that the people responsible had faced no consequences, and that
absence of accountability sat beside her fear like an open wound: if nothing had happened to him
before, why would anything stop harm from finding her now? When she finally sought help, it was
after a week in which an animator’s headphones were tossed onto the table with a sharp clack, a
related sound and movement that left her shaking for hours and unable to finish her shift. She came
in tired, guarded, and afraid that her life would keep shrinking unless she learned how to stop
living as though danger were still waiting in the next room.
