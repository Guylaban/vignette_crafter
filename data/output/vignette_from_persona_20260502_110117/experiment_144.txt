Arjun was 29, a South Asian shopowner living above the small convenience store he ran with long
hours and little privacy, and when he first described what happened he kept circling back to how
ordinary it had seemed before it turned violent. He had been in a relationship with a man he
trusted, someone who had started with charm and affection and then, over time, became controlling
and physically abusive. The final episode he could not shake came after a tense argument at home,
when his partner grabbed him, shoved him against the kitchen counter, and hit him hard enough that
the smell of sweat and anger seemed to fill the room. Arjun said that even now, if a stranger in the
shop leaned too close, or if a customer came in after working out or on a hot day with body odour
hanging around them, he felt a sudden jolt of alarm. Certain conversation topics, especially
anything about couples arguing, “discipline,” or what people “should have done,” could pull him
straight back into that night without warning. He had come to believe his body was betraying him,
that it kept reacting as if danger were still present even when he was safe, and that he could not
rely on himself because he had frozen during the assault and then stayed for too long afterward.
More than once he said, with obvious shame, that he would never forgive himself for not doing more
to stop what happened.

In the weeks after the abuse ended, those same triggers began to shape his sense of the world. A
customer approaching the counter from behind or entering the shop late in the evening could make him
feel threatened in an instant, as though any unfamiliar man might suddenly become aggressive. He
found it harder to feel safe at night than during the day, so he kept the lights on longer than
necessary and watched the street through the front window, listening for footsteps. Sweat or body
odour was especially potent because it seemed to carry the whole scene back with it; if someone
stood near the till after a long day, he would stiffen and feel his chest tighten before he had even
fully registered why. In his mind, the fact that his body responded so fast confirmed what he feared
about himself: that he was still vulnerable, still unable to protect himself, and that if he had not
acted differently then, he might fail to act again. That belief made him more watchful around
strangers, more likely to scan faces and hands, and more likely to avoid any situation that felt
unpredictable.

At other times the memory came without warning and took over the present. Arjun described standing
in the shop restocking shelves when, for no obvious reason, the full memory of the assault would hit
him—his partner’s face, the shove, the kitchen counter, the smell—and he would lose track of what he
was doing. Sometimes the memory arrived with no clear image at all, only the full emotional
intensity of that moment, his body flooded with dread and rage as if the abuse were happening again.
When he remembered it with images intact, the rage was intense and hot, directed not only at his ex-
partner but at himself for staying silent. Those recollections fed directly back into his negative
view of himself: each surge of rage seemed to confirm that his body and mind were not under his
control, that he could not trust his own reactions, and that he had failed in a basic moral duty to
stop what happened. The more he doubted himself, the more he became alert to anything that might
signal danger. He watched men’s movements more closely, became jumpy when a stranger approached the
shop entrance, and found himself pulling back from casual contact because he did not trust his own
judgment about who was safe.

That vigilance, in turn, made his triggers more likely to strike. Because he was always scanning for
threat, he noticed sweat, body odour, sudden changes in tone, and loaded conversation topics more
quickly and more intensely than before; what might once have passed him by now became a signal that
danger could be near. He began stepping away from customers, shortening conversations, and sending
his partner to close the shop with him on nights when he felt especially on edge. He was embarrassed
by how much this had narrowed his life and strained the relationship that remained important to him,
but he said the alternative felt worse: if he let his guard down, he feared he would be caught off
guard again, and if he were caught off guard, he was certain he would prove once more that he could
not rely on himself. By the time he sought help, Arjun was exhausted by the cycle of sudden
memories, rising anger, constant checking of the doorway, and the private conviction that his own
body had turned against him. What brought him to treatment was the fear that he was becoming
permanently trapped in this state, and what he most hoped for was some way to feel safe in his own
shop and at night without living as if the assault were still about to happen.
