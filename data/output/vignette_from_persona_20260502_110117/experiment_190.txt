people’s words and suppressing his own. He was divorced, living alone in a small apartment above a
print shop, and still working part-time on manuscripts, though lately the work had become harder to
trust. The abuse had taken place years earlier, during a relationship with a partner who could turn
a dinner table into a battlefield. Raymond remembered one evening in particular: the sharp tone of
correction over a trivial remark, the shove that sent him against the kitchen counter, the way the
other man’s face seemed to harden into something almost unfamiliar. What lingered was not a
continuous story but a series of snapshots, each one fixed and vivid in his mind—a hand on his
collar, the edge of the sink, the offender standing over him. He said the memory did not feel like
his own life at all, as if he were looking at a scene that belonged to someone else, and when it
came back he felt a fierce, hot rage that startled him with its intensity.

Those moments had made criticism dangerous in a way that other people did not seem to understand. If
a colleague marked up a sentence or a friend corrected a detail, Raymond could feel his body tighten
before he had even finished reading the comment. Violence in a film, a raised voice in the street,
even the sight of a man who resembled his former partner in build or posture could pull him back
into that kitchen with unsettling force. In those moments he found himself reaching almost
automatically for distraction—checking email, turning on the radio, scrolling through articles,
anything to keep his mind from settling on what he had seen or heard. He also avoided anything that
reminded him of the man who harmed him: certain neighborhoods, familiar restaurants, particular
aftershaves, even the route he once used to drive to their shared apartment. At work, if an editor-
in-chief corrected him in front of others, he would spend the rest of the day buried in copy,
deliberately keeping his attention on commas and line breaks so he would not have to feel the surge
of anger or fear that rose up under the surface. He had come to believe that allowing himself to
feel strong emotion was dangerous, that once he opened the door he might never get control back.

The more Raymond pushed the memory away, the more it seemed to return in fragments. He could be
reading a manuscript and suddenly see again the offender’s hand, the counter, the look on the man’s
face; at other times the anger would flash so quickly that he would slam a file shut or leave his
desk to walk the block until it passed. Because the recollection arrived as isolated pictures, it
never settled into a coherent past event he could place behind him, and because it felt alien, it
left him with the strange sense that something had been done to him that had not quite happened to a
real person. That was when the old appraisal returned with force: that he was somehow the kind of
man this happened to, too passive, too easily managed, not fully a person at all but something to be
used. He would tell himself that normal emotions were gone for good, that whatever warmth or ease he
once had had been stripped out of him. On bad days he noticed that this belief itself made the
memory feel more distant and unreal, as if he were watching yet another scene from outside himself
rather than remembering his own life. The sharper his self-blame became, the more the snapshots
seemed to detach from him, and the more his mind treated them as evidence that he was already
changed in a fundamental way.

Over months, the habit of distraction and avoidance narrowed his world. He stayed late at the office
to avoid evening solitude, declined invitations if he thought a guest might be blunt or critical,
and kept his apartment quiet so he would not have to notice his own reactions. Yet the very effort
to stay away from reminders made him scan for them constantly. A man laughing too loudly in the
hallway, an offhand correction from a younger colleague, a television argument from the apartment
next door could set him on edge before he had time to explain it to himself. When he then tried to
shut down the feeling, the old images came back with greater force, and with them the rage and the
sense of being objectified all over again. That in turn made him more certain that something was
fundamentally wrong with him, that he was made for this kind of treatment and would never recover
the ability to feel normally. By the time he sought help, he was exhausted by the loop itself:
frightened by criticism, violence, and any glimpse of the offender; flooded by brief, alien pictures
of the assault; and left in a life increasingly organized around keeping emotion out. What finally
brought Raymond in was the fear that his shrinking world would become permanent, and the faint hope
that he might one day remember what happened to him without feeling less than human.
