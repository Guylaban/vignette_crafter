child in the crush, heard the sudden screaming, and had to force himself to keep moving because
wounded civilians were already being carried toward the tent where he was working. He later
described that moment as the point at which he began to feel that his own judgment could not be
trusted. He had come to believe that what happened to him was deeply unfair, that the people
responsible had faced no consequences, and that the world had simply moved on as if nothing had
happened. Those beliefs stayed with him and sharpened his sense that he could not rely on himself to
keep himself safe.

Back in the United States, Kofi worked as a social media manager for a nonprofit in a busy downtown
office, but the ordinary noise of the open-plan workspace kept pulling him back into a state of
alertness. A delivery truck backfiring on the street outside could make him flinch hard enough to
spill coffee on his desk, and a colleague approaching from behind would leave him visibly jumpy. He
became superalert in a way that exhausted him; he watched exits in meetings, checked the reflection
in darkened computer screens, and constantly scanned his environment for signs of threat whenever he
was on the train, in the parking garage, or even walking his dog in the evening. His concentration
suffered at work, and he found himself rereading the same posts over and over because his mind kept
drifting to whether he had missed something dangerous nearby. The more he believed that the attack
had been unjust and unresolved, the more his body seemed to stay braced for another disaster.

To manage that constant strain, Kofi began to distance himself from the memory of the blast and
everything around it. He avoided thinking about the event altogether, quickly switching to music or
scrolling through his phone whenever the images started to surface. When thoughts did break
through—usually at night or when news footage of overseas violence appeared on social media—he would
feel himself detach from the memories, as if he were pushing the whole scene away from his mind to
keep it from overwhelming him. That strategy brought brief relief, but it also left him more
unsettled. The more he tried not to think, the more on edge he felt, and the easier it was for small
noises, sudden movements, or crowded spaces to catch him off guard. His watchfulness increased
because he no longer trusted his memory or his instincts, and the hypervigilance fed the startle
response he was trying to outrun.

Over the following months, the pattern spread into his home life with his wife. He started arriving
in the bedroom after she had already turned off the lights, partly because he did not want to talk
about the deployment and partly because he did not want to sit still long enough for the images to
come back. He told himself that if he kept busy, kept scanning, kept his attention moving, he could
stop himself from spiraling, but the effort made him more tense and irritable. His wife noticed that
he was present in the room but not fully there, and he hated the shame that followed when she asked
what was wrong and he could not explain it. At work, he began turning down assignments that required
him to be in the office late, especially on days when security guards were making their rounds or
sirens were audible from the street, because those details tightened his body instantly. His
avoidance narrowed his world, but it also convinced him that something was wrong with him, because
each time he tried to manage the fear alone, he ended up feeling less capable and more watchful than
before.

Kofi finally sought help after he nearly dropped his phone in a meeting when a door slammed behind
him and he realized he had been sitting with his jaw clenched for most of the hour, unable to follow
what anyone had said. He was exhausted by the constant vigilance, the jumpiness, and the way his own
attempts to push the memories away seemed to keep the fear alive. What brought him in was the fear
that he was becoming unreliable at work and at home, and the hope that he might be able to stop
living as if the war were still one step behind him.
