Karim was a 52-year-old Middle Eastern actor who had come to treatment still living in the long
shadow of the night his wife died. He described the event with a strange, distressing clarity: there
had been sirens, a blur of voices, and then a terrible silence broken by his young daughter’s crying
and the sounds around them that would later haunt him in pieces. Since then, ordinary media content
could seize him without warning. A news segment, a dramatic scene on television, even a passing
radio report with similar sounds could suddenly make him feel as if he were back at that moment. In
those flashes, he would immediately go along with whatever others wanted to feel safe, letting
colleagues choose where to sit or what to do, because being in control felt dangerous and being
agreeable felt like the only way to keep harm from returning. He also began to avoid being alone in
a room or building, and crowded places such as shopping centres or public events, because the same
exposure made him feel exposed and vulnerable, as if any space could turn on him.

What frightened Karim most was how those reminders pulled the whole event into the present. When he
heard children crying in particular, his body tightened and he became certain that something
terrible was about to happen again; similar sounds could do the same, even if he could not always
say why. In those moments his concentration fell apart, and he found it impossible to follow a
script, a conversation, or even simple instructions. At work, this mattered immediately: between
rehearsals he would drift from the page, miss cues, and then apologize too quickly, falling into the
habit of pleasing directors and castmates so no one would question him. He had come to believe that
even when others told him he was safe, it was impossible to trust that safety, because the fear
arrived so quickly and so fully that reassurance felt shallow. The stronger the fear, the more he
watched and listened for trouble, and the more ordinary noises and sudden crying seemed loaded with
menace. That constant threat monitoring made the world feel booby-trapped, and the next cry, the
next loud sound, the next fragment of a report on television could become another trigger.

Karim’s memories of the trauma did not come to him as a full story. He described them as
disconnected pieces that did not fit together: a hand reaching, a face turned toward him, the wet
shine of a hospital floor, his daughter’s voice breaking. At the same time, the event returned with
photographic vividness, as if he were looking at still images in his mind rather than remembering it
in sequence. That style of recall made the memory especially intolerable. When the images came, they
intensified his guilt on recall, and he would sit with the certainty that he should have done more,
that he had failed to stop what happened. He was haunted by the belief that the people or
institutions that should have protected his family let him down, and that the people responsible had
faced no consequences. Those thoughts did not stay abstract for long; when his memory surfaced in
fragments, they fed his anger, shame, and horror, and those feelings made him more convinced that
the world was unsafe and unfair. He would replay what he had seen and not seen, and the lack of
accountability hardened into bitterness, while his own inaction felt unforgivable.

Over the months after the death, Karim’s efforts to manage the fear began to reshape his memory and
the story he told himself about what had happened. He stayed around people, never wanting to be
alone in a room or building, and he filled empty hours with company or work. He went along with
whatever others wanted, even in small things, because it felt safer than risking disagreement. But
these habits kept him from sitting with the images long enough to make sense of them, so the memory
remained in broken pieces and came back with the force of photographs. At the same time, avoiding
crowds and public places kept him from normal routines and from any chance of discovering that some
spaces could be endured; instead, each retreat seemed to confirm that he was not safe. The more he
arranged his life around other people’s needs, the more he felt trapped by the fear and the guilt,
and the more he told himself that he had been powerless then and was still powerless now. By the
time he sought help, he was exhausted by how quickly media content, crying children, and even a
similar sound in the street could send him into a familiar spiral of fear, mistrust, and self-blame.
He came to therapy hoping to stop living as if danger were always about to return, though what he
feared most was that he would never be able to trust safety again or forgive himself for what he
believes he failed to do.
