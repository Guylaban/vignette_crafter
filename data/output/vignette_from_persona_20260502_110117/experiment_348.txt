Mere began to unravel after a life-threatening bout of illness that had started with what she first
dismissed as a stubborn chest tightness while she was loading equipment before a rehearsal. By the
time she reached the hospital, she was struggling to breathe, and the smell of disinfectant in the
corridor became welded in her mind to the panic of not knowing whether she would make it through the
night. She remembers the cold of the examination room, the humiliation of being prodded and listened
to, and the way her own body seemed to turn against her in front of strangers. Even now, as a
46-year-old musician with a busy life in Australia, she can be thrown off by the faintly clinical
smell of a medical office or by the simple act of drawing a full breath when she is singing or
climbing stairs; a breathless moment can drop her straight back into that hospital fear, and
anything resembling a physical examination feels charged with the same threat.

In the months that followed, Mere began waking in the dark to check whether she was safe, lying
still and listening to her breathing before she would let herself settle again. She described her
body as reacting before her mind had time to catch up: a flutter in her chest, a sharp intake of
breath, a pulse of alarm that seemed to arrive without warning. Those night checks and the constant
bodily vigilance made her feel as though there were only a very limited number of places where she
could relax at all, and even then she trusted them only briefly. As her sleep became more broken and
her sense of safety narrowed, she started to see the illness as proof that she was inadequate, that
her reactions since then showed she was a lousy coper, and that she could not trust herself to do
the right thing if something happened again. That self-criticism did not stay abstract; it pushed
her toward punishing herself with the thought that she deserved to suffer because of what happened,
and it fed the painful habit of thinking about how life would have been different if the illness had
never occurred.

Those appraisals and the ongoing sense of danger became intertwined in the way Mere managed her
days. Because she believed she was not handling things properly and could not rely on her own
judgement, she began avoiding hospitals, ambulances, and routine physical examinations, putting off
appointments and finding reasons not to go near anything medical unless she absolutely had to. At
the same time, her fear that danger could arrive through her body kept her scanning for signs of
breathlessness and reacting quickly to any sensation that reminded her of the original illness. If
she noticed her breathing change while singing, she would withdraw from rehearsal or move to the
edge of the room, telling herself she needed to stay in control. When the smell of antiseptic
drifted through a clinic entrance or from a nearby pharmacy, she would turn away before she had
fully registered it, already bracing for the old panic. These patterns made it easier, for a moment,
to feel less exposed, but they also kept her life moving around the illness rather than away from
it.

Over time, that narrowing had concrete costs. As a musician, Mere had once spent much of her week
moving between rehearsals, venues, and the occasional performance in unfamiliar places, but now she
planned everything around how safe a space felt and how easily she could leave if her body began to
alarm her. Her husband had noticed how often she asked to sit near exits or to skip medical checkups
she used to manage without fuss. Mere was aware that the more she avoided, the more convinced she
became that she truly was fragile and unreliable, and the more she watched her body for warning
signs. In the second half of the night, she still woke to check whether she was safe. In the day,
the smallest breathless sensation could pull her back to that hospital corridor, and the smell of a
procedure room could undo hours of careful composure.

By the time she sought help, Mere was exhausted by how tightly her world had shrunk and frightened
by how easily her own body could set everything off. She came in not only because the memories of
the illness still felt vivid and dangerous, but because she feared she was becoming a person who
would never trust herself or move freely again. What she most hoped for was to breathe, sleep, and
work without feeling that every clinic, every smell, and every tightening in her chest meant the
danger was back.
