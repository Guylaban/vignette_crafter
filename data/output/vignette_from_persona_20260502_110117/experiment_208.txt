Imran, a 53-year-old South Asian painter, came to treatment after years of trying to keep what
happened to him sealed away. He had been a civilian living with his wife and children in a city that
was suddenly cut across by fighting, and the event that now organized so much of his life began on
an ordinary afternoon when he was painting an interior wall for a customer. A blast hit nearby. The
smell of smoke and dust rushed through the building, people were shouting, and for a moment he had
no control at all over where to go or what would happen next. He remembers being thrown down in a
cramped position, his body pinned and twisted in a way that still returns to him vividly; when the
memory comes, he feels as if his own body is back in that same posture. What stays with him most is
the overwhelming grief of it, and the strange, almost unreal quality of the whole scene, as if it
were too extreme to have really happened to him.

In the months after he left that place and rebuilt life in the United States, certain moments pulled
him straight back. The smell of smoke, burnt wiring, or even a chemical odor on a job site could
make him go rigid, and the sharp crack of a door slamming or a bang from construction work would
send his heart racing and his body into a state of tense readiness. Those triggers did not just make
him anxious; they brought the memory back in fragments, with his muscles tightening as though he
were once again trapped on the floor, and with it came the thought that the world was a dangerous
place and that he was no longer capable of doing the work that had once defined him. He had made his
living as a painter for decades, proud of being steady, careful, and useful with his hands. Now,
when he heard an unexpected noise, he would instantly scan the room, checking exits, watching faces,
measuring whether he could react fast enough. He was certain that ordinary sounds could turn
dangerous without warning, and that sense of being on guard only deepened his belief that nothing in
life felt meaningful anymore.

Imran tried to manage this by staying busy. He took on more work than he needed, filling every spare
hour with painting, repairs, errands, and small tasks around the house so that there would be less
room for remembering. He also avoided news reports about bombings, attacks, or other events that
resembled what had happened to him, because even a brief clip on television could bring back the
smell, the helplessness, and the body memory all at once. Yet the harder he worked to outrun the
memories, the more they seemed to return in private moments: when he was scraping old paint from a
wall, when he caught an unfamiliar odor in a hallway, when a passing truck backfired outside the
home. Each time, the recollection felt less like a story and more like an event happening again
inside his body, and each time it reinforced his conviction that the world remained dangerous and
that he himself was no longer the man he used to be.

That loss of meaning sat heavily on him. He would tell himself that if he could just understand why
it happened to him, perhaps he could make sense of the suffering, so he went over it again and again
in his mind, searching for reasons and fault. But the rumination never settled anything; it kept the
event close, made the bodily flashback more likely, and left him more watchful, more tense, more
startled by sounds that would not bother most people. When he felt his body tightening, he became
even more determined to stay occupied and to avoid anything that might remind him of war, which
narrowed his life further and made the world feel even less safe. At home, his wife noticed that he
was present in body but often distant in spirit, and he himself felt the erosion of the identity
that had once given him pride.

By the time he sought help, Imran was exhausted by the cycle. He was most afraid that the memories
would keep taking over his body and his mind, leaving him permanently unable to paint, work, or feel
that life had purpose again. What brought him in was not only the startle, the vigilance, and the
constant tension, but the fear that his world had become small and dangerous enough that he could no
longer live in it without being pulled back to that afternoon of smoke, noise, grief, and
helplessness.
