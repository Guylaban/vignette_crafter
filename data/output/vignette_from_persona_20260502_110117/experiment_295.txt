Kwame was a 23-year-old African American urban planner who had grown up in a busy part of the city
and prided himself on staying composed under pressure, but after an assault six months earlier he
had become increasingly consumed by fear and self-reproach. It had happened outside a corner store
late in the evening, after a man who seemed drunk had started shouting at another customer and
hurled a bottle across the sidewalk; when Kwame stepped back, trying to avoid the confrontation, the
man turned on him, shoved him hard against the brick wall, and hit him before two bystanders finally
pulled the assailant away. What stayed with Kwame were not words or a coherent story but a few vivid
pictures: the bottle leaving the man’s hand, the flash of the brick wall behind his head, the
assailant’s face contorted with anger, and the sour smell of alcohol hanging on the man’s breath.
Since then, when someone near him throws a cup, snaps at a colleague, or even speaks with a tone
that feels sharpened by irritation, he feels his body react before he can think. He jumps at
ordinary sounds in the office, flinches when a coworker walks up behind his desk without warning,
and watches movement in his peripheral vision with the sense that danger is already arriving. Those
moments immediately pull him into familiar conclusions: he had been let down by the people and
systems that should have protected him that night, and if something as small as a raised voice or a
tossed object can still unsettle him this much, he must not be able to deal with even the slightest
upset. He has come to believe that he cannot stop bad things from happening to him, no matter how
careful he tries to be.

In the weeks after the assault, Kwame began avoiding any setting that might turn tense. At work,
where he spends long hours in an open-plan office and in meetings with contractors, he sidesteps
disagreement, volunteers little when discussions become heated, and will quickly agree to decisions
rather than risk confrontation. If a site visit takes him near a bar entrance or he catches the
smell of alcohol outside a restaurant, he changes direction, feeling the old alarm rise in his
chest. When anger appears in other people, even in a controlled voice, he reads it as the start of
something dangerous. Those triggers push him into strategies that seem, in the moment, like the only
way to keep control: he spends hours gambling online to distract himself and feel a jolt of
excitement that drowns out the fear, and when the memory starts circling he becomes trapped in self-
blame, dwelling on what he did wrong, replaying his hesitation outside the store, and telling
himself he should have noticed sooner, should have left faster, should have done something to
prevent it. Yet the more he tells himself he failed, the more certain he becomes that he is weak and
incapable, and the more he withdraws from any situation that might involve disagreement or force him
to trust other people’s judgment. The avoidance has narrowed his world: he declines after-work
gatherings, keeps to himself in meetings, and has become less collaborative with colleagues, which
has begun to affect his work in ways he notices with shame.

The memory of the assault is not something Kwame can tell in a smooth sequence. When it comes back,
it arrives as those same isolated snapshots, and each one floods him with the same helpless terror
he felt on the sidewalk. He often has to stop what he is doing, sit down, and wait for the wave of
feeling to pass. In those moments he loses words for it altogether; he knows what happened, but
cannot describe it cleanly, as if language itself falls away. That overwhelmed state feeds his habit
of dwelling on what he did wrong and confirms his belief that he is helpless. It also keeps the
assault alive in the present: the bottle image, the shout, and the smell of alcohol make ordinary

office, or a stranger’s abrupt movement on the sidewalk can send him back into the same fear. Once
he is startled, he is more likely to scan for threat, to read anger everywhere, and to avoid or

deepened his sense that he is failing at handling even ordinary stress.

Kwame finally sought help after he caught himself freezing during a meeting when a senior planner,
