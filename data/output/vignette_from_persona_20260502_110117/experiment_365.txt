Martin, a 56-year-old European American man who had built his life around movement, came to therapy
after more than a year of trying to live with the aftermath of a driving accident that had changed
him and injured another person. He and his wife had been returning from an evening out when, at a
turn he misjudged, his car struck a pedestrian crossing late in the dark. Martin had never before
been in anything like it. He remembered the sickening thud, the sight of the other man on the
pavement, and the sirens arriving while Martin stood frozen, certain he had ruined not only a
stranger’s body but his own identity. As a dancer, he had always understood himself as precise,
attentive, and responsible with other people’s bodies; after the accident, he felt that he had
failed in the one role that mattered most to him. That belief settled quickly into a hard self-
judgment: he would never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened, and he should
have protected others and failed.

In the months that followed, certain songs would catch him off guard and pull him back to the
evening in a way that felt immediate rather than remembered. A familiar tune in a restaurant, or
music playing softly in a shop, could suddenly make him picture the crash again and re-enter the
moment of impact. Crowded spaces had a similar effect. When he was in a busy lobby, at a performance
venue, or even on a packed sidewalk, the crush of bodies and noise made his mind jump to the chaos
of that night and to the thought that he had been too slow, too careless, too little in control.
Seeing a car hit by accident in a film, on the news, or even briefly on the street sharpened the
same conviction: he had failed in his most important role in life. Each trigger seemed to feed the
self-accusation, and the self-accusation in turn made the triggers more potent, because now the
music, the crowding, and the crash images did not just remind him of the event; they seemed to
confirm his moral failure.

Trying to live with those thoughts, Martin began to manage his days by narrowing them. He avoided
going to or near the place where the event happened, taking longer routes across town and turning
down errands that would bring him close to that intersection. He tried hard not to think about the
trauma, pushing the scene away whenever it rose into his mind. He also dwelled on how he used to be
before the event, replaying the younger version of himself who moved with confidence through
rehearsals and streets alike, as if comparison might explain what had been lost. But the effort to
keep it out of awareness left him more strained rather than less. The more he suppressed, the more
his mind seemed to stay on guard for any hint of the memory breaking through, and the more difficult
it became to concentrate on anything else. He could no longer follow choreography as fluidly, would
lose counts in rehearsal, and found himself unable to relax even when he knew he was in a safe
place. At night he lay awake needing to stay alert, as if vigilance might prevent another disaster,
but the cost was exhaustion and a constant sense that something terrible could happen again at any
moment.

By the time he sought help, the circle had tightened. Because he was so keyed up and unable to
settle, ordinary stimuli became easier to misread as warnings, and songs, crowds, and any image of a
collision could seize his attention before he had a chance to steady himself. His sleeping became
more fragmented, his concentration worse, and he found himself living in a smaller and smaller
world, with work and marriage both shaped by the effort to keep from being overwhelmed. Even in the
studio where he once felt most alive, the sound system could become a threat if the wrong song came
on, and he would tense, scan, and struggle to keep his composure in front of others. What finally
brought Martin in was not a single new event but the realization that he could no longer keep the
burden hidden from his wife or his company, and that his life had begun to organize itself around
fear of his own thoughts. He said he was most afraid that he would never get back the man he used to
be, yet he was also hoping that, with help, he might finally stop punishing himself long enough to
sleep, work, and move through the world again.
