Amina was a 68-year-old African woman who had been married for many years and was now unemployed,
living a quiet life that revolved around her home, her routines, and the small circle of places she
still considered tolerable. Her history of trauma dated back to years of physical abuse by a
romantic partner, abuse that had left her with a painfully specific sense of what danger felt like
in her body. She described one night in particular when she had been forced to sleep alone after a
fight, lying awake in a room that smelled faintly of damp earth from the open window, and hearing
every creak of the house as if it were a warning. In the present, that kind of situation still
unsettled her immediately: sleeping alone, or in an unfamiliar place, made her tense and compliant,
and she found herself going along with whatever others wanted because it seemed safer than risking
disagreement. When she felt emotionally vulnerable, the same thing happened; she became careful,
agreeable, and eager to keep the peace, as though safety depended on making herself easy to manage.

Amina’s account of the abuse did not come to her in a smooth story. She said the memory forced its
way into her mind when she did not want it to, especially when she was caught by surprise by a smell
of soil after rain or by outdoor air drifting in through a doorway. At those moments the whole scene
could return without warning: the room, the threat in his voice, her own helplessness. Often she
pushed the memory away before she could fully recall it, because once it started it felt impossible
to stop. She noticed that the more she tried to block it, the more the fragments seemed to intrude
at odd times, even while she was trying to do ordinary things around the house. The memory was
especially vivid in places that reminded her of vulnerability, and those moments fed back into the
way she now understood the world.

What had grown from all this was a narrowed sense of where she could be safe. Amina said she only
felt safe in a very limited number of places, and even inside her own home she felt on guard. She
would sit facing the door, listen for sounds in the yard, and avoid settling fully into bed because
she needed to stay alert in case anything happened. Sleeping had become difficult, not only because
the night reminded her of being trapped, but because she believed that letting herself drift off
meant letting her guard down. The memory of the abuse kept confirming that fear. Each time it
surfaced, it made the world feel less predictable, and she became more certain that danger could
reach her anywhere, even in the rooms she was supposed to trust. If a smell or a setting brought the
past back, it did not feel like a distant recollection; it felt like proof that the threat could
return at any moment.

Over time, Amina’s efforts to manage this narrowed life made the triggers more conspicuous. She kept
herself busy with other things so she would not be alone with her thoughts, filling the day with
chores, small tasks, and anything that would keep her mind occupied all the time. Yet the busier she
tried to be, the more noticeable the moments of stillness became, and the more sharply she reacted
when a sudden smell of soil drifted in from outside or when she found herself alone in bed. Her
people-pleasing also deepened this pattern. Because she was so focused on keeping others calm and
accommodating, she spent long stretches in situations she did not want, which left her more depleted
and more emotionally exposed; and when she felt that exposure, she was even more likely to search
for danger in her surroundings. In that way, the very strategies she used to stay safe kept her near
the conditions that would set off the fear again.

By the time she sought help, Amina’s world had become increasingly small. She was exhausted from
working hard at keeping busy, from monitoring every room, and from avoiding sleep whenever possible
because the night felt like the doorway to being trapped again. The pattern had also begun to affect
her relationships and daily life: she found it hard to relax with others, hard to be alone, and hard
to trust that her own home could remain ordinary. What finally brought her in was the realization
that she could not keep living this way without help. She was most afraid that one night, in the
dark and alone, the memory and the danger would feel indistinguishable again. What she hoped for was
simple but urgent: to feel safe in her own house, to sleep without having to stay on watch, and to
stop living as though the past might break in at any moment.
