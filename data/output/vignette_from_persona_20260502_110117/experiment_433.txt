Mina Haddad, a 63-year-old single Middle Eastern woman who worked for years as an auditor, came to
treatment months after her younger brother was taken hostage during a violent incident abroad and
later found dead. The details of that day remained seared into her because she had first learned of
the crisis by phone while he was on a work trip, then spent hours hearing fragments from relatives
and officials before the confirmation came. What stayed with her most was not only the loss but the
helplessness of waiting with no control, imagining where he might be, whether he was frightened, and
whether anyone could reach him in time. Since then, Mina had found herself scanning for danger in
ordinary life, sleeping lightly, and feeling as if her body never fully stood down.

Certain situations pulled her back with particular force. A locked conference room at her office
could make her chest tighten before she even sat down, because the closed door and lack of an easy
exit reminded her of her brother being trapped and unreachable; in those moments she became
watchful, checking the handle, the windows, and who else was nearby. The same happened when she
visited a wooded park near her apartment, where a trail, a patch of trees, or the smell of damp
earth could suddenly make the present feel thin and unsafe, as if she were back in the same helpless
uncertainty of the search. Even a news story, a photograph, or a family member mentioning victims in
another context could jolt her into the same state, and she had come to dread these reminders
because they seemed to arrive without warning. As she experienced those repeated surges, Mina began
to believe that she could not trust the world to remain orderly or kind, and that if danger could
strike her family once, it could strike again anywhere; she also felt betrayed by her own body,
which reacted with racing alertness long after she knew she was safe.

Over time, Mina’s fear spread beyond the original reminders. She started avoiding unfamiliar places
because, in her words, she could not assess the danger there, and she preferred routes, restaurants,
and shops she knew well. At work she would accept only meeting rooms she had seen before, and she
arranged her schedule so she would not have to arrive early at empty buildings or stay late after
others had gone. That caution had practical consequences: she declined an assignment that would have
required travel to a client site she had never visited, something she would once have taken pride in
managing. The more she narrowed her world, the more she felt exposed to the possibility that bad
things could happen without warning, and that sense of vulnerability deepened her belief that she
had no way to stop them. She interpreted her own watchfulness as evidence that the danger was still
near, and the constant monitoring made her feel even less secure.

The memories themselves intruded in a way Mina found unbearable. When they came, they did not arrive
as a distant recollection but as a complete return of the event, with images, sounds, and bodily
terror all at once: the phone ringing, voices speaking too fast, the stale air in the room where she
waited, the sickening dread that her brother was somewhere suffering. Sometimes a smell in the
office corridor or a sudden silence in her apartment could bring the whole scene back without
warning, and she would freeze, gripped by the same terror she had felt at the time. On nights when
she could not fall asleep or woke repeatedly, the memories were worse, and she lay listening for
signs of danger, her mind replaying the hours of helpless waiting. In those moments she felt certain
that her body was still reacting as if the crisis were ongoing, which made her more convinced that
she was not truly safe.

Mina’s loss of faith in people, life, and even a higher power sat underneath much of this. She spoke
quietly about how she no longer believed others would protect what mattered or that the world made
moral sense, and that bleakness made her even more alert to possible threats. Because she expected
danger and betrayal, places she could not fully read felt even riskier, and reminders of victims
felt like proof that suffering could appear anywhere. Those same beliefs also made the memories more
powerful: when she recalled her brother’s death, she did not just remember what happened, she felt
confirmed in the idea that tragedy was random, unstoppable, and personally waiting for her. Mina had
finally sought help when she realized she was spending more and more time avoiding places, sleeping
poorly, and living as if she were always on guard, and what she most hoped for was some way to stop
feeling that immediate surge of fear while also fearing that, if she let her guard down, another
loss would catch her unprepared.
