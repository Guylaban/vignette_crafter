Marian, an 80-year-old African American widow who had spent most of her working life as a policy
analyst, came to treatment months after a life-threatening motor vehicle collision that had upended
the steady routines she had always relied on. She had been driving home in the early evening when
another car, coming fast from a side street, struck her vehicle with such force that she was thrown
hard against the seatbelt and the car lurched into a spinning stop. In the immediate chaos she
remembers the sharp, burnt smell of overheated metal and plastic, the sting of the cold air on her
skin once the door was forced open, and the driver beside her moving suddenly in the wreckage. Those
details still felt dangerously alive to her. A burnt odor drifting from a passing car, the smell of
alcohol on someone at a family gathering, or even a sudden chill on her arm in an air-conditioned
room could make her body tighten at once, and Marian would become immediately alert to every quick
gesture around her. She described herself as watching people’s hands and shoulders before she could
take in what they were saying, certain that any sudden movement might mean something bad was about
to happen. The same sensory cues also reached straight into her memory without warning: she might be
standing in her kitchen or walking through her office building and, with no clear transition, find
herself flooded by the scene at the crash site, as if the whole event had been dropped back into the
present.

What made this especially distressing was that the memories did not come back evenly. Marian said
that some moments were painfully clear — the scream of metal, the smell of burning, the instant she
realized she could not move the car — while other parts were completely blank. She would try to tell
herself the sequence from beginning to end, but before she could fully recall it she found herself
pushing the memory away, almost reflexively, as if looking at it directly would overwhelm her. Yet
the moments that remained vivid carried such force that they altered how she read ordinary life.
Because the crash had unfolded with sudden movement, heat, and the acrid smell of burning, she had
come to fear movement and sensation in a way that surprised her. A person turning quickly beside her
in the elevator, a nurse reaching across her in a doctor’s office, or a colleague stepping abruptly
into her path at work could make her feel on guard before she had even thought about it. These
reactions fed her memory as well: once her attention was pulled into that state of alarm, the event
felt closer, and pieces of the crash came back with greater intensity. At times she found herself
acting as though she were back in the collision, gripping the counter, freezing in place, or
scanning for danger with the same helpless urgency she had felt in the car.

Over the following weeks, Marian began arranging her days around the things that set her off. She
avoided parking garages and intersections that reminded her of the accident, and she became careful
about where she sat in public so no one could approach too quickly from behind. At the office, where
she still did policy work part-time, she chose a desk near a wall and kept her door closed whenever
possible; the open-plan movement of younger staff passing suddenly behind her made her tense and
distracted, and she worried that if she looked rattled people would think she was losing her
competence. Even with these precautions, the fear never fully settled. The more she stayed on guard
for sudden movement or a strange smell, the more ordinary moments seemed loaded with danger, and the
more often her mind was pulled back toward the crash. That constant watchfulness made the sensory
reminders feel even more potent, and the reminders in turn strengthened the sense that danger could
appear without notice. Marian noticed herself becoming more withdrawn at family dinners and more
reluctant to accept rides, telling herself that she was safer if she kept control of where she was
and what she could smell or feel on her skin.

By the time she sought help, Marian was exhausted by the way her life had narrowed around these
fears. She had begun to miss parts of her work because she dreaded the commute and the
unpredictability of hallways, elevators, and colleagues’ sudden gestures, and she grieved the ease
with which she had once moved through the city. What finally brought her in was a frightening
episode in which a passerby’s abrupt turn and a trace of alcohol on his breath made her feel, for a
few seconds, as if she were back in the wreck itself. She is most afraid that this will keep
happening and that she will never again feel fully safe in her own body. At the same time, she hopes
that treatment might help her hold the memory without being ambushed by it, and let her return to
ordinary movement, ordinary smells, and ordinary days without bracing for disaster.
