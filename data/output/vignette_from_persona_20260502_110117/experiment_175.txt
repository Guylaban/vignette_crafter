Ravi Patel had worked as a paramedic in Chicago for more than three decades, and for most of that
time he had thought of himself as the kind of man who could stay steady when others could not. That
belief changed on a winter evening when he and his partner were called to a warehouse district site
after a spill from a damaged industrial container. The air inside the loading bay had a sharp
chemical sting, and the first thing Ravi noticed was the smell of alcohol mixed with something
metallic and bitter. Within minutes his throat burned, his eyes watered, and several workers began
coughing as the corridor filled with darkness from a power failure that made the narrow space feel
even smaller. Ravi had later said that what made the scene unbearable was not only the toxicity
itself, but the helplessness of being there in his uniform and still not being able to keep everyone
safe. The memory of that night now returned to him with startling force whenever he walked past a
hospital entrance, heard an ambulance pull up, or even glanced toward a dark hallway at work;
sometimes the memory came on without warning from something as ordinary as a passing whiff of
alcohol-based sanitizer. When it arrived, he did not experience it as a distant recollection but as
a full return of the same terror, his body jolting as if he were back in the warehouse, and he would
have to stop whatever he was doing because the emotion became too overwhelming to manage.

In the months after the incident, Ravi found himself spending a great deal of time replaying every
decision he had made that night, trying to work out how it could have been prevented. He would lie
awake at home after a shift, turning over small details he believed might have changed the outcome,
and the rumination gave him a grim sense of control even as it wore him down. That habit of mental
review came to structure his days: if he could keep his mind busy with endless analysis, he would
not have to sit alone with the memory and the wave of fear that came with it. He also began keeping
himself constantly surrounded by people. At home he sought out his wife, children, neighbors, and
even casual company from coworkers, and on days off he made sure not to be alone for long. He had
come to believe that isolation left him vulnerable to being ambushed by the memory, and being with
others gave him a kind of cover, even if he did not talk about what was happening. Yet the more he
tried to manage the experience by filling every hour, the more the memory seemed to break through
when he least expected it. A smell in a supermarket aisle, the dimness of the garage at dusk, or a
glimpse of medical equipment could suddenly pull the full event back into the room, and each
intrusion left him more determined to avoid places that reminded him of that night.

Ravi’s efforts to shut out the reminders gradually narrowed his world. He began avoiding hospitals
except when absolutely necessary, took longer routes to stay away from ambulance bays, and started
delaying routine physical examinations because the exam room, the white walls, and the sound of
medical staff moving in the corridor all felt too close to the scene of the spill. At work this
became harder to manage. As a paramedic he could not completely escape the settings that now
unsettled him, but he became vigilant about where he stood, how dark a hallway looked, and whether
an alcohol smell lingered in a room after cleaning. That constant monitoring kept him on edge, and
the very things he scanned for—workplace hazards, dark spaces, the sharp scent of alcohol—were the
same cues that brought the memory rushing back. Once the memory surged, it strengthened his need to
avoid even more, and he would redouble his efforts to stay busy, stay in company, and keep away from
medical environments. In turn, those strategies made the reminders feel more immediate: the less he
allowed himself to enter a hospital or sit quietly alone, the more foreign and threatening those
places became, and the more power they seemed to have when he could not avoid them.

By the time he came for help, Ravi was exhausted by the strain of living this way. He still
functioned, but only by arranging his life around avoidance, constant contact with other people, and
repeated mental revisiting of the spill, and he felt increasingly ashamed that his work as a
paramedic now carried him into the very settings he feared most. What brought him in was the sense
that he could no longer keep tightening the circle around himself without losing more of his life
and his marriage. He was most afraid that one unexpected smell, one dark corridor, or one routine
examination would open the memory again and leave him flooded with the same terror in front of other
people. At the same time, he was hoping that treatment might help him bear the memory without having
to outrun it, and that he could return to work without feeling that every hospital doorway was
waiting to pull him back into that warehouse.
