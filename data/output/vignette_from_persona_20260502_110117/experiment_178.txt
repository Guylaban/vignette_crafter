Minh was 27 and had built his adult life around movement and deadlines, working as a journalist in a
busy American city and trying to keep pace with a relationship that was, on the surface, stable. He
sought help months after leaving a violent romantic partner, describing a point in the relationship
when arguments at home escalated into physical abuse that left him feeling cornered by the man who
was supposed to be safest. What stayed with Minh was not only the fear of the assaults themselves,
but the way ordinary moments afterward became charged with danger: a late bus ride home after work,
being left alone in an elevator with an unfamiliar man, or a delay in a text reply from someone he
cared about could all make his body tighten before he could explain why.

After the relationship ended, Minh noticed that those moments pushed him into habits that felt
protective at the time. On buses he would sit rigidly near the exit, avoid eye contact, and spend
the ride replaying what he believed he had done wrong in the relationship, dwelling on every small
argument and every time he thought he should have left sooner. He had also started never allowing
himself to feel or express anger, especially not toward men, because anger seemed to him dangerous
and pointless. Even food became part of that pattern: when he felt overwhelmed, he would restrict
what he ate, telling himself he did not deserve comfort and that keeping himself controlled was the
only way to stay safe. The less he let himself feel, the more he caught himself scanning the street,
the train platform, or the bus aisle for signs of threat, as if restraint could keep him from being
caught off guard again.

The memories came back with a force that made the past feel present rather than remembered. When a
bus jolted suddenly or slowed unexpectedly in traffic, Minh would be thrown back into the apartments
and hallways where he had been hurt, his chest tightening and his skin going hot as though the abuse
were happening again in real time. Once the memory started, he could become so overwhelmed by
emotion that he had to stop whatever he was doing, even in the middle of writing an article, and sit
with his head down until the wave passed. At those moments he also noticed physical pain in the same
places his body had been struck during the assaults, a sensation so convincing that it deepened his
sense that his body was still carrying the event. Those eruptions of memory did not feel like
distant recollections; they pulled him back into the relationship, and with them came the certainty
that he must have failed in some fundamental way to let it happen.

That belief sharpened his negative appraisals. Minh had come to believe that he was inadequate, that
he should have been stronger, quicker, or wiser, and that people could not be trusted because harm
could come from anyone once they decided to turn. He also found himself repeating the thought that
you can never know who will harm you, a conclusion that felt less like a fear than a fact learned
the hard way. Those beliefs did not stay abstract. They made the sight of men on the bus, in the
newsroom corridor, or walking behind him on the sidewalk feel like a warning sign, and they made
delays feel loaded with menace: if a source did not answer a message promptly, or if his partner
took longer than expected to return home, Minh assumed something bad was happening. Even when others
told him he was safe, he found it impossible to believe; reassurance sounded naïve to him, as though
they did not understand how quickly safety could turn. At night he often woke to check whether he
was safe, listening for footsteps, checking locks, and scanning the bedroom before he could settle
again.

The more he lived this way, the more his beliefs hardened. Because he assumed danger was always
near, he watched people closely and withdrew from situations that might leave him alone with men or
dependent on uncertain plans, which narrowed his world and kept his mistrust intact. He worried that
if he let his guard down, he would be hurt again, and each new precaution seemed to confirm how
unsafe the world was. By the time he came to treatment, he was still in a relationship but found
himself keeping parts of himself hidden even from his partner, afraid that anger, hunger, or need
would make him vulnerable. What finally brought him in was the exhaustion of sleeping in fragments,
the dread he felt every time the phone stayed silent a little too long, and the growing fear that he
would live as though the abuse were still happening. He said he wanted help because he no longer
wanted every delay, every bus ride, and every man nearby to feel like a threat, but he was most
afraid that if he stopped bracing himself, something terrible would happen before he could react.
