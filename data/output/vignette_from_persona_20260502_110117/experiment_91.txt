Thomas, a 56-year-old European American combat pilot, came to treatment after a long stretch of
trying to outrun the aftermath of a life-threatening accident that occurred during a routine flight
training mission. He had been handling a high-performance aircraft when a sudden mechanical failure
and explosive bang jolted the cockpit; the plane dropped sharply, warning lights flashed, and for a
few terrifying moments he was sure he would not get the aircraft back under control. He survived,
but in the weeks that followed he found that even ordinary bodily sensations could carry him back to
that day. If he felt dizzy or lightheaded on standing, or if he came down with a flu-like sense of
feeling physically unwell, his mind immediately went to the cockpit and the brief, sickening loss of
control. The same happened with sharp bangs in the environment — a slammed door in the hangar, a
backfire from a vehicle, even an unexpected pop on a television program — each sound struck him as
though danger were already in the room. In those moments his body became tense and ready to react,
and he found himself scanning his surroundings, startled by movement near him as if every quick
gesture might signal an emergency.

What unsettled Thomas most was not only the sensations themselves but what they seemed to prove. He
had come to believe that the world was dangerous, that the assumptions he had built his life on had
been shattered, and that something in him had died that day. He described feeling dead inside, as if
the accident had hollowed out the part of him that once felt steady and purposeful. That conviction
made the triggering sensations even more ominous: dizziness no longer felt like a passing physical
symptom, but like evidence that catastrophe could return at any moment; a bang did not just remind
him of the accident, it confirmed his belief that the world could turn lethal without warning. He
had always trusted training, procedure, and control, and after the accident he felt those beliefs
were completely destroyed. His sense that danger was everywhere made him wary of his own body, and
his own numbness seemed to him like proof that he was not the same man who had once flown
confidently and lived with a sense of mastery.

Over the months that followed, Thomas began arranging his life around the need to feel safe and in
control. He avoided hospitals, ambulances, and physical examinations whenever he could, even when
his wife urged him to get checked after minor illnesses. He gave up pleasant activities he used to
enjoy, not because he no longer wanted them, but because enjoyment itself felt irresponsible and
exposed, as though letting his guard down would invite another disaster. He also began restricting
his eating strictly, telling himself that controlling food was one domain in which he could still
impose order on a body that had otherwise become unreliable. Yet the more he tried to manage things
this way, the more his mind returned to the accident and the more certain he became that the world
truly was dangerous. Each avoidance choice seemed to confirm that there was something to fear: if
hospitals and examinations were unbearable, maybe there really was a hidden problem in his body; if
he had to micromanage food to feel steady, maybe he could not trust himself at all. That inward
conclusion deepened his emotional numbness and his shattered beliefs, making it harder to imagine
any future that did not revolve around vigilance and loss.

At the same time, those habits kept his body on alert. By narrowing his world, he found himself
watching himself more closely, bracing for the next dizzy spell, the next bang, the next sudden
movement near him. The effort of staying guarded was exhausting, and the tension never fully left
his muscles. He noticed it at work in the flight operations environment, where the noise of
equipment, quick motion on the tarmac, and emergency vehicles crossing nearby would jolt him into
instant alarm. Instead of easing with familiarity, his alarm made him more determined to avoid
medical settings and any situation that might remind him of vulnerability. His wife had begun to
notice that he declined dinners, skipped golf with friends, and grew terse whenever someone
suggested a checkup. What started as an attempt to protect himself had become a pattern that kept
reinforcing the very world he feared: the more he avoided, the more dangerous everything felt, and
the more threatening his own reactions became. It was this tightening cycle, along with a recent
episode of dizziness that sent him into a panic when he heard an ambulance outside the base clinic,
that finally brought him to seek help. Thomas said he was most afraid that he had become permanently
trapped in a life of hyperalertness and emotional emptiness, but he was also quietly hoping that
treatment might help him feel safe in his own body again and reclaim some sense that the world was
not entirely ruined.
