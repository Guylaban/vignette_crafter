Layla, an 18-year-old Middle Eastern woman who worked as a junior architect in a small downtown
studio, came to treatment after months of feeling unable to switch off at night and increasingly
worn down by a constant sense of danger. The traumatic event that changed things was not something
that happened to her body, but something she witnessed and has never been able to unsee: late one
evening, while walking a few blocks from her office after staying late to finish a presentation
board, she saw a traffic collision at an intersection near the bus stop she used every day. A man
was thrown from the impact and lay motionless on the pavement while people shouted and cars braked
around him. Layla remembered the metallic sound, the smell of smoke, and the way everyone seemed
frozen before rushing in all at once. She stood on the sidewalk, useless and horrified, hearing
someone call for help and feeling certain that she should do something but not knowing what. Since
that night, what stayed with her was not only the image of the man on the road but the feeling that
danger could break into an ordinary evening without warning and leave her powerless to stop it.

In the weeks that followed, Layla found herself carrying the event with her into work and home life
in ways that were hard to explain to others. She became intensely watchful in the city streets
around her studio, scanning intersections, sirens, sudden movements, and the behavior of drivers
whenever she left the office or stepped outside for lunch. She noticed herself checking the road
before crossing more than once, listening for engines, and tensing whenever she saw someone near the
curb. At the office, even the sound of a car door slamming below her window could make her look up
sharply. What frightened her most was not a single memory but the sense that the world had become
unpredictable and that she needed to stay ready for it. Layla had come to believe that if she let
herself relax, even for a moment, something terrible might happen again and she would miss the
warning.

Night became the hardest part of the day. She would lie in bed in her apartment, lights off, phone
facedown, trying to sleep but unable to let her guard drop. Her body felt as if it were still on the
sidewalk that evening, waiting for the next crash, the next cry for help. She delayed turning out
the lamp because she did not want to be caught unaware, and she often listened for noises in the
hallway or outside on the street before allowing herself to close her eyes. When she did sleep, it
was shallow and uneasy, interrupted by the fear that she had not stayed alert enough. The effort of
maintaining that level of watchfulness left her exhausted during the day, but she told herself that
being tired was safer than being unprepared. Over time, she started taking longer routes home to
avoid the intersection, leaving work earlier when she could, and asking friends not to meet her near
busy roads. At the office, where she was usually expected to concentrate on drawings and models, she
found herself repeatedly distracted by the outside world, as though part of her attention was always
stationed at the window, guarding against catastrophe.

The emotional cost of all this accumulated quietly but steadily. Layla felt ashamed that she could
not shake off what she had witnessed, and anger rose in her when she thought about how quickly an
ordinary street had turned into a scene she could not control. She also felt a deep, lingering
horror at the image of the injured man, and guilt that she had frozen rather than acted faster.
Those feelings made it even harder for her to settle at night, because once she was alone in the
dark the memory returned with force, and the need to stay vigilant seemed to make perfect sense. Her
apartment, once a place where she sketched after work and listened to music, became a space she
associated with scanning the lock twice, checking the window, and waiting for some unnamed threat to
announce itself. The more she lived this way, the more she began to feel that alertness was the only
thing standing between her and another devastating scene.

By the time she sought help, Layla said she was no longer functioning the way she wanted. She was
missing deadlines because she was so tired, pulling away from friends, and dreading the nights in
particular. The memory of the crash still appeared whenever she approached traffic, but it was the
constant state of being on guard that had become the most exhausting part of her life. She came to
treatment hoping to sleep without feeling she had to keep watch and to stop living as though danger
were always waiting just beyond the next street corner. What she feared most was that if she ever
truly relaxed, she would be caught off guard again and something terrible would happen before she
could prepare for it.
