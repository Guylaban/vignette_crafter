Arjun was a 20-year-old South Asian writer who had come for help after months in which the death of
his older sister had come to shape nearly every part of his life. She had died unexpectedly in a
road accident on her way home from work, and because the call came late at night, the memory that
stayed with him was not a clear picture but the abrupt sound of his phone vibrating on the desk
beside his unfinished draft, followed by his mother’s voice breaking before she had fully said what
had happened. In the weeks after the funeral, Arjun found himself staring at the blank pages of his
notebook and feeling as if the future had been stolen along with her. He had once imagined finishing
school, publishing his writing, and eventually moving out; now he said he could not make even small
plans without a wave of dread. When friends asked about holidays, classes, or deadlines, he would
deflect, not because he was indifferent, but because planning felt to him like tempting fate. He
kept asking his mother, a cousin, and sometimes even a close classmate to tell him he was okay,
asking the same questions again if the first answer did not settle him. Anything that reminded him
of his sister—a song she liked, the smell of the tea she used to make, a scarf left hanging near the
door—was pushed away, hidden, or avoided entirely.

As Arjun made sense of what had happened, his thoughts hardened into a set of painful conclusions.
He had come to believe that he used to be a happy person, and that the accident had ended that part
of him; now, in his words, he was “always miserable.” More deeply, he felt that the world had become
a dangerous place and that his beliefs about how life worked had been completely destroyed. People
could be fine one moment and gone the next; plans, safety, and ordinary routines no longer seemed
trustworthy. Because of those beliefs, he began to treat future plans as dangerous in themselves, as
if writing them down would invite another loss. He avoided setting goals for his work as a writer,
stopped agreeing to social events weeks in advance, and even hesitated when offered a stable
freelance opportunity because committing to it felt unbearably final. Each time he withdrew or
sought reassurance, the relief lasted only briefly, and then the same conclusions returned with more
force: if he could not tolerate imagining tomorrow, it must be because tomorrow was not safe. The
more he tried to get others to reassure him or to steer around anything connected to grief, the more
convinced he became that he had lost his former self for good.

Those same beliefs also kept his body in a constant state of alarm. Arjun said he could not relax
even when he knew he was in a safe place, and his apartment, his classroom, even the quiet café
where he sometimes wrote all felt unsettled to him. He jumped at ordinary sounds—a delivery truck
slamming a tailgate, a door closing too sharply, a phone buzzing on the table—and his sleep became
fragmented, with trouble falling asleep and waking several times through the night. He would lie
there replaying the call about his sister, or picturing his mother’s voice, and his mind seemed
unable to let go of the idea that something terrible could happen again. After a night of little
sleep, his fear of the world felt even more believable, and the sense that life was unsafe made him
more likely to avoid planning, more likely to seek reassurance, and more likely to scan for
reminders of loss. In that way, his efforts to protect himself only fed the very misery and sense of
danger he hated. As the months passed, his writing suffered; he missed deadlines, stopped replying
promptly to editors, and declined invitations to read at a campus event because he could not imagine
standing in front of people and pretending he was all right.

By the time he sought therapy, Arjun was exhausted by the narrowness of the life he had built around
not being hurt again. He had come after a night when a car backfiring outside his building jolted
him awake and left him lying in bed, shaking, unable to settle, and then the next morning he caught
himself asking his cousin for the third time whether he seemed “normal.” What he feared most was
that this version of him—miserable, guarded, unable to trust the future—had become permanent, and
what he most hoped for was some way to remember his sister without feeling that every reminder or
plan would open the same unbearable wound.
