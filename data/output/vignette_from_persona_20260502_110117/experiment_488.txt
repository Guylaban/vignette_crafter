painfully vivid to her: the angle of the hallway light, the harshness of the shouting, the pressure
in her ribs and shoulders. Other moments were simply gone. She would say that she felt she was
remembering only pieces, with whole stretches missing, and that the gaps were almost as disturbing
as the fragments she could see so clearly. When the memory returned, she felt the same physical pain
in the same parts of her body, as if her muscles were being dragged back into it.

rushing back. Being forced to do something, even in ordinary situations, struck her as especially
dangerous because it echoed the loss of control she had felt during the assault. If a supervisor at
work barked an order or a family member pressed her to agree before she was ready, she would feel
her stomach tighten and her attention narrow. Shouting had the same effect. A raised voice from
another room, a neighbor arguing in the hallway, even a loud television could make her freeze and
feel suddenly unsafe. Reminders of victims, too—news stories, a woman looking frightened on the bus,
or a client who seemed vulnerable and cornered—would pull her back toward the assault by making her
think of how easily someone could be overpowered. These moments did not just upset Nadia; they made
her body and mind shift into high alert, and she became convinced that danger could break in at any
time.

As that fear took hold, Nadia began scanning constantly for signs of danger. At work as a
housekeeper, she found herself checking every entrance before she settled into cleaning, listening
for footsteps in the hallway, and watching the faces and voices of the people around her for any
hint of anger. At home she would stop mid-task to listen for sounds that might mean someone was
entering the apartment, especially when her husband was out or the children in the building were
being loud in the corridor. Her concentration suffered because so much of her mind was spent
tracking what might happen next. Even when she was scrubbing sinks or folding laundry, part of her

reminder of a frightened victim could set off the same surge of alarm. She had come to believe that
staying alert was the only way to keep herself safe, yet it left her unable to relax long enough to
feel present in her own day.

Over time, that constant vigilance began to shape how Nadia moved through the world. She started
turning down extra jobs that involved late hours, avoided certain apartment buildings where she
thought she might be boxed in, and found excuses to leave rooms when voices rose. These habits
relieved her briefly, but they also kept her life shrinking around the fear. At work, she became
slower and more distracted, needing to reread instructions and pausing often because her mind kept
drifting back to watchfulness. At home, her husband noticed that she sat near the door and listened
intently whenever someone passed in the stairwell. Nadia hated how easily she startled and how

be reckless. The assault had taught her, in the most concrete way possible, that being controlled,
shouted at, or caught off guard could become unbearable in an instant.

What finally brought Nadia to seek help was that the strain was beginning to spill into every part
of her marriage and work. She was afraid that if she kept living this way, she would lose her
ability to function at all, and she was even more afraid that one loud voice in the wrong place
would send her back into the same terror and pain. At the same time, she was hoping for something
she had not allowed herself to imagine for months: to be able to move through her home, her job, and
a simple conversation without constantly waiting for the next sign that something terrible was about
to happen.
