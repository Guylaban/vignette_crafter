Mali was 61, a sculptor who had spent most of her adult life working in a bright, cluttered studio
at the back of her home, where clay dust collected on her apron and the smell of solvents and
plaster usually felt familiar and safe. She had come in because, for months now, the ordinary sounds
and scents of daily life had begun to unsettle her in ways she could not easily shake. The problem
had started after an evening two winters ago when she was walking home from a neighborhood arts
reception and witnessed a violent assault in the alley beside the gallery. A stranger had come up
fast from behind another man, and the sound that stayed with Mali was not only the shouting but the
quick, heavy footsteps on the pavement just before it happened. In the confusion, a strong cologne
drifted toward her from someone passing close by, mixing with the cold air and the metallic smell of
the alley, and those sensations lodged together so tightly that even now a whiff of perfume could
bring the whole scene back with startling force.

At first, Mali tried to tell herself she had only been shaken by what she had seen, but she noticed
that certain things now made her body react before she had time to think. Footsteps behind her in a
hallway, a burst of cologne in an elevator, or the sense that something might happen without warning
could leave her jumpy and physically tense, with her heart racing and her muscles bracing as if she
needed to turn and protect herself. What frightened her most was how quickly night had come to feel
unsafe. During the day she could still manage the studio and errands, but after dark she found
herself checking the locks, listening for movement outside, and feeling that the house was not
entirely hers to relax in. She had come to believe that people could not be trusted, that the world
was dangerous, and, more painfully, that there was something wrong with her for not being able to
put the event behind her.

The memory itself was part of what kept the fear alive. Mali described the scene as more real and
vivid than ordinary things, as if it remained fixed in a sharper color than the rest of her life.
When she tried to tell her husband or the intake clinician what exactly happened, she often lost the
thread and could not finish, her thoughts scattering at the moment she reached the alley. She could
not make it into a clear story even to herself; it remained a fragment of footsteps, perfume, panic,
and the sense of danger arriving too quickly. That brokenness in memory made her distrust her own
mind, and the mistrust spread outward: if she could not hold the story together, she reasoned, then
perhaps she was weak or defective in some fundamental way. The more vividly the memory returned, the
more it seemed to confirm that the world was unstable and that she had somehow failed to cope like
other people.

Those vivid recollections also taught her body what to fear. The smell of a stranger’s perfume in a
store, a set of footsteps coming up behind her in the studio corridor, or an unpredictable
interruption at work could pull the alley back into the present, and in those moments she would feel
the same sharp physical reaction she had on the night of the assault. In turn, the startle and
vigilance made the memory easier to catch: once she was scanning for danger, any passing sound or
scent seemed to cue the scene again, and the image returned with an almost bodily certainty. Mali
began to move more cautiously through her days, turning her head when someone entered the studio,
staying tense when she worked alone late, and avoiding the narrow alley near the gallery whenever
she could. Even in the place where she made sculpture, where she once liked the quiet rhythm of
tools on stone, she now found herself monitoring for footsteps and bracing against surprise.

Over time, the feeling that she must stay alert everywhere deepened her belief that the world was
not safe and that something in her was damaged for not being able to feel otherwise. She had started
sleeping lighter, especially at night, waking to small sounds and listening for movement before
settling back, and she finally came for help when her husband noticed how often she paused at the
door before leaving the house and how much she had shrunk the radius of her life. Mali was not only
afraid of another sudden scare; she was afraid that if this kept going, she would lose confidence in
herself entirely and stop going into her studio at all. What she hoped for, beneath the fear, was a
way to feel safe in her own body again without having to live as though every footstep or passing
scent meant that danger was already at her back.
