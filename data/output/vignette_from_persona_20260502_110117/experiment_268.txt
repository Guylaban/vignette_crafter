Mariam was a 36-year-old Middle Eastern woman who had built her life around hard physical work, long
shifts on construction sites, and the steady routines of a marriage that had otherwise felt
dependable. The trouble began after a sudden, life-threatening illness that struck without warning
and left her frightened by how close she had come to dying. One evening she was at home when she
became acutely ill, her body failing in a way that felt immediate and unfamiliar, and she remembers
the panic of not being able to trust what was happening inside her. After that, ordinary sensations
took on a dangerous meaning. If her heart beat a little faster on the job, or if her body felt
unusual, she became jumpy and easily startled, as though danger might be about to break in again. At
night she lay in bed tense and braced, unable to settle, sometimes taking a long time to fall asleep
and sometimes waking repeatedly, her body feeling as though it were still waiting for the next
collapse. At work she noticed herself scanning for anything that might signal trouble, but that
watchfulness only made her feel more wound up and unable to relax.

In the weeks that followed, Mariam came to believe that her reactions meant something was badly
wrong with her mind. She was certain that the intensity of her fear and the way she stayed keyed up
must mean she was going crazy, and that belief made the experience even more alarming. She also
became convinced that she had no future worth imagining, because if her own body had turned against
her once, she felt helpless to stop bad things from happening again. Those thoughts made it harder
for her to tolerate being still with herself, and she began pushing the feelings away rather than
sitting with them. When the memories or the bodily tension rose up, she threw herself into work or
kept adding tasks around the house so she would not have to think. After exhausting shifts, she
would eat more than usual in the evenings, not because she was hungry but because the food gave her
something immediate and dulling to focus on. She described it as a way to distract herself or feel
better for a little while. At other times she emotionally numbed herself, trying not to feel
anything at all, as though if she stayed blank enough she might not have to face what had happened.

But the more she relied on those strategies, the more they seemed to confirm the frightening story
she had built about herself. When she noticed that she was overeating, overworking, or going
emotionally flat, she took it as further evidence that she was losing control. Each episode fed her
fear that her reactions meant she was going crazy, and her hopelessness deepened because she could
not see a way back to feeling like herself. Her sense of helplessness also intensified: if she could
not stop the distress, she concluded, then she could not stop the next disaster either. That made
the body-on-alert feeling even more persistent, and the cycle became harder to interrupt. Some
nights she would leave work so drained that she ate standing over the kitchen counter without
tasting the food, then went to bed with her muscles still tight, listening for her own heartbeat and
waiting for sleep that would not come.

At the construction site, this cycle had begun to affect her functioning in concrete ways. Mariam
still showed up and kept working, but she often overextended herself, taking on tasks past the point
of fatigue because staying busy felt safer than thinking. She told herself that if she kept moving,
she could outrun the fear. Instead, she became more exhausted and more irritable, and her marriage
had started to absorb some of the strain, with her husband noticing how distant and shut down she
had become in the evenings. Even when she tried to rest, her body stayed tense, and a sudden clang
of metal or a shouted instruction on site could make her jump hard enough that coworkers noticed.

By the time she sought help, Mariam was worn down by the combination of sleeplessness, constant
tension, and the conviction that she was not thinking or reacting normally. What finally brought her
in was the fear that this pattern was becoming her whole life: work, food, and numbness on one side,
panic and dread on the other. She hoped for relief from the feeling that her body was always on the
edge of danger, but what frightened her most was the possibility that her mind had been permanently
damaged by the illness and that she would never be able to trust herself again.
