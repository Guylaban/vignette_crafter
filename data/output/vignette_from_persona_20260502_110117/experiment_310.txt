shoulder jammed against him in the rush to get away. Even now, the memory arrived with an awful
clarity that felt more real than the everyday moments around him, as if those images had been burned
into him while ordinary life had stayed faint and less substantial. What he hated most was the shame
that came with remembering it. He had been embarrassed by how frozen he felt, by how he had failed
to help more decisively, and that shame made him recoil from the memory as if it proved something
ugly about him.

In the weeks after, Daniel found that certain things at work could pull him back into that night
with almost no warning. If a customer stood too close at the checkout lane, leaning over the counter
while he bagged items, his body tensed before his mind caught up, because the memory of being
trapped in that crush of people came back through his skin. If someone behaved badly in the store —
shouting at a cashier, demanding special treatment, cutting in line with a sneer — he felt a surge
of anger and alarm that seemed bigger than the moment, because disorder and selfishness now carried
the flavor of the disaster for him. Even the feel of a particular rough, gritty texture could jolt
him: the kind of coarse material on a torn box corner or an abrasive cleaning pad would make him
think, with no logic he trusted, of the debris and broken surfaces from that day. Those moments did
not stay contained. They brought the whole scene rushing back in vivid fragments, and the sharper
the recollection became, the more intensely he noticed the world for threats that resembled it.

next bad thing might announce itself through some small physical closeness or another person’s bad
behavior.

shattered entrance or the frightened woman flashed up, he would tell himself it had not really

more he pushed, the more the pictures returned on their own, and the more vivid they became, the
more he felt he needed those same defenses again. Sometimes, after a day that had left him pinned by

sharp, private pain that felt easier to bear than the shame and terror of what he could see in his

him more on edge afterward, more primed to notice every close-pressed stranger in the aisle and

back in harsher detail and tying his attention even tighter to the same triggers.

Over time, Daniel’s world narrowed around that cycle. He found himself choosing the end of the aisle
with the most room, stepping back from customers, and making sure he had an escape route whenever
the store got crowded. When someone brushed past him in a tight space, the memory would hit with
full force; when the memory hit, he would clamp down harder, try not to think, or resort to hurting
himself to break the loop. That only made the images return sharper, and with them came a need to
watch for the next proximity, the next sign of misbehavior, the next coarse texture that might set
it all off again. At home, his wife noticed how little he said about work and how quickly he changed
the subject when she asked about the store. He had begun telling himself, especially on bad nights,

the images. He was coming in hoping to find a way to make the memories feel less like present-tense
emergencies, and fearing that if they stayed this vivid much longer, he would keep retreating from
work, from his wife, and from the life that still felt precariously close to the disaster.
