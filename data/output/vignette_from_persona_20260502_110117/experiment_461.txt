At 77, Minh Tran still described himself first as a firefighter, even though he had been retired for
years and now lived with his wife in a quiet apartment near their adult daughter’s family. The event
that had never quite left him happened decades earlier, when he was a civilian caught in a war zone
in Southeast Asia and a shell landed close enough to throw him against a wall and fill the air with
dust, smoke, and a ringing bang that seemed to split the world open. He remembered the scene with
striking clarity: the flash, the smell, the bodies moving in fragments, the feeling that everything
he saw was happening just beyond him. Even now, he could say the details as if they were stamped
into him, yet the memory also felt oddly foreign, like it belonged to someone else rather than to
the man he had become.

In the years that followed, certain sounds and situations brought the event back with immediate
force. Hearing names that sounded like the men and places from that period could catch him off
guard, and an ambulance passing outside his building could make his chest tighten before he even
knew why. Explosion-like bangs, whether from construction, dropped cookware, or fireworks in the
neighborhood, were especially potent. When those reminders appeared, Minh would find himself
scanning windows, doorways, and the street below for signs of danger, as if threat might emerge from
any corner if he failed to watch closely enough. He began avoiding going to sleep and especially
lying in the dark, because the quiet and stillness gave the memory room to force its way into his
mind. In bed he could feel the old scene arriving uninvited, and he would turn on a lamp, sit up, or
pace the apartment rather than let the dark close in around him.

What unsettled him most was how quickly those reminders seemed to pull him back into the wartime
moment. A single ambulance siren, a shouted name in the corridor, or a sudden bang could bring the
shelling back with such vividness that he felt the memory was no longer something he recalled but
something that invaded him. Once it arrived, it did not stay in the past; it crowded into his mind
when he did not want it there, and the more clearly it came, the more helpless he felt. Minh had
come to believe that if he started thinking about it deliberately, he would not be able to handle
it. He also compared himself harshly to others, telling himself that somebody else would not have
gotten into that situation, or would have found a way out when he did not. At his age, he rarely
said these things aloud, but he carried the conviction that the event had reduced him, leaving him
feeling like an object acted upon rather than a person with agency.

Those appraisals shaped his days. Because he feared that thinking about the event would overwhelm
him, he tried to push it away whenever it surfaced, yet the effort to avoid it often made the memory
more persistent. He found himself thinking about revenge in brief, bitter bursts, imagining getting
even with the people responsible, and though he knew those thoughts did not solve anything, they
gave his fear and anger a direction. They also made him more watchful, more certain that harm still
lurked nearby. When he was scanning every sound and movement, he was less able to settle, and the
night became even harder; the dread of lying down in the dark kept him awake, and the longer he
stayed alert, the more opportunities there were for the memory to intrude. On the rare nights when
he dozed off in a chair or after leaving a light on, he would wake abruptly to the sound of a
passing truck or a radio report and feel the memory surge back in with its full vividness,
reinforcing his sense that he could never fully control it.

At home, these patterns had narrowed his life. His wife noticed that he kept lights on later than
necessary and sat facing the hallway, listening for sounds that others barely registered. He avoided
resting in the evening, and the exhaustion made him more irritable and more convinced that he was
failing as a husband and man. Even in retirement he still spoke with the clipped practicality of a
firefighter, but lately he had become quieter, less willing to join family gatherings if they were
in busy places where an ambulance might pass. What finally brought Minh to treatment was a night
when a nearby explosion from construction jolted him awake and he spent the rest of the dawn
patrolling the apartment, certain something terrible was about to happen. He said he was tired of
living as if every loud sound might kill him, and he was most afraid that if he let himself think
about what happened, he would lose control entirely; what he hoped for, though he said it carefully,
was to sleep in the dark again without feeling as if the war had followed him home.
