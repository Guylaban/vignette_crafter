Dara, a 52-year-old HVAC technician, came for help after a toxic chemical exposure at work that had
left him feeling permanently changed. He had been servicing an older commercial cooling unit in a
closed mechanical room when a leak released a strong chemical vapor. Within minutes his throat
burned, his chest tightened, and he became lightheaded enough that he had to be pulled out by a
coworker. Even after the incident ended, the memory of standing in that sealed room, breathing air
that suddenly did not feel safe, stayed vivid for him. Since then, he has found it impossible to
trust safety even when others reassure him that he is fine. His wife can tell him repeatedly that
the house is secure, the doctor can tell him his tests look acceptable, and coworkers can joke that
he is overdoing it, but Dara still feels as if danger is hidden just beyond what he can see. He
describes his body as constantly tense and ready to react, as though it is braced for another sudden
exposure, another invisible mistake in the air.

In the weeks after the event, Dara began living as if any moment of ease could make him vulnerable.
He had come to believe that relaxing was dangerous, because the one time he had felt ordinary and
focused on the job was the moment the chemical leak caught him off guard. If he sat down after work,
let his shoulders drop, or tried to watch television without scanning the room, his mind went
immediately back to the sealed mechanical room and the feeling that he had not noticed the threat
soon enough. So he stayed busy, pacing the house, doing small repairs, cleaning tools that were
already clean, and avoiding anything that could become stressful because stress seemed to sharpen
the sense that something was about to go wrong. At home he preferred not to be alone in a room or
building; if his wife stepped out to run errands, he often followed her around the house or stood
near the front window until she returned. Being by himself in the basement or garage was especially
hard, because the silence made him feel trapped with his own body and thoughts.

As time passed, those fears began to shape nearly every part of his day. At night Dara walked
through the house checking doors and windows again and again, touching the locks with the same tight
concentration each time, unable to settle until he had looked more than once. Even then, the relief
never lasted long. He would lie in bed with his muscles rigid, listening for sounds from outside and
waiting for the next sign that something was wrong. Because being at ease felt so unsafe, he stopped
resting properly after work and avoided the quiet moments that used to help him recover from a long
day on the job. On difficult days he called in sick or took longer routes home to avoid anything
that might add stress, and the narrowing of his routine began to spill into his marriage. His wife
noticed that he no longer sat with her in the evening unless he was also checking the house, and
their conversations often became strained when she tried to reassure him. The more she told him he
was safe, the less he could believe it; he experienced her reassurance as kind but detached from the
reality his body was living in. His vigilance had become its own daily requirement, and the more he
watched for danger, the more convincing danger seemed.

Dara’s work has also become harder to manage. As an HVAC technician, he still enters buildings,
service rooms, and basements, but he now does so with a tightness in his chest and a constant
awareness of exits, ventilation, and what might be in the air. He has begun avoiding tasks that feel
especially stressful, and when a job requires him to work alone in an enclosed space, he feels a
surge of dread that can linger long after he leaves. What once was an ordinary part of his trade now
feels like repeated exposure to the very conditions that frightened him most. He has finally sought
treatment because the pattern is wearing him down and his wife is worried about how little he sleeps
and how rarely he seems able to relax. Dara is not only afraid of another chemical exposure; he is
afraid that if he lets his guard down for even a moment, he will miss the next warning sign. What he
wants most is to feel safe enough to sit still in his own home, trust a locked door without checking
it repeatedly, and be able to do his work without living as though danger is always one breath away.
