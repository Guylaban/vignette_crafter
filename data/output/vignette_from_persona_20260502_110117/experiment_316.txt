Minh Tran had been a tax inspector for more than twenty years, a job that suited his habit of
noticing small inconsistencies and sitting with uncomfortable paperwork, yet over the past year even
ordinary days at the county office had begun to feel precarious. He described the original trauma as
unfolding in private, behind the closed door of the apartment he shared at the time with a romantic
partner he had trusted completely. What started as shouting became physical abuse that left him
cornered, ashamed, and unable to predict what would happen next; the unpredictability of those
moments, and the sensation of being trapped with his back against furniture and no clear way out,
still made his body tense when he found himself tired after long shifts, physically uncomfortable in
a stiff chair, or faced with situations at work that changed without warning. On those days, he
noticed his mind sliding immediately toward danger, as if the old threat had returned, and he would
scan hallways before entering conference rooms, sit with his back to the wall in the records area,
and glance over his shoulder in the parking garage as though someone might be following him.

The memory did not arrive in a neat sequence. Minh said fragments of it would break through without
warning while he was reviewing files or waiting at a traffic light, only to vanish again before he
could place them, and there were stretches of the event he simply could not recover no matter how
hard he tried. At other times the memory forced its way into his mind with a force that made his
chest tighten and his concentration splinter. Each intrusion left him more convinced that he had
been betrayed by someone he trusted and that he had been reduced to an object to be controlled
rather than a person with any real standing. He had come to believe that nothing good could happen
to him anymore, a conviction that darkened the rest of his week and made even small demands feel
like proof that life was only going to narrow further.

Those appraisals fed directly into the way he moved through the world. If he already felt
dehumanized and certain that betrayal was waiting for him, he became hyperaware of anything that
might signal harm: a raised voice in the office, a late-night text from an unknown number, a
maintenance worker entering a room unexpectedly. The feeling that he was unsafe unless he could see
the whole room was not just a preference but a rule he felt compelled to follow, and when he could
not manage that, he became increasingly alert to who was behind him and where the nearest exit was.
That vigilance, in turn, made ordinary unpredictability intolerable. A schedule change from his
supervisor, a crowded elevator, or an unannounced visit to a client site could leave him unsettled
for the rest of the day, and the discomfort itself became a warning sign that something bad was
imminent.

The loop tightened over months. When Minh’s body reacted as if danger were present, the tension
often pulled the memory back in: the pressure in his shoulders, the urge to check behind him, the
effort to keep his back to a wall would suddenly open a corridor to the old scene, and once the
fragments appeared he would be left with the same bleak certainty that he had been betrayed and
diminished beyond repair. He then grew more fearful of tiredness, because fatigue seemed to lower
his ability to keep himself safe, and more wary of discomfort, because a crowded train, a hard
office chair, or a long meeting could make him feel trapped in the way he once had been. In
response, he started avoiding small risks and unnecessary changes wherever possible. He rearranged
his desk so he could watch the door, took longer routes through the building, and quietly reduced
social plans after work, telling his wife he was simply worn out. Over time that shrinking routine
carried a cost at home and at work: he became less present, less easygoing, and more preoccupied
with making sure nothing could surprise him.

By the time he sought help, Minh was still functioning, but only by keeping himself tightly arranged
around vigilance and avoidance. He worried that if he stayed in this state much longer, the belief
that nothing good could happen to him would harden completely, leaving him isolated from his wife
and unable to trust anyone again. What finally brought him in was the fear that the exhaustion he
had been pushing through at work was making the memories and the sense of danger more frequent, and
he wanted to know whether there was any way to feel like a person again rather than someone always
bracing for the next blow.
