Somchai, a 60-year-old South Eastern Asian fisherman, came to treatment after months of feeling
unable to settle since the day a storm took his younger brother from him. He and his brother had
gone out before dawn in their small boat, as they had done for years, checking the nets off the
coast while the village was still dark and quiet. The weather turned faster than expected, the waves
rose hard against the hull, and in the confusion Somchai watched his brother lose his footing and
disappear into the black water. By the time rescue teams searched the area, there was nothing they
could do. Since then, Somchai has carried the memory of that morning like a weight that never fully
leaves him. He speaks of it in a flat, distant way, saying he feels dead inside, as if some
essential part of him stayed out at sea with his brother. He also says that surviving when his
brother did not feels wrong, and the guilt has hardened into a kind of private punishment.

In the months that followed, Somchai began to see other people differently. He came to believe that
people cannot be trusted, because the sea, the weather, and even the warnings from others had all
seemed unreliable that morning, and no one had been able to protect his brother. That mistrust
widened beyond the event itself. He stopped relying on crew members the way he used to, watched
faces carefully for signs that someone might be careless or hiding something, and found himself
assuming that if he let his guard down, something else terrible would happen. At the same time, the
sight and sound of reminders in daily life started to hit him hard. When he heard a sudden gust of
wind rattling the tin roof at home, or saw dark water slap against the harbor wall while unloading
nets in the early morning, his chest would tighten, his heart would pound, and his body would react
before he could think. He became intensely watchful around storms and rough weather, bracing himself
as though danger were already returning. What frightened him most was not only the memory of losing
his brother, but the thought that the world was still the same unsafe place that had taken him away.

Those physical surges fed back into his beliefs about the world. Each time his body reacted so
strongly, Somchai took it as proof that he had not left the danger behind and that he could not
trust anything around him to be safe. The more his hands shook and his breathing shortened when
clouds gathered over the water, the more certain he became that vigilance was the only thing keeping
him from being caught off guard again. In that sense, the very sensations that alarmed him made his
mistrust feel reasonable and necessary, as if his body were confirming what his mind already feared.
He began sleeping badly, lying awake long after his wife had turned off the lamp, listening for
weather changes and replaying the moment his brother slipped from sight. When he did sleep, it was
light and broken, and he woke tense and drained.

Over time, Somchai avoided the parts of his work that brought the memory closest. He stayed closer
to shore when he could, let others handle the rougher trips, and hesitated whenever the forecast
hinted at wind. The changes strained him at work and at home, because fishing had always been tied
to identity, routine, and family life. He did not say much about his distress to his wife, but she
noticed how withdrawn he had become and how often he sat silently after returning from the harbor.
Somchai himself described that silence as emotional numbness, though it felt less like calm than
like being sealed off from everyone else. Even when others tried to comfort him, he could not fully
take it in. Instead he kept turning back to the same conclusion: if he trusted too easily, if he
relaxed for even a moment, he might be punished again by losing someone else.

By the time he sought help, Somchai was exhausted by the constant strain of feeling on edge and
ashamed of surviving. He had finally come because the nights had become too long and the sea too
difficult to face each morning, and he was afraid that his body would keep reacting this way
forever. He hoped therapy might help him sleep and lessen the surges of fear and anger that still
rise whenever weather, water, or a sudden noise brings his brother’s death back before him. More
than anything, he wanted to believe that remaining alive did not mean he had failed his brother, and
that trust might someday feel possible again.
