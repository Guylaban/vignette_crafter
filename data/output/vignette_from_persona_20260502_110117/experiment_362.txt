when the accident happened, and the ordinary confidence she had brought into her classroom changed
in a single afternoon. She had been driving home after school with her husband when she misjudged a

injured, and Ayesha has never been able to let herself call it “an accident” in the casual way other
people did. In the days afterward, she found that the memory did not sit in the past at all. It
arrived as a sequence of frozen pictures: the light at the intersection, the bicycle tipping, the
man on the pavement, her own hands locked on the wheel. When she tried to speak about it, she could
sometimes smell the sharp, bitter scent of hot rubber and dust from the road, and that smell seemed
to pull her straight back into the scene, as if the afternoon were still unfolding around her.

What frightened Ayesha most was how complete the moment felt when it returned. The event did not
come back as a story she could tell herself had ended; it came back with the force of happening now,
and the more she was reminded of it, the more immediate it became. Hearing a colleague mention a car
crash in the staff room or seeing news about accidents would make her chest tighten and her mind
snap back to that intersection. Even something as simple as being praised for her teaching — a
parent thanking her for the way she handled a child, or the principal complimenting her lesson plans
— could unsettle her. She would feel the positive attention and, instead of comfort, a strange
confusion and dread, because any moment that made her feel proud or seen seemed to threaten the
memory’s return. In those moments, she would suddenly be back in the car in her mind, replaying the
impact as though she were still trapped inside it.

Ayesha had come to believe that the accident happened because of the way she acted, that her own
lapse had caused another person’s suffering, and from that she drew a harsher conclusion about
herself. She no longer felt capable of doing the things that had once defined her — being calm,
competent, and reliable — and she began to see herself as permanently changed, a person who did not
belong among others in the same way anymore. That sense of being an outsider colored the rest of her
life. At school, she kept to herself in the staff room and let conversations drift past if they
turned toward driving, roads, or anything that might turn into talk about accidents. She noticed
that even when the discussion was only casual, her body reacted before she could think: her mind
would flash to the crash, and the old images would crowd in again. Because the memory felt so vivid
and alive, any mention of accidents seemed to open the door to it, and once it was open she became
more convinced that she was dangerous, damaged, and fundamentally different from the other teachers
around her.

Over the following months, Ayesha started arranging her days around the hope of never being caught
by those moments of praise, joy, or accident talk. She worked harder to avoid situations where she
might be singled out for good performance, even though teaching had once been a source of pride. At
home, she grew quiet when her husband tried to reassure her that anyone could make a mistake,
because hearing that only sharpened her self-blame. The more she judged herself as no longer
capable, the more quickly she scanned for signs that something might remind her of the crash, and
