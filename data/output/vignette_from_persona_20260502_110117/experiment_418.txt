Mai was 29, South Eastern Asian, and worked as a combat pilot, a role she had once carried with iron
discipline and pride. The trauma that brought her to treatment was not something that had happened
to her body, but to someone she loved: during a mission briefed as routine, she received word that
her younger brother, who had been travelling with family overseas, had died suddenly in a civilian
accident while she was still in the air. She described the moment of hearing it as being stripped of
orientation all at once—her headset, the cockpit lights, the clipped voice of command, and the
flatness of the message fused together so sharply that the scene stayed with her. Since then, any
discomfort in her body, even the simple unease of sitting too long in a briefing room or feeling a
knot in her stomach before a sortie, could pull her straight back into that day. Talking about the
event did the same. So did hearing other people speak about loved ones being lost; those
conversations, even when casual, could make her go still and feel the whole memory crash over her
with no warning. Mai said that when the memory arrived, it did not feel like a story she was
recalling but like a film playing over and over, each detail fixed and inescapable, as though she
were looking at photographs of the moment she heard the news.

In the months after the loss, Mai began to believe that there was something wrong with her as a
person. What haunted her most was the conviction that she should have protected her brother and
failed him, even though she knew rationally she had not been physically present. That thought
sharpened her sense that she could not rely on other people, because no one had been able to keep
the worst thing from happening and no one could make it right after. Those appraisals gathered force
each time someone tried to talk with her about the loss or asked how she was coping, because the
conversation itself would bring the scene back with such force that she experienced it as if it were
happening again. The more fully the memory returned, the more certain she became that she had failed
in some essential way, and the more defective she felt for not being able to move past it like other
people seemed to.

To manage it, Mai started trying hard not to think about the trauma at all. She avoided reminders
whenever she could, especially when colleagues or friends brought up bereavement, family, or the
pain of losing someone; she would change the subject or leave the room. She also began steering
clear of new friendships and relationships, telling herself that it was easier not to let anyone
close enough to ask questions or notice when she went quiet. In the squadron, she kept conversations
professional and brief, and after flights she often went straight home rather than joining others
for food or drinks. That narrowing of her life made it easier, for a moment, to keep the worst
thoughts at bay, but it also left her more alone with them. With fewer people around, there were
fewer chances to be reassured or pulled back into ordinary life, and her belief that she could not
rely on others hardened further. When colleagues asked gentle questions or made well-meaning
comments about loss, she heard them as reminders that could not be trusted.

The effort to suppress thoughts did not give her relief for long. Mai found that the harder she
pushed the memory away, the more it seemed to rise up in fragments: the exact tone of the phone
call, the sensation of being unable to act, the image of her brother’s face appearing in her mind
with the hard clarity of a photograph. Once that happened, the memory itself became a trigger, and
she would feel the full scene rush back into the present, often without any clear warning from the
outside world. In turn, that sudden replay made her more determined to avoid anything that might set
it off, which kept her scanning other people’s speech and her own body for signs of discomfort. She
became tense in ordinary interactions, especially if someone mentioned death, family, or anything
resembling her loss, because she feared the next surge would come before she could stop it. The
cycle left her exhausted and more convinced that there was indeed something deeply wrong with her,
since she could not control what happened in her own mind.

By the time she sought help, Mai was still flying, but the cost of keeping herself contained had
become visible at work and at home. She was increasingly isolated, declining invitations and
avoiding getting close to anyone new, and she feared that if she let herself talk about her brother
or hear others speak about loss, the memory would engulf her again. What finally brought her in was
the sense that the suppression was failing and her world was shrinking around the fear of being
caught off guard. She hoped treatment might help her stop feeling as though one terrible moment
defined who she was, but what frightened her most was the possibility that the memory would keep
ambushing her forever and prove, beyond doubt, that she really could not protect the people she
loved.
