Maha, a 44-year-old Middle Eastern prosecutor, first began to feel unsafe during the months she
spent working in a war zone, when ordinary days were repeatedly interrupted by shelling, shouted
orders, and the sense that danger could arrive from anywhere and at any moment. One afternoon, while
she was walking between a courthouse annex and a temporary office, a man’s voice behind her sounded
uncannily like the voice of someone she had heard on the radio calling out names during a bombing
aftermath. She turned sharply, certain she was being followed, and for a split second she thought
she saw someone watching her from across the street. Nothing happened in that instant, but the
feeling lodged in her body. After that, the very things she once moved through without noticing
began to set off alarm: a related voice in a corridor, the sense that a car had slowed too long
beside her, the impression that someone was tracking her route home. Each time those experiences
appeared, her body snapped into vigilance, and she found herself scanning exits, counting people
behind her, and feeling that she had to keep moving until she reached one of only a handful of
places that still felt safe.

In the months that followed, Maha’s nights changed as much as her days. She started waking in the
dark to check the doors, the windows, and the hallway, unable to settle unless she had confirmed
that she was safe for the moment. Even in bed beside her husband, she would listen for unexpected
sounds and tense at every loud bang from the street, every raised voice from a neighboring
apartment, every abrupt slam in the building. The more alert she became to these noises, the more
they seemed to confirm that danger was near. Her world narrowed to a small circle of places she
trusted: a corner of her own bedroom, the interior of her office with the door shut, one sheltered
route between home and work. Outside those limited safe zones, she moved as if exposed, convinced
that she might be watched from a window, followed through a parking lot, or caught off guard by a
voice that sounded too familiar.

As that vigilance took hold, it changed the way Maha understood herself. She had once thought of
herself as steady and capable, the kind of person others relied on in court, but she came to believe
that she could not deal with even the slightest upset without falling apart. A minor delay at work,
a noisy street, a disagreement with her husband could leave her feeling overwhelmed and exhausted,
as though she had no reserve left. She described herself now as always miserable, not because there
was one constant catastrophe, but because she felt she could no longer relax into her life. The more
she saw the world as dangerous, the less room she had for ordinary pleasure; she stopped lingering
after work, avoided unnecessary errands, and chose routes and schedules around places where she
might be seen or tracked. At work, where the courthouse lobby and parking structure exposed her to
strangers and sudden noise, she became increasingly rigid about arrival times and exits, and
colleagues noticed that she no longer stayed late unless she had to.

Maha’s fear had become self-reinforcing in small, relentless ways. Unexpected sounds kept pulling
her back into alertness, and that alertness made the familiar feel threatening again: a man’s voice
in the hallway, a vehicle idling outside, a glance from a passerby. Because she was so primed to
notice being watched or followed, she found more and more occasions that seemed to prove it, and
each one tightened her sense that the world was dangerous and that she had only a few safe places
left. She and her husband had begun to work around her routines rather than challenge them, which
preserved peace at home but also made her life smaller. Sleep offered little relief because she
still woke during the night to check whether she was safe, and by morning she was already braced for
the next sound, the next possible shadow, the next moment she might have to decide whether she was
being tracked.

What finally brought Maha to treatment was not a single dramatic event, but the accumulation of
these narrow boundaries and sleepless checks, along with a growing fear that she was becoming
unrecognizable to herself. She sought help after realizing that her life had contracted into
vigilance and avoidance, and that she no longer believed she could cope with even ordinary
disruption. More than anything, she hopes she can feel safe enough to sleep through the night and
move through her city without constantly waiting for the next voice, the next footstep behind her,
or the next reminder that she sees the world now as a dangerous place.
