to tell the story now, she has to stop, because the fragments come back with such force that she
feels flooded by the same fear and helplessness she felt that night. The memory does not stay away
when she wants it to; it forces its way in while she is at work, while she is trying to cook rice at
home, even while she is standing at the sink after a long day. Since the disaster, she has come to
believe that she will never be able to feel normal emotions again and that she no longer belongs
anywhere, as if the storm took not only her home but also the place she had in the world. She is
certain that she cannot stop bad things from happening to her, and that conviction has made every
strange noise feel like a warning. A tray dropping in the restaurant kitchen, a metal door slamming
in the back alley, even the hiss of the soda machine can make her flinch and turn toward the sound
before she knows she has done it. At night she checks the locks more than once and lies awake
listening, because she feels safer when the daylight is out and the world is quiet, and less safe
when darkness settles over the apartment.

Over the months that followed, Lan’s expectation that disaster could strike again seemed to keep her
mind tethered to the storm. She scanned for danger constantly, and that high alertness made the
memory easier to provoke: a sudden bang from the restaurant’s ice machine or the snap of a weather
alert on a customer’s phone would pull the scene back into her head in broken flashes. Sometimes she
would be wrapping burgers or wiping counters when the image returned so abruptly that she had to
pause, grip the edge of the stainless-steel table, and breathe until the wave of emotion settled

feel safe. She began sleeping lightly, waking at the smallest noise, and each restless night left
