small studio attached to his home, described his distress as beginning with the bombing that tore
through the public market where he had been buying supplies years earlier. He had been standing near
the fruit stalls when the blast hit, and what remained most vivid for him was not an abstract fear
but the exact way his body had been thrown and pinned, the dust in his mouth, the ringing in his
ears, and the sight of people scrambling through smoke. Even now, when the memory forced its way
into his mind without warning, it arrived with extraordinary clarity and detail; he could see the
broken awning, smell the burning, and feel himself folded into the same position he had been in on
the ground. At those moments, he said, it was as if his body were back there, trapped in the same
posture, and the intensity of that bodily reenactment left him shaken for hours.

Over time, those intrusions came to mean something dangerous to him. Mikaere had come to believe
that the memory could seize him anywhere and that, if it could do that, then he could not rely on
himself to stay steady when it mattered. He spoke with the weary certainty of a man who had tried to
reason his way through fear and lost confidence in his own judgment. The same experience also made
other people feel unreliable; during the aftermath of the attack, he had seen how quickly a normal
day could turn chaotic, and he now felt that he could not rely on other people to keep him safe
either. From there, the world itself seemed less like a place he moved through and more like a place
waiting to turn on him. He began to talk about the world as dangerous in an almost matter-of-fact
way, as if that were simply the lesson the bombing had taught him.

Those beliefs shaped the way he lived. Mikaere started asking for reassurance more and more, phoning
his sister after small outings to check that he had sounded all right, and sometimes calling a
neighbor just to hear someone say he was okay. He tried hard to control his emotions in public,
keeping his face still and his voice measured because he feared that if he let himself show too
much, he would lose control and confirm his own sense that he could not rely on himself. Going out
became something he approached cautiously and then began to avoid whenever possible. The local

narrowed around the studio and the rooms he could manage without too much strain.

out, he had fewer ordinary experiences to interrupt the sense of threat, and the longer he stayed
home, the more the bombing stayed central in his mind. When he tried to contain his feelings and
push through alone, the effort left him tense and watchful, which made the memories more likely to
break through; once they did, the vividness of them and the bodily feeling that accompanied them fed
right back into his conviction that he was not capable of managing what happened inside him. The
more he sought reassurance, the more he noticed how much he needed it, and that only deepened his
belief that he could not trust himself or count on others to notice when he was slipping. In recent

the bombing, only to find the scene arrive in full detail that night, with his body seeming to lock

Mikaere came for help now because the circle had started to take more from his life than he could
