parking lot outside her office. A man from a neighboring building, already agitated, came toward her
with something in his hand that she only registered at first as a weapon-like shape; the motion was
sudden, the shouting close enough to hear through the glass. In the scramble that followed, Marisol
acted on panic and certainty that someone was about to be hurt. She shoved him away with force, he
fell badly against the concrete and sustained a serious injury, and the memory of that night never
settled into a clean story afterward. What stayed with her was the sight of him going down, the
sound of impact, and then the shock of realizing that her own action had done this. Since her
husband’s death years earlier, she had already been living with a kind of private grief, but this
was different: she described it as a wound of guilt, the feeling that she had crossed some line she
could never uncross.

In the weeks after the incident, Marisol began to notice that when she remembered the event she was
flooded by overwhelming grief before she could even picture it clearly. Sometimes the emotion
arrived without any image at all, a hard surge in her chest and stomach that left her nauseated and
shaking; at other times she tried to pull the scene back together and found that there were parts
she simply could not remember no matter how hard she tried. That blankness frightened her almost as
much as the memory itself. She had come to believe that if she could only reconstruct the missing
pieces, she would know whether she had been reckless, whether she had used too much force, whether
she had missed a chance to stop before it was too late. Instead, the gaps kept her stuck with the
worst possibility, and each attempt to fill them in only made the grief feel more immediate and more
unbearable.

The strangest part, Marisol said, was how easily the memory could be stirred by things that seemed
ordinary to other people. Weapons or objects used as weapons were the fastest route back: a
screwdriver on a workbench, a wrench in a garage, even the black edge of a tool in a kitchen drawer
could make her body tense before she had time to think. Confrontations had the same effect. If two
coworkers raised their voices in a meeting, or if she saw an argument unfold in a grocery store
parking lot, the scene from that night would flare inside her, and with it the same grief and
uncertainty. She did cybersecurity analysis from home three days a week now, but even the office
could feel unsafe; she found herself scanning entrances, noting who was standing too close to whom,
and rehearsing how quickly a harmless disagreement could become physical. Her fear was not only that
another confrontation might lead to injury, but that it would drag her back into the same emotional
flood and the same missing fragments she could not master.

Over time, Marisol began arranging her life around avoiding those moments. She stopped walking past
the side lot at work and started coming in through the loading dock if she had to go in at all. She
turned down after-hours team dinners because bars and crowded sidewalks made unexpected conflict
feel too close. At home she kept her eyes away from the kitchen drawer where the big tools were
stored, and when a movie or news report showed a weapon, she would leave the room. These choices
made sense to her: if she could keep weapons, trauma-related events, and confrontations out of
reach, maybe she could keep the grief from breaking open again. But the avoidance narrowed her
world. She became more isolated from colleagues, less likely to linger after meetings, and more
dependent on carefully controlled routines that left little room for spontaneity or comfort.

By the time she sought help, Marisol was exhausted by how tightly the memory and the triggers seemed
to chase one another. A tool handle, a heated exchange, even a story on the evening news about a
violent incident could pull her back into the night at the office, and then the grief would rise so
fast that she would flee, shut down, or spend hours trying to force the scene into order. The more
she tried to make sense of what she had done, the more the missing pieces seemed to mock her, and
the more she feared she might be one accident away from becoming that person again. What finally
brought her in was the realization that her world had become organized around not being reminded,
and that the cost was growing too high. She wanted relief from the flood of grief and from the
constant watching for danger, but what she feared most was that if she stopped avoiding, she would
be overwhelmed by the same unbearable memory and never come back from it.
