Dennis Alvarez was 68 years old, married, and still working part-time as a school counselor, but the
assault that had happened years earlier continued to organize his days in ways that felt both
humiliating and unavoidable. He had been walking back to his car after a late community meeting when
two men crowded him near a strip of storefronts, one of them reeking of alcohol, the other shoving
him hard enough that the pavement seemed to lurch upward. Dennis remembered it not as a continuous
scene but as a handful of pictures: the glare of a parking lot light, a fist driving toward his
face, the slick edge of a curb under his shoulder, the sour wash of beer on a stranger’s breath.
When he tried to think about it, the same terror rushed back through him as if he were there again,
trapped in those few frozen moments, and even now fragments of the memory could break loose without
warning and vanish just as quickly, leaving him shaken and disoriented.

Over time, certain ordinary experiences began to hook directly into that memory. The smell of
antiseptic in the hospital where he later went for treatment after the assault could hit him in a
hallway or examination room and instantly call up the panic of being pinned helpless on the ground.
Alcohol smell had the same effect; a passing whiff from someone in a grocery store or at a family
gathering could pull him back into the fear of that night. He noticed, too, that when someone
started getting emotionally close to him, the old helplessness returned in a different form.
Intimacy made him feel exposed, as though closeness itself could become a way of being overpowered,
and that sense of threat sharpened the memory until it felt immediate again.

In the months that followed, Dennis began managing those reactions by trying to leave his body
whenever the fear rose too sharply. He would deliberately go blank, stare through people, or focus
so hard on the pattern of tiles or the pressure of his feet that he could make himself feel far
away. He avoided other people whenever he could, taking lunch alone in his office at school and
finding reasons not to linger after meetings. With his wife, who had always been affectionate and
emotionally open, he pulled back from intimacy and kept conversations practical, because being
emotionally close felt like standing too near the edge of that old danger. He had come to believe
that if he stayed distant enough, no one would get close enough to hurt him.

But those efforts brought their own costs. The more he kept away from people, the less practiced he
felt in being around them, and the more startling it became when a student’s parent leaned in with
the faint smell of alcohol on his clothes or when the disinfectant odor from the school nurse’s
office drifted into the counseling suite. His deliberate blanking out helped for a moment, yet it
also left him feeling less grounded, more vulnerable to sudden pieces of the assault surfacing in
the middle of a workday. Each time that happened, he became more determined to avoid situations that
might stir it up again, including closeness with his wife, whose concern he could feel but could not
bear to meet directly. In that way, the fear kept circling back: the memory intensified his attempts
to shut down and withdraw, and those very habits made the smells, the fleeting emotional closeness,
and the sudden fragments easier to encounter and harder to tolerate. At school he still functioned,
but he was increasingly tense in meetings, guarded with colleagues, and preoccupied with finding
exits, as if any ordinary interaction could unexpectedly turn threatening.

By the time he sought help, Dennis was exhausted by the narrowness of his life and troubled by how
alone he felt in his own marriage. What finally brought him in was realizing that he had begun to
dread the school nurse’s hallway and even family dinners if there was wine on the table, and that he
was pushing away the very people he most wanted to stay connected to. He hoped therapy might help
him stay present when the terror surged and stop living as though every approach, every smell, and
every moment of closeness might become the assault all over again.
