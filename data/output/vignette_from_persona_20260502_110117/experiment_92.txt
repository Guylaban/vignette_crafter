prided herself on being composed and practical, first began to change after she witnessed a serious
accident near a bus stop on her way home from work. She had been standing in the city crowd waiting
to cross when a commotion broke out, people surged in every direction, and she saw a stranger
collapse hard to the pavement while others shouted and backed away. The noise, the press of bodies,
and the helplessness of watching from the middle of a packed sidewalk stayed with her. In the weeks
afterward, crowded places became sharply intolerable: grocery stores, shopping centres, public
events, and especially bus riding, where the density of strangers and the stop-and-go motion made
her feel trapped and on edge. Certain conversation topics could do it too, especially anything about
accidents, injury, or “what can happen out in public.” When those moments hit, Margaret did not
experience them as ordinary discomfort; they convinced her that the world was dangerous and that she
had to be especially careful because you never know what can happen next. She had come to believe
that she was permanently an outsider now, as if everyone else moved through shared spaces with an
ease she no longer possessed.

Those appraisals quickly shaped how she managed herself. Margaret tried to force herself to think
only positive thoughts, telling herself that she should focus on blessings, stay upbeat, and not
“give the bad thing more room than it deserves.” She used that strategy almost like a mental
discipline, repeating it whenever the danger feeling rose. She also began avoiding the site of the
event altogether, taking long detours around the area where the accident had happened, even when it
added time to errands or made her husband drive out of the way. If a route required the bus or
passed through a crowded commercial area, she refused it when she could. At first she told herself
this was sensible caution, a way to keep steady by not dwelling on what had happened. But the more
she pushed herself only toward positive thinking, the more artificial her reassurance felt, and the
more she noticed how fragile it was. When a conversation at dinner turned to a local street injury,
her effort to paste over the fear with upbeat thoughts only made her feel more unsettled, and the
underlying belief returned with greater force: the world was dangerous, and she could not trust
herself to relax. She became even more convinced that she had to stay on guard at all times, which
made her scan every crowded room and every unfamiliar face for signs that something might go wrong.
That watchfulness also made the triggers feel closer and harder to escape, because the more she was
braced for danger, the more she detected it in the very settings she most feared.

Over the months, Margaret’s world narrowed around that pattern. She still went to work, but even in
the network office she found herself turning down invitations that involved public transport or busy
venues, and she stopped accompanying her husband to crowded shopping centres. She would sit in the
car outside instead of going inside with him. Each time she successfully avoided a crowd or the bus,
the relief was immediate, and that relief confirmed for her that caution was necessary. Yet the
short-term comfort fed the same conviction that had taken hold after the accident: if she needed to
escape or keep herself wrapped in positive thoughts just to get through an ordinary day, then the
danger must really be there. The strategy that was meant to steady her instead reinforced the sense
that she was perpetually vulnerable and set apart from other people, and once that belief was in
place, even ordinary public settings began to feel like potential traps. A brief mention of a
collision on the radio, a dense line at the pharmacy, or the thought of walking back past the bus
stop could quickly bring the whole alarm system back online. She found herself tense before leaving
the house, checking exits and planning how to avoid any place where strangers might crowd her. Her
husband noticed that she was less likely to join family outings, and Margaret felt ashamed that her
life had become organized around not being where other people were.

She came for help after realizing that her careful routines were no longer keeping her world small
enough to feel safe. The accident had been years ago, but the feeling that she was an outsider, the
certainty that danger could appear anywhere, and the constant effort to think positively and steer
away from crowds had never really loosened. What frightened her most now was that the next ordinary
outing might become another proof that she was right to fear the world, and what she hoped for was a
way to sit on a bus, walk through a crowded place, and not feel that she had to disappear from it.
