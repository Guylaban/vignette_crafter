As time passed, Maya’s sense of herself darkened alongside her view of the world. She had once
imagined herself as steady in crisis, but now she saw her reactions—her fear, her vigilance, her
need to arrange every detail—as proof that she was a lousy coper. That belief kept pulling her back
to the bombing in her mind, as if she needed to rehearse the scene again and again to locate where
she had failed. The memory became more vivid whenever she judged herself this way, returning with
the same metallic taste, ringing ears, and smoke that made it feel less like remembering than being
dropped back into it. That belief made her scan herself and her surroundings differently, so that
the same small noise or abrupt movement that she might once have brushed off now snagged her
attention and felt like evidence that she could not trust her own judgment. The more she decided she
was not coping well, the more she treated ordinary moments as potential warnings. That appraisal
made it harder for her to trust her own judgment, and when she recalled the bombing, the memory did
not arrive like a normal recollection. The conviction that she was a lousy coper sent her back to
the scene over and over, as if she had to find the moment she failed and prove to herself that she
should have been stronger. Each return made it harder to step into ordinary situations without
bracing for disaster, because the memory itself seemed to confirm that she was not built to handle
what came. Because she was already convinced that disaster was always close, a loud tray dropped in
the hospital cafeteria or a uniformed officer at a doorway could snag her attention at once and take
on the force of a warning. The thought that she could not trust herself to read danger correctly
meant that even a laugh in the corridor or a bright, celebratory mood around her felt suspicious, as
if it were hiding the next blow. It came back with full sensory force: the metallic taste of dust,
the ringing in her ears, the smell of smoke, the sight of blood on clothing, all of it feeling
completely real across her senses at once. The fragments would come in the wrong order, leaving her
unable to tell whether she heard the blast before seeing people fall or whether the shouting began
first. When the memory hit like that, Maya would avoid the places and routines that seemed most
likely to call it up again — meals in the hospital cafeteria, public celebrations, even lingering
near uniforms — because she did not trust herself to stay steady if the scene returned while she was
there. She kept her rituals going for the same reason, counting and repeating phrases until the
images dulled enough for her to keep functioning. Because the memory felt more vivid than ordinary
life, it would be set off by the kinds of things she had come to fear in daily life: an unexpected
bang in the street outside her apartment, a loud tray dropped in the hospital cafeteria where she
sometimes volunteered, or the sight of a uniformed officer at the entrance to a building. Each cue
pushed her back into the same conviction that disaster could strike without warning and that she
would be left unprotected again. She became especially unsettled by people misbehaving or acting
arrogantly; a brusque tone at work or someone ignoring a line in public could stir the old sense
that order could collapse in an instant. The sense of danger itself had become proof to her that
life had changed beyond repair, and she began to accept the thought that she was now someone who had
to live on guard. Feeling that threat so persistently, she came to believe not only that the world
was unsafe, but that this frightened vigilance was simply who she was now.

Those experiences kept feeding her habits. Maya began choosing seats with great care wherever she
went, favoring corners, backs to walls, and clear views of exits. At the hospital, in the church
basement, and even in small neighborhood cafés, she scanned other people closely for signs that they
might harm her, reading posture, hands, and faces with the intensity of someone trying to stay
alive. Loud or unexpected sounds kept her on high alert, and she would stiffen before she could
think. At the same time, her repeated mental rituals and food restriction offered a temporary
illusion of control, but they also kept her body in a state of tension that made every sound and
movement feel more dangerous. Because she never stayed long enough in those moments for anything to
settle, her body never got the evidence that the danger had passed. The counting, the restriction,
and the careful control made the threat feel as if it were still active, exactly as real as it had
been on the day of the bombing. The more she tried to manage risk through these routines, the more
she felt exposed to it. Her wariness made it hard to relax even in spaces that should have felt
familiar, and her growing distrust of authority figures and uniforms complicated the work that had
once given her meaning. She started avoiding staff meetings where police liaisons or emergency
personnel were present, and she would find excuses not to linger after services if a uniformed
visitor arrived.

By the time she sought help, Maya was exhausted by the way the bombing still governed her days. She
was no longer only remembering what happened; she was living as though danger might return at any
moment, and every vivid flash of the event seemed to confirm that her fear was justified. What
finally brought her in was realizing that she had begun shrinking her life around these fears—eating
less, watching everyone, choosing her seats like a survivor planning an escape route—and that even
her work as a chaplain was being narrowed by the conviction that the world, and the people who were
supposed to safeguard it, could not be trusted. She said she wanted to stop feeling as if the blast
were still happening in her body, and she was most afraid that if she could not find a way to loosen
her grip on control, she would remain trapped in a life organized entirely around preventing the
next catastrophe.
