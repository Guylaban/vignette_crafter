a community art fair together, still laughing over whether the glaze on Robert’s blue cups had come

ambulance crew took over, and later had to answer the questions that came when it became clear that
Daniel would not be coming back. What stayed with Robert was not only the shock of the scene but the
abruptness of it: one minute Daniel was alive beside him, the next he was a memory everyone else
seemed to handle better than Robert could.

In the months that followed, Robert found that anything that carried Daniel’s presence could catch
him off guard. A particular cedar-smoke scent from the kiln area, the sound of men laughing at the
hardware store, a song Daniel used to hum, even the sight of the folding tables he used for fairs
could bring a hard stop to his day. He would turn away from family photographs and leave rooms when
someone mentioned a holiday plan that Daniel should have been part of. At home, he began clearing
away small reminders more quickly than anyone else noticed: Daniel’s old coffee mug, a flyer from
the last exhibition they had discussed, a voicemail he could not bring himself to replay. He told
himself it was easier not to open the door to that pain than to keep getting hit by it.

Robert also became wary of anything that made the loss feel present in a more hopeful way. When his
wife suggested a weekend trip or mentioned booking a winter show, he felt a jolt of alarm rather
than comfort. Positive feelings themselves had started to feel unsafe to him, as if enjoying a good
day was an invitation for something terrible to be taken away again. If a kiln load came out
especially well, or a customer praised his work, he noticed himself flattening his reaction, pushing
the pleasure down before it could settle. He had come to believe that if he let himself feel too
happy, he would be blindsided by another loss, and that belief made even ordinary moments of
satisfaction feel fragile and suspect.

to visit their daughter in Oregon, but he now avoided making any future plans beyond the next few

put off decisions about the studio that used to come easily. The calendar on the kitchen wall stayed
half empty. He said he did not want to promise anything he might not be able to bear, but beneath
that was a deeper fear that looking ahead meant risking hope, and hope made room for disappointment.
The result was that days became smaller and more repetitive, organized around what could be managed
without imagining what might come next.

always bracing for a wave of grief to break through. The less he allowed himself to feel, the more

Daniel without immediately shutting down, and to live in a way that did not feel as though the
future itself had become something to avoid.
