know that people were hurt, and the sight of blood on the roadside and on the vehicle door stayed
with her in a way she could not shake. In the first weeks afterward, even talk about accidents could
jolt her; if coworkers mentioned a deer strike, a rollover, or a wreck on the county road, she felt
her body tense and her attention snap outward, and she began staying up until very late so she would
not have to lie in bed with the images waiting for her. The more she tried to keep herself busy and
postpone sleep, the more she found herself scanning every parking lot, shoulder, and stretch of road
for signs of danger, as if watching closely enough might keep something awful from happening again.

Certain reminders pulled her back with unnerving force. If she smelled hot metal, dust, or the sharp
roadside odor she associated with that evening, the scene seemed to rush forward in fragments,
though never as a full, orderly story. She would catch herself pushing the memory away before she
could fully recall it, and she described the recollection as belonging to someone else rather than
to her, as if she were standing beside a stranger’s disaster instead of her own experience. Even so,
when the memory surfaced, she felt the same unease in her stomach and an urgent need to get away
from it. Those moments quickly fed her sense that she had acted in ways that went against who she
was. Leila had always seen herself as careful, useful, and someone who would step in when others
needed help, and she could not let go of the belief that she should have protected the people
involved and failed them. That conviction darkened her mood further; she came to believe she used to
be a happy person but now was always miserable, and the thought of herself as someone who had let
others down sat heavily with her long after the scene had passed.

refusing invitations if she thought anyone might bring up the crash, and if coworkers started

constantly even in the forest office or while loading equipment, her eyes moving to every entrance
and passing vehicle. Yet the very act of trying to manage things this way seemed to keep the memory
active. When she was alone at night, the unfinished fragments would return more easily; when she had
stayed up too late and was exhausted, the smell, the blood, and the image of the crumpled car
intruded with more vividness, and she would again feel that it was not fully her own memory. In
those moments, the same belief about failing to protect others would flare back up, and with it the
conviction that she had become a different, worse version of herself. The cycle made her feel
trapped between guarding against danger and being haunted by what she had seen.

that evening and would never stop feeling miserable because of it.
