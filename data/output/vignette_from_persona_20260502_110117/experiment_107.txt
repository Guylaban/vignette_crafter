Omar, a 40-year-old Middle Eastern software engineer, had been living in Houston with his wife when
the storm hit their neighborhood with a violence that felt unreal even to him now. He had gone to
the apartment complex garage to move the car after the first reports of flooding, and within minutes
the water was up around his legs, rising fast and carrying debris, while somewhere nearby an alarm
shrieked and an ambulance siren cut in and out through the rain. He remembered the sound of metal
grinding against concrete, the panic in people’s voices, and the shock of cold water pulling at him
as he tried to reach higher ground. In the weeks that followed, he found that the memory would come
back with startling force: sometimes the scene returned in pieces and he could not put the order
together correctly, and sometimes it landed all at once, fully sensory, as though he were back in
the garage with the same water, the same noise, and the same helpless urgency. When that happened,
he would even hear fragments of the sounds from that night in his head, vivid enough to make him
grip the edge of a desk or stop walking until it passed.

Omar had come to believe that somebody else would not have gotten into that situation, and he
carried that comparison with a harsh sense of personal failure. He told himself that nothing good
could happen to him anymore and that he used to be a happy person but now was always miserable.
Those appraisals made every reminder feel dangerous: if the world could turn that suddenly against
him once, then it could do so again at any time. He became jumpy at unexpected movement nearby,
reacting as if he were in immediate danger when a colleague stood up quickly beside his cubicle or
when his wife came up behind him and touched his shoulder without warning. In the office, where he
spent long hours writing code, he found himself checking the hallway and stairwells before he could
settle at his desk, and in restaurants or parking garages he always identified the escape routes
before he felt comfortable enough to sit down. The more he was overtaken by the memory of the flood,
the more his body seemed to live in that same emergency, and the more certain he became that he had
to scan for danger because the memory itself proved how quickly things could turn.

As these episodes repeated, Omar tried to manage them by forcing himself to think only positive
thoughts and by pushing away anything that reminded him of hospitals, ambulances, or physical
examinations. He told himself that if he stayed upbeat and refused to dwell on the disaster, he
could keep the fear under control. But the effort left him more tense and vigilant, and the very act
of suppressing what had happened made the memories break through more sharply and with less warning.
When they did, they carried the threat with them: the sounds in his head, the sense that it was
happening again, and the certainty that he was never really safe. That heightened alarm fed back
into his thinking, and after another night of reliving the garage scene he would be left even more
convinced that nothing good could happen to him anymore. He noticed that he was no longer the happy
person he used to be, and each failed attempt to stay positive seemed to confirm that loss.

His alarm also pushed him toward other habits that kept the fear alive. He ruminated about how to
get even with the “assailant,” a word he used for the storm and the sudden force that had trapped
him, imagining in a grim, repetitive way how he might have acted differently or how he would not be
caught helpless again. Those thoughts did not bring relief; they left him more keyed up, more
watchful, and more ready to flinch at touch or movement. Even at home he would start if his wife
came into the kitchen too quickly or if the doorbell rang unexpectedly, and he hated how exposed he
felt in places without a clear exit. The loop had narrowed his world. He had begun avoiding medical
settings altogether, turning down a recommended checkup after a minor chest tightness episode
because the waiting room, the smell of disinfectant, and the idea of being examined felt too close
to the storm’s helplessness. Over time, that avoidance, the constant scanning, and the grim private
rehearsals of revenge left him more isolated from his wife and more strained at work, where he
feared appearing distracted or weak. What finally brought him to seek help was realizing that the
memories were becoming more intrusive and more vivid, and that his life was shrinking around them.
He hoped to stop feeling as though danger were always about to arrive, but he was most afraid that
if he let his guard down even briefly, the flood and everything it had taken from him would come
rushing back.
