began to pulse and the building lights flickered out. At first he assumed it was a routine drill,
but within minutes the hallway outside his door filled with the sharp, bitter smell of smoke, and
the announcement over the intercom became choppy with static. By the time he reached the stairwell,
he could hear people shouting several floors below and feel the crush of uncertainty around him: no
one could say where the smoke was coming from, whether it was contained, or which route was safest.
What stayed with him most from that night was not only the smell but the bodily sensation of not
feeling right in his own skin—his stomach turning, his head light, his legs unsteady as if he might
faint. In the weeks afterward, those moments of feeling physically unwell and those sudden,
unpredictable shifts in his surroundings became tightly linked in his mind with the thought that he
had failed at the most basic task of keeping himself safe.

Before the fire, Minh had been proud of being careful, steady, and composed. He worked as a policy
analyst, a job that depended on weighing contingencies and anticipating what could go wrong, and for
most of his adult life that attentiveness had felt like a strength. Afterward, it became something
much more frightening. When he felt even slightly ill—an uneasy stomach before breakfast, a brief
wave of dizziness in a meeting, the kind of fatigue that used to mean nothing—he would become
convinced that he could no longer trust himself to judge danger correctly. If his body could betray
him without warning, he reasoned, then so could his mind. He had come to believe that his reactions
since the event meant that he was going crazy, especially when the fear rose quickly and made his
thoughts race in circles. He started checking the office exits whenever the building made an odd
sound and scanning his surroundings for signs of trouble, telling himself that he had to be
especially careful because you never know what can happen next. That vigilance felt protective to
him, but it also confirmed, each time he did it, how unsafe the world now seemed.

The smell of smoke was the most immediate way the memory returned. If a neighbor’s barbecue drifted
through the apartment courtyard, or if a passing car left a hot exhaust smell near the parking
garage, Minh would feel his chest tighten before he had fully identified the source. In those
moments he became certain that his body’s alarm was accurate and that he could not rely on himself
to stay calm or to make a good decision if another emergency came. He began to mistrust his own
reactions in ordinary situations, especially when something felt off but he could not explain why,
and that mistrust made him more alert to every faint scent, every flicker of uncertainty. At work,
where he spent his days in a quiet government office reviewing reports and drafting recommendations,
he started avoiding the conference room closest to the kitchen because burnt toast from the break
area could send him back to the stairwell. He would open windows in the middle of winter and excuse
himself from meetings if someone mentioned an evacuation plan, a wildfire update, or anything that
hinted at unpredictability.

Over time, his life narrowed around these fears. He no longer lingered after work to talk with
colleagues, and he had begun asking his wife to handle more of the errands that involved crowded
places or unfamiliar routes. He disliked how often he had to apologize for leaving early or seeming
distracted, but he was more afraid of what might happen if he ignored the warnings his mind kept
sending. The more he watched for signs of danger, the more often he noticed his own racing pulse,
queasy stomach, and brief spells of dizziness, and each episode seemed to prove that he was not the
man he had been before the fire. The fear of unpredictability did not stay abstract; it followed him
into elevators that jerked between floors, into weather alerts on his phone, even into the simple
experience of feeling tired after lunch. What had begun as a response to one night in a smoke-filled
building had become, in his experience, evidence that he must remain on guard at all times, because
a moment of inattention could mean he would freeze, panic, or make some disastrous mistake.

By the time he sought help, Minh was exhausted by the effort of staying careful. He came after a
week in which a faint burning smell from a delivery truck in the office parking lot sent him home
early, ashamed and shaken, and he realized he was no longer sure how to tell the difference between
actual danger and his own body’s warnings. He wanted to believe he could trust himself again, but
what frightened him most was the possibility that the next surge of fear would confirm his worst
thought—that something in him had broken, and that one more unpredictable moment could send him
spiraling further.
