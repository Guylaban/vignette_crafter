Arjun Patel had grown up in a small apartment in Queens with the sounds of his parents’ marriage
leaking through the walls, but one night in particular had never loosened its grip on him. He was
nine when he heard his mother crying in the kitchen and found his father shoving her against the
counter, a lamp falling and shattering beside them. Arjun remembered freezing in the doorway, his
chest tight, unable to make sense of the scene except that it felt like terror itself had entered
the room. Even now, he could not put the event into words without stalling; it remained less a story
than a rush of bodily fear and fragments — the crash of glass, his mother’s sobbing, his own small
hands gripping the frame. When that memory returned, it brought back the same terror he had felt
then, and he hated how suddenly it could happen, as if something in the world could open the whole
scene without warning.

In the years after, Arjun came to believe that the men who hurt women like that never truly paid for
it. His father had faced no consequences that mattered to him as a child, and that belief settled
into a bitter conviction that bad things happened and no one stopped them. He also grieved a version
of himself he felt he had lost; he used to think of himself as easygoing and even cheerful, but
after the divorce and the years of strain that followed, he described himself as almost always
miserable, as though the life he had wanted had been taken and never given back. He was certain that
he could not stop bad things from happening to him or to the people he cared about, and that
helplessness sat underneath nearly everything he did. When he recalled the old violence, the memory
did not stay contained; it immediately fed his sense that the world was unfair and ungoverned, which
in turn made the fear feel more urgent and more unavoidable.

Arjun worked as an accountant in a downtown office, where the silence of the rows of computers and
the sudden clang of a filing cabinet could jolt him far more than he wanted anyone to know. A
colleague dropping a heavy binder, a door slamming in the hallway, or even a sharp argument in the
break room could bring the whole scene back in a flash. In those moments his body reacted before he
could think: his heart raced, his shoulders locked, his stomach clenched, and he felt keyed up as if
something dangerous were about to break into the office. He stayed superalert at work and in public,
watching doorways, scanning voices, and sitting where he could see exits. The more his body stayed
on guard, the more the memory seemed to rush in uninvited; when his muscles tensed and his pulse
jumped, the old kitchen scene became easier to summon, as though his own alarm system were calling
it up. That same flood of remembered terror then deepened what he already believed — that no one had
protected his mother, that no one would intervene now, and that he remained powerless — and he would
leave the office unsettled, carrying the conviction that danger could arrive at any moment.

Over time Arjun began arranging his life around the possibility of being caught off guard. He
avoided family gatherings where loud voices might rise, and he kept his apartment television low
because shouting from a show could set off the same physical surge. At work he volunteered less and
lingered after meetings so he would not have to pass through crowded hallways when people were
irritated or hurried. His divorce had narrowed his daily world further; he said he no longer had the
energy to be the man he had once imagined being, and he often withdrew rather than risk being
exposed as tense or afraid. Yet the withdrawal only gave his mind more room to replay what had
happened and to return to the same bleak conclusions. As the memories came back, so did the bodily
alarm, and as the alarm stayed high, the old conviction hardened that harm was always just outside
the frame. By the time he sought help, Arjun was exhausted by living as if he were always bracing
for the next blow. What brought him in was a recent incident at the office when a coworker raised
his voice in a tense meeting and Arjun felt his chest seize so sharply he had to step into the
stairwell to breathe. He was most afraid that he would remain trapped in this state forever, and
most hoping that treatment might help him feel safe enough to stop living as though danger were
already in the room.
