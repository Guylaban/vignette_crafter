Frank had been a North American salesman for most of his adult life, and even at 70 he still moved
through his days with the tidy habits of someone used to reading people quickly and keeping a
conversation on track. That confidence changed after an assault that occurred one evening when he
was leaving a small bar near a hotel where he had been meeting a client. He had stopped in for a
drink after work, the smell of alcohol thick in the doorway and around the counter, and remembers
turning toward the exit just as a man he later recognized from the neighborhood came too close,
invading his space and striking him in the confusion that followed. What lingered most was not only
the pain and fear of the attack itself, but the way the offender’s face seemed to reappear in
ordinary life afterward: in passing strangers on the street, in a man standing too long near a
storefront, in any abrupt movement that resembled that night. In the weeks after, those sights and
even the odor of alcohol could bring the whole scene back in an unwanted rush of thoughts, and Frank
found himself bracing the moment he caught a whiff of beer or liquor, as though his body knew before
his mind could catch up.

From the beginning, these reminders pushed him toward silence and distance. He began trying hard not
to think about the trauma, pushing the memory down whenever it surfaced, and he numbed his feelings
so thoroughly that his wife said he seemed present only in body. He also avoided going to or near
the place where the event happened, even though it was close to one of the routes he used for work
and social errands. That part of town had once been part of his sales territory, but now he would
take longer detours, cancel a visit if he had to pass the block, or ask his wife to drive while he
sat rigidly in the passenger seat and stared ahead. He told himself this was common sense, that
staying away kept him safe, yet the strategy came at a cost: it shrank his world and made his days
feel increasingly managed around fear rather than choice. He noticed that the more he arranged his
life to avoid reminders, the more he had to admit how much the assault was running things.

As the months went by, Frank began to look at himself through the assault’s lens. He came to believe
that the person he had become was unrecognizable to him, and that there was something wrong with him
as a person because he had not handled the situation better. He replayed the evening in fragments
and settled on self-blame, telling himself the event happened because of the way he acted, because
he had lingered at the bar, because he had not been more alert, because he had not anticipated the
man’s hostility. Those beliefs did not sit quietly; they made every reminder feel charged with
accusation. When he caught the smell of alcohol from someone walking past him at a shopping center,
or saw a face that remotely resembled the offender’s, he was thrown back into the same uneasy
certainty that he was damaged and at fault. The reminders were no longer just reminders of danger;
they seemed to confirm his view that something in him had been changed in a fundamental way, and
that sense of being flawed made the intrusions feel even harder to dismiss.

At home, Frank’s wife had noticed how little he spoke about what had happened. He did not describe
the assault unless pressed, and even then he would cut himself off, saying he did not want to dwell
on it. He kept his feelings numb because if he let them in, he feared he would be overwhelmed by
shame and anger. Yet the more he tried to suppress the thoughts, the more they returned in quiet
moments—when he was driving past the neighborhood, when a customer mentioned meeting over drinks,
when he stood near the liquor aisle in a grocery store. Each reminder seemed to reinforce the belief
that he was not the man he had been, and that reinforced belief made the reminders feel newly
threatening. He found himself watching for the offender in crowds, scanning faces more carefully
than before, all while telling himself that he ought to be able to shake it off. That internal
argument wore him down, and the distance he kept from places, people, and feelings began to leave
him isolated from both work and home.

What finally brought Frank to seek help was the sense that the circle was tightening rather than
easing. He had tried to carry on, but he was increasingly avoiding even ordinary routes connected to
his sales work, and his wife had started to worry about how little of himself he seemed willing to
show. Frank was troubled that a smell, a face, or the thought of that block could still seize him so
completely, and he was most afraid that the assault had permanently altered who he was. At the same
time, he still hoped that if he could understand why these reminders hit so hard, he might stop
living as if danger were waiting around every corner and recover some version of himself that felt
recognizable again.
