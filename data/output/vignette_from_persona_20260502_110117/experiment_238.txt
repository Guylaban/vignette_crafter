Daniel Mercer, a 49-year-old European American city council member, first noticed that the war had
not ended for him in the years after his deployment when ordinary life began to feel arranged around
danger. During a convoy mission in Afghanistan, an explosion tore through the vehicle ahead of his,
leaving him pinned under shouting, dust, and the metallic smell of blood while he tried to make
sense of broken radio calls and flashes of bodies moving in and out of view. What stayed with Daniel
was not a clean story but fragments: the blast itself, a gloved hand, someone screaming his name,
and then gaps he could not fill. Even now, when he tries to explain what happened, he loses the
thread halfway through and is left embarrassed and angry with himself. Remembering it brings back
the same terror he felt in the moment, and the missing pieces make him feel as if the danger is
still unfinished, still waiting to be identified.

In the years that followed, Daniel came to believe that his own body had betrayed him. He described
it as reacting before he could think, jerking hard when a spouse brushed past him unexpectedly in
the kitchen or when staff in the council office came up behind his chair. He had become convinced
that the world was a dangerous place and that he had to be on guard all the time, because even when
others assured him he was safe he could not believe it. That conviction sharpened every jolt of
alarm: a sudden touch, a slammed door in City Hall, footsteps in a hallway after a late meeting.
Those moments sent him back to the same half-remembered terror, and the alarm made the gaps in his
memory feel more ominous, as though the missing details must contain something he had failed to
notice.

To keep himself from being swallowed by that uncertainty, Daniel developed a set of habits that
shaped his days. He put on loud music or left the television on for hours, filling the house with
noise so there was less room to notice the images that came up when things were quiet. He monitored
his thoughts constantly for signs that he was “going crazy,” checking himself for drift, for images,
for any hint that he might be losing control. He also avoided being touched in almost any form,
pulling away from his wife when she reached for his arm and stiffening when constituents tried to
shake his hand at public events. At first these strategies gave him a brief sense of control, but
they also made the memory more slippery. When he drowned out silence and kept scanning his mind, the
fragments of the blast never settled into a coherent account, and the story remained broken in the
same places. The less he could tell clearly, the more certain he became that something was badly
wrong with him, that his body’s reactions and his fragmented recall meant he was still unsafe, still
damaged.

That conviction fed the vigilance. Daniel started watching doorways, parking lots, and the
expressions of people around him with the seriousness of someone on patrol, and he slept badly,
lying awake after council meetings or waking at 3 a.m. with his heart racing and the television
still murmuring in the next room. The poor sleep left him more on edge the next day, and the added
strain made every unexpected touch or sudden noise feel sharper. In turn, those alarms brought the
battlefield back into his mind in flashes, and with each return he felt again that same hot surge of
fear, followed by the thought that his body was still stuck in the war. He found himself retreating
further into noise, into thought-checking, into distance from others, and each retreat made it
harder for him to gather the memory into words or to trust that the present day was different from
the one he survived overseas.

By the time he sought help, the cost had become hard to ignore. At home, his wife had stopped trying
to comfort him by touch because he flinched so often; at work, he had begun avoiding crowded
receptions and staying close to exits at public meetings, which colleagues noticed but did not
understand. Daniel was not asking for reassurance so much as for relief from the exhausting
certainty that he was permanently on guard. What frightened him most was the fear that this pattern
was becoming his new normal, and what he hoped for, though he struggled to say it plainly, was to
sleep without scanning, to remember the event without being pulled back into its terror, and to
believe that safety could feel real in his body again.
