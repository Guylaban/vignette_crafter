which made what happened to him on a winter night still feel, in his telling, like a violation not
only of his body but of everything he believed the job stood for. He was working as a parole officer
in a cramped county office on the edge of town when he was taken aside during a routine transport by
officers he knew from years of coordination with local law enforcement. A disagreement about a
client’s behavior escalated in front of him, and he watched police use force that, in his view, went
far beyond any need for control. He remembered the flash of a hallway light, the echo of raised
voices, then the hard impact of being shoved and pinned while someone he had trusted as a
professional ally acted with a cruelty that left him stunned. From that night on, Daniel came to
believe he had been betrayed by someone he trusted, that what happened to him was deeply unfair and
nothing had been done about it, and that the very people and institutions meant to protect him had
let him down. Those beliefs settled quickly into his account of the event; he could not think about
it without moving back and forth between anger and a kind of stunned disbelief, as though the
details themselves were intolerable and proof of a larger injustice.

In the weeks that followed, ordinary moments at work began to carry a charge they had not had
before. If a supervisee arrived late, spoke back, or acted disrespectfully, Daniel felt a flash of
heat and a tightening in his chest, as if misbehavior itself announced that something dangerous was
underway. He had come to read stress in the office as a warning sign, and when his own heart started
racing during a tense meeting or after a difficult phone call, it confirmed for him that he was not
simply uneasy but that danger might be near. That bodily alarm made him think again of betrayal and
unfairness, and it sharpened his sense that he had been left unprotected; at the same time, it made
any unruly behavior feel bigger, less containable, and more personally threatening than it might
have once seemed. He began to scan his surroundings constantly for signs of danger, watching
doorways, hands, tone of voice, and the mood in a room before he could fully register what was
happening. Unusual silence was especially hard for him. If a hallway at the office went quiet or a
room fell still, he felt something dangerous was about to happen, and that silence could pull him
back toward the memory he tried not to fully recall. He found himself pushing the memory away before
he could complete it, because once it came close, it felt so extreme that it was hard to believe it
had actually happened to him; the event existed in his mind as an incomplete picture, with certain
fragments vivid and the rest missing, as though the mind had broken away from it to protect itself.

sense of humiliation—were enough to keep the injustice alive, and the more he tried to suppress the
memory, the more his body and thoughts seemed to circle back to the same conclusion that he had been
wronged and abandoned.

anything that made him feel trapped; at work he kept conversations brief, stood near exits, and
preferred to review files alone rather than stay in rooms where silence might suddenly fall. His
wife noticed that he became jumpy at home, too, startling when a refrigerator door slammed or when
the house went quiet at night. Even when he tried to rest, his attention drifted outward, scanning,
waiting, and the work of staying alert made him feel as if the danger had never really ended. What
troubled him most was that the same bodily surge, the same racing heart, could send him back into
the story of being betrayed, unfairly treated, and failed by the institutions around him. At those
moments, people misbehaving at work or on the street no longer looked merely rude or difficult; they
looked like evidence that the world remained hostile and that he could still be caught off guard.
The memory, always partial and half-repulsed, fed the sense that something had been covered over or
left unresolved, and that unresolved quality fed his outrage and his watchfulness in turn. He became
more certain that he could not trust silence, authority, or his own body when it warned him.

By the time he finally sought help, Daniel was no longer able to dismiss the pattern as stress from
a demanding job. He came in after a week in which a young supervisee’s defiance at the office set
off his heart racing, made him scan the room for danger, and left him preoccupied with the old sense
of being betrayed all day. At home he had begun to dread quiet evenings, because the silence in the
house seemed to press the memory toward him again, fragments and all, and he no longer wanted his

constantly on guard. What he feared most was that the same unfairness and abandonment would keep
repeating itself—that he would stay trapped between the half-remembered event and the certainty
that, once again, no one would protect him.
