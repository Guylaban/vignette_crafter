Rafael Mendez, a 58-year-old forensic analyst, had spent most of his adult life believing that
careful observation and procedure could make sense of chaos. That assumption was shattered on a
weekday afternoon when a man-made disaster struck the municipal records building where he was
working. Rafael had been in a lower-floor evidence room when the blast hit, hard enough to send a
wave of heat and debris through the corridor and buckle the lights overhead. In the immediate
confusion he heard alarms, cracking concrete, and people shouting names from the stairwell while
smoke began seeping under the door. He escaped with others through a side exit, but the image that
stayed with him was of a workplace he knew intimately turned into a place he could not read or
control in a matter of seconds. In the months that followed, what unsettled him most was not only
the memory of the explosion itself but the way ordinary spaces suddenly felt unknowable. He began
avoiding unfamiliar places whenever he could, telling his wife that if he did not already know the
exits, the crowds, and the layout, he could not assess the danger. A new restaurant, a different
route to the grocery store, even an unfamiliar parking garage could make him tense up before he
stepped inside, as though his body were bracing for another sudden rupture.

At work, the consequences of that changed way of moving through the world became harder to hide.
Rafael still came in, but he took the same route, parked in the same spot, and resisted assignments
that required him to visit new sites unless there was no one else available. The municipal building
had once been a place where his expertise felt reliable; now, when he walked into an unfamiliar
evidence storage facility or a temporary office set up after a fire inspection, he felt his
attention narrow immediately to the exits, the windows, the noise in the hall. He described being
jumpy, easily startled by a printer slamming shut or a coworker appearing at his elbow. His body, he
said, never fully settled anymore. Even at home in the quiet of the evening, he noticed a constant
tension through his shoulders and jaw, as if some part of him remained on duty for danger that might
arrive without warning. This left him exhausted and irritated, and it fed his growing conviction
that his reactions since the disaster showed he was a lousy coper. He had built an identity around
being the man who stayed calm when others panicked, the one who could sort evidence, follow the
facts, and remain steady under pressure. Now he felt that those traits belonged to someone he used
to be. He was no longer sure he could do the things that had defined him for decades, and that loss
struck him as humiliating as well as frightening.

Rafael’s view of the world had become darker in the same period. He had come to believe that the
world is a dangerous place, and he found himself thinking that danger could appear anywhere people
gathered, especially in unfamiliar settings where he could not quickly judge what might go wrong.
That belief made him even more determined to keep to predictable routines, which narrowed his life
in ways his wife noticed before he admitted them himself. He stopped joining her when she wanted to
try new neighborhood places and declined an invitation from relatives to meet at a restaurant across
town. The avoidance brought short-term relief, but it also left him feeling increasingly cut off
from ordinary life and from the part of himself that used to move through the city with confidence.
When he had to cross an unfamiliar lobby or enter a new office building for work, he found himself
scanning every corner, as if vigilance alone might prevent another disaster. Over time, the strain
showed in his posture and in the way he hesitated before agreeing to simple changes in routine.

What finally brought Rafael to seek help was the recognition that the narrowing of his life was no
longer something he could explain away as caution. He had been awakened the previous week by a
sudden clang from a neighbor’s remodeling work and found himself sitting upright, heart racing,
unable to settle for nearly an hour. His wife told him he was becoming unreachable, always on edge,
always checking, always saying no. Rafael came to treatment not because he believed the disaster was
over, but because he feared he was being transformed by it into someone permanently guarded and
diminished. More than anything, he wanted to recover a sense that he could move through the world
without feeling constantly threatened, and he feared that if nothing changed, the man he once was
would remain out of reach.
