Mereana had been 46 when her life split apart in a way that still felt impossible to hold in one
piece. She was a forensic analyst in a busy county laboratory, the kind of job that required steady
hands, concentration, and a capacity to sit with disturbing evidence without flinching. It had
happened on an ordinary evening after work, when her husband rang her from home and, in a few
seconds that seemed to stretch and fracture, she heard the sound of him choking, gasping, and then
going suddenly quiet. By the time she reached him, he was gone. In the months that followed, Mereana
came to experience that death not as a finished event but as a sequence of frozen images: the phone
in her hand, the sound in the receiver, his body on the floor. She described the memory as only a
few moments, each one like a picture that appeared too sharply, as if lit from inside. At the worst
times, when the memory forced its way back, she would also hear the sounds she had heard that night
in her head, especially the choking and the abrupt silence, and the flood of emotion would become so
intense that she had to stop whatever she was doing.

From the beginning, Mereana’s understanding of what had happened was shaped by devastation and
betrayal. She had lost faith in people, in life, and in any higher power she once assumed would make
suffering make sense. She spoke with flat certainty that her life had been destroyed by the trauma,
and that nothing in it felt meaningful anymore. Those beliefs were not abstract to her; they shaped
her days. Work, which had once given her structure and a sense of competence, now felt like an arena
she had to force herself into. On the mornings when she was supposed to go in, she would sit in the
dark kitchen and stare at her uniform, telling herself that she could not face other people,
ordinary conversation, or the sight of her own reflection in the staff bathroom. She began avoiding
work whenever she could, taking sick leave, arriving late, and making excuses that sounded plausible
to colleagues. At home, she stayed up until very late because the idea of sleep felt like
surrendering to the hours when the images came most easily. She ate little, sometimes restricting
food entirely, because hunger seemed more manageable than the sick heaviness in her stomach and the
sense that she did not deserve comfort. Over time, these habits became part of the way she tried to
survive what she believed life had become.

Yet the very strategies that gave her temporary relief deepened the meanings she attached to the
loss. Each morning she managed not to go to work, she felt more certain that nothing meaningful
remained for her and that the life she had known really was over. The empty hours at night and the
days when she barely ate left her more drained and more alone, and that exhaustion made the world
feel even less trustworthy and less alive. At work, when she did manage to appear, the lab itself
became a place where memory could ambush her. A phone ringing sharply in another office, a
colleague’s cough cut off mid-breath, or the sudden hush after a door closed could bring back the
sounds from that night with force. When that happened, the memories came not as a story but as
fragments: a picture of the phone, a picture of the hallway, a picture of her husband’s body,
accompanied by the internal replay of those frightening sounds. Because she was already so depleted
from staying up late, avoiding food, and dragging herself through work when she did go, she had less
capacity to steady herself; the memory would overwhelm her faster, and she would have to stop, sit
down, or leave the room. The more often that happened, the more convinced she became that her life
had been permanently damaged and that she could no longer trust herself to function.

By the time she sought help, Mereana was living in a narrow and punishing rhythm. She pushed herself
to keep some parts of work going because the lab was also the place where bills were paid and
appearances were maintained, but every shift felt threatened by the possibility of hearing something
that would send her back to that night. She avoided eating through much of the day, stayed awake
until the house was silent, and then lay in bed fearing sleep and the return of the images. What
finally brought her to therapy was the realization that the pattern was tightening rather than
easing: the more she withdrew from work, sleep, and food, the more meaningless and ruined her life
felt, and the more power the memories seemed to have over her. She came because she was frightened
that she was disappearing into the aftermath of his death, and because despite everything she still
hoped there might be some way to live without feeling that the world had ended with him.
