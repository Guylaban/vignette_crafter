world around him felt altered in a way he could not explain away. The collision happened on a winter
evening when a delivery van ran a red light and struck the driver’s side of the car while he was
turning out of a lot near a busy intersection. He remembered the metal lurching, the sound of glass
and horns, and then a strange discontinuity in the rest of it, as if the minutes after impact had
been broken into fragments he could not put back together. Since then, when he tries to tell the
story, Jamal can only offer pieces: the steering wheel twisted against him, someone speaking through
a window, the smell of coolant and smoke, his own hand reaching for a seatbelt that seemed suddenly
impossible to trust. The rest feels missing, and when he deliberately tries to summon the full
sequence, it slips away from him. That incomplete memory has left him with no clear account even in
his own mind, and he has come to doubt himself in a deeper way, wondering whether he will ever do
the right thing if something dangerous happens again. He is also haunted by the sense that the
people and institutions that should have protected him failed him, because traffic lights, other
drivers, and the systems meant to keep the road safe had not stopped the van from hitting him. What
frightens him most is how unreliable his own body now feels to him; since the injury, he has been
certain that it might betray him again without warning, and that belief has made him watchful about
every small change in how he moves or breathes.

In the weeks after the collision, those convictions narrowed his life. Jamal began avoiding going
out whenever he could, arranging groceries and errands around the quietest times or asking others to
do them, because leaving the apartment felt like stepping back into a place where another sudden
impact could happen. He also pulled away from people he had known before the event, especially
former students and fellow dancers he used to see regularly, because their questions and their
sympathy made him feel exposed and because he could not bear the possibility that they would notice
how uncertain he now felt. In therapy he found himself doing something similar, steering away from
discussion of the crash whenever the conversation came close to the parts he could not piece
together, almost as if not looking too directly might spare him from the shame of not being able to
explain it. These habits were not random to him; they made sense because he no longer trusted his
judgment or his body, and because he feared that if he let himself dwell on the event he would be
flooded by what he could not control. Yet the more he kept himself from the outside world, the more
the world seemed dangerous. He became tense in public spaces, needing to sit with his back against a
wall so he could see the room, and when something took longer than expected—a bus that did not
arrive, a delayed return call from the mechanic, a pause in the street outside his building—he
automatically assumed something bad was happening. Unexpected or loud sounds, especially a sharp
brake squeal or a slammed door, left him instantly on high alert. Those moments then pushed him back
into his avoidance, making him less willing to go out again and more determined to keep contact with
people to a minimum.

Jamal also noticed that the gaps in his memory fed the same spiral. Because the crash lives in
fragments, he keeps scanning for signs that he is missing something important, and that uncertainty
intensifies his sense that danger could be present before he can name it. The broken recall also
deepens his self-doubt: if he cannot even tell a clear story of what happened, he reasons, how can
he trust his own understanding now? That uncertainty spills into everyday situations, where he
hesitates before decisions that once would have felt automatic, and then avoids the situation
altogether because he is afraid of choosing badly. His mistrust of his body works in the same way. A
small ache, a sudden stiffness, or a momentary shortness of breath can make him feel that something
is wrong, and once that alarm is raised he monitors everything around him more closely, listening
for noises and positioning himself for escape. In that way his effort to stay safe has begun to keep
his threat system permanently switched on. Months after the crash, he has found himself teaching
less at the studio, refusing invitations from old friends, and spending much of the day inside his
apartment where he can control the furniture and the angle of his chair. He has come for help now
because the life he built around movement and connection has become smaller and smaller, and because
he is tired of living as though every delay, every sound, and every uncertain sensation means the
danger is back. He is most afraid that he will remain trapped in this watchfulness forever, but he
still hopes that if he can make sense of what happened, he may learn how to trust himself enough to
move through the world again.
