mind, a series of frightening pictures: a child’s shoe in the mud, a wall falling in sections, a

happened years ago; for Minh it arrives all at once, vividly and bodily, with the same smell, the
same terror, and the same sense that he is again standing in that broken street.

In the months afterward, certain moments repeatedly pulled him back toward that day. The wail of an
ambulance near the courthouse made his stomach drop, and hospital corridors with their antiseptic
odor carried him straight into the aftermath of the quake, as if he could already hear the collapse
and the cries. He noticed that similar weather was especially hard: when dark clouds gathered and
the rain came in sheets, he became tense before he could think, watching the sky with the feeling
that something terrible was about to happen again. Those cues did not merely unsettle him; they

onto legal briefs, meetings, or television, and when the images broke through anyway he tried to

his senses and feared he would be overwhelmed by it.

restorative. He stopped allowing himself to unwind, staying busy late into the evening and filling
every lull with work or practical tasks. The more the memory arrived in those complete sensory
flashes, the more determined he became to keep it at bay, and the harder he pushed it down, the more
forcefully it seemed to surface when an ambulance passed, when he walked by a hospital entrance, or

not to leave home rather than risk encountering the sights and sounds that he knew could set the
whole sequence off.

the courthouse he remained composed, but he was increasingly guarded about anything that might make
him pause, listen, or remember. In private, he was exhausted by the effort of keeping his thoughts

sensory memory, and what he most hoped for was to be able to walk outside, hear a siren, or sit
