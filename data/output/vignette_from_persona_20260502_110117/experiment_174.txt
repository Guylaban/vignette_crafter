Mereana Tane had worked for most of her adult life as a network engineer, a role she still held
part-time from a home office in Brisbane after her husband’s death. Long before she sought help, she
had lived with the aftermath of an assault that occurred late one evening when she was coming back
to her car after a work dinner. A man she did not know came up too close, reeking of alcohol, and
when she turned away from him he grabbed her before she could reach for her keys. She remembered the
shock of being held, the helplessness of not being able to stop what happened, and the way her body
felt exposed and overpowered in a way that left her ashamed for months afterward. What troubled her
most was not only the violence itself but the sense that, in that moment, she had acted in ways that
went against who she believed herself to be; she had frozen, then complied with demands she still
found humiliating to recall. Since then, Mereana had come to believe that bad things could happen to
her no matter how carefully she managed herself, and that belief had become part of how she moved
through the world.

At first she tried to put the event behind her, but the body-based reminders kept forcing their way
in. The smell of alcohol in a lift, a colleague leaning in for a hug at a farewell, or someone
speaking to her with an easy emotional closeness she had not invited could hit her with such force
that she would pull back sharply, stiffen, or leave the room. She found herself avoiding intimacy in
any form, not only romantic but also the warmth of friends and relatives, because affection had
become tied in her mind to danger and to the shame of being caught off guard. When those reminders
appeared, she responded by tightening her life around control: she restricted her eating with a
severity that felt almost disciplined to her, and she gave up pleasant activities she used to enjoy,
including Sunday gatherings and swimming with a neighbour, because pleasure felt indulgent and
unsafe. In her mind, these habits gave her something she could govern when so much else had once
been taken from her. Yet the more she withdrew, the more alone and strained she felt, and that
isolation fed the same beliefs that had been forming since the assault: that she was damaged, that
her body bore the mark of what happened, and that she had somehow failed herself. Those thoughts
made her even more likely to pull away from affection and closeness the next time they were offered,
and to retreat further into strict routines that she experienced as the only reliable protection.

Her sense of threat had become constant and practical. Everywhere she went, Mereana scanned for
potential weapons or dangerous objects, whether she was in the kitchen, at the train station, or
walking through the open-plan office where she occasionally supervised junior staff. She noticed
knives, bottle openers, screwdrivers, even a heavy keyring in a stranger’s hand, and could not
easily dismiss them. Even when people assured her she was safe, she found it impossible to believe
them; the assurance never touched the part of her that expected danger to arrive quickly and without
warning. She also reacted with alarm when someone touched her unexpectedly, such as a hand on her
shoulder while she was bent over her laptop or a casual brush against her arm in a crowded corridor.
These reactions were not separate from her coping but woven into it. Because she was constantly
watching for danger, she became more determined to keep control wherever she could, and the strict
eating, the refusal of enjoyment, and the avoidance of closeness all deepened. At the same time,
those strategies kept her life narrow and deprived, leaving more space for fear and for the belief
that her world was permanently unsafe.

Mereana eventually sought help after her daughter told her she had become impossible to reach: she
no longer came to family dinners, declined hugs from the grandchildren, and grew tense at the
slightest unexpected touch. The final push came after a work meeting in which a new contractor,
laughing too loudly, carried the smell of alcohol from a nearby pub into the conference room;
Mereana had frozen, then spent the rest of the day checking exits and locking herself into a rigid
meal plan that night. She presented now hoping for some way to feel safe without having to live as
though every kindness concealed a threat, though what frightened her most was that she might never
fully trust another person’s reassurance, or her own body, enough to let herself be close again.
