Miriam, a 48-year-old African American rabbi, came to treatment after more than a year of living in
the shadow of a bombing that tore through the community center attached to her synagogue during a
crowded weekday event. She had been in a side hallway when the explosion hit, and what stayed with
her was not only the blast itself but the split-second confusion afterward: the ringing silence,
people shouting for others to get down, smoke drifting under the doors, and the sick certainty that
she should have done more. In the months that followed, she found that any explosion or bang sound
could pull her back into that hallway with startling force. A car backfiring near the church where
she led a memorial service, a dropped tray in the synagogue kitchen, even a sharp sound from a movie
trailer could make her freeze and look around as if the next impact were already on its way. She
also became acutely uneasy if she felt watched or observed, as though other people’s eyes carried
the same danger as the moment before the blast, and she began avoiding news stories about attacks
and disasters because even a brief headline could leave her shaken for hours.

Miriam’s sense of danger had become constant, and it shaped the meanings she gave to everything
else. She was certain that she could not trust safety, no matter how often her husband or
congregants reassured her that the building had been repaired and security improved. Even when
others told her she was safe, she found it impossible to believe them. Nights were hardest; once the
day quieted, her mind seemed to go back to the bombing, and she lay awake listening for sounds in
the house, feeling that darkness made danger easier to hide. She described herself as constantly
scanning her surroundings for signs of threat, checking doorways, exits, and the faces of strangers
when she walked into meetings or services. That vigilance fed the way she understood herself. She
had come to believe that she must have failed in a decisive way, that she would never forgive
herself for not doing more to stop what happened, and that because such violence could erupt without
warning, nothing good could really happen to her anymore. Those thoughts did not drift through her
mind in isolation; they hardened every time she noticed herself scanning a room or tensing at a
sound, as if her own fear proved that the world remained unsafe and that her regret was justified.

Over time, the efforts Miriam made to keep herself from being overwhelmed began narrowing her life
and, in turn, kept the event close. She asked colleagues and family not to mention the bombing
unless absolutely necessary, because being reminded of it by others could set off hours of
agitation. She arranged her days around avoiding reminders, but the effort also meant that the
traumatic event remained unfinished in her mind, ready to return whenever someone referenced the
community center or asked about the anniversary. She also stopped initiating sex with her husband
and often pulled away when he reached for her, not because she no longer loved him but because
closeness in the dark left her feeling exposed and unable to relax. That same urge to protect
herself spilled into her work: at the synagogue, she lingered near exits, watched the parking lot
before and after services, and mentally mapped every room before greeting congregants. Her constant
scanning sometimes made her even more alert to possible danger, so that a loud laugh, a slammed
door, or an unfamiliar person standing too still could set her on edge. The more she monitored for
threat, the more she noticed signs that seemed to confirm it, and the more she avoided situations
that might remind her of the bombing, the more vivid and immediate it remained in her mind. News
coverage about attacks could still undo her for the rest of the day, and a single bang could revive
the feeling that disaster was already entering the building again.

By the time she sought help, Miriam was exhausted by the effort of staying braced for impact. She
said she was no longer frightened only by the original bombing, but by the way her own life had
become organized around it: the nights she dreaded, the intimacy she avoided, the constant watching,
and the sense that she had failed in a way she could never repair. She came because she was tired of
living as if the next terrible thing were always about to happen, and because she feared that if she
did not get help, she would keep withdrawing from her husband, her congregation, and the parts of
life she still hoped might somehow be good again.
