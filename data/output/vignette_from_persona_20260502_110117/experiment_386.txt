David, a 62-year-old African American stock broker who had been divorced for years, came to
treatment after months of carrying a grief that never seemed to settle. The loss that organized
everything in his mind was sudden and violent in its finality: one winter evening his younger
brother, the person he called every day after market close, died unexpectedly after collapsing at a
family gathering. David had been in the room, had watched relatives rush around him, and then had
watched the body afterward with a kind of stunned disbelief he still could not fully put into words.
What stayed with him was not a continuous story but broken pieces that arrived without warning — the
sound of a chair scraping back, a hand reaching across a table, his brother’s face shifting in a way
he could not hold onto. When he tried to deliberately remember the night, he found that the harder
he reached, the more the memory slipped away, leaving an incomplete picture and a sense that the
rest was missing.

In the weeks that followed, those fragments became the starting point for hours of rumination. David
would sit alone in his apartment after work and go over and over why it had happened to him, turning
the same questions over with the precision he once brought to trades and portfolios. He kept trying
to make the memory whole, to reconstruct the exact order of events and locate the moment he should
have noticed something, but the fragments never fit cleanly. That struggle seemed to intensify his
distress: the more he tried to force the memory into place, the more he found himself with only
sudden pieces and a blank stretch around them, and the more he was left with the feeling that he had
somehow missed the truth of what happened. He began to avoid anything that reminded him of a loved
one he had lost — calls from relatives, old photos, the church basement where the gathering had been
held — because each reminder seemed to pull him back toward the same unsatisfying search.

At the same time, the memory did not stay politely in the past. Certain moments in his day could
snap it open: the ring of a hospital phone, the sight of someone folding into a chair at work, even
the quiet after the closing bell at the brokerage. In those moments David could suddenly feel as if
he were back in that room, with a rush of horror and anger so strong that he would tense, swallow
hard, and stare ahead until it passed. He described these episodes as brief but terrifying, as if he
were acting for a few seconds as though the stressful experience were happening again. The power of
those surges made him even more determined to avoid grief reminders and to suppress thoughts of the
trauma altogether, because he was certain that once he let himself start thinking about his brother,
the flood of feeling would come back with it. Yet the effort to push it away never lasted. The more
he strained not to think about the death, the more the fragments surfaced anyway, and each return of
the memory brought the same sharp wave of fear, guilt, and shame.

David gradually came to organize his days around not being caught by those moments. He stopped
visiting his sister’s house, changed the route he took past the family church, and refused to open
the box of his brother’s belongings that had been set aside for him. He noticed that if he kept busy
at the brokerage, if he stayed late and buried himself in numbers, the thoughts were quieter for a
while. But the calm never lasted long, because the memory would intrude in pieces, and once it did,
his body would go on alert. He became more watchful for signs of being overwhelmed, and the fear of
another flashback made him feel brittle and on edge. In that state he sometimes took too many risks,
driving too fast after work or making impulsive decisions on the trading floor, almost as if acting
boldly could outrun the helplessness he felt. He knew the behavior could put him in harm’s way, but
in the moment it also gave him a sense of being less trapped.

Over months, this pattern narrowed his life. He and his adult children noticed that he was less
available, more irritable, and harder to reach when family conversations turned toward loss. At
work, colleagues saw the change in the once-steady man who now occasionally stared past his screen
as if something had pulled him away. David finally sought help after a particularly vivid episode in
the brokerage office, when the phone rang and he was flooded with the feeling that he was back in
that room again. He is afraid that if he keeps trying to manage this alone, he will remain stuck
between chasing the missing pieces and forcing them out of mind, while the fear and the risky
moments keep building. What he wants most is to remember his brother without being ambushed by
fragments and to stop living as though the loss is still about to happen.
