Layla, a 46-year-old single Middle Eastern woman working as a pharmacist, came for help after more
than a year of feeling as if her world had narrowed around one terrifying event. Her son had been
rushed to hospital with a sudden, life-threatening illness, and Layla still remembered the
fluorescent corridor, the squeal of the ambulance doors, the smell of antiseptic, and the way his
small body looked against the sheets during repeated examinations. In the weeks after he survived,
she found herself unable to shake the sense that she had failed him somehow. She had come to believe
she was a weak person, that there was something wrong with her as a person, and that nothing good
could happen to her anymore. Those beliefs shaped the way she lived: she tried hard not to think
about what had happened, pushed away any thoughts that might take her back to that night, and began
steering clear of hospitals, ambulances, and physical examinations whenever she could.

At first Layla told herself she was simply trying to cope, but the more she dwelt on why the event
had happened to her family, the more convincing her own explanations became. If something so
terrible had struck her child, she reasoned, it must say something about her, about how fragile or
inadequate she was. The more she searched for meaning, the more she landed on the same painful
conclusions, and those conclusions made it even harder to step near anything connected with the
illness. Even now, as a pharmacist, she avoids the part of her work that brings her close to medical
transport and emergency supplies when she can, and she dislikes going with relatives to appointments
where examination rooms, stretchers, or ambulance sirens might be present. Her avoidance has reduced
the moments that trigger her, but it has also kept the event vivid and unresolved in her mind; every
effort to shut it out seems to leave it more charged, more capable of taking over her thoughts.

Layla’s sense of threat also began to spread beyond the original hospital scene. She said she is
unable to relax even when she knows she is in a safe place, and she lives on edge in a way that
feels unfamiliar to her family. Ordinary sounds—a cart rolling in the pharmacy, a phone buzzing
sharply, a door slammed in her apartment building—make her jump in a way that embarrasses her. She
sleeps poorly, falling asleep only to wake again, alert for the next disturbance. Because she is
always braced for something terrible, she has grown more convinced that she is weak, and that a
person who cannot relax or sleep must be fundamentally damaged. In turn, those judgments make her
withdraw further from reminders of the illness and from any situation that might expose her to the
same helpless feeling, as though staying away can prove she is not as fragile as she fears.

Over months, the pattern tightened. The more Layla avoided medical cues and tried not to think about
the event, the more distant and abnormal she felt from other people, especially from colleagues who
seemed able to speak calmly about sickness, injections, or emergencies. Each time she noticed her
heart racing at the sound of a siren outside the pharmacy, she took it as evidence that there was
something wrong with her as a person, and that belief deepened her conviction that nothing good
could happen to her anymore. That hopelessness made her even more vigilant and jumpy, always
scanning for cues that her son’s illness might return or that another family catastrophe might be
waiting around the corner. The more she monitored for danger, the more impossible it became to
relax, and the more she relied on avoidance to get through the day.

Recently, the strain reached a point where Layla could no longer ignore it. A nearby ambulance
passed the pharmacy one afternoon, and the sound left her shaky for the rest of the shift; that
night she lay awake replaying the hospital corridor and the image of her son being wheeled away. She
finally sought help because she is frightened that the life she rebuilt around avoiding reminders is
becoming smaller and smaller, and because she is tired of feeling weak, defective, and permanently
marked by what happened. More than anything, Layla hopes she can stop living as though another
catastrophe is always about to begin, and she fears that without help she will remain trapped in the
same alarm and self-blame that followed her child’s illness.
