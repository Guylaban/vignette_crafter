sudden death of his younger sister, Elena, in a highway crash on the outskirts of Chicago. He had
been the one to answer the call from the hospital, to speak to the police, and to identify her
belongings after she was pronounced dead. What stayed with him was not a coherent story but jagged
fragments: the fluorescent light in the viewing room, the buckle of her handbag, the exact red knit
scarf she had been wearing that morning, and then stretches of blankness where he could not place
what had been said or done. When he tried to remember, he would be hit by a wave of guilt so intense
that he had to stop; he was certain he should have insisted she leave later, should have called her
back, should have somehow prevented the trip. Those memories were not stable recollections but
broken flashes that came with a crushing emotional force, and the force itself made him dread any
quiet moment in which his mind might wander back to her.

In the weeks that followed, Luca noticed that the memory itself made him feel physically sick and
flooded with fear, horror, and shame, and the reaction spilled into his days at work. At his studio,
where he supervised fittings, fabric selections, and late-season deadlines, he began keeping himself
occupied from morning until night, taking on extra design tasks, answering emails immediately, and
lingering over small adjustments long after they were necessary. He made no plans beyond the day in
front of him. He had once been meticulous about travel, collections, and even dinners with his
husband, but now he avoided any future plans because he could not bear the thought of committing to
a life that might be interrupted again. When the memory of Elena returned, especially in the quiet
of his office after the staff left, it overwhelmed him so completely that he would shut his laptop,
leave the room, or force himself into another task. The more he tried not to think about the
accident, the more he found himself organising his hours around not being alone with his thoughts.

At first he believed constant busyness would keep him safe, yet the same effort to suppress thoughts
of Elena began to make the memories feel more insistent. When he sat through a fitting and caught a
glimpse of a scarf thrown over a chair, or saw the glare of headlights on wet pavement while walking
to his car after work, his body reacted before he could reason with it: his heart raced, his chest
tightened, and a wave of heat and panic left him shaky and alert. That physical jolt confirmed his
fear that something inside him had been damaged by what happened, and with every reminder he became
more convinced he could not afford to let his guard down. The stronger the fear and guilt became,
the harder he worked to keep his mind occupied, and the more he pushed away any future-oriented
conversation with his husband about vacations, renovations, or even dinner reservations. Luca had
come to believe that imagining the future invited loss.

The memory did not only intrude more often; it also changed shape under the strain. Because he kept
trying to shove it aside, the pieces that remained were the most brutal ones, while other parts
stayed blank, which left him unable to build a full account of that night or to reassure himself
that he understood it correctly. He would remember Elena’s scarf, the voicemail he never deleted,
and the look on the police officer’s face, but not the sequence between them, and that partial
clarity only deepened his guilt. He was left with isolated images and an emotional certainty that he
had failed her, and from there he would swing back into fear and shame so strong that he could not
sleep or sit still with his own thoughts. The more overwhelmed he felt by the fragments, the more
determined he became to avoid anything that might invite them back, and the more he filled his days
with work, errands, and mindless scrolling late into the night.
