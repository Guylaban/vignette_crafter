Malia, a 46-year-old veterinarian originally from Oceania, had built her life around competence and
calm before she ever arrived in the United States. During a deployment to a civilian area caught in
war, she had been driving a vehicle marked for medical supply runs when a sudden blast shattered the
road ahead. In the chaos that followed, people shouted, metal screamed, and someone grabbed her
shoulder from behind to pull her down. What stayed with her was not a single clear sequence but a
broken collection of fragments: the flash, dust in her mouth, the feeling of being hauled sideways,
a glimpse of a child’s shoe in the road. When she tries to remember it now, the memory comes back
like a film that restarts without warning, and with it comes a wave of shame that she cannot explain
away. She has come to believe that what happened to her was deeply unfair and that no one has ever
really answered for it, and she says that remembering makes her feel as if she should have prevented
it somehow, even though she knows that is not logical. The more the scene returns to her, the more
certain she becomes that she cannot rely on herself, because her mind seems unable to hold the event
together in a way that would let her feel in control.

In the years since, ordinary moments have become charged with the same sense of danger. In her work
as a veterinarian, she stiffens when a technician touches her without warning at the exam table,
even when it is only a hand on her arm to pass a chart. Driving with other people is difficult
enough that she usually insists on taking the wheel herself; sitting as a passenger leaves her
watching mirrors, shoulders tight, convinced that if she is not in control she will miss the moment
something goes wrong. Loud noises, especially sudden bangs in the clinic or a kennel door slamming,
make her jolt as if the road blast were happening again. Each of these moments seems to confirm what
she has already decided: that bad things can arrive without notice, and that she cannot stop them
from happening to her. She has come to see the war as proof of a deeper injustice, something deeply
unfair that was imposed on her and left unresolved, and the memory of it keeps feeding that
conviction rather than settling it.

That same conviction shapes how the memory behaves in her mind. When the clinic is busy and a dog
barks sharply from a treatment room, the sound can pull up another piece of the blast, and the scene
starts replaying in her head in the same disjointed, relentless way. When she remembers the event,
she does not get a clean account; instead, the fragments arrive with the same shame, the same sense
that she failed somehow, which strengthens her belief that she cannot trust herself to anticipate or
manage danger. Those thoughts make her even more alert for signs that something is about to go
wrong. She watches hands, doorways, tires on wet pavement, and the faces of strangers with a
vigilance that never quite turns off. At home, even when her husband tries to reassure her, she
cannot relax in the safe place she has built with him. At night she lies awake because she feels she
must stay alert, as if sleep would be an invitation for disaster.

Over months, that watchfulness has narrowed her life. She avoids being touched unprepared and has
arranged her work so colleagues call her name before stepping into her space, but even then she
startles easily. She has reduced the driving she does with others, turning down social rides and
family outings because the passenger seat makes her feel trapped and vulnerable. Her husband has
noticed how often she seems somewhere else, scanning a room instead of settling into it. The more
she scans for danger and braces for the next shock, the more readily ordinary cues connect back to
the war memory, and the more those reminders renew her certainty that she is at the mercy of events
she cannot control. That certainty, in turn, makes every loud sound, every unexpected touch, every
ride in someone else’s car feel like evidence that the past can return at any moment.

By the time she sought help, Malia described feeling worn down by the constant effort of staying on
guard. Her sleep had become light and fragmented, and she was exhausted from never fully letting
herself rest. What finally brought her in was the fear that this way of living had become permanent,
that she would keep carrying the same shame, the same sense of helplessness, and the same distrust
of herself into every part of her marriage and work. What she most hoped for was not to forget what
happened, but to be able to remember it without feeling as though it were still happening and
without needing to keep her whole life arranged around the next possible blow.
