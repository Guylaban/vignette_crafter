Ezekiel, a 68-year-old widowed African American policy analyst, came to treatment after months of
feeling as if his body had never quite returned from a medical emergency that nearly killed him.
Before the illness, he had been the kind of man who managed long reports, tracked legislation, and
prided himself on steady judgment. During a winter afternoon at home, alone in the kitchen while
reheating soup, he suddenly felt an intense pressure in his chest and a wave of breathlessness that
forced him to sit down on the floor. He remembers the ambulance ride in fragments: the bright
ceiling lights passing overhead, the smell of antiseptic, the abrupt voices of strangers, and the
terrifying certainty that he might not see his late wife’s photograph on the mantel again. In the
hospital, he was told that his condition was life-threatening, and he underwent treatment that left
him exhausted, disoriented, and frightened by how quickly his own body had turned against him.

In the weeks afterward, Ezekiel found that the memories of that day were not stored as a settled
story. At times he could recall the sequence of events almost mechanically, but it felt completely
numb, as if it had happened to another man; at other times, the scene came back with such force that
it felt as though the monitor alarms were sounding again in the present, not in the past. When that
happened, he had to stop whatever he was doing and sit quietly until the wave passed. The memory was
especially potent when he was at home, in the same kitchen where the episode had begun, or when he
noticed a tightness in his chest after climbing stairs. Those moments pulled him back into the
hospital room so vividly that he could hardly distinguish recollection from immediate danger.

As the physical crisis receded, Ezekiel’s fear did not. He became increasingly convinced that his
body could not be trusted. He would catch himself checking his pulse, his breathing, and the feel of
his chest when he walked from the bedroom to the kitchen, as if he were monitoring a machine that
might fail without warning. He had come to believe that his body kept reacting as though it were
still in danger, even when he was safe, and that belief sharpened his sense of threat. The more he
noticed tension in his shoulders, a racing heart, or a flicker of dizziness, the more certain he
became that another collapse could happen at any moment. Even inside his own home, where he had once
felt most at ease, he felt on guard, as if he needed to stay ready for the next abrupt medical
emergency.

That steady state of alertness made the memory of the illness easier to trigger and harder to shake.
A twinge in his chest while bending to pick up files for work, or a moment of fatigue in the
afternoon, could bring the hospital back with alarming vividness. Then the image would flood him:
the stretcher, the masked staff, the helplessness of not knowing whether he would survive. At those
times he was overwhelmed by emotion and had to stop speaking mid-sentence, sometimes in the middle
of drafting policy notes at his office. His concentration suffered, and colleagues noticed that he
was slower to respond in meetings and less willing to take on tasks that once came easily. The tense
vigilance spread into his workday and into evenings at home, where he would sit stiffly in his
armchair, listening to every sensation in his body.

The same episodes deepened his negative appraisals. Ezekiel told himself that he would never feel
normal emotions again, only this strange flattened fear and numbness, and that his body had become
an enemy that could turn on him without warning. He no longer trusted his own physical reactions; to
him, every palpitating heartbeat seemed like proof that something dangerous was brewing beneath the
surface. Those beliefs fed his watchfulness, and the watchfulness made each bodily sensation feel
more ominous. Over time he began avoiding long walks near his apartment, hesitating before cooking
alone, and staying close to a chair whenever he felt even slightly unsteady. He also withdrew from a
few social dinners, embarrassed by how often he needed to sit down or excuse himself to “check
something,” though he never fully put that fear into words.

By the time he sought help, Ezekiel was exhausted by the constant effort of bracing for the next
sign of danger and by the shame of feeling unlike himself. He said he was most afraid that he would
remain trapped in this state forever, unable to trust his body or feel fully alive again. What
brought him in was the growing cost of living like this in his own home and at his desk, and the
hope that he might learn how to face his body without waiting for it to betray him.
