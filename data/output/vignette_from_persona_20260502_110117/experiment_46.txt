Rangi, a 63-year-old married financial analyst of Oceania background, came to treatment describing
how a single life-threatening accident had reorganized his days around danger. Years ago, on his way
home, he was caught in a violent crash that left him trapped long enough to believe he might die
before help reached him. He remembers the moment with a frightening immediacy: when he tries to tell
it, the scene comes back as if it is happening now, not as something finished. The smell inside the
vehicle, the dark enclosure around him, and the sharp sounds of cars passing or approaching are not
distant reminders to him but forceful keys that unlock the whole event. He can be sitting in his
office reviewing figures, hear a vehicle outside the building, and suddenly be back in that instant,
hearing the same sounds in his head and feeling as though the danger has returned without warning.
The memory does not sit safely in the past for Rangi; it bursts forward with such vividness that it
pulls his attention into the old scene and, in turn, makes ordinary smells or darkness feel charged
with the same threat.

Since the accident, Rangi has come to believe that he has to be on guard all the time because you
never know what can happen next. That conviction shapes how he moves through the world. He scans
exits as soon as he enters any room or building, taking stock of doors, hallways, and where he could
get out quickly if something went wrong. If he thinks about the accident directly, he becomes
convinced he will not be able to handle it, so he pushes the memory away and tries not to dwell on
it. Yet the more he tries to keep it at bay, the more easily fragments of it break through. A dark
conference room, the smell of exhaust near the parking garage, or the sound of cars pulling up
outside his office can bring the full memory back without warning, and once it arrives it feels
immediate and impossible to dismiss. Rangi’s own sense that he must stay constantly careful makes
the memory more available, while the intrusive memory itself seems to confirm that vigilance is
necessary.

What frightened Rangi most was how his body began to react before he had even had time to think. He
would wake during the night to check whether he was safe, sometimes sitting up suddenly with his
heart racing, needing to look around the bedroom before he could settle. In those moments, he felt
certain that if he let his guard down, something terrible could happen again. The same rush of alarm
came when the memory returned during the day: his body tensed, his attention narrowed, and he was
already in a state of danger before his mind had caught up. This bodily alarm made the memory feel
even more present and convincing, as if his reactions proved that the accident was not really over.
Once his body was primed like that, the world itself seemed full of possible danger, and he became
more alert to smells, darkness, and the approach of cars, noticing them almost instantly and bracing
himself before he had a chance to think it through.

Over the months after the accident, Rangi began altering his routines in ways that reached into both
home and work. In the office tower where he works as a financial analyst, he avoids lingering in dim
corridors and is noticeably uneasy in dark meeting rooms, preferring seats near doors and windows.
He notices car sounds in the street below and around the parking area with a start that pulls him
out of concentration, and he finds himself monitoring the environment instead of the spreadsheets in
front of him. At home, his wife has seen him wake during the night to check the locks and the hall,
then listen for sounds outside before returning to bed. He knows these habits are driven by his
fear, but he also experiences them as necessary precautions, the only way to stay safe. Even so, the
more he remains on guard and the more often he searches for exits or scans for danger, the more
readily everyday cues seize him and the more vividly the old scene returns. By the time he sought
help, Rangi was exhausted by this cycle and increasingly worried that his life had shrunk to
managing threat. He came in hoping to regain some sense that the accident belongs to the past, while
admitting that what he fears most is letting his guard down and being caught off guard again.
