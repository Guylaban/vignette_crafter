María had lived in the same apartment for years in a city where she now answered phones for a call
center, but she still carried herself as if the ground might shift without warning. Decades earlier,
when she was a younger woman, a terrorist attack had torn through a public place she had been near,
killing people around her and leaving her with a memory she still could not fully assemble. She
could say the facts in fragments, that there had been shouting, smoke, a burst of chaos, and the
terrible knowledge afterward that lives had been lost, yet she would often stop mid-sentence
because, as she put it, she could not seem to put the frightening event into words. There were
pieces she knew had happened, but no matter how hard she tried she could not bring them back, and
other parts of the scene remained blank. What stayed with her most vividly was not a clear story but
the bodily force of it: the tightening in her neck, the feeling that something was already breaking
around her before she even understood what was happening.

Over the years, anything that touched those lost pieces of the past could pull her back into the
same alarm. When coworkers or customers on the phone talked about people losing loved ones, María
felt her chest tighten and her neck begin to ache, as if grief itself were a warning. Mention of the
event, even indirectly, made her mind stall against the missing parts, and because she could not
make the memory orderly or complete, she was left with the sensation that the threat had never
really passed. Those moments quickly fed her belief that she had no future and that she had failed
in the most important role in her life, because she had not been able to protect anyone or make
sense of what happened. She would think, with a heavy certainty, that if such a thing had happened
once, it could happen again, and that she was powerless to stop bad things from happening to her.

In response, María began shaping her life around those moments of alarm. She stopped going near the
place where the attack had happened, even if it meant taking longer routes or turning down
invitations from relatives who wanted to pass through that part of the city. She avoided
anniversaries connected to the event, keeping busy and refusing family gatherings if she sensed the
date approaching. At home and at work, she also kept people at a careful distance, resisting
emotional closeness and intimacy because being attached felt like making herself more vulnerable to
loss. Yet the more she organized her days around these protective habits, the less opportunity she
had to talk through what had happened, and the more unreachable the event felt. The story stayed
trapped in sensations and gaps, making it even harder for her to find words for it, and the silence
around it deepened her sense that she had been left with something unspeakable.

Her job at the call center made the pattern harder to escape. Customers sometimes spoke about
bereavement or family emergencies, and those calls could leave her shaken for the rest of the shift.
She would rub her neck under the headset, feel the old ache settle in, and then go home determined
to keep others, even her adult children, at arm’s length. That distance brought its own sting. She
had come to believe that she no longer knew how to be the sort of wife she had once been, and after
her husband’s death she interpreted her guardedness as further proof that she had failed in her most
important role in life. Each attempt to avoid reminders seemed to confirm her hopelessness. The less
she faced, the more unreal and incomplete the memory became; the more incomplete it became, the more
those same themes of loss and talking about the dead could catch in her body and send her back into
fear. Even walking past a street sign near the old site, or hearing a phrase about someone’s mother
or husband, could set off the same spiral of blankness, neck pain, and the conviction that she could
not keep herself safe.

By the time María sought help, the pattern had narrowed her life. She was no longer only trying to
stay away from the place and the dates; she was trying to stay away from anything that might make
her feel close enough to lose someone again. What finally brought her in was the exhaustion of
living with that constant vigilance and the fear that if she kept avoiding the memory, she would
spend whatever future she had left with the same sense of helplessness. She hoped, quietly and with
little confidence, that therapy might help her find words for what happened and make the future feel
less empty than it does now.
