after a cyclone tore through the coastal town where he and his wife had lived. During the storm, the
house shook so violently that he could hear the roof panels slamming and, outside, the strange
silence between gusts before the next surge of wind and water. He lost power, phone service, and
then his wife, who was swept away when they tried to reach higher ground. In the chaotic hours that
followed, the smell of brackish mud and splintered timber, and the bitter taste of dirty water and
fuel, seemed to lodge in him. Even now, when the memory comes back, Mikaere says it is hard to
believe it happened to him at all; it feels too extreme, like something he watched happen to someone
else. Yet the memory is not distant. At night, especially when he wakes in the dark, the smell of
damp sludge can rush back, and with it the taste he remembers from the floodwater, as if his body is
replaying the disaster before he can think.

In the weeks after the cyclone, Mikaere began to notice that moments of ease were not restful for
him but dangerous. If he found himself feeling happy for a few minutes—at work after finishing a
difficult audit, or at home when a conversation with his sister briefly lifted his mood—he would
suddenly feel exposed, as though calm invited punishment. Those moments were followed by a sharp
pull into smoking, stepping outside for cigarette after cigarette, and by a reflex to shut down any
chance of talking about what had happened. He avoided conversations with neighbors about the storm’s
aftermath, and when coworkers asked how he was managing, he gave a brief nod and changed the
subject. He had come to believe that if he let himself relax, something terrible would happen again,
and that belief made even ordinary warmth feel unsafe. The more he protected himself by smoking,
avoiding rest, and keeping silent, the more he felt his world narrowing around the same thought: he
could not stop bad things from happening to him. That sense of helplessness deepened his conviction
that the world was dangerous and left him feeling dead inside, as if whatever had been vibrant in
him before the cyclone had been drained away.

The more numb and helpless Mikaere felt, the more his mind seemed to scan for danger. Unfamiliar
places became intolerable because he could not assess what might happen there, so he stopped taking
new routes for work and avoided visiting clients in offices he did not know well. At home, unusual
silence could jolt him immediately; if the neighborhood went quiet or if a meeting at the office
dragged on longer than expected, he assumed something bad was unfolding just beyond view. Those
expectations were not abstract. They were tied to the disaster itself, when the air had gone eerily
still before the next violent blast of wind, and when delays in hearing from emergency services had
felt like confirmation that help would not come. Once that kind of threat state was active, his
memory became more vivid and invasive. A quiet room could bring the smell of wet earth back to him,
and the taste of salt and fuel, and those sensations made the world around him seem newly menacing.
Even a brief moment of happiness, a softer evening at home, could pull him back into the storm in
miniature, as if his body mistrusted peace and kept searching for the next collapse.

To manage all of this, Mikaere clung to habits that seemed protective in the short term but kept
feeding the pattern. He smoked to steady himself, avoided relaxing because stillness felt dangerous,
and refused to talk in any depth about his wife’s death or the aftermath. Those strategies gave him
the illusion of control, but they also kept his grief and fear unprocessed, and that only
strengthened the belief that he was permanently unsafe and on his own. The more he withdrew, the
more unfamiliar even ordinary places felt, and the more silence, delay, or a sudden good mood at
work could trigger the sense that danger was near. His avoidance also meant that his days remained
organized around escape rather than recovery; he stopped meeting friends after work, declined
invitations to family gatherings, and became less available in his role as an auditor because he
could not tolerate being sent somewhere unpredictable. By the time he presented for help, he was
sleeping poorly, waking into darkness with his heart racing, and spending much of the day braced for
the next sign that disaster was about to strike. What finally brought Mikaere in was the fear that
he was disappearing into a life built around smoke, silence, and vigilance, and he said he was
hoping, more than anything, to feel safe enough to rest without waiting for the world to break
again.
