able to leave it behind. That conviction fed a constant sense that he had to be especially careful
because one never knew what could happen next.

Over time, those appraisals shaped how he lived. Aung began trying hard not to think about the event

himself relax would make him vulnerable. He also avoided television programmes, films, and articles

quickly if a scene included smoke or a hospital corridor. In his retirement routine, he became

he was still in control. Yet the same vigilance that drove those actions also made him pull back
from ordinary life. A faint burnt smell from a neighbour’s cooking on the apartment stairwell, or
the stress of a family disagreement, could set him on edge and send him back to his effort to
suppress the memory.

surface, they felt strangely alien, as if the event belonged to someone else rather than to him, and
the sequence of what had happened was confused and difficult to put in order. He would try to force

deepened his belief that something was wrong with him, and that his body’s reactions meant he

sound or a hot draft against his skin, he became more focused on danger, and that hyperalert state

settled him for long.

pushing away the memory and any comfort tied to it, the emotional force would return in raw waves,

watchful in the home he shared with his wife, pausing at odd noises, listening for unexpected sounds

life, and because a part of him still hoped he might learn how to feel safe in his own body again.
