chair, a body leaning too quickly into his space, the sense that he could not see who was behind
him. Those moments had left him with a body that stayed tense and primed for danger, as if he were
always bracing for impact. In restaurants, at family gatherings, and even in his own studio, he
automatically chose the seat with his back to the wall or the clearest view of the exits, and before
entering any new place he would map an escape route without thinking.

Over time, that constant sense of danger shaped how he moved through the world. Matiu became
vigilant for dangerous people, scanning faces and posture the way he once scanned captors for signs
of aggression. Sudden movements by others put him immediately on guard; if a client reached suddenly
for a camera bag, or if someone crossed behind him too quickly in a crowded gallery opening, his
chest tightened and his whole body locked into readiness. Because he felt so exposed, he began to
rely on rituals that made temporary sense to him. He kept asking his wife whether he seemed
“normal,” whether she thought he looked frightened, whether he was actually okay. In public he would
check in with his brother, his assistant, or even strangers he trusted a little, seeking reassurance
that he was safe and not about to lose control. He knew these questions could sound excessive, but
from his point of view they were the only thing keeping the fear from spilling over.

The problem was that the very habits meant to help him stay safe kept the danger feeling close. Each
time he sat near an exit, studied the room for threats, or asked someone to confirm he was fine, he
was silently telling himself that the threat was still there and that he could not trust his own
judgment. The more he watched for danger, the more danger seemed to fill the room. His body stayed
on alert through the day, shoulders raised, jaw tight, ready to move at the smallest shift in
someone else’s behavior. At work this became harder to hide. As a photographer he often needed to
move through crowded event spaces, but he started turning down assignments that would place him in
unfamiliar venues or near dense crowds. On shoots he would arrive early so he could identify the
safest seat or exit path before anyone else entered the space, and he would spend more energy
checking who was moving where than on the photographs he was meant to take. His wife noticed that
even at home he positioned himself carefully on the couch, never quite settling, and that he was
always looking toward the door.

The cycle had narrowed his life. Matiu found himself relying on others to calm him, yet their
reassurance never lasted long enough, so he needed it again the next day. He could feel that the
questions, the scanning, and the escape planning were changing how people responded to him, but he
also felt unable to stop because the fear returned so fast whenever someone moved abruptly or he
entered an unfamiliar place. Lately he had begun declining family outings and avoiding venues where

isolated. He sought help now because he was tired of living as if every room contained a threat, and

his ability to protect himself; what he hoped for, though he said it quietly, was to sit through a
meal, a shoot, or a meeting without having to keep proving to himself that he was still safe.
