Maya, a 33-year-old graphic designer, first sought help after months of living as though she were
still being watched. The stalking had begun with a man she had briefly spoken to after a
neighborhood event, and what made it terrifying was not any single dramatic confrontation but the
way he seemed to materialize in ordinary places: outside her apartment building, near the café where
she worked on her laptop, and once beside her on the bike path when she was commuting home. The
sight of a bicycle now pulled her back to that stretch of path and the knowledge of being shadowed
from behind; when someone walked too close behind her, or a cyclist slowed near her, her body
reacted before she could think. She would feel a surge of danger in her chest, certain that
something was about to happen even when nothing was obviously wrong. In those moments she became
convinced that the world was a dangerous place and that people were not what they seemed. She had
come to believe that the people responsible had faced no consequences, which made the threat feel
ongoing rather than over. That belief sharpened the way she read every glance and footstep, and the
more she felt herself being watched, the more the old fear returned.

Over time Maya’s days narrowed around that fear. At work, if she had to leave the design studio to
meet a client or pick up lunch, she would automatically scan reflections in shop windows and check
who was behind her. She made sure to stay near an exit in cafés and meeting spaces, and before she
felt comfortable anywhere she always identified escape routes. She avoided going anywhere near the
street where the stalking had been most intense, even when it was the quickest route home, because
simply being there made her feel as if she had invited danger back into her life. These precautions
gave her brief relief, but they also kept the whole episode vivid and close. Each time she mapped
exits or watched the door instead of her laptop screen, she was reminded again that she did not feel
safe. That constant vigilance fed her conviction that the world was dangerous and that others could
not be trusted, and it left her more alert to any sign of being followed. The more she scanned, the
more often she noticed a figure walking at the same pace or a cyclist lingering nearby, and those
moments immediately triggered the sense that she was under threat again.

Maya began to notice that her own coping style was keeping the fear alive. Because she avoided the
street where it happened, she never had the chance to see it as ordinary again; every detour made
the place feel more loaded, more like proof that something terrible had occurred there. Her habit of
staying close to exits and plotting escape routes also kept her attention fixed on danger, which
made her jumpy and easily startled. A coworker appearing suddenly at her desk or a bike bell ringing
behind her could send a jolt through her whole body, as if she had been caught off guard by
something far worse. Those alarm reactions made her even more certain that she was not overreacting
but correctly sensing danger, and that certainty pushed her back into the same routines of checking
and avoiding. In turn, the more she lived that way, the more she felt the world was unsafe and other
people were not what they seemed.

By the time she came to therapy, Maya and her husband had started planning errands around where she
could park, walk, and leave quickly. She had nearly stopped riding her bicycle, though it had once
been her favorite way to get around the city. She found herself declining invitations if they
involved crowded sidewalks, unfamiliar neighborhoods, or any situation where she would have to turn
her back on people. At work she was still functioning, but she was exhausted by how much of her
attention went to watching doors, mirrors, and approaching footsteps instead of her designs. What
finally brought her in was a recent evening when she left the office late, saw a cyclist slowing
beside her, and felt her heart lurch as if the stalking had resumed; she knew then that she could
not keep arranging her life around escape routes and constant scanning. Maya said she wanted to
believe the danger belonged to the past, but what frightened her most was the sense that it never
truly had.
