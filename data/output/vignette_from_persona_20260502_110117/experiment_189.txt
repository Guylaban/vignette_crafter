Amina, a 72-year-old widow from East Africa, came to treatment after years of trying to outrun
memories of the months when her home region was caught in civil war and she was forced to remain in
the area as a forester, moving through familiar woodland roads under unfamiliar danger. She
described one night in particular as the turning point: she had been sheltering in a small ranger
outpost when a blast erupted somewhere beyond the trees, the force of it shaking the corrugated roof
and leaving everyone silent for a split second before shouting broke out. In the dark, with no clear
sense of where the danger was coming from, she had felt certain that anyone nearby might be hiding
something, and that uncertainty has stayed with her ever since. Now, even after years in the United
States, she still notices how quickly her body tightens when people seem suspicious to her, as if
she must read every glance and movement for danger before it is too late.

At first she tried to manage this fear by keeping the world loud and busy. At home she would put on
the television or blast music, especially at night, because silence left her too aware of the dark
corners of the room and of waking up disoriented in the middle of the night. In the apartment where
she lives alone now, the switch from sleep to darkness can still bring back the same helpless
alertness she felt in the war zone, and she has learned to keep a lamp on or the TV humming just to
get through the hours before dawn. She also spends much of her day constantly scanning her
surroundings, checking doorways, windows, and anyone lingering too long outside her building. In
public she is vigilant for dangerous people, watching faces and hands, measuring whether someone is
acting strangely. These habits once made her feel more in control, but over time they have begun to
sharpen the very moments she fears, keeping her attention locked on signs of threat and making
nighttime, sudden bangs, and people who seem off to her feel even more charged.

The result has been a narrowing of her life. Amina avoids being outdoors after dark and finds
herself leaving the radio on in the house even when she would rather sit quietly and pray. When a
car backfires or a heavy door slams, she flinches hard enough that her chest aches, and afterward
she keeps checking the street and the hallway for movement. At work, where she still takes part-time
forestry consults and visits a wooded preserve with other staff, she struggles to focus because
every snapped branch or distant crack can pull her attention toward the possibility of an explosion.
If a colleague pauses too long near a trailhead or speaks in a low voice with someone she does not
know, she becomes guarded and watches them rather than the trees she once knew so well. She has
started to say no to evening meetings and has reduced the number of places she goes alone, partly
because she does not trust the dark and partly because scanning everyone and everything leaves her
exhausted.

Amina is not embarrassed to admit that these patterns have become a way of life, but she is
frightened by how little relief they now give her. The loud music, the television, the constant
looking over her shoulder—these are the very things she reaches for when she feels unsafe, yet they
keep her on edge and make the night feel longer, the dark more active, the world more full of
possible attack. She is seeking help now because her sleep has become too broken and her world too
small, and because she is tired of living as though every bang, every shadow, and every unfamiliar
person might bring the war back into the room. What she hopes for most is to rest in the dark
without fear, and what she fears most is that if she cannot, she will end up isolated in a life
organized entirely around danger.
