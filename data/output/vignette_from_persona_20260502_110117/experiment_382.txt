Clarence had grown up in a rural pocket of West Africa where the house sat near open ground and
trees, and where the smell of damp earth after rain still carried, for him, an uncanny closeness to
childhood. It was there, as a boy, that he repeatedly witnessed his father’s drunken rages toward
his mother: a door flung open hard enough to shake the frame, then slammed again; the sharp, sour
smell of alcohol on a man’s breath; his mother’s frightened voice breaking under the noise. What
Clarence remembers now is not a single clear scene but scattered pieces that never quite join up. He
can call up the feeling of standing frozen near the doorway, his chest tight and his stomach hollow,
yet when he tries to deliberately remember what happened he finds the details slip away. Sometimes,
without any image at all, the full emotional force returns and he is flooded with the same dread and
helplessness, as if the fear itself is the memory.

Over the years he came to notice that certain moments in the present could pull him back into that
old state with little warning. Walking through a patch of woods near the warehouse on his lunch
break, or even standing by the loading dock when the air smelled wet and green after a storm, could
make his body go on alert before he understood why. A door in the warehouse opening too suddenly, or
banging shut behind a coworker, would send a jolt through him. The smell of alcohol on a man at the
bus stop or at a family gathering could be enough to turn his stomach and make his shoulders
tighten. Because those sensations felt so linked to the feeling rather than to a clear picture,
Clarence found himself bracing against them by drinking heavily, taking medication or using drugs
when he could get them, surrounding himself with people so he was never left alone with his
thoughts, and sometimes picking arguments or creating drama just to keep his attention elsewhere.
These habits were not random to him; they were a way of outrunning the wave of feeling before it
took hold.

Yet the more he leaned on those strategies, the more tangled his experience became. Drinking and
other substances dulled him enough to keep going through the day, but afterward his recollection
felt even more broken, as though the fragments had slipped further apart. When he was constantly
talking, joking, or stirring up conflict, he had less space to sit with what had come back, but the
memories remained unworked-through and more likely to return as raw emotion. He began to notice that
the very things he used to steady himself also made him more sensitive afterward: after a night of
drinking, the smell of alcohol on someone’s clothes could hit harder; after days of socialising to
stay distracted, a quiet moment alone could make the woods of his childhood and the slam of a door
feel suddenly near again. Even at work, where he had spent years in the warehouse, a sudden bay door
crashing shut could make him tense and irritable, and he would start looking around, ready for a
confrontation or a way to move the feeling out of himself.

As these reactions grew more familiar, Clarence organized his life around avoiding them. He kept
himself busy with people, preferred noisy rooms, and made sure there was often something to drink or
some other way to blunt the edge of what came up. The trouble was that each attempt to keep the
memories at bay seemed to leave him with less distance from them, not more. A familiar smell or
sound could now arrive already primed with dread, because his mind had learned to expect the old
terror from the first hint of alcohol or a door slamming hard enough to echo. He became quicker to
pick fights, quicker to leave silence behind, and more dependent on whatever could crowd out the
feeling before it took over. At home, after his divorce, the emptier evenings were worst, and he
found himself circling between drink, company, and agitation rather than ever sitting still long
enough to sort out what he had lived through.

By the time he sought help, Clarence was exhausted by the pattern and frightened by how much of his
day was being arranged around keeping his own mind away from him. He worried that if he stopped
drinking, stopped filling every hour with company, or stopped provoking others to keep himself
activated, the old fear would rush back in full force and leave him unable to function. What he
hoped for, even now, was some way to face the memories without being swallowed by the feeling they
brought with them, and to walk through a warehouse doorway, a stretch of trees, or a passing whiff
of alcohol without being thrown back into the house of his childhood.
