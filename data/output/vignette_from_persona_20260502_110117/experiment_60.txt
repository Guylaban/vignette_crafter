caregiver’s violence was ordinary enough to feel both unforgettable and somehow unreal. As a child,
she was hit, shoved, and cornered in ways that taught her to stay small and constantly alert, but

recall specific moments in fragments—a hand raised over a kitchen sink, the sting of fear in a
hallway, the smell of bleach—then the images vanish as quickly as they came. At other times she can
speak of what happened with a flat, distant numbness, as if she were describing abuse that happened
to someone else. What unsettled her most was that the abuse felt so extreme, so outside what she
later understood as normal family life, that it has often seemed hard for her to believe it truly
happened to her at all.

Those broken memories have long shaped how Marisol moves through the world. Because the past can
surface without warning, she has come to avoid anything that might stir it up: difficult
conversations, family gatherings, crowded places, anything unpredictable or stressful. She keeps her
apartment meticulously ordered, cleaning and reorganizing again and again because the physical
routine gives her a sense of control when her mind does not. She also finds herself eating far more
than she needs, especially in the evening, using food to blunt distress that otherwise feels hard to
contain. Her work as a physical therapist has become a carefully managed environment; she can
function, but she chooses the most familiar routines and tries to limit situations that feel
emotionally taxing. In practice, that means she often turns down extra responsibilities and leaves
herself little margin for anything that could upset the balance she has built.

Marisol’s sense of danger is not vague. In ordinary situations she feels threatened by strangers who
approach too quickly, and in waiting rooms, store aisles, or the clinic lobby she will quietly angle
herself so her back is against a wall and she can see the room. When she rides the elevator at work
or hears footsteps behind her in a corridor, her body responds as if something bad is about to
happen. At night she wakes and checks whether she is safe, scanning the apartment before she can
settle back down. Those moments of threat have a way of pulling the past forward: if a man in the
parking lot comes too close, or if someone raises a voice unexpectedly, a fragment of childhood can
flash into mind and then disappear, leaving behind the same cold certainty she knew as a girl. In
that sense, the danger she feels in the present feeds the brokenness of the memory, and the memory
in turn makes ordinary contact with strangers feel even less safe.

Over the years, Marisol has organized her life around the hope that if she stays controlled enough,
nothing will break through. She cleans, avoids stress, and keeps herself busy, but the relief never
lasts. The more she tries to keep distress out, the more keyed up she becomes, watching doors,
watching faces, watching for movement near her shoulders. That constant guarding makes it harder for
her memories to hold together; they come as flashes, detached and unreal, just enough to remind her
what she is trying not to feel. Then the numbness and the uncertainty push her back toward more
control, more cleaning, more avoidance, and more overeating at night when she feels depleted. Her
eating, in turn, is followed by the same disappointed, heavy sense that she has not really escaped
anything.

clinic that let her keep a wall at her back. Even in her own apartment she became more restless at
night, waking to check locks and windows, and then lying awake with old fragments drifting in and
out of awareness. The accumulation of this has left Marisol feeling worn down and ashamed,
especially because she has always prided herself on helping other people regain function. She

her life. What frightens her most is that the past may keep invading her present in pieces she
