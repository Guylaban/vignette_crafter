Kwame had been a professor for most of his adult life, a man used to speaking calmly in lecture
halls and offices, but the event that brought him to treatment had nothing to do with his work and
everything to do with his younger sister. More than a year earlier, during a heavy storm season, she
had been driving home when floodwater rose quickly across a low stretch of road. He had received the
call from a nephew frantic and breathless, saying the car had been found near a bridge and that
rescuers were searching the water. Kwame spent that night at the edge of the river with his family,
smelling mud, gasoline, and stagnant water, watching headlights sweep across a floodplain that
seemed to swallow sound. His sister was later recovered alive, but the memory of that night remained
fixed in his mind with the smell of water and the sight of moving floodwater under harsh lights.
Since then, water, flooding, and even large bodies of water had become potent reminders of what had
happened, especially when he encountered them unexpectedly around campus, near drainage channels
after rain, or on his drive home past the harbor. Related smells could seize him just as powerfully,
carrying him back to that night in an instant.

What troubled Kwame most was how quickly his body now responded before he could think. When a storm
passed and runoff collected in the quad outside his office, or when he caught a damp, riverlike
smell in a hallway after cleaning, his chest tightened and his muscles braced as if something were
about to happen again. He described it as his body reacting to danger before his mind had time to
catch up, a jolt that would come before any clear thought and leave him feeling exposed and
unsettled. In those moments he was flooded not only with fear but with horror and a sharp, lingering
shame that he had been powerless to protect his sister. He would tell himself, even while sitting
safely at his desk, that he should be able to calm down, yet he found he could not relax in a safe
place once those sensations had started. The physical alarm felt convincing to him; because his body
responded so strongly, he became more watchful for anything that might set it off again.

Over time, that expectation of alarm made the world feel narrower. Kwame found himself scanning
weather reports before leaving for work and choosing routes that avoided roads near water. On
campus, he became uneasy when rain pooled around the science building or when maintenance sprayed
down walkways, and he noticed his attention snagging on the odor of wet concrete after storms. At
first he tried to push through, but each encounter seemed to confirm how vulnerable he still felt,
and the sense of threat intensified rather than eased. The more he noticed his body tense at the
sight or smell of water, the more he trusted those signals, and the more he anticipated that danger
might arrive again without warning. He began sitting through lectures with a portion of his mind on
alert, monitoring his breathing and the tightness in his chest instead of fully engaging with his
students. Even in his apartment, where nothing was objectively wrong, he remained unable to settle,
as if some part of him were waiting for the next cue.

As the months passed, Kwame’s avoidance became part of his routine. He stopped taking evening walks
by the riverfront he had once enjoyed, declined invitations that involved boating or waterfront
restaurants, and rearranged errands so he would not have to pass the reservoir near his
neighborhood. When rain was forecast, he would work from home if possible, telling colleagues only
that the weather made commuting difficult. Those choices brought temporary relief, but they also
kept him distant from ordinary life and increasingly aware of how many places now felt risky. He
began to feel embarrassed by the extent of his reactions, especially because he had always seen
himself as composed and in control. The shame made it harder to talk about what had happened, so he
remained largely silent with friends and family, even as his sister urged him to stop carrying it
alone. The silence left him alone with the same warning system that had been set off at the
floodbank, and the alarm seemed to come faster each time he met a reminder.

By the time he sought help, Kwame was exhausted by the constant vigilance and by the way his
professor’s life had shrunk around weather, smells, and sudden surges of stress. A tense week of
heavy rain on campus finally pushed him to make an appointment after he found himself gripping the
edge of his desk during a lecture, heart racing at the smell of damp carpeting drifting in from the
corridor. He came because he was tired of feeling that his body had its own mind and because he
feared one more reminder would leave him unable to work or be present for the people who still
mattered to him. What he most hoped for was simple: to be able to stand in a safe place again
without waiting for his body to sound the alarm.
