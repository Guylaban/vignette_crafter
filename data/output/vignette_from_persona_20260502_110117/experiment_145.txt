park where she worked in IT support, a job that kept her moving between cubicles, server rooms, and
the parking lot at all hours. She had first felt the change in herself after a deployment years
earlier, when a night patrol was shattered by the sudden roar of helicopters overhead and the sharp,
burnt smell of a vehicle fire drifting through the air. In that moment she had thought with complete
certainty that something was about to explode, and the body memory of it never really left her. Even
now, the smell of burnt toast from a break-room microwave or scorched wiring from an overloaded
outlet could pull her back so fast that her heart raced and her muscles jolted before she had time
to think. The sound of helicopters or low aircraft passing over the building did the same, and at
work she would stiffen in the middle of a ticket call if she heard rotors or a plane banking too low
outside the window. Those reminders made ordinary situations feel dangerous, especially when a
stranger approached her in a hallway, at the copier, or in the parking lot; she had come to believe
that people she did not know could be a threat before she could judge their intent, and she found
herself repeatedly looking behind her to make sure no one was following.

Over time, Maya tried to manage that alarm by not letting anyone near the story. She avoided talking
with colleagues or family about what had happened or what it had been like afterward, not because
she wanted to be distant, but because words made the images come back and she did not trust herself
to stay in control if she opened the subject. She also avoided anything that might stir up strong
emotion, including films, arguments, and even reunions that might force her to feel too much at
once. When the tension became unbearable, she sometimes hurt herself physically to stop thinking
about the event, describing it as a blunt way to interrupt the spiral for a few minutes. Yet these
efforts left her more on edge rather than less. The act of scanning behind her in a store aisle,
crossing a parking lot, or tensing at the sound of an aircraft kept her attention fixed on danger,
and the more she watched for it, the more readily she felt threatened by strangers approaching in
ordinary places. Her body had become quick to react, and once she was flooded with that response,
she was less able to think clearly about whether anyone was actually unsafe.

The same strategies also changed the world around her. Maya arranged her days to reduce exposure to
reminders, which meant taking the long way into the office to avoid the loading dock where the smell
of overheated metal sometimes drifted in, declining after-work gatherings, and keeping conversations
with coworkers practical and brief. At first this seemed to help, but it also made the reminders
feel larger and more dangerous when they inevitably appeared. A burnt odor in the workplace or a
helicopter passing overhead no longer felt like passing inconveniences; they became proof to her
that danger could break into an ordinary day at any moment. Once that happened, her fear pushed her
back into the same pattern of isolation and control, and the cycle narrowed. She found herself more
watchful in the office, more certain that a stranger in the hallway might mean harm, and more
inclined to leave quickly before her emotions surged. The self-harm, the refusal to speak about the
trauma, and the effort to avoid emotional strain all gave her short relief, but they also kept the
event alive in fragments that returned through the very things she was trying to outrun.

By the time she sought help, Maya was exhausted by the vigilance. She had noticed that she was
spending too much of her workday checking behind her, bracing when a visitor walked too close, and
feeling her chest tighten whenever something smelled burnt in the building. She had begun to worry

would either hurt herself again or become so guarded that she could no longer function at work or

safe in her own workplace and around other people again.
