Mina was a 43-year-old married pharmacist who had built a life in the same American city where she
had raised her children, yet she moved through her days with the wary precision of someone who never
fully felt safe. Her childhood in a Middle Eastern home had been marked by physical abuse from a
caregiver who enforced obedience with blows, shouted commands, and the sharp smell of alcohol on his
breath. Years later, the smell of alcohol in passing at the pharmacy or in the corridor outside a
restaurant would hit her with unexpected force. So would the clipped tone of authority, especially
when a supervisor or uniformed person gave her an instruction without explanation. At work, when a
doctor’s office called with orders, or when a manager spoke briskly across the counter, Mina’s body
reacted before her mind could catch up: she felt as if danger were present even when nothing was
actually wrong, her attention narrowed, and she became unable to concentrate on filling
prescriptions she had done thousands of times.

What made those moments so potent was the way they pulled her back into the original abuse. Mina
described her memories of it as more real and vivid than ordinary life, as though the old kitchen,
the raised hand, and the smell of liquor were “right there again.” Sometimes the memory came with
such overwhelming emotion that she had to stop whatever she was doing and go into the back room
until she could breathe. Other times she could recount what happened in a detached, numb way, saying
it felt as if it had happened to a stranger. Yet even the numbness did not leave her untouched;
after these intrusions, she would look around for the nearest exit, scan faces for anger, and brace
herself for the next order. The memory and the sense of current danger fed one another, and both
made the workplace one of her most difficult environments. Because the pharmacy was full of
requests, instructions, and the possibility of catching alcohol on a customer’s breath, she began
avoiding extra shifts and stepped back from tasks that brought her into contact with physicians,
police officers, or anyone in authority. She had also started avoiding situations that might make
her feel happy or hopeful, because hope felt exposed to her, as though any moment of relief might be
followed by a blow.

As the months went on, Mina came to believe that nothing in her life felt meaningful anymore and
that she could not stop bad things from happening to her. She saw her silence, her shrinking world,
and her inability to protect herself as evidence that she had failed in her most important role in
life. Those appraisals deepened the danger she felt: if bad things always happened and she had
already failed, then every command, every smell, every sudden memory seemed to confirm that she was
still trapped in a life she could not control. They also made the old scenes return with greater
force; when she thought about being powerless and having failed, the childhood abuse felt closer and
more complete, and her memories became harder to set aside. The same beliefs made ordinary cues
around her more threatening too. A boss’s request, the sight of a security guard, or the faint scent
of alcohol on a customer could all become signs that she was about to be hurt again, and she would
flinch as if she were in immediate danger.

To cope, Mina hurt herself physically to stop thinking about the event, usually in private after
work when the pressure of the day and the old fear became unbearable. For a brief time, the pain
would pull her away from the images, but afterward she felt even more ashamed and defeated, which
only strengthened her sense that she had failed and that nothing meaningful was left to hold on to.
The self-harm also left her more on edge the next day, more jumpy to sudden voices and more
determined to avoid anyone in authority or uniform. At home, she kept herself from birthday dinners,
family outings, and small celebrations her husband tried to plan, because anything hopeful seemed
risky and because she worried that happiness would only set her up for more disappointment. The cost
was visible in her marriage and in her work: she had become quieter with her husband, more withdrawn
with her children, and less willing to take on the responsibilities that once gave her pride. By the
time she sought help, it was after a week in which the smell of alcohol from a customer, a
supervisor’s abrupt instruction, and a vivid flashback at the pharmacy had left her shaking at the
counter and afraid she would hurt herself again. What brought Mina in now was her fear that she was
losing herself completely, and her hope, however faint, was that she might finally learn how to live
without feeling hunted by the past.
