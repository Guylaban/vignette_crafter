during a reporting trip and tortured over several days by security forces who wanted names, sources,
and admissions he could not safely give. He remembered the first interrogation room most clearly
because of the sounds: a child crying somewhere down the hall, thin and persistent, and the scrape
of a chair as someone leaned in close and said a name that sounded like one of his old contacts.
Even now, when he hears children crying in a grocery store or on the sidewalk outside his apartment,
or when a familiar name is spoken in conversation, his body reacts before he can think. He feels
suddenly lightheaded, as if the room tilts, and he becomes jumpy to ordinary noises that would not
bother most people. In those moments he pulls away from people, avoids seeing former colleagues he
once socialised with, and tells himself he has to keep a low profile. He has also begun skipping
meals or restricting food, saying it helps him feel less exposed and gives him some sense of
control. What Minh has come to dread is not only the sound itself but the rush of what it brings
back: the torture, the helplessness, and the way his mind reaches for explanations he cannot bear.

The memory does not come back to him in a clean sequence. When he tries to deliberately remember
what happened, he finds it difficult to access the details, and what does surface feels strangely
unreal, almost impossible to believe happened to him. There are stretches he cannot account for no
matter how hard he tries. Yet the gaps do not bring relief. They leave him more on edge, scanning
other people closely for signs that they might harm him, watching faces, hands, and posture with a
vigilance that feels exhausting but necessary. He startles at a door banging shut or a pan dropping
in the apartment below, and the jump itself sends him back into the same half-remembered terror. In
that heightened state, he becomes convinced that other people can see what happened to him just by
looking at him, as if the torture has marked his body in ways he cannot hide. He has begun to
believe there is something wrong with him as a person, and that he acted in ways that go against who
he is. Those thoughts deepen his fear and shame, and they make it harder to sit with anyone who knew
him before, especially people from his old professional circle, so he keeps cancelling plans and
staying home. The more he avoids, the less chance he has of finding reassurance, and the more the
memories and bodily reactions feel like proof that he is damaged.

Minh’s days have narrowed around these reactions. He will leave the house for work assignments and
feel his attention split between the story in front of him and the possibility of threat behind him.
At his desk, if someone nearby laughs too suddenly or a child cries outside the newsroom, his
shoulders tense and he loses the thread of what he is reading. Later he replays the event in his
head, dwelling on how it could have been prevented, as if finding the correct decision might undo
what was done to him. But the rumination only keeps the scene active, and when the torture memory
comes into sharper focus he feels the same helpless anger, horror, guilt, and shame, which makes him
even more certain that he is unsafe and fundamentally altered. In turn, that sense of danger makes
him watch people even more closely, and the watching feeds the conviction that he can spot harm
before it reaches him. The cycle has reached into his eating as well; after a day of being on guard,
he often restricts food because eating feels too vulnerable, though by evening he is weaker and more
dizzy, which he experiences as another bodily sign that something is wrong.

Over the months after his release and divorce, Minh’s avoidance has begun to affect his work and his
relationships. He has turned down lunches with former colleagues, missed informal meetings, and
spent long stretches alone in his apartment. He says it is easier that way, but the isolation leaves
him with more time to replay the torture, more time to notice the unreliability of his memory, and
more time to anticipate danger in ordinary sounds and faces. What finally brought him to seek help
was a recent morning in which a child crying in the apartment hallway, followed by a neighbour
calling out a name that reminded him of the interrogation, made him dizzy enough that he had to sit
on the floor outside his own door. He is seeking help now because he fears that if this continues he
will keep shrinking his life until there is nothing left of the person he was, and because, despite
his shame, he still hopes he can learn to feel like himself again.
