Omar Khalil, a 37-year-old married firefighter from a Middle Eastern family, came to treatment after
months of feeling as if his life had narrowed around one terrifying period in which he had been
stalked. The stalking had begun with small, unsettling details he could not dismiss: a bicycle left
repeatedly outside his station and later near his home, a shadowy figure seeming to appear at the
edge of streets he used on his commute, and the sickening sense that he was being followed or
tracked even when he tried to tell himself he was overreacting. What made the experience especially
potent was that it invaded the ordinary spaces of his day—driving to work, walking into a building,
stepping outside after a shift—so that physical locations no longer felt neutral. During the most
frightening stretches, Omar would enter a room and immediately scan for exits, and he became
intensely alert to unexpected or loud sounds, because each abrupt noise felt to him like the start
of something he had to prepare for.

As the stalking continued, those triggers began pulling the event back into him with little warning.
Seeing a bicycle locked near the firehouse, passing a familiar intersection, or noticing a car
pacing him on the road could instantly bring the full memory back, not as a distant recollection but
as a rush that made him stop what he was doing. He described how the memory would arrive in pieces
he could not hold onto for long: he knew what had happened, yet when he tried to explain it he found
himself unable to find the words, and the emotion rose so quickly that he had to break off and sit
in silence. Each return of the memory seemed to confirm what he had already started to believe about
himself. Omar had come to think that he had permanently changed for the worse, that the fear and
jumpiness meant he was going crazy, and that his inability to handle the situation showed he was
weak in a way he had never allowed himself to be before. The more the memory broke through, the more
it fed those beliefs, and the more ashamed and altered he felt.

Those appraisals quickly spread into the way he moved through the world. Believing that he had been
weakened and was no longer trustworthy in his own judgment, Omar became even more on guard. He
watched other people closely for signs that they might harm him, especially when he was off duty or
alone, and the moment he entered any room or building his eyes went to doors, hallways, and exits.
Because he was convinced that danger could appear without warning and that he was no longer the man
he had been, he began treating ordinary places as if they were potential traps. A quiet restaurant,
the grocery store, the parking lot outside his station, even the gym near his home all became
settings where he felt the need to check who was behind him and whether someone might be tracking
his route. He avoided certain streets and found himself making detours that lengthened his commute,
and after a while the bicycle itself—once merely a detail in the stalking—became enough to set his
whole body on edge.

What frightened Omar most was how the threat state seemed to keep his mind tied to the event. An
unexpected bang in the station, a slammed locker door, or footsteps too close behind him would send
him back to the same conviction that he was under watch and had to stay ready. That vigilance, in
turn, made him think even more harshly about himself: if he had to keep scanning, if he could never
relax, then something in him must be fundamentally damaged. On the worst days, the memory would
surface while he was already alert, and the combination left him feeling overwhelmed and exposed, as
though he were failing at the very job that depended on steadiness. Over weeks and months, this fed
a tightening cycle in which every sign of being followed or every sharp sound confirmed his fear,
and every confirmation deepened his sense of permanent change and weakness. He withdrew from
colleagues, stopped lingering after shifts, and became wary even in the company of other people
because he could not shake the sense that someone might mean him harm.

By the time he sought help, Omar was exhausted by living this way and frightened by how small his
world had become. He said he wanted to keep protecting his family and doing his work, but he was
most afraid that if he continued alone he would either break down completely or remain trapped in
this state of watching, checking, and reliving.
