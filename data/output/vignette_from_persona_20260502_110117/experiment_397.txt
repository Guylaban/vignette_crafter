Mai had been working late in a downtown tax office during the spring rush when the man who had been
lingering near the building followed her to the parking garage, shoved her into his car, and held
her captive for hours that felt like they stretched beyond time. During that period she learned how
quickly ordinary movement could turn into danger: a hand reaching across the seat, a door opening
too fast, footsteps in a hallway. Even now, long after she was brought home, she could not enter a
room without scanning for the nearest wall, and she would sit with her back pressed flat against it
so she could see every door and anyone who came close. In the office she chose the chair facing the
entrance; in restaurants she picked corners; at home she kept the lights on and listened hard for
the smallest sound in the hall or the scrape of a key at her apartment door. Unexpected movement
nearby made her jerk as if she had been struck, and she hated how quickly her body betrayed her, how
instantly it seemed to know she was not safe.

What made the reactions worse was the way they fed her thoughts about what had happened. Each time
she startled, each time she caught herself counting exits or listening for someone entering her
home, she became more certain that the event had not only been terrifying but somehow her fault. She
replayed the evening in fragments, fixating on the moment she had smiled politely when the man asked
a question outside the building, the moment she had not crossed the street sooner, the moment she
had been too tired to stay with coworkers. In her mind, those small choices became proof that she
had invited the danger by acting carelessly. At the same time, she seethed over the fact that the
people responsible had faced no consequences. The investigation had stalled; the man had never been
found; the system that was supposed to protect her seemed to have simply moved on. That lack of
accountability hardened her sense that what happened to her could happen again, and that no one
would stop it.

Mai spoke about her body in a flat, ashamed voice that made clear how deeply the captivity had
altered her sense of herself. She said she felt humiliated by what had happened to her body, and
although she did not describe everything in detail, the shame was visible in the way she avoided
mirrors and changed clothes quickly, as if exposure itself were dangerous. Since the assault, she
had become noticeably guarded at work, declining dinner invitations and leaving early whenever the
office emptied out. Her tax consulting job now felt fraught in ways it had not before: the quiet
evening hours when she used to finish returns in peace were the same hours she now dreaded, because
the sound of the elevator opening or a sudden movement beside her cubicle could send a surge through
her chest and leave her frozen, certain that someone was there to trap her again. She had started
checking the lock on her apartment door several times before bed and keeping her phone within reach,
listening intently for any sign that someone was coming in.

Over weeks, the constant alertness wore away at her confidence and deepened the conviction that she
had somehow brought the ordeal on herself, while the absence of consequences for the perpetrator
kept the whole experience feeling unfinished and unbearable. She came to therapy after a night in
which a coworker dropped a stack of files beside her chair and she nearly shouted, then sat rigid
for the rest of the evening with her back against the wall, unable to concentrate and shaking with
anger at herself for being so “weak.” By then she was exhausted by the vigilance, the shame, and the
bitterness that no one had paid for what was done to her. What she hoped for now was not to forget,
but to stop feeling trapped in her own body and in every room she entered; what frightened her most
was that she might never feel safe unless she was pressed against a wall, listening for the next
person to come in.
