Mariam was 24, a single Middle Eastern woman who worked as a tailor in a small shop above a bakery,
where bolts of fabric were stacked shoulder-high and the front windows looked straight onto a busy
street. The crash happened on her way home from work, late enough that the light had already gone
flat and gray. Another car struck the side of the taxi she was in hard enough to throw her forward
against the seatbelt; for a few terrible seconds she was trapped in metal and noise, convinced the
vehicle might catch fire or be hit again. Even now, when she spoke about it, she described the
memory as if it were not past tense at all. She would say it played over and over like a film she
could not stop, and when it came back she felt the same terror rush through her body, the scene
snapping into the present so vividly that she could almost hear the screeching tires and feel her
chest tighten as though the impact were happening again.

In the weeks that followed, Mariam came to believe that she had to be especially careful because you
never knew what could happen next. The world, in her mind, had become a dangerous place, and people
were not what they seemed; a driver, a stranger at a curb, even a calm-looking road could turn
threatening without warning. Those beliefs pushed her into constant monitoring of everything around
her. On her walks to the shop she scanned intersections, mirrors, and parked cars for signs of
danger, and in the tailor shop she kept herself near the doorway or with her back against a wall so
she could see the room. At night, too, she could not settle into sleep easily because she felt she
needed to stay alert, as if lowering her guard would invite another catastrophe. She would mentally
rehearse worst-case scenarios on the bus stop bench or while pinning hems at work, telling herself
that if she imagined every bad outcome in advance she might be ready for it.

The more Mariam scanned, the more the fragments of the crash returned. A sudden horn outside the
shop, the vibration of a passing truck, or the sight of a dented car door could pull her back into
the scene with such force that she felt herself reliving it rather than remembering it. That sense
of the event happening right now made the memories even more invasive, and the replayed images
carried the same terror she had felt in the taxi. She responded by narrowing her world further. She
avoided driving altogether and refused rides whenever she could, even from relatives, choosing long
walks or missed opportunities over the possibility of being in a vehicle. At first she told herself
this was simply prudence, another way of being careful, but it gradually spilled into her
assumptions about everything else: if she had to keep scanning every street and every face, it must
be because danger was everywhere and other people could not be trusted. Her vigilance in turn made
her tense and restless, always on alert for what might go wrong next.

In turn, that constant state of alertness fed back into the memories. When Mariam was exhausted from
sleeping poorly because she could not let herself fully relax, the crash scenes came more easily,
and the more they replayed, the more determined she became to guard against anything that might
resemble it. Yet the guarding itself seemed to keep the event alive. The taxi ride she took once
after the accident, because she had no other choice, became a turning point: every jolt of the car,
every glance at surrounding traffic, every thought about what could happen if another driver lost
control sharpened her fear until the whole ride felt like a countdown. Afterward, she noticed that
the image of the crash was even more vivid, not less, as though her effort to stay prepared had left
the memory primed and ready to break through. She began declining extra hours if they would require
travel, which strained her relationships with her employer and made her feel increasingly isolated,
but she still told herself the only sensible way to live was to keep checking, keep expecting the
worst, and keep herself positioned where she could see the room.

By the time she sought help, Mariam was exhausted by the effort of staying safe. She described
wanting to stop living as if the next intersection or unfamiliar face were a threat, yet she was
frightened that if she let her guard down even for a moment, she would be caught unprepared and the
crash would happen again in some other form. She came to treatment because the constant alertness
was wearing her down, her sleep was breaking apart, and the world had begun to feel smaller and more
dangerous than she could bear. What she hoped for most was to be able to ride in a vehicle, sleep
without bracing herself, and walk into her shop without feeling that every sound or movement might
become the start of disaster.
