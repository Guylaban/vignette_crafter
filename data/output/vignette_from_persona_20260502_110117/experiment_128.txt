relied on felt to her like something that had been taken apart and never properly put back together.
She described the original ordeal vividly: the long hours in the hospital, the sharp antiseptic
smell that clung to the corridors, the sight of needles, drips, and machines surrounding her child’s
bed while she waited for updates she could barely absorb. What stayed with her most was not a single
picture but the feeling of terror that seemed to flood her body whenever she remembered those days,
as if the whole event could rush back without warning. Even now, talk of people losing loved ones
could pull her straight back into that emotional state, and hospital smells or the sight of medical
equipment could make her feel as though she were once again standing helplessly beside that bed. In
those moments she was struck by the thought that she had permanently changed for the worse, that she
had acted in ways that went against who she was, and that she simply could not stop bad things from
happening to her or to the people she loved.

Those beliefs shaped the way she lived afterward. Mona began to avoid going out unless it was
absolutely necessary, because the world felt full of reminders she could not control. She tried to
push the memories out of her mind whenever they surfaced, and she avoided hospitals, ambulances, and
physical examinations so completely that even ordinary medical appointments became fraught. After
the event, whenever something triggered the memory, she would be overwhelmed by grief rather than

unbearable wave of feeling made her pull back further from people and places that might stir it up
again. She noticed that once the memory broke through, it did not stay contained: hearing a friend
mention a relative in the ICU, or passing a clinic with that familiar smell, could make the whole
scene force its way into her mind when she least wanted it there. And when it did, the old
conclusions returned with fresh intensity—she had not only been powerless then, she was still
damaged now, and because she could not make danger stop, it made sense to keep herself away from
situations that might remind her of it.

Over time, that way of coping began to feed the very fears it was meant to quiet. Because Mona was

seemed to confirm that she had become a different person, someone less capable and less like the
woman she had once been. She found herself increasingly convinced that she had violated her own
values during her son’s illness, especially in the moments when she had had to stay composed or make
decisions while feeling inwardly shattered; those judgments deepened her belief that something in
her had been permanently altered. The more she tried to keep the memories out, the more brittle her
mind felt, and the more frightening it became when they returned with that same tide of grief. She
was also more easily set off by other people’s losses, because any conversation about bereavement

increasingly wary of social gatherings, where such topics might arise without warning.

herself she most valued. What she hoped for was not to erase the memory of her son’s illness, but to
be able to think about it without being flooded by grief or pulled back into the conviction that she
was broken and powerless.
