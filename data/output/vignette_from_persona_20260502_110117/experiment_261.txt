Margaret, a 78-year-old European American woman, had spent most of her life as a graphic designer in
a quiet suburban home with her husband, but the storm that upended her world had made the ordinary
feel treacherous. During the natural disaster, the house shuddered for hours, windows rattled, and
in the dark she could hear doors banging somewhere in the building and outside in the wind-lashed
street. She remembered clutching the hallway wall while debris struck the siding and a neighbor’s
voice carried through the chaos about someone who had lost family in the flooding. Even now, the
sharp slam of a door in a shop, the smell of alcohol on someone passing too close, or talk of people
losing loved ones could send her straight back to that night. In those moments, Margaret felt that
what had happened to her had shattered any belief she had once held about how life worked; the
world, she had come to believe, was dangerous, and what had happened was deeply unfair, made worse
by the sense that nothing had ever really been done to put it right.

Afterward, Margaret found herself trying to make sense of it by going over every detail again and
again, dwelling on how the event might have been prevented, as if some missed choice could be found
if she thought hard enough. She cleaned and reorganized drawers, shelves, and papers repeatedly,
needing the order of her apartment to feel like a small defense against disorder, and she also tried
to erase the memory altogether, shoving away thoughts of the storm whenever they surfaced. But the
more she fought it, the more the memory returned in a form that would not settle into the past.
Sometimes she could recall the flooding and the banging doors with a strange numbness, as if it had
happened to a stranger; at other times it came back with such force that it felt present again, not
remembered but re-entered, and the strangeness of it only deepened her conviction that the whole
thing was unreal, almost impossible to believe had happened to her at all. Those moments made her
more determined to keep everything controlled, because if the memory could surge back so vividly,
she reasoned, then she had to stay ready for anything. Her cleaning became even more repetitive, her
mind kept returning to prevention, and both efforts seemed to keep the event close rather than
distant.

That vigilance made her acutely alert to the world around her. Doorways, latches, and sudden bangs
in her daily routine drew her attention immediately; in the apartment building, a neighbor’s door
slamming in the corridor could make her flinch and leave her tense for hours. The same was true when
she was out with her husband at the grocery store and caught the smell of alcohol from another
shopper or overheard a family speaking about a recent death; each of these moments seemed to confirm
that danger and loss were never far away. Margaret responded by scanning for what might go wrong
next, and the scanning itself kept the storm alive in her mind. The more she watched for those
signs, the more often the memory broke through, carrying that same numb, unreal quality, and she
would return home unsettled and resume cleaning cupboards, straightening labels, and checking rooms
over and over until the apartment felt temporarily manageable. In turn, all that effort to control
and erase the experience left the event less, not more, contained: the memory stayed vivid enough to
be reactivated by the smallest cue, and the cues themselves seemed to multiply in her mind because
she was now looking for them everywhere.

By the time she came for help, Margaret felt worn down by the narrowness of her life. She had
started avoiding the lobby when residents entered or left, and she no longer enjoyed errands that
might expose her to a slamming door or the smell of alcohol in public places. Her husband noticed
that she was quieter, more preoccupied, and increasingly absorbed by the question of how the
disaster could have been prevented, as though the answer might finally make the world seem less
threatening. What brought her in was the fear that she was becoming trapped in a cycle she could not
break: the more she tried to push the storm out of mind and impose order on her home, the more often
the memory returned and the more dangers she seemed to find in ordinary life. Margaret was not only
afraid that another disaster could happen, but that she would never again feel that the world made
sense, and what she most hoped for was some way to live without constantly bracing for the next door
slam, the next reminder of loss, or the next sudden return of that night.
