Rohit Shah had been a reliable, composed interior designer for most of his adult life, the kind of
man who could walk a client through a renovation with calm precision and an eye for detail. That
changed after his older brother died suddenly, leaving Rohit stunned by how ordinary the day had
begun and how violently it ended. One afternoon his wife had called to say there had been a
collapse, a rush to the hospital, and by the time Rohit arrived the story had already closed around
a loss he had not had time to absorb. He remembered the hard brightness of the corridor, the smell
of disinfectant, and the awful finality in the doctor’s voice. In the weeks afterward, funerals and
cemeteries became unbearable for him, not because he believed anything specific would happen there,
but because the silence, the flowers, and the sight of grieving families made the loss feel newly
real. Even hearing people talk about someone losing a loved one could catch him off guard; he would
tighten, go still, and feel as if he were back in that hospital corridor. The anniversary of his
brother’s death carried particular force. As it approached, Rohit found himself scanning the
calendar, feeling dread build days in advance, and noticing the date in a way that made it hard to
think about anything else. On those days he became consumed with mentally rehearsing worst-case
scenarios, telling himself that if he imagined every possible disaster, he might not be blindsided
again.

Rohit had come to rely on those rehearsals as a kind of armor, especially after a trigger. If a
client mentioned a funeral they had attended, or if he drove past a cemetery on the way to a site
visit, his mind would immediately begin running through what could go wrong next, who else he might
lose, and how he would cope if it happened without warning. He told himself this was preparation,
not panic. In his private reasoning, the more he anticipated catastrophe, the less helpless he would
feel. Yet the effort never settled him for long. The moment his body registered danger, his heart
would race and his muscles would brace before he had even fully understood what had set him off.
Sometimes, in the middle of a perfectly ordinary meeting, he would be hit by a wave of sensation so
vivid that he felt as if the hospital scene were happening again, complete with the same helpless
certainty that something irreversible was unfolding. That bodily jolt made him more determined to
stay on guard, and he would retreat further into rehearsing disasters, trying to think several steps
ahead of every imaginable loss.

Over time, the habit widened. Rohit began avoiding dates and anniversaries connected to his
brother’s death, declining family gatherings if they seemed likely to drift toward remembrance. He
also started steering clear of anything that reminded him of someone he had lost, even small,
unexpected things like a song playing in a store or a colleague’s story about an uncle or friend who
had died. At work, where his job often required him to visit clients’ homes and discuss personal
preferences in intimate detail, grief-related talk could appear without warning. A homeowner
describing a memorial corner for a parent, or a vendor mentioning a recent funeral, could make Rohit
tense and abruptly change the subject. Afterward, he would carry the unease with him and watch
himself more closely, convinced that another hit of that same shock might be coming. The more his
body reacted before his mind could catch up, the more he leaned on worst-case rehearsal to feel
steady enough to keep working, and the more those grim mental drills seemed to prime him for the
next surge of alarm. His irritability grew in the same period. At home he snapped more easily with
his wife, then felt ashamed and withdrew, which only left more time alone with his thoughts and the
calendar dates he dreaded. He became less present at dinner and more preoccupied with checking the
time, as if vigilance itself could keep loss from taking him by surprise.

By the time Rohit sought help, the pattern had narrowed his life. He was still functioning, still
taking on design projects, but he felt trapped in a cycle of bracing, avoiding, and being jolted
back into the same raw moment of loss. What finally brought him in was the realization that the next
anniversary was approaching and that he was already organizing his days around it, trying to keep
grief reminders away and preparing himself for catastrophe in a way that no longer felt protective.
He was frightened that if he stopped rehearsing the worst, he would be hit by the same unbearable
shock all over again; at the same time, he hoped that treatment might help him face the date without
feeling as though his body were constantly waiting for the next death.
