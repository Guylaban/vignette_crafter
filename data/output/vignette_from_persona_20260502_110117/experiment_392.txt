Rashid, a 64-year-old South Asian warehouse worker, came to understand his symptoms as beginning
with the night he was held captive many years ago, when a late evening had already settled over the
place where he was trapped and the darkness seemed to deepen every sound. Even now, the same hour of
the day can tighten his chest before he notices what is happening; when the clock edges toward that
time, he becomes watchful and uneasy, feeling that the danger from back then could return with the
evening. Nighttime has become especially hard for him. If he wakes in the dark, or if the room is
quiet and dim, he feels less safe than in daylight and starts scanning the room, his attention
pulled toward every shadow and movement. His fear also flares when someone mentions snakes or shows
a photograph, because snakes were part of what made that captivity feel so terrifying and
uncontrollable to him, something alive and hidden that seemed to turn the whole experience into a
trap.

In the months and years after the event, Rashid began arranging his life around those moments of
fear. He has come to dread going to sleep or even lying down in the dark, because once he is in bed
his mind starts moving toward the old scene and he feels unable to settle. To keep himself from
being overwhelmed, he asks his wife, and sometimes his adult son, again and again to reassure him
that he is okay. He also checks his body constantly, running his hands over his arms, chest, and
legs for any sign of danger or injury, as if proof that he is still intact might keep something bad
from happening. Yet these efforts bring their own costs. The more he avoids lying in the dark and
the more he relies on others to calm him, the more convinced he becomes that he cannot face the
memory on his own. He has started to believe that if he thinks about what happened, he will not be
able to handle it, and from that belief it is only a short step to the bleak thought that he cannot
stop bad things from happening to him and that his life may not be worth living anymore.

That sense of helplessness and fragility now colors how he moves through the day. At the warehouse,
where he spends long shifts among stacked pallets and narrow aisles, he is superalert and watchful,
constantly scanning for anything that might signal trouble. He frequently looks behind him,
especially when he hears footsteps in the aisle or a cart rolling past, because he feels certain
that danger could be closing in without warning. The evening shift is worst: as the light outside
fades and the building grows quieter, he becomes even more guarded, and by the time he gets home he
is already braced for the night. His fear of darkness has made ordinary routines difficult. He
delays going to bed, sits up with the television on, and keeps the bedroom light burning longer than
he wants to admit, because lying there in the dark brings the captivity back in vivid fragments and
makes him feel exposed. The same avoidance that gives him temporary relief also keeps those triggers
close to the surface. The more he fights sleep and monitors his body for signs of threat, the more
keyed up he feels when the clock reaches that same hour again, or when a sudden dark room or a
mention of snakes catches him off guard, and the cycle leaves him feeling less and less safe.

By the time he sought help, Rashid was exhausted from living in this narrowed, guarded way. His wife
had begun sleeping separately because of his restlessness and repeated questions for reassurance,
and he worried that he was becoming a burden at home and less reliable at work. What brought him in
was not only the fear that he would keep losing sleep, but the deeper dread that his life was
shrinking around the memory of captivity and that he might never feel truly safe again. He said he
wanted help because he was tired of living as if danger were always just behind him, and because
despite everything he still hoped he might sleep through the night without fear.
