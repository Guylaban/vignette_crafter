her students, when another car skidded through the intersection and struck her side of the vehicle
with enough force to spin the whole car across two lanes. She remembered the violent lurch of her
seatbelt cutting into her shoulder, the shattering glass, and the sound of metal folding in on
itself; after that, the event seemed to break apart. Certain moments stayed painfully bright—her own
hands locked white on the wheel, the smell of coolant and burnt rubber, a glimpse of the other car’s
crumpled hood—while other stretches were blank, as if someone had lifted them away. When she has
tried to explain the crash since then, the story comes out in fragments and then stops; she loses
the thread, goes quiet, and feels embarrassed that she cannot make the sequence make sense. That
inability to hold the memory together has fed her sense that something in her life has been
permanently damaged. Malia had once described herself as a happy person, but now she says she is
always miserable, that nothing feels meaningful anymore, and that she no longer belongs anywhere,
not even in the classrooms and faculty meetings that once made her feel useful. Those beliefs have
left her waiting for danger and scanning for signs that she is somehow outside the world other
people still move through safely.

In the weeks after the collision, Malia became intensely alert to anything that reminded her of the
crash. Talk about accidents in the staff lounge, a student mentioning a pileup on the highway, even
a passing remark about insurance claims could make her feel as if the room had tilted. She also
found herself caught by images of cars hit by accident in news footage or on social media, and she
would look away too late, already flooded with the same sick jolt she felt at the scene. At times
the world went strangely distant; she would begin to feel detached or unreal, as if she were
watching herself from a few feet away rather than standing in her own kitchen or office. Those
moments frightened her because they seemed to confirm what she already feared: that she was no
longer fully herself. The more she believed she had lost happiness, meaning, and belonging, the more
she watched for signs that the world was about to split open again, and the more ordinary
reminders—conversation, images, even a sudden silence on the road—became potent enough to pull her
back into the worst afternoon of her life.

That fear has kept Malia living as if another collision could happen at any moment. She wakes during
the night to check whether she is safe, often sitting up before dawn and listening for traffic
outside the bedroom window, and during the day she is superalert, watching intersections, rearview
mirrors, and the speed of cars in a way that leaves her tense and exhausted. On busier mornings she
crosses the campus parking lot with her shoulders rigid, scanning for backing vehicles, and she
cannot relax even in her office because any abrupt noise can bring a rush of panic. At times the
danger feels immediate rather than remembered: she can suddenly feel or act as if the stressful
experience were actually happening again, gripping the desk, staring ahead, and struggling to orient
herself. The more her body stays on guard, the harder it becomes for her to think clearly about the
accident, and the memory returns in those same disconnected pieces, sharper in some places and blank
in others, which only deepens her conviction that she has been fundamentally altered. Malia says the
emptiness and disconnection make her think she is not who she used to be, and that there is no
meaningful future left to inhabit.

Over time she stopped taking the longer route that passed the intersection where the crash occurred
and began asking her husband to drive whenever possible, especially in rain. She has also become
careful about what she watches or discusses, changing the subject when colleagues talk about road
incidents and avoiding news clips that show wrecked vehicles. These habits have narrowed her life in
practical ways: she leaves campus earlier than she needs to, turns down social plans that would

departmental meeting because a brief discussion of a freeway crash made her go blank and tremulous.
She came hoping to feel like herself again, though what she most fears is that the crash has already
made that impossible.
