toward emergencies rather than away from them. After her husband’s death, she kept working and tried
to hold her routines together, but the accident that changed things for her happened on an ordinary
afternoon drive home from base. A car ahead of her was struck hard in an intersection, metal folding
in on itself with a brutal suddenness, and for a moment she was back in the confusion of sirens,
impact, and urgent movement. She was close enough to see the damage, close enough to smell the
sharpness of airbag dust and burned rubber, and that scene lodged in her mind with such force that
later she could not describe it cleanly; she knew what had happened, but when she tried to tell it,
the words would not come. Even thinking about it brought a wave of feeling so intense that she had
to stop, and when the memory surfaced it did not feel like something in the past so much as
something happening again, right there in front of her. At times, when a memory came unbidden, she
found herself suddenly acting as if the crash were unfolding all over again.

In the weeks that followed, Marianne noticed how easily her attention snapped toward anything that
seemed to resemble that day. A similar car model in a parking lot, the sight of a crumpled bumper,
even an uncomfortable feeling in her own body could set her on edge. Unexpected or loud sounds made
her hyperaware in an instant, and her body stayed braced, tense, as though it were waiting for the
next impact. The more those reminders appeared, the more her mind and body seemed to prepare for
danger, and she began to experience moments of sudden fear and alarm that felt startlingly
immediate. What she hated most was how ordinary things could hook her back into that state. She came
to dread driving past intersections, and she scanned traffic with a vigilance that left her
exhausted. When a memory of the crash broke through, the alarm in her body intensified; and when her
body was already wound tight, even a minor sound in the environment could make her jump and believe
something bad was about to happen.

Marianne tried to manage this by not letting herself think about it. She pushed the event away,
distracted herself with television or chores, and often ate excessively in the evening to blunt the
distress that built up during the day. For a while, these habits gave her a sense of control, but
they also kept her from settling the experience in any way that might have made it less raw. Because
she worked long shifts and moved through the ward and parking lot on alert, she often ate in private
to get through the tension, then returned to work feeling unsettled and vaguely ashamed. The more
she worked to suppress the memory, the more forcefully it seemed to return, and the more overwhelmed
she became when it did. In turn, those bursts of alarm made her even more likely to seek
distraction, to eat past the point of comfort, and to avoid letting herself sit quietly with what
had happened. That pattern had an unintended cost: the very things she used to steady herself kept
the accident feeling close, and each new reminder in daily life seemed to find her already braced
and ready to panic.

By the time she sought help, Marianne had become wary of simple discomforts in her own body, of loud

unexpected memory could pull her back into the wreck. What frightened her most was the fear that
