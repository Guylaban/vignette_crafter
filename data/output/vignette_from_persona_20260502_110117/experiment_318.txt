upright, smiling when directed, and making her body look effortless. But that poise had shattered on

to require emergency care, and Marilyn has carried the scene ever since as if it were stamped into
her mind. What has haunted her most is not only the sound and the sight of it, but the conviction
that she should have prevented it. She has come to believe that somebody else would not have gotten
into that situation, that she should have seen it sooner, stopped sooner, been more careful, more
alert, more deserving of ordinary mercy than she was.

home, in the quiet of the condo she shares with memories of her husband, she felt unable to relax,
as though her muscles were waiting for a blow. Her shoulders stayed tight, her jaw clenched without

constant tension made it hard for her to settle into a book, follow a conversation, or keep track of

herself as much as she watched the road, and that state of being on alert kept returning the
accident to the center of her mind.

As the weeks passed, the accident came to feel less like something that had happened and more like
evidence against her character. Because her body remained so keyed up, she found herself scanning
for signs of danger in parking lots, at crosswalks, and anywhere she had to move around other
people. Each jolt of tension seemed to confirm her own harsh conclusions: she was permanently
humiliated by what happened, exposed as careless in a way no one should see. Marilyn said the shame
felt public even when she was alone, as if the woman she struck, and anyone who heard about it,

down, the more certain she became that the accident said something permanent and unforgivable about
her.

refused a few errands she used to enjoy, and she finds herself preoccupied during appointments, her

together until she now lives as though she must stay on guard against both another catastrophe and

her find a way to remember what happened without feeling condemned by it.
