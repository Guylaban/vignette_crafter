Tane, a 54-year-old Māori combat pilot, came for help after nearly a year of living as if danger
were always one step away. The event that set everything in motion had happened during a training
sortie over open water: a sudden mechanical failure, the aircraft shuddering hard enough to rattle
his teeth, warning lights blooming across the cockpit, and the long, terrifying descent toward a
strip of sea he had no intention of meeting. He remembered the sound of the engine changing pitch,
the radio crackling with clipped instructions, and the ugly certainty that if he misjudged the glide
or the ditching angle by even a few seconds, he would not make it out. He survived, but the body he
returned in seemed to him to be only partially the same one that had climbed into the cockpit that
day.

In the weeks after the accident, Tane found himself repeatedly circling the same conclusions. He
told his wife that he would never be able to feel normal emotions again, that something in him had
gone flat and wrong since the crash. He described feeling as though he did not know himself anymore,
as if the calm, competent man he had always understood himself to be had been permanently altered in
that cockpit. More than once he said, with a grim sort of certainty, that he had been permanently
changed for the worse. Those beliefs did not stay abstract; they made the world feel newly
hazardous. If he could no longer trust his own mind or body, then he could not trust the ordinary
spaces of life either, and he began to move through them as though they might fail him at any
moment.

At home in Auckland, where he and his wife had once treated the house as a place to decompress
between deployments, Tane became preoccupied with exits, windows, and doorways. Before settling on
the couch, he would scan the room and quietly identify the fastest route out. In restaurants he
chose seats with his back to a wall and eyes on the entrance, and even in familiar places he stayed
on guard until he had mapped the nearest escape route. He found that he felt most uneasy in enclosed
spaces where he could not immediately see how to get out, and that unease followed him into daily
life. He was on watch even inside his own home, listening for sounds he could not explain and
checking the locks more than once, as if vigilance alone might prevent catastrophe. His wife noticed
that he no longer relaxed into an evening meal or a shared television show; part of him seemed
always to be somewhere ahead of the moment, calculating how to get clear if something went wrong.

As the months passed, the same harsh conclusions kept feeding that watchfulness. Because he believed
he had lost access to normal feeling and that the accident had permanently damaged him, he treated
his surroundings as if they were unreliable too. The more he feared that something in him had
changed for good, the more determined he became to stay alert for any sign of trouble, and the more
he scanned for danger in everyday settings. In the cockpit he had once relied on rapid assessment
and control; now he brought that same intensity home, unable to let himself settle. The habit spread
outward: at the supermarket he stood near the exit, in the garage he checked what path he would take
if he needed to leave quickly, and on the street he watched other people and moving vehicles with a
tension that never fully eased. What had begun as a reaction to one emergency became a way of living
that left little room for rest.

Tane’s work sharpened the problem. As a combat pilot, he returned to an environment full of alarms,
procedural checks, briefing rooms, and tight spaces that could suddenly feel like the beginning of
another failure. Even when nothing was wrong, he remained braced, vigilant for cues that might
signal danger, and that stance made it harder for him to concentrate or feel present with his
squadron. He became quieter with colleagues and more withdrawn with his wife, not because he wanted
distance, but because he feared that if he let his guard drop he would miss the first sign that
something was about to go wrong. By the time he finally agreed to therapy, he was exhausted by the
effort of staying ready all the time and frightened by how much of his life had been reorganized
around escape routes and constant surveillance. What he most hoped for was to feel like himself
again, and what he feared most was that he had already lost that version of himself for good.
