Miguel had spent most of his working life in community organizing, the kind of job that kept him in
church basements, union halls, and housing meetings across his city, speaking on behalf of people
who rarely felt heard. At 63, divorced and living alone, he had recently found that the very
settings where he used to feel useful were becoming harder to tolerate. The accident that brought
him to treatment had happened years earlier, on a wet evening when a vehicle struck him hard enough
to leave him pinned and half-conscious, with police and paramedics crowding around him in dark
uniforms, their voices clipped and urgent. Even now he could remember certain flashes with painful
sharpness — the gleam of wet pavement, the pressure in his chest, a uniform sleeve near his face —
while other parts were blank no matter how hard he tried to reach them. He knew the event had been
life-threatening, but when he tried to describe it, the words would not come in any orderly way; he
would stop, shake his head, and say that he knew what happened without being able to put it into
language. There were whole stretches of the scene he simply could not retrieve, and that missingness
unsettled him almost as much as the fragments he could see.

Over time, Miguel came to believe that the accident had happened because of the way he acted, that
some wrong choice or hesitation of his own had put him there. He carried that conviction with a
flat, almost defeated heaviness, saying he felt dead inside, as if the part of him that used to care
and connect had gone missing on that street. He also noticed something more frightening: his body
kept reacting as if the danger were still present, even when he was safe, and that reaction seemed
to prove to him that he had not really recovered. Because he saw his own body as betraying him, the
jagged memories felt even less trustworthy, and when he tried to explain the accident to others he
would get stuck, unable to find words, which only deepened his sense that he had somehow failed to
make sense of what had happened. The more he returned to those gaps and fragments, the more
convinced he became that he had to blame himself, and the more he withdrew into a muted, numb state
that felt like the only way to hold himself together.

That self-blame also changed what he noticed in the world. Miguel became especially alert to other
people expressing anger, even when their voices were not truly raised. In meetings, a mild criticism
from a colleague or a sharp look from a tenant could hit him like a threat, and his mind would jump
back to the accident and to the thought that he had somehow brought danger on himself. A man in a
police-style jacket passing the office door could stop him cold. Those sights and tones did not
remain isolated impressions; they fed straight back into his sense that his body was still on edge
for a reason, and that he must be the sort of person who ended up in danger because of his own
actions. After those moments, he would pull away from the conversation, go quiet, and later find
himself once again circling the event in pieces, trying to account for what he had done and why he
could not remember enough to explain it properly. What frightened him most was that the clearer
flashes — the uniforms, the urgency, the feeling of being trapped — made the blank sections feel
ominous, as if there were something in the missing pieces that confirmed his fault.

In the months that followed, Miguel started arranging his life around these reactions. He avoided
crowded municipal buildings when he could, and in his organizing work he began sending others to
meetings he once would have led himself. When tensions rose, he kept his distance from anyone he
anticipated might sound angry, telling himself he was merely trying to stay professional. But the
more he stepped back, the more isolated he felt, and the more often he returned to the same deadened
conclusion that he was broken in a way no one else could see. He had finally sought help after
leaving two meetings in a row because a calm but irritated tone in the room made his chest tighten
and his hands tremble, leaving him ashamed and worried that he was becoming unable to do the work
that had given his life structure. He came to therapy hoping to understand why his body still
behaved as if the accident were happening now, and fearing that if he could not make sense of the
missing parts and the blame he carried, he would keep shrinking away from the life he had spent
decades building.
