neighborhood in Atlanta changed in an instant: shouting in the hallway, a heavy pounding at the

stepped into the hall he saw officers with hands on their weapons, their flashlights sweeping across

him was the helplessness of being treated as dangerous without warning. Afterward, whenever a siren
rose outside their building or he heard sharp knocking in the hallway, his body reacted before his
mind could catch up. He would jerk in his chair, feel his chest tighten, and scan the room or the
street with an alertness that left him exhausted by the end of the day. At work, where he designed
lesson plans in a quiet office and often reviewed materials late into the evening, even a coworker
appearing silently at his cubicle could make him flinch hard enough to spill coffee across his
papers.

Over the following months, Amadou became increasingly watchful in ways that seemed reasonable to
him, almost necessary. He checked who was outside before opening the apartment door, listened for
footsteps in the stairwell, and sat with his back to the wall in restaurants. He had come to believe
that danger could arrive without pattern, and that if he relaxed even briefly he might be caught off
guard again. That constant alertness fed into the way the memory returned. When the recollection
surfaced, it did not come to him as a full story he could tell from beginning to end. More often it
arrived as a wave of unease in his chest, a hot surge of dread with no clear picture attached, and
he would have to stop whatever he was doing. If his wife asked what he was thinking about, he would
shake his head because he could not seem to put the event into words. He knew it happened, and he
knew what it felt like, but the harder he tried to explain it, the more the details slipped away. At
times the emotional force came first, arriving without an image at all, as though his body
remembered what his mind could not organize.

certain something bad was about to happen. He stopped taking the train home after dark because every
uniformed figure on the platform seemed to sharpen the old fear. The less he spoke about the
incident, the more it seemed to inhabit him in fragments, appearing as a rush of feeling or a bodily
startle rather than a narrative. When he tried to recount what happened to his wife, he could get
only as far as “they had me against the wall,” and then he would go silent, overwhelmed by the force
of what came back. She noticed that he was sleeping lightly, rising at every sound from the street,
and that he had grown irritable and tired, but he mostly explained it as staying careful. Careful,
for Amadou, meant being superalert, scanning the room in his office, at home, and even while driving
past flashing lights on the highway.

hard, unable to speak for several minutes. He was frightened by how quickly his body took over and

stop feeling as if he had to watch every corner of every room, and he feared that if this continued
he would become a stranger to his own home and to the work he had built his life around.
