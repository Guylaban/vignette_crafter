Malia, a 21-year-old Pacific Islander woman working as a nurse, came to treatment after months of
feeling as though the war zone she had survived had never really ended. During a deployment abroad,
she had been caught in repeated attacks in which sirens wailed overhead, explosives and gunfire
shattered the night, and she had to keep moving while treating the wounded under impossible
conditions. What stayed with her most was not just the danger itself but the raw force of what she
remembered afterward: when the images came back, she felt a surge of rage so intense that she had to
stop whatever she was doing, and at times the memory was so emotionally overwhelming that she could
not keep talking. She knew exactly what had happened, but when anyone asked her to describe it, she
would lose the words, as if the experience lived in her body more than in language. Even now, the
sound of sirens outside the hospital or a sudden loud bang in the hallway could pull her straight
back into that night, and she would notice her own heart pounding before she could think clearly.

Back home, Malia found herself responding to ordinary life as if danger were always about to break
through. She was constantly scanning her surroundings for signs of threat, watching doorways, exits,
and the expressions of strangers with a vigilance that left her exhausted. She became irritable with
coworkers and at home with her partner, sometimes snapping sharply when someone came up behind her
or when the unit became noisy, and she hated how quickly anger rose in her. At moments, she would
suddenly feel and act as if the stressful experience were happening again, stiffening, staring, or
moving with a frantic urgency that startled the people around her. Those reactions made sense to her
because the memory itself carried such force: once it surfaced, the rage and overwhelm flooded back,
and the feeling that she could not put it into words left her even more unsettled, as though there
was no safe way to contain it.

Because sirens, loud noises, and the pounding of her own heart were so tightly linked to those
memories, Malia began reshaping her life around the hope of not being caught off guard. She avoided
exercise because raising her heart rate made her think of alarm, and she would stop short of
anything that made her pulse race. In her job, the hospital’s code alarms and ambulance sirens were
unavoidable, so she often positioned herself near quieter corridors and lingered at the edges of the
unit, checking the environment over and over instead of settling into a task. That constant scanning
kept her on edge and, in turn, fed the same irritability and angry bursts she hated in herself; she
felt as if she had to stay hard and ready or she would be blindsided. Her attention to threat also
made the memories come more easily, especially when a siren passed outside the building or a monitor
beeped sharply, and each return of the memory renewed the sense that danger was imminent.

Over time, Malia also began avoiding situations that might make her feel happy or hopeful. She had
once looked forward to going out with her partner after shifts, but she started turning down
invitations, telling herself it was easier not to let her guard down. The fewer moments of relief
she allowed herself, the more tightly she watched for danger, and the less she could settle after
work. Even brief attempts to relax could be interrupted by an intrusive recollection that left her
overwhelmed and unable to speak, and the frustration of that silence often spilled into more
irritability. In the last few weeks, a siren outside the emergency department and the jolt of her
own racing heart during an attempted workout both sent her mind back to the deployment in a way that
felt immediate and terrifying. What finally brought Malia in was not only the severity of her
symptoms, reflected in how high her distress had become, but the fear that she was becoming a
different person at home and at work. She said she wanted relief from the constant scanning and the
sudden flashes of rage, and she was most afraid that if she kept living this way, she would lose her
relationship and her ability to feel safe anywhere again.
