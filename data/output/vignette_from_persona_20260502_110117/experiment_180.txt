Daniel Varga had been a graphic designer in a downtown agency for six years, the kind of work that
kept him in front of glowing screens late into the evening and sent him home after dark. Before the
trauma, he had liked that quiet commute, the city thinning out around him as the office emptied.
Afterward, night carried a different meaning. The event had happened during a routine stop: he was
pulled over on a dim side street by officers who had mistaken him for someone else, ordered out of
the car, and handcuffed while one of them shouted questions he could barely process. He remembered
the hard beam of a flashlight in his eyes, the metallic flash of a weapon, the uniforms crowding
close, and the panicked certainty that he was about to be hurt for something he had not done. When
the mistake was finally sorted out, he was left shaking, humiliated, and unable to make sense of how
quickly ordinary authority had become something dangerous. Even now, he would say the worst part was
not only what happened, but how helpless he had felt while men in uniforms stood over him and his
face was exposed to them.

In the months that followed, Daniel found himself scanning the world for anything that resembled
that night. Waking in the dark was especially bad; if he opened his eyes and could not immediately
orient himself, his body would jolt as if the room itself might turn hostile. Similar cars on the
street could seize his attention so completely that he would stop walking and watch until they
passed, half convinced the same kind of stop could happen again. Crowded places carried their own
weight, because there was nowhere to step back, nowhere to keep a clear view of who was near him. In
those moments his thoughts narrowed around one simple conclusion: if he let himself be caught
unaware again, he would not be able to protect himself. He had come to believe that safety depended
on keeping an edge of control, even if it cost him comfort.

That belief shaped the habits that grew around the fear. Daniel began drinking more heavily at
night, and on some evenings he would take medication or use drugs to blunt the rising dread before
sleep. He said it was easier to dull himself than to lie awake listening for imagined footsteps or
sirens, easier to numb his body than to feel it surge at the sight of a police uniform in a lobby or
a weapon on a passerby. He also started covering his face with his hands when panic spiked in
public, a brief, embarrassed gesture that gave him the illusion of shrinking out of view. These
strategies helped him get through the worst moments, but they also made his days smaller. He started
declining invitations to crowded galleries and after-work gatherings that had once been part of his
social world. At the office, he asked to leave before dark whenever he could, and when deadlines
kept him late he became distracted, watching reflections in glass doors and the parking lot outside
rather than his designs.

The more he reorganized his life around avoiding reminders, the more certain he became that he had
to stay vigilant. A patrol car outside his building could ruin an entire afternoon; a cluster of
people at the train platform could send him to the restroom until the crowd thinned. When he had
slept badly, the next night was worse, because waking in darkness made him brace before he was fully
conscious, and that bracing made him want relief even faster. In that state, alcohol or whatever
pills were available felt less like a choice than a necessary escape. He knew the pattern was
costing him, especially after his divorce, when his ex-wife told him she no longer recognized how
distant and guarded he had become. Still, he could not shake the conviction that his fear was
realistic and that lowering his guard would invite disaster.

By the time he came to treatment, Daniel was exhausted by the life he had arranged around threat. He
was no longer only afraid of police uniforms or similar cars; he was afraid of what his own body did
when it was reminded of them, and of the way nighttime seemed to strip him of any certainty that he
was safe. He sought help after another sleepless week, when he drank to get through the evenings,
covered his face in a crowded store until his hands ached, and realized he was building his days
around keeping panic at bay rather than living them. What he most hoped for was to sleep without
bracing for danger and to walk through the city again without checking every car and every uniform,
though what frightened him most was the possibility that, sober and unguarded, the terror would rush
back just as hard as it had on that dark street.
