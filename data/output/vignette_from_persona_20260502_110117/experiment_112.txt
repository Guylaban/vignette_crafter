Arjun was 22, single, and worked as a UX designer in a glass-walled office downtown, where most days
meant long stretches at his laptop, headphones on, neck bent toward wireframes and user flows. The
hurricane had struck his coastal hometown the year before, late at night, after the power had
already gone out. He had been sheltering with his family in a cramped interior hallway when the wind
began to roar like something alive, and water forced itself under the doors in a cold brown sheet.
By morning, the house smelled of mud, salt, and gas, and he remembered wading through debris with
his neck strained upward, trying to keep his breathing steady while carrying what he could. Since
then, any tightening in his neck could pull him back with startling force. When the pain flared
after hours at work, or when he felt vaguely unwell and shaky in the middle of the day, the
sensation did not stay ordinary for long; it slipped into the disaster memory, and suddenly he could
taste the brackish, metallic flavor he associated with that night, as if it were in his mouth again.
Sometimes, before any picture came, he was hit by the full heaviness of panic and dread from the
storm, a raw emotional surge without a clear image, and it left him stunned at his desk.

As those episodes repeated, Arjun came to dread what they seemed to mean about people around him. He
had been helping neighbors before the water rose, and he still remembered how quickly some of them
had disappeared once evacuation became urgent, leaving doors open and promises unfinished. In his
mind, that night had shown him that people were not what they seemed, and that sense of betrayal
colored almost everything afterward. When a coworker spoke warmly about “team trust” in a meeting,
Arjun found himself watching for the slight shift in tone, the hidden exit, the moment a person
might turn and leave him to handle things alone. He felt isolated and set apart from his roommates,
from his colleagues, even from other young men in the city who seemed to move through life without
constantly measuring danger in every exchange. More and more, he believed he no longer belonged
anywhere, because home had become a place that could vanish and other people could not be counted on
to keep it intact.

Those beliefs made the memory itself harder to hold as a simple past event. The flood now felt so
extreme and unreal that he sometimes had to stop and remind himself it had happened to him at all,
as if he had watched it in a film rather than lived through it. Yet the strangeness of that
disbelief did not quiet him; it made him monitor his body and surroundings even more closely,
waiting for the next sign. A sore neck after a day hunched over design software, the brief wave of
nausea when he skipped lunch, the smell of damp concrete in the office stairwell, or a news alert
about storms on his phone could all become potent reminders. And once those reminders arrived, they
did not just summon the memory; they confirmed his suspicion that something in the world was always
preparing to break. He had become wary of strangers and cautious even with familiar people, assuming
there was more hidden under the surface than they showed. That suspicion fed the next wave of alarm,
because if people could not be trusted, then any ordinary interaction—a colleague offering help, a
friend asking why he was quiet—could feel like the start of another abandonment.

Over months, Arjun began arranging his life to avoid whatever might bring that sequence back. In the
office he adjusted his chair again and again to keep pressure off his neck, and when the sensation
persisted he would invent reasons to step outside, gulping air on the sidewalk until the taste in
his mouth faded. He kept to himself at lunch, telling others he was busy with deadlines, and he
declined invitations that required travel or even weather-related planning. The more he withdrew,
the more detached he felt, and the more certain he became that he was on the outside of things
looking in. What brought him to treatment was that the loop was tightening: an ordinary neck ache
could now derail an entire afternoon, leave him tasting the floodwater again, and drive him back
into the conviction that he had been left behind in a world where no one could truly be trusted. He
sought help because he was frightened that he was becoming permanently cut off from other people,
and because part of him still hoped that if he could understand why his body kept dragging him back
there, he might find a way to belong somewhere again.
