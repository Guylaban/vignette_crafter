seen police vehicles flooding the intersection, officers shouting for people to move back. The
violence was brief but unmistakable: broken glass, screaming, smoke, and the sense that the city had
become dangerous in an instant. Since then, the helicopter sound that once blended into the
background now made her stomach clench, and the sight of police uniforms or any public display of
violence could catch her off guard in a way that felt immediate and bodily. Those moments did not
just startle her; they pushed her into a harsh judgment about what had happened to her and what it
had done to her identity. She found herself thinking that she used to be a happy person, but now she
was always miserable, as if the attack had taken away a part of her that could not be recovered.

In the months that followed, Mona began to feel that she did not know herself anymore. Before the
bombing, she had seen herself as composed, efficient, and good at keeping her family and work life
steady. Now, when she walked into a building and noticed security or heard a helicopter passing, she
felt an abrupt drop inside herself and then the thought that she was no longer the woman she had
been. She would look in the mirror and feel detached from the face looking back, as though the
frightened expression had become permanent. What disturbed her most was the sense that other people
could see what happened to her just by looking at her. She became self-conscious in meetings,
convinced colleagues could tell she was damaged, and she would measure her own face for signs of
fear before stepping out of the car or greeting a client. The more police presence, violence in the
news, or aircraft noise appeared around her, the more those appraisals hardened: each cue seemed to
confirm that the attack was not over in any meaningful sense, because it had left visible marks on
who she was and how others would read her.

At work, Mona tried to carry on as a project manager, but the environment itself kept placing her
near reminders she could not easily avoid. She worked in a downtown office where police cars were a
familiar sight outside the building, and once, during a routine emergency drill, the sound of a
helicopter passing overhead sent her pulse racing and left her distracted for the rest of the
afternoon. After that she started planning routes, timing errands to avoid crowded streets, and
declining lunches or after-work gatherings when the topic turned to news footage or public violence.
The effort to keep herself together took so much attention that her confidence at work began to
fray. She worried not only about being seen as fragile, but about confirming that she was in fact no
longer herself. When she noticed a police officer in the lobby or heard rotor blades overhead, she
would tense and immediately think of how miserable she had become, as if the cue itself were proof
that happiness was something she had lost for good.

Over time, these reactions made the world feel smaller and less familiar. Mona described moving
through her days with a constant sense that her true self had been displaced, leaving behind someone
watchful, drained, and ashamed. She did not say the attack had ruined everything, but she spoke with
a quiet certainty that it had made her visible in the wrong way, as if her distress were written
across her face for strangers and coworkers to read. That belief made her withdraw further, and the
withdrawal made her feel more estranged from herself. By the time she came to therapy, she was still
functioning on the surface, yet she felt trapped in the knowledge that helicopters, police, and
violence could bring the whole experience rushing back into her body and into the conviction that
she was no longer the happy person she had once been. What finally brought her in was the fear that
this sense of being altered and exposed was becoming permanent, and her hope was that she might
learn how to feel like herself again without bracing for every siren, uniform, or passing aircraft.
