Margaret, an 80-year-old European American woman who had worked for decades as a social worker, came
to treatment after years of living with the aftereffects of a violent relationship that she still
described with a kind of stunned disbelief. When she was younger, she had been involved with a man
she trusted at first, a man who became physically abusive in private and whose temper seemed to
erupt without warning. She could still picture it in fractured pieces: the hallway, a hand gripping
her arm, a door slamming hard enough to make her jump, the awful sense of being trapped alone with
him. Even now, she said, the memory would not settle into a proper sequence; it came back as a few
stark moments, each one like a photograph she could not arrange into a whole story. Looking back,
what overwhelmed her most was grief, not only for what she had endured but for the person she had
been before it happened.

In the years that followed, certain moments and places became charged with danger for her. If she
found herself alone with a man, if a relative or other known man entered the room, or if she heard a
door open or slam, her body reacted before she could think. The sound of a door in her apartment
building or at the grocery store could jolt her into the past, and the sight of a man standing too
close could make her stomach tighten and send her scanning for the nearest exit. Those triggers
seemed to pull the old fragments of memory into the present, and with them came the conviction that
the world had changed beyond repair. She had come to believe that people could not be trusted, that
the assumptions she once lived by had been destroyed, and that what happened to her had left her
somehow dirty, contaminated, and set apart from other people. The more she remembered, the more she
felt those beliefs harden. The more she felt trapped by the image of that frightened woman in the
past, the more any man, any familiar face linked to family or acquaintanceship, or any sudden sound
of a door could seem ominous and personally aimed at her.

Margaret tried to manage this in ways that made sense to her at the time. She began avoiding the
parts of the day and the situations she knew might bring nightmares, staying busy late into the
evening or going to bed at odd hours in an effort to outrun them. She also tried to erase the memory
from her mind, pushing away the scenes that arrived in those picture-like flashes, though the harder
she tried, the more they seemed to press back. She spent long stretches thinking about why the event
had happened to her, searching for some explanation that might restore order, but the questions only
seemed to deepen her sense that the world was fundamentally unsafe and that she could not trust
anyone not to harm her. Her efforts to blank out the past and make sense of it also kept the
memories near the surface; whenever they surfaced, they brought fresh waves of mistrust and shame,
and those beliefs in turn made her more likely to avoid sleep, social contact, and any setting where
men might be present.

By the time she sought help, her daily life had narrowed considerably. As a retired social worker,
Margaret still lived independently, but even ordinary routines had become organized around
vigilance. She entered buildings with her eyes on the exits, noting who was nearby and where she
might move quickly if she needed to leave. She described herself as superalert and watchful, never
fully settling, and her sleep was broken by difficulty falling asleep and by waking in the night.
The nights felt especially precarious because she feared the dreams that seemed to gather in certain
hours, and the lack of sleep made her even more on guard the next day. That constant state of
alertness also made her more susceptible to seeing danger in the presence of men or hearing a door
as a warning, and each new alarm pushed her back toward avoiding people, erasing memories, or trying
again to figure out why it had happened. In her mind, those attempts were necessary protections, yet
they also kept confirming that the world was unsafe, that she remained contaminated by what was done
to her, and that her life was still organized around the original violence.

She finally came for treatment because she was exhausted by the cycle and frightened by how much
smaller her world had become. What she most wanted was to sleep without dread and to feel less
haunted by the certainty that she could never fully trust another person again.
