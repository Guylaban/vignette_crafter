after months of feeling as though ordinary life had narrowed around one violent relationship. The

door that made her ears ring. Afterward, even small reminders seemed to carry the force of that
night. A sudden bang in a client’s home, a heavy cabinet door in the studio, or being left alone in
a room with a man she did not know well could make her body brace before she had time to think. She
began arranging site visits so a coworker would go with her, avoiding dark stairwells, empty parking
areas, and any circumstance that felt like being trapped in the same helplessness. She said that
when she was near men she did not know, she watched their hands and shoulders, and if she ever heard
loud noises she felt her whole body spring into readiness, as if danger were about to strike again.

What stayed most alive for Lani was not a neat story she could tell, but fragments that came with
missing pieces. When she tried to explain what had happened, she would lose the thread partway
through, staring down and starting over, unable to finish. At other times, remembering the abuse
brought a wave of guilt so intense that she felt she deserved it for not leaving sooner. Sometimes
the memory arrived differently, as a sudden rush of terror and shame with no clear image attached,
only the bodily certainty that she was back in danger. Those moments made her scan the room
immediately, and she often found herself looking behind her, checking doorways and reflections to
see whether someone was following her. The memory of the assault also fed the meanings she had begun
to attach to her life: she had come to believe her life had been destroyed by what happened, that
she no longer belonged anywhere, and that people could not be trusted. Those beliefs made every
startled glance and guarded explanation feel reasonable to her, because if others were unsafe and
her own life had already been ruined, she could not afford to be careless.

was the only way to avoid being hurt again.
