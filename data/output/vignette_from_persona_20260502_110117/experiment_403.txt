Lina, a 45-year-old Middle Eastern woman who worked as an auditor, described the trauma as something
that still arrived in pieces rather than as a single whole. During a combat deployment years ago,
she had been in a convoy when an explosion tore through the roadway ahead of them. She remembered
the sudden bang, the sharp chemical smell that clung to the air, and then the frantic movement of
people shouting, ducking, and trying to find cover. What remained of the event in her mind was
fragmented: a flash of bright metal, a hand reaching, the sound of someone calling a name, and then
long stretches she could not recover no matter how hard she tried. She felt ashamed that she could
not assemble the scene into a complete picture. The missing parts made her certain that she must
have failed others in some crucial way, and over time she came to believe that the event had
happened to her because of the sort of person she was, someone not vigilant enough, not capable
enough, not the kind of person who could protect those around her. She would say that even now her
body kept reacting as if danger were still present, with her heart lurching and her muscles bracing
before she had time to think.

In the months after the deployment, Lina noticed that certain moments would pull her straight back
toward that unfinished memory. Waking from a nightmare left her disoriented and immediately on
guard, and if she caught a whiff of a man’s cologne in an elevator or the hall of her office
building, she would freeze and scan for who was nearby. The same happened with any sharp bang on the
street or a door slamming in the office, where the sound could send her mind toward the convoy and
the explosion before she could stop it. These reminders did not just unsettle her; they made the
missing parts of the event feel even more glaring, as though the memory itself were refusing to give
her the truth. In those moments she would fall into replaying the scene in her head, asking how life
would have been different if the explosion had not happened, and whether she should have done
something to prevent the deaths and injuries she believed were hers to explain. The more she tried
to reason it through, the more she became convinced that the nightmare, the smell, and the loud
sounds were proof that her body still knew something she did not, and that she must be inherently
flawed for still reacting this way when everyone else had moved on.

As her self-blame deepened, Lina’s sense of where she could be safe narrowed dramatically. She came
to feel that only a very limited number of places were truly secure, and even there she insisted on
sitting where she could see the exits. In restaurants and at work she always identified escape
routes before she allowed herself to settle, and in open or exposed spaces she felt unable to
breathe comfortably, as though vulnerability itself were a threat. She frequently looked behind her
to check whether someone might be following, and at the office she chose a desk position that let
her keep the room in view. Her work as an auditor, which had once suited her carefulness, became
exhausting because every corridor, parking lot, and conference room demanded a fresh assessment of
danger. The more she scanned, the more danger she seemed to find, and the more danger she found, the
more convinced she became that her body was correct to stay activated. At the same time, her
constant checking left her less able to sit with her memories; the more she tried to keep track of
every door, stranger, and noise, the less stable the traumatic scene felt in her mind, breaking into
still smaller fragments that she could not put back together.

Over the years Lina built habits meant to keep herself from being caught off guard, but those habits
gradually sharpened both her fear and her shame. She avoided open spaces, took longer routes between
buildings, and planned her days around places she believed she could control. Yet even in the
safest-feeling locations, the next perfume in a hallway or the next unexpected bang could restart
the whole process, leaving her tense, hyperalert, and certain that she had to keep searching. The
very act of checking her surroundings made her feel that danger was all around her, and every time
she failed to calm herself she interpreted it as further evidence that something was wrong with her
character and with her body. That belief fed back into the nightmares, the scanning, and the urge to
imagine alternate histories in which she had somehow protected others. Recently, after a loud
construction blast near her office sent her ducking under her desk and unable to recover for the
rest of the day, Lina finally sought help because she could no longer sustain the routine of
vigilance, avoidance, and self-accusation. She said she was most afraid that she would never feel
safe anywhere again, but she was also hoping that therapy might help her stop living as if the
explosion were still about to happen.
