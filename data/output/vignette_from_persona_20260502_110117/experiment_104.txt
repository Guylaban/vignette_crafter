Mere had been a widowed shopowner for a little over a year when she first came to the clinic,
carrying the kind of exhaustion that had settled into her body since her husband’s sudden death. He
had collapsed after an ordinary evening out, with no warning she could make sense of, and the shock
of losing him had left her with a memory that never felt settled into the past. When she tried to
describe that night, she found herself stalling and restarting, as if she could not build a clear
story even for herself; the images came instead in fragments, like a film that kept breaking loose
and running over and over in her mind. She remembered the smell of alcohol on his breath when he
came home, the confusion in his face, the terrible moment when she realised something was badly
wrong, and then the blur of panic, voices, and loss. Even months later, that memory would force its
way into her mind without warning, especially when she was confronted by reminders of bereavement:
talk of people losing loved ones, funerals, cemeteries, or grief-related events. Hearing a customer
at her shop mention a recent funeral, or seeing flowers and black clothing near a chapel on her way
to work, could pull her straight back into that night, making her feel as though the event were
happening again.

Those reminders did more than bring back the memory; they quickly altered how Mere saw herself and
what she expected from life. She had come to believe that her life had been destroyed by the trauma,
and that there was no future ahead of her. At times she said she did not know herself anymore, as if
the woman who had once run the household, shared plans with her husband, and moved through the world
with ordinary confidence had vanished with him. The sense of a ruined life and an unrecognisable
self made the intrusive memory feel even more dangerous and more inescapable, because if nothing in
her world could be repaired, then there was nothing for her mind to return to except the loss. In
those moments, the memory replayed with greater force and seemed to confirm the worst of what she
feared. The more it took hold, the more she became convinced that her future had ended with her
husband’s death.

Mere’s body lived in that conviction. She often reacted as if she were in danger even when nothing
was actually wrong, scanning rooms, bracing at sudden sounds, and feeling unable to relax even in
places she knew were safe. In the shop, she would startle when the door opened too quickly or when a
customer’s voice rose unexpectedly, and her body would tighten before she had time to tell herself
she was all right. That constant sense of threat made the grief reminders around her feel sharper
still. If someone spoke warmly about a spouse who had died, she could feel her chest seize and her
mind race toward the same black conclusion: that loss could strike again, that she was already
living in its aftermath, and that anything connected to death or mourning had to be kept at a
distance. Alcohol smell was especially upsetting; when a customer came in after drinking, or when
she caught the sour scent on someone passing by, it could jolt her with the same false alarm, and
she would back away, struggle to think clearly, and sometimes feel suddenly as if she were back in
the frightening hours of her husband’s collapse. The threat in her body and the memory in her mind
fed each other, and each reinforced the other’s reach into her daily life.

Over time, those reactions narrowed her world. Mere began avoiding funerals, cemeteries, and grief-
related events, even when she thought she ought to attend. She had missed a cousin’s memorial
service and stopped going near the cemetery where her husband was buried, telling herself she could
not bear the smell, the sights, or the way such places opened the wound too wide. At work, she
shortened conversations when customers started discussing illness or bereavement, and she learned to
turn away from anyone who smelled of alcohol. Yet the harder she worked to keep such reminders out,
the more they seemed to ambush her. An innocent conversation in the shop about a neighbour’s
widowhood, the sight of a funeral program left on the counter, or the faint trace of liquor on a man
asking for change could suddenly crash through her defences and throw her back into the same
panicked state. Each intrusion made her feel more certain that she was broken, that she had no
future, and that the person she used to be was gone. By the time she sought help, Mere was
frightened not only by the memory of her husband’s death, but by how little control she felt over
when it returned, and she hoped treatment might help her sleep, keep the memories from forcing
themselves in, and allow her to feel like herself again before the life she believed had been
destroyed disappeared completely from reach.
