Arthur, a 74-year-old European American tutor, sought help after months of feeling increasingly
cornered by reminders of the flood that had torn through his town. He had been teaching a small
group of high school students in the library when the weather alerts first sounded, then the power
failed, and by the time he reached home the street was already filling with brown water. He
remembered forcing open the front door with the rain coming sideways, the sour, muddy smell rising
up from the basement, and the frantic effort to get his partner’s photographs and a few papers onto
the shelves before the water climbed higher. Even now, when he thought about that night, the smell
could return with unnerving force, and the scene would seize his attention without warning while he
was reading or preparing a lesson. At other times he could recall it in a strangely flat way, as if
it had happened to someone else entirely, and that numb, detached quality made him feel uneasy in
itself, because he could not trust that the event had really stayed where it belonged.

Those intrusions shaped the way Arthur lived afterward. When the memory forced its way into his
mind, he tried to push it away by pretending it had happened to another man, almost as if distance
could make it less dangerous. He also found himself ruminating on how the flood might have been
prevented—whether he should have checked the river forecast earlier, whether he had waited too long
to leave, whether a different decision might have spared him the helplessness he felt watching the
water spread through the house. The more he went over those possibilities, the more determined he
became to avoid anything that might stir up stress: he stopped reading the news, cut short social
plans, and made excuses not to drive near the river. In his tutoring work, he began preferring the
same quiet table near the wall in the back room of the community center, where he could see the
whole space and keep his back protected; if he could not sit that way, he felt exposed and
distracted. He noticed too that if a student or staff member took longer than expected to arrive, he
immediately assumed something bad was happening—an accident, another emergency, some unseen threat
that needed watching for.

That sense of danger became increasingly active in ordinary places. Arthur started scanning his
surroundings constantly for signs that something was wrong, his eyes moving to exits, windows, and
the faces of strangers before he could stop himself. A delayed text from his partner, or a brief
silence when the phone should have rung, could send him into the same tense vigilance he felt during
the flood watch, as if he were once again waiting for news that would confirm things were about to
go badly. The more he stayed on alert, the more he reached for the habits that gave him momentary
relief: he withdrew from busy errands, chose routes that seemed less stressful, and held himself
rigidly against a wall in waiting rooms or cafés. Yet these efforts did not calm him for long. When
he withdrew and avoided strain, the flood memory had more room to enter his mind unexpectedly, and
with it came the stale river smell and the flat, unreal feeling that he was watching another
person’s disaster. Those episodes left him even less certain about his surroundings, and he would
begin scanning again, convinced that if he did not keep watch he might miss the next sign of
trouble.

Over weeks and months, the pattern narrowed his life. He became less available to his students and
more hesitant about accepting new tutoring sessions, especially if they involved crowded places or
uncertain schedules. His partner noticed that Arthur seemed preoccupied even during calm evenings,
as if some part of him remained stationed at the window, waiting for water that would not recede
from his mind. What finally brought him to seek help was not only how often the memory broke in, but
how exhausted he felt by the constant effort to keep danger at bay and to keep the flood at arm’s
length. Arthur said he was most afraid that he would never again feel ordinary safety—that delays,
smells, and sudden recollections would continue to dictate where he sat, where he went, and how
fully he could be present with the people he cared about. At the same time, he hoped that if he
could stop living as though the disaster were about to happen again, he might reclaim some
steadiness in his work and in the relationship that had begun to feel smaller around his vigilance.
