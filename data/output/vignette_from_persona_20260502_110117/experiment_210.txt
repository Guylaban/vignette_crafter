Samira, a 34-year-old divorced agricultural worker from a Middle Eastern family, came to treatment
describing a steady narrowing of her life since she witnessed a traumatic event three years earlier
in a field outside the town where she was working seasonally. One afternoon, while she and several
others were harvesting, she saw a young mother across the road suddenly collapse after a
confrontation nearby turned violent; the details she clung to were the baby’s cries, the way people
on the roadside looked at one another before anyone moved, and the sense that ordinary conversation
and ordinary faces could hide danger. In the weeks after, she found that certain conversation
topics—anything about neighborhood conflict, family disputes, or what “really happened” in
public—could pull her right back into that moment, and the sound of babies crying had the same
effect, sharp and immediate, making her chest tighten as though she were again standing in the dust
and heat with no way to make sense of what she had seen. People acting suspiciously, even small
things like a man lingering too long near the irrigation shed or a coworker whispering while looking
over her shoulder, made her body tense and her mind race toward the possibility that another bad
event was about to unfold. Each of these reminders seemed to confirm her fear that the world was not
reliably safe, and over time she came to believe that people were not what they seemed, that she had
lost her faith in people and in life itself, and that she no longer belonged anywhere.

At first, Samira tried to outrun those reactions by forcing herself into order. She began cleaning
and organizing repeatedly at home, wiping counters, folding clothes, lining up containers, and
checking the same spaces again and again after work, telling herself that if her surroundings were
neat enough and under control, her thoughts would settle. She also tried to push the memories out of
her mind and made herself think only positive thoughts, repeating comforting phrases when the images
came up, but the effort left her exhausted and tense. The more she scrambled to shut everything
down, the more her mind seemed to return to the same suspicious details from that day, and the same
fear that the world could turn dangerous without warning. In that way, the very strategies she
relied on to manage the reminders seemed to deepen her distrust: every time she had to work so hard
to keep herself together, it felt to her like proof that she could not trust her own mind, and that
if she had to force positivity and bury the memories, then the truth underneath must be unbearable.
She began to read other people’s glances as concealment, to see distance in conversation as evidence
that she did not truly belong, and to feel that if she could not even speak honestly about what she
had witnessed, there was nowhere in her life where she fit.

The practical consequences spread through her days. On the farm, Samira became cautious around
coworkers she had once chatted with easily, especially if they spoke in hushed tones or mentioned
family matters, and she noticed herself stepping away when a baby was brought to the worksite by a
relative. She would tell herself to stay positive and keep moving, but that only made her more
vigilant, scanning faces and rehearsing whether someone was acting suspiciously. When she got home,
she cleaned until late at night, trying to wash away the feeling that danger had followed her there,
yet each repeat of the ritual left her with the same bleak conclusion that nothing in people or in
life could be counted on. Even in moments of temporary calm, she felt cut off from others and from
any secure place in the world. She had been divorced shortly after these symptoms intensified, and
though she did not describe the separation as the cause, she spoke about it as another confirmation
that she no longer belonged anywhere.

Samira sought help after realizing that she was spending most of her waking hours either avoiding
reminders, scrubbing or arranging things to feel in control, or bracing herself against the next
intrusive image. What finally brought her in was the fear that the constant suspicion and effort
were taking over her ability to work and to live with any ease. She said she was tired of feeling as
though every suspicious glance, every upsetting conversation, and every baby’s cry could send her
back to that roadside scene. What she most hoped for was a way to feel safe around other people
again, and what frightened her most was that, without help, she might go on living as if she had no
place in the world at all.
