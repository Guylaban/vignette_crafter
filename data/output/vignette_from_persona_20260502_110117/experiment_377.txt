Daniel Weiss was 45, a married journalist who had built his life around being the person who went
toward difficult stories rather than away from them. That changed after he was held captive while on
assignment abroad, when armed men blindfolded him, kept him in a cramped room, and repeatedly
grabbed him by the neck to force him to stay still and obey. He was released alive, but he came home
with the uneasy sense that part of him had never left that room. In the weeks that followed, the
memory would force its way into his mind when he did not want it to, and when it came it did not
feel like a story he was recalling from a distance. Daniel described it as if the scene were
projected in front of him, like a film that played over and over: the dim light, the pressure on his
throat, the sound of men moving just outside the door. Each replay brought back the same terror he
had felt at the time, with no real sense that the danger belonged to the past.

At first he tried to keep working, telling himself that returning to the newsroom would restore some
control. Instead, he found himself on edge in ways that made ordinary life feel dangerous. He was
superalert and watchful wherever he went, scanning hallways, exits, and the faces of strangers as if
he were still waiting for someone to come for him. Unexpected or loud sounds—someone dropping a
binder in the newsroom, a door slamming in the apartment building, a sudden shout from the
street—would send him immediately into alarm. He knew, intellectually, that he was safe, but he
often reacted as if he were in danger even when nothing was actually wrong. That constant state of
alertness made it harder for the captivity memory to stay in the background; the more keyed up he
became, the more readily the images broke through, and every intrusion arrived with the same
visceral terror. Once those memories surged, they also sharpened the things his body had learned to
fear, so that pain in his neck, a hand placed on his shoulder by a colleague, or the smell of
alcohol drifting from someone’s breath could suddenly feel loaded with threat.

Over time, Daniel began organizing his life around avoiding those jolting moments. He took longer
routes through the office to avoid crowded lifts and narrow corridors, and he became careful about
social lunches because the smell of alcohol from a nearby table could make his stomach tighten and
his attention snap back to the captivity. He asked his wife not to hug him from behind and would
stiffen if she touched the back of his neck while passing him in the kitchen. What frightened him
most was not only the memory itself, but how quickly it could be set off by something as small as a
touch or a twinge of pain in his neck, as if his body were still waiting for the next grab. Because
he was constantly on guard for sounds, for contact, and for the faintest echo of the room where he
had been held, those cues began to dominate his days. The more he watched for danger, the more
places and situations felt contaminated by it, and the easier it became for the captivity to return
uninvited.

By the time he sought help, Daniel had become increasingly withdrawn at home and less settled at
work, where interviews in busy environments and unexpected noise in the newsroom left him tense and
distracted. He was ashamed that, after surviving something so extreme, he still found himself
flinching from a hand on the neck or feeling trapped by the smell of alcohol at a colleague’s
dinner. What brought him to treatment was the realization that he was no longer simply remembering
what happened; he was living as though it could happen again at any moment. He came hoping to
recover enough calm to be with his wife without bracing himself, to sit through a workday without
constant scanning, and to stop feeling that his own mind was dragging him back into captivity.
