changed her life happened at work, during a day that had started as routine and then turned abruptly

easily the scene could have turned violent if anyone had panicked or tried to force a response from

violence around her — voices rising, tension in a room, even a sense that someone might turn
aggressive — can make her body tighten before she has time to think.

What has made those moments especially powerful for Marianne is how quickly they have come to stand
for her own fragility. If she catches sight of blood, even in a clinical context, she is flooded
with the same inner recoil and the same bodily alarm; if she begins to feel physically unwell, she
is no longer able to trust what is happening inside her. In those moments she has come to believe
that she is a weak person, someone who could not withstand what others manage, and that belief has
spread into a deeper sense of loss. She says that nothing in her life feels meaningful anymore, and
that she does not know herself anymore. The woman who once felt steady at work now experiences
herself as altered and diminished, as if the exposure took away not only her health in that instant
but also the person she thought she had been.

Those appraisals have shaped how Marianne moves through her days. Because she sees herself as weak
and changed, she avoids thinking about anything discussed in therapy once she leaves the room; she
finds it easier to push the material away than to sit with thoughts that confirm how broken she
feels. She also goes along with whatever other people want to feel safe, especially her partner,
because disagreement now feels dangerous and she is afraid that standing her ground will expose her
as incapable or difficult. Even making plans for the future has become hard for her. She notices
herself deflecting invitations and delaying decisions about trips, work arrangements, and ordinary
commitments, as if naming a future would require confidence she no longer has. In this way her sense
of lost meaning and identity has narrowed her life, and the narrowing has made her feel even
smaller.

The same beliefs have also made her more alert to the very things that unsettle her. On days when
she feels unwell, she scans her body for signs that the old collapse is returning; if there is blood
in the maternity unit, she watches herself watch it, bracing for the moment when she will become
overwhelmed. Violence, or the hint of it, feels even more dangerous now because she is convinced she
would not be able to cope with confrontation or protect herself. Marianne’s attention has become
organized around avoiding those states, and the more she monitors for them, the more salient they
seem. A vague stomach upset can pull her straight back into the old fear, and that fear makes the
world look full of possible threats. What began as a single chemical exposure has turned into a life
organized around vigilance.

Over the months, this pattern has affected both work and home. In the maternity setting, where she
once moved with confidence, Marianne has started stepping back from emotionally intense situations
and staying close to tasks that feel predictable. At home she keeps her world smaller, agreeing to
plans she does not want and then feeling resentful and ashamed that she could not simply say no. Her
partner has noticed how often she retreats after appointments, and Marianne herself can see the cost
of protecting herself this way, but she also fears that if she stops complying or if she thinks too
deeply about the exposure, the old panic and sense of failure will return. What brought her to seek
help now was realizing that this shrinking life had become the rule rather than the exception. She
came in hoping to understand why she cannot feel like herself anymore, and fearing that if nothing
changes she will lose the remainder of the meaning she still longs to recover.
