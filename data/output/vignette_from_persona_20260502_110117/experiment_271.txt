trained her to read risks in reports and regulations but had not prepared her for what happened one
winter afternoon at work. She had been in a government building where a maintenance problem in an
adjacent area released a toxic chemical, and at first the smell had seemed merely odd, a sharp burnt

and hurrying toward the exits, and Marisol found herself caught in the confusion, trying to make
sense of whether the smell meant danger or whether she was overreacting. She remembered the smoke
alarm tones, the burnt smell hanging in the air, and the unsettling alcohol-like odor that later
came to trigger her most strongly, because it had been present in the aftermath and in the materials
used to clean the area. Even now, a whiff of smoke or a burnt scent could make her feel abruptly as
if she were back in that corridor, and the alcohol smell from sanitizer, mouthwash, or spilled
liquor could do the same, snapping her attention toward danger before she had time to think.

What stayed with Marisol was not only the event itself but the way it seemed to expose her as
someone who had failed to recognize what was happening fast enough. She came to believe that she
could not rely on herself, that she could not trust that she would do the right thing if something
similar happened again, and that her own judgment had been dangerously slow. At times she said she
felt as if something inside her had been permanently altered; she feared she would never be able to
feel normal emotions again, only a narrowed, braced state of alarm. Those thoughts fed directly into
her daily habits. She began replaying the incident in her mind, turning over how it might have been
prevented and where she had gone wrong, as if enough analysis might restore a sense of control. She
also started watching television and reading news stories with care, avoiding programmes or articles
about fires, chemical spills, or industrial accidents because they made her feel exposed and ashamed
at the same time. When she did encounter a reminder, the thoughts came back harder, and the same
self-doubt deepened: if she could not trust herself, then the only answer was to keep thinking, keep
checking, keep rehearsing.

That sense of unreliability also shaped how she moved through the world. Marisol became vigilant
about where she sat, choosing a chair with her back to a wall or near a clear path out, and when she

orient herself, and only then sit, already watching the room for anything that seemed off. The

that danger was immediate. Because she was always on guard, smoke from a street vent, a burnt odor
from a neighbor’s cooking, or the alcohol smell in a conference room could seize her attention at

possible threats everywhere, which seemed to confirm that she truly was unsafe.

tired of living as if every room might become the scene of another disaster, and she feared that if
she did not learn how to trust herself again she would keep shrinking her world, one doorway and one
smell at a time.
