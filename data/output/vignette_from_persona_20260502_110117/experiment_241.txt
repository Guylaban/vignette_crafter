officer even off duty: measured, upright, watchful. But in the war zone where he had been assigned
as a civilian adviser years earlier, those habits had not protected him from what happened on a
narrow street outside a clinic after a sudden explosion tore through a crowd gathered for medicine
and food. He had been close enough to smell the dust and burned fuel, close enough to hear people
calling for children, and close enough to see the confusion that followed when the wounded could not
be moved quickly. What stayed with Rafael was not only the violence of that moment but the feeling
that he had failed in some basic human duty. He had come to believe he was a weak person, that he
should have protected others and did not, and that he would never forgive himself for not doing more
to stop what happened. Those convictions settled over him and, over time, shaped the way the event
lived in his mind.

heat, the same metallic smell, the same cries and dust, as if the scene were happening again in
front of him. At other times he could call up the facts in a flat, numb way, but even then the
distance felt disturbing, as though he were describing something that happened to a stranger rather
than to a man who had stood there and done too little. He was often caught between those two forms
of remembering: one that flooded him with guilt and made the whole moment feel completely real
across all his senses at once, and another that left him strangely empty, which in its own way felt
like another sign of weakness. The more he revisited the scene in his mind, the more those
appraisals hardened. Each time he remembered the crowd, the cries, and his own paralysis, the old
judgment returned with greater force: he had failed to protect others, and that failure proved what
kind of man he was.

That was how the loop began to take hold in daily life. Rafael worked as a Navy Officer in a support

corridor, or the sight of a crowded intake area on a ship or base that echoed the chaos of the
clinic street. When that happened, the scene would surge in with such vividness that he could feel
the heat on his skin and hear the voices as if they were beside him. Afterwards he would tell
himself again that he was weak, that a stronger man would have acted, that he had no right to call
himself protective or competent. Those thoughts did not calm him; they drew him back to the event,
and the more he replayed it, the more real and humiliating it became, as if every sensory detail
were evidence against him. Even when he could only recall it in a blank, detached way, the numbness
itself fed the same conclusion: something was wrong with him, and it must mean he had failed in a
deeper sense.

Over the years he began arranging his life around the fear of being pulled into that scene again. He
stayed busy with paperwork, kept conversations practical, and avoided talking about the war zone
with colleagues or with the partner he lived with, because saying it aloud seemed to risk opening
the door to the full experience. At home he could sometimes be seen sitting rigidly when helicopters
passed overhead or when neighborhood noise unexpectedly snapped sharp and loud outside the window.
