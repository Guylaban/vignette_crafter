through a crowded intersection and struck a man crossing against the light. She had not meant to hit
him, but in the instant before impact she had felt the vehicle lurch, heard the sharp thud, and then
the shouting that followed as people ran toward the street. The memory of that scene stayed fixed
around a few brutal details: the sound of voices rising around her, the sight of cars twisted at odd
angles after the accident, and the wrenching pain in her neck from bracing herself in the seat. From
the beginning she was haunted by the belief that the injury happened because of the way she acted,
and that she would never forgive herself for not doing more to stop it. She described the episode as
permanently humiliating, not only because it became public knowledge in her political world, but
because she felt exposed as someone who had failed in a moment that mattered.

Afterward, certain reminders set off an immediate wave of distress. When people raised their voices,
even in ordinary arguments at home or in meetings, Marianne felt her body react before she could
think: her chest tightened, her pulse jumped, and she became overwhelmed with fear, horror, anger,
guilt, and shame. The same happened when she noticed neck pain after a long day, because it brought
back the way her body had felt during the crash, and when she saw wrecked vehicles or even imagined
cars hitting by accident in traffic. Those moments did not just frighten her; they pulled fragments
of the event into her mind. She found that when she tried to deliberately remember what happened,
the full memory was oddly difficult to access, but when it surfaced it came in bits and pieces, with
the sound of shouting seeming to play in her head and the physical pain in her neck returning as if
the body were remembering before the mind could. Each reminder sharpened her conviction that she was
to blame, that something in her conduct had caused the injury and left an indelible stain on her
reputation.

That judgment about herself began to organize her days. Marianne started avoiding anything that
could be stressful, and eventually anything that might bring up strong feelings at all, whether fear
or even moments of happiness that felt too vulnerable to trust. She stopped driving completely and
refused to be a passenger in a vehicle, making colleagues arrange separate transportation for her
