Nabil, a 64-year-old divorced Middle Eastern man now working in politics, came to describe a war-
zone relief deployment from decades earlier as the event that still organized much of his daily
life. He had been sent to coordinate supplies after bombardments, moving through streets where smoke
hung low and the burnt smell of wrecked buildings clung to his clothes. In that setting, strangers
were not simply unfamiliar people but could be armed men, informants, or desperate civilians with
unreadable intentions; military talk in briefings and on radios had carried the same sharp urgency,
and silence between explosions had felt worse than noise because it signaled that something lethal
might be near. Nabil spoke of one stretch of hours as coming back in disconnected pieces: a damaged
doorway, a room gone unnaturally quiet, the acrid smell of burning, a face he could not place, and
then a surge of the same terror he had felt at the time. When he tried to remember the event in
order, the images would not hold together; they arrived as fragments, and when they did come he
became so overwhelmed that he had to stop and shut the memory down.

Since then, Nabil had learned to live around those reminders, but they were hard to avoid in his
current work, where he still had to talk with unfamiliar people at fundraisers, meet staff he did
not know well, and sit through policy discussions that sometimes turned to military matters. A
stranger leaning too close, a sentence about an army operation, or the whiff of something burnt from
a kitchen or a street fire could pull him back toward that old terror, and he would immediately try
to control what showed on his face. He described forcing his voice to stay even, distracting himself
with the next agenda item, and throwing himself into endless tasks so there was no room left for
memory. Those habits briefly helped him get through public events and committee meetings, but they
also seemed to set the stage for the memory to return later, stripped of context and more intense
for having been held down. At night or after a long day, when he finally stopped moving, the images
would intrude in those same broken pieces, and the harder he had worked to keep them out, the more
forcefully they came back.

As the memory kept breaking through, his sense of danger spread beyond reminders of the original
deployment. Nabil had come to believe that if he was caught off guard once, he could be caught off
guard again anywhere, and that even ordinary quiet might mean something was about to happen. Unusual
silence in his house, or in his office after colleagues left, made him feel that a threat was
gathering just out of sight. He found himself repeatedly checking that doors and windows were locked
before he could sit down, and he constantly scanned his surroundings in restaurants, corridors, and
parking lots for signs of danger. That vigilance, however, made the memories easier to trigger;
every time a burnt odor drifted in from outside or a stranger approached to ask a question, he was
jolted into the same fragmentary terror, and once it took hold he would need to stop what he was
doing. The more his world felt unsafe, the more he tried to control his emotions and occupy himself
with work, yet those efforts kept him keyed up and watching, which in turn made the event feel
closer and more immediate.

Over months, Nabil’s attempts to outrun the memories had narrowed his life. He stayed late at the
office, filled his calendar, and used constant busyness to avoid being alone with his thoughts, but
the relief never lasted. At home, he noticed himself checking locks several times before bed,
listening for sounds in the hallway, and growing tense if the apartment was too quiet. He had become
wary of talking with strangers unless he absolutely had to, and he dreaded any conversation that
drifted toward the military or war, because even a passing reference could bring the pieces rushing
back. What finally brought him to seek help was the realization that he was no longer simply
remembering the war; he was living as though it might restart at any moment, and the strain was
beginning to spill into his relationships and his work. Nabil said he wanted to keep serving, but he
was afraid that if this kept tightening its grip, he would end up exhausted, isolated, and unable to
trust either silence or his own mind.
