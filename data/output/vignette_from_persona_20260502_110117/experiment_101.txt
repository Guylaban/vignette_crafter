Raymond “Ray” LeBlanc, a 67-year-old widowed military medic of North American background, came to
treatment after months of feeling as though a single collision had never really ended. He described
the original trauma with the practiced, halting precision of someone who had spent a lifetime in
emergency settings and still could not make the memory sit in order. Years earlier, while riding as
a passenger in a vehicle, he had been caught in a life-threatening motor vehicle crash that left him
with the sense that the cabin had filled with chaos in seconds: metal striking metal, the violent
shudder of impact, and a burnt smell that seemed to cling to everything. In the aftermath, even now,
he would sometimes catch alcohol on someone’s breath and feel his body react before he could think;
the smell of alcohol, cars hit by accident, and the faint burned odor of overheated engines or
damaged wiring could bring a surge of alarm with physical force. He said that when one of these
reminders appeared, his heart would pound, his muscles would brace, and he would become jumpy and
easily startled, especially by ordinary sounds that other people barely noticed. Ray had worked for
years as a military medic, and he noticed the irony that he had once been the person others relied
on in crises, but now a backfiring truck in a parking lot or a door slamming in the clinic lot could
leave him flinching and scanning the surroundings.

What made the reactions more frightening to him was what they seemed to mean. He had come to believe
that the event happened because of the way he acted, and that he could not trust himself to do the
right thing if something dangerous happened again. Beneath that was a harsher judgment: he saw
himself as weak, and those beliefs drove him into a pattern of constant thought monitoring, as
though he were checking his own mind for early signs of “going crazy.” He tried hard not to think
about the trauma, forcing the images away whenever they appeared, but the effort itself left him
tense and self-conscious. This self-scrutiny also fed his physical fear; the more he watched for
trouble inside himself, the more he noticed the pounding in his chest and the jolt he felt at small
noises, and the more he interpreted those sensations as proof that something was wrong with him.

The memory itself had a broken, alien quality that kept intruding despite his efforts. When he tried
to explain what happened, he would lose the thread and could not finish, which embarrassed him and
made him feel even less competent. He said the memory felt like it belonged to someone else rather
than to him, as if he were describing another medic’s worst night. At times, when the scene
returned, he would hear things in his head that he had heard during the frightening event, as though
the crash were replaying its own sounds inside him. Those fragments made the event feel immediate
rather than past, and they sharpened his earlier self-blame: if the memory came back in pieces, with
sounds attached to it, he took that as evidence that he had not handled it well enough and that
there was something flawed in him. When he tried to talk through it, the missing pieces and the odd,
detached quality of the recollection often sent him back to the same disturbing cues—burnt smell,
alcohol, the sight of wrecked cars—because they seemed to be the only things that made the story
feel real.

Over the months that followed, Ray’s efforts to manage the problem narrowed his life. He stopped
driving and refused to ride as a passenger, arranging his days around not needing a vehicle. He
avoided thinking about the crash, yet his mind kept circling back to it; when he noticed himself
monitoring his thoughts, he would become more convinced that he was weak and unreliable. The
avoidance made his world smaller and kept the memory from settling into a coherent account, so when
it surfaced it felt even more alien and unfinished. In that state, the old smells and images became
more potent, and he began to notice them everywhere: alcohol at social gatherings, a scorched smell
from the clinic’s parking lot, a twisted sedan on the roadside. Each reminder brought the body-based
alarm back quickly, and each alarm seemed to confirm his fear that he was not steady enough to
trust. He found himself increasingly jumpy around family and neighbors, wary of leaving the house,
and even in the military community where he had once felt capable, he now worried that one sudden
sound would expose how on edge he had become.

By the time he sought help, Ray was exhausted by the cycle and ashamed of how small his life had
become. He missed his late wife keenly and admitted that the loneliness made it easier to brood over
the crash, but what finally pushed him to call was realizing he had begun arranging every errand,
visit, and meal around the possibility of a smell or a sound setting him off. He wanted to stop
feeling as if he were bracing for impact all day, and he was most afraid that the weakness he saw in
himself would keep shrinking his world until he could no longer trust his own mind or go anywhere
without fear.
