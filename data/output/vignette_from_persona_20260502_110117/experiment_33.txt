Marisol had been on duty in the days before the hurricane made landfall, a 39-year-old soldier who
was used to discipline, chains of command, and carrying herself with a steady face. What happened
stripped that away in a way she still found hard to put into words. The wind came in violent sheets,
rain striking sideways, and in the middle of the night the power failed. Sirens wailed somewhere
beyond the base, then there were loud cracks as debris hit metal and glass, and she remembered
looking toward the doorway and realizing there was nowhere that felt fully safe. Police and
emergency vehicles were everywhere afterward, their lights flashing through the storm and through
the flooded streets as people were moved, counted, and directed. Since then, weather that felt even
a little like that night—thick cloud cover, low pressure, sudden wind, the smell of rain before a
storm—could pull her back into a state of alarm before she consciously knew why. Loud noises had the
same effect: a slammed vehicle door, a dropped pan in the kitchen, a burst of fireworks in the
distance. Even the sight of police near her neighborhood or while driving past an incident on the
road could make her body tense, because it reminded her of the authority presence that had
surrounded the chaos and the fact that, in that moment, she had been dependent on other people’s
decisions. Marisol came to read these moments as evidence that she was failing herself. She had been
trained to keep moving, to assess a scene, to be useful, and instead she felt like she reacted too
quickly, too weakly, too messily. She told herself her reactions since the storm showed she was a
lousy coper, and at times the shame was so sharp that she felt like an object being carried through
events rather than a person directing her own life.

In the months that followed, her sense of danger did not settle. She constantly scanned her
surroundings for signs that something might go wrong, her eyes moving over exits, windows, crowds,
and parked cars whenever she entered a room or stepped into a new building. She always identified
escape routes before she could feel comfortable anywhere, whether she was in a grocery store, at
church, or arriving early for a shift on base. If she could not immediately see how to get out, her
chest tightened and she would stand near doors or keep her back to a wall. Ordinary sounds made her
jump in a way that embarrassed her, and she began to brace for them before they happened, listening
for the next bang, shout, or crack. That constant watchfulness fed her belief that she could not
stop bad things from happening to her, because if danger could appear so quickly despite all her
checking, then she was never really in control. The more certain she became that she was helpless,
the more vigilant she grew, and that vigilance in turn made the world seem full of threats. It also
shaped what set her off: because she was always anticipating harm, weather changes, sudden noises,
and police presence became even more potent, each one arriving already wrapped in fear.

As this went on, Marisol’s days narrowed. At work she found herself choosing posts and routes based
on exits and cover, and she grew reluctant to stay late if storm clouds gathered over the base. In
her civilian life she began avoiding stores when the forecast looked unsettled, and she would ask
her husband to drive if there was any chance of heavy traffic near sirens or construction noise. She
knew these choices made sense in the moment—if the sky darkened or a police cruiser passed, her body
reacted before her mind could catch up—but they also kept reinforcing the idea that the world was
dangerous and that she was not strong enough to meet it. Because she already felt defective and
powerless, each avoidance decision seemed to prove the point. She would think, with a bitter kind of
certainty, that a person who had truly coped well would not need to map every exit or flinch at
every sound. That thought could send her right back into scanning, as if by searching harder she
might finally find a guarantee that nothing bad would happen.

By the time she sought help, Marisol was exhausted by the effort of trying to stay ahead of danger.

could not bear to spend the whole evening calculating exits while the weather shifted outside. She

police presence and the sound of equipment clanging in the distance left her shaking and ashamed in
front of her unit. What brought her in was not only how often she felt on edge, but how convinced
she had become that this was the rest of her life unless someone helped her make sense of it. What
she feared most was that she would remain trapped in that helpless feeling, always scanning, always
bracing, always expecting the next loud sound or dark sky to prove again that she could not stop bad
things from happening.
