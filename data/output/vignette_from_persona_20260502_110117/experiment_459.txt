Ravi, a 29-year-old South Asian married man who worked as a painter, came to treatment after months
of feeling as though ordinary life had become a series of threats. The robbery had happened late one
evening when he was walking back from a supply store with paint cans strapped in one hand and his
phone in his pocket, near a narrow stretch of sidewalk between shuttered shops. Two men came up fast
from behind, one grabbing his arm while the other shoved something hard against his side and
demanded his wallet. Ravi remembered the panic of being pinned in place, the metallic glint of the
blade, and the frantic scramble of his own hands before they took his cash and phone and disappeared
into the dark. Since then, waking abruptly from a nightmare about the robbery would jolt him into
immediate tension, his body already braced before he was fully awake. A sudden recollection of the
event could strike him in the middle of work, and certain streets, alleyways, or the stretch of
sidewalk outside hardware stores would make the night come back in fragments so vividly that he had
to stop what he was doing and look around.

What Ravi described as most disturbing was how incomplete the memory felt. When he tried to remember
the robbery on purpose, he could not access the full sequence; he got only broken pieces, the
pressure at his side, the men’s voices, the sense of being cornered, and then blankness where he
expected the rest. That missing middle made him feel intensely guilty, as if there was something he
should have done to stop it or to protect himself better, and the more he tried to fill in the gaps,
the more he felt that the memory slipped away. Those partial recollections fed a conviction that
people could not be trusted and that he could not rely on anyone else if danger appeared. He had
come to believe that the world was made up of people who could turn on him without warning, and that
belief made him feel set apart even from his wife and siblings, as though they were on the outside
of something he alone carried.

Over the following weeks, Ravi’s sense of being unsafe became constant. He found himself scanning
every street, parking lot, and shop doorway for anyone who looked as if they might move too quickly
toward him, and he noticed his shoulders staying tight even when nothing was happening. That body-
on-alert state made him irritable at home and quick to snap at minor interruptions, with angry
outbursts that surprised his wife and left him ashamed afterward. The more he believed other people
could not be trusted, the more alert he became for signs of threat, and that constant checking made
even a routine walk to a job site feel dangerous. Because he was always braced for the next attack,
nightmare awakenings or a flash of the mugging memory would leave him more tense still, his pulse up
and his temper shorter, as though his body were already defending him before his mind had caught up.

The memory itself kept pulling him back into the same fear. In the middle of painting a client’s
living room, a glimpse of a dark doorway or a man stepping close behind him could bring the robbery
to mind suddenly, and the fragmentary image would immediately make him scan the room and the
hallways with renewed urgency. At the same time, the guilt that came with trying to remember made
him feel ashamed and alone, which reinforced his belief that no one else could be depended on to
understand or help. That sense of isolation made him more watchful rather than less: he avoided
walking alone after dark, crossed the street when he saw groups gathered near convenience stores,
and took longer routes to job sites whenever a location felt too similar to the place where the
robbery had happened. Even in places that should have felt familiar, his surroundings now seemed
capable of turning dangerous without warning, and his heightened vigilance made small cues feel
loaded with meaning.

By the time he sought help, Ravi’s world had narrowed. He was still working, but he had started
declining evening jobs and leaving sooner than he used to, which strained his income and made him
feel like he was failing his family. He was exhausted by the cycle of waking from nightmares,
getting flooded by fragments of memory, scanning for danger everywhere, and then feeling angrier and
more convinced that he had to protect himself because no one else would. What brought him in was the
fear that the vigilance was taking over his life and his marriage, and what he most hoped for was to
sleep without jolting awake and to move through the city again without feeling as if every corner
might hold the same hands, the same blade, and the same helplessness.
