Michael Hargrove, a 55-year-old single European American counselor, described a life that had
narrowed around one evening at a community center where he had volunteered after work. During a
heated dispute in the hallway, someone hurled a chair and a plastic bin across the room, and the
sudden crash was followed by the sharp, burnt smell of an overheated electrical panel and the
unmistakable odor of smoke drifting in from a small fire that had started behind a wall. Michael had
been close enough to see people bolt for the exits, and though the fire was contained quickly, the
chaos lodged in him as a man-made disaster that felt both sudden and absurdly close. In the weeks
after, he found himself avoiding friends because invitations to dinner, bars, or even someone’s
apartment brought him too near conversation about “that night,” and he had come to dread any setting
where he might have to think about it. He also monitored his own thoughts with grim vigilance,
checking constantly for signs that he was “going crazy,” because the event had left him convinced
that if he let his mind wander toward it, he would not be able to handle what came next.

What Michael noticed first was how his body seemed to betray him. He would catch a whiff of burnt
toast from a neighbor’s kitchen or smoke from a passing truck and feel his chest tighten before he
had time to tell himself he was safe. If someone nearby slammed a mug down, tossed a coat across a
chair, or threw a set of keys onto a desk, his stomach lurched and he would scan the room for an
exit without fully deciding to do so. Those cues pulled up more than alarm; they carried the event
back into him in fragments. Sometimes, when he tried not to think about it, he would still be
flooded by the same raw terror he had felt in the hallway, but without any clear picture of the
faces, the room, or the sequence of what had happened. At other times he would suddenly smell smoke
or singed wiring in his mind as vividly as if it were in front of him. The memory itself felt oddly
detached, as if it belonged to another man entirely, someone he had watched rather than lived as
himself. Because the sensations arrived so abruptly and stripped of context, Michael became more
certain that his body was still reacting as if danger were present, and that the world could turn on
him again without warning.

That conviction did not stay abstract. Each time a smell or a thrown object jolted him, the memory
came back stronger and more bodily, and the more it came back, the more threatening it felt. He
began to think he had to be especially careful because you never know what can happen next, and he
interpreted every flare of panic as evidence that something was still wrong inside him. The more he
braced himself against those moments, the more he felt trapped in the memory’s emotional intensity,
especially when it rose without any image at all, leaving him shaken and ashamed in his office after
a session or in the parking lot after work. He tried to avoid thinking about the event altogether,
telling himself that even one deliberate effort to remember would overwhelm him, yet that avoidance
offered little relief. Instead, the memory seemed to return in the background, shaped by the very
effort to push it away, and he found himself watching his own mind for signs of breakdown with
almost the same intensity that he watched doorways and hallways for danger.

Over months, the pattern spread into his life as a counselor. Michael could still sit with clients,
but after sessions he became reluctant to join colleagues for lunch, and he turned down long-
standing plans with friends because he feared the casual talk might drift toward fires, violence, or
“stressful experiences.” At home he avoided cooking when there was any chance of a burnt smell, and
he kept windows open even in cold weather if he thought he might catch a whiff of smoke. Objects
thrown in frustration—someone slapping a file onto a desk, a client tossing a pen, a neighbor
throwing a bag into a dumpster—would spike him into the same unease that had first followed the
disaster. Each time, he felt the old memory stir, and each time that stirred fear seemed to confirm
his belief that his body was unreliable and that he remained on the edge of catastrophe. In that way
his vigilance and his efforts to keep the event out of mind only kept the sensations alive, and the
sensations kept convincing him that danger was still near. By the time he sought help, he was
exhausted by the loop of smells, thrown objects, and internal monitoring, and he said what
frightened him most was not only the memory itself but the fear that if he kept living this way, he
would lose confidence in his own mind and his ability to remain the calm, competent man his work had
always required.
