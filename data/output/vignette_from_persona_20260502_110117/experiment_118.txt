Leila was 53, a married Middle Eastern ship captain who had spent most of her adult life on the
water, where she prized order, vigilance, and the ability to stay composed in rough conditions. It
was precisely that self-command that she felt had been stripped away by what happened in childhood.

that smelled of cooking oil and dust, where she learned to brace herself for footsteps in the hall
and the sudden opening of a door. What remained most vividly were pictures: the flash of a belt
lifted in the air, the pattern on the floor tiles as she crouched, the angle of her father’s
shoulders when he came toward her. Even now, some memories arrived as pictures with no words
attached, while at other times the emotional force would hit her without any clear image at all, a
wave of terror and shame that felt as if it belonged to someone else. As she grew older, she came to
believe that the people and institutions that should have protected her had let her down, and that
belief hardened into the conviction that the world was dangerous and that she had lost something
essential from the community and culture she once felt part of. She spoke with grief about how,
before the abuse, she had imagined herself as open and confident; now she found herself dwelling on
that lost version of herself and on how the violence might have been prevented if someone had
intervened.

Those appraisals colored nearly everything that followed. Because she saw the world as dangerous and
felt let down by those who should have kept her safe, Leila lived as if danger could reappear at any
moment, even in places that were objectively secure. She said she was unable to relax even when she
knew she was in a safe place, and at home she listened for sounds that might mean someone was
entering, pausing over every creak in the building or movement in the stairwell. Nights were harder
than days; in the dark she became especially watchful, certain that sleep made her vulnerable in the
same way childhood had. The same beliefs also made her push away anything that might bring the
trauma back into her mind. When images or bodily memories surged up, she tried hard not to think
about what happened, and she found herself caught in long bouts of prevention rumination, turning
over how the abuse could have been stopped, who should have noticed, and what she might have done
differently. That mental effort did not calm her for long; it kept her keyed up, and the more she
scanned for danger, the more her body stayed tense and her mind seemed to circle back to the old
scenes she was trying to keep away.

The memories themselves were not steady or predictable. At times, when she heard a heavy footstep on
a metal deck or a door slam in a way that sounded too much like the old house, the whole emotional
intensity of the childhood violence would flood her with no clear picture at all, leaving her
shaking and ashamed. At other times, a visual fragment would appear so sharply that she felt briefly
transported back into the room where she had been trapped. Those moments made her more determined to
suppress the thoughts and images, but the harder she strained not to think about them, the more they
persisted at the edges of her awareness and the more nights she spent listening for intruders rather
than resting. On the ship, where she was responsible for the vessel and crew, this constant
vigilance became exhausting; she could still do her work, but it took effort to stay focused when
part of her was always attending to sounds, shadows, and movement, and she began to withdraw from
social contact on shore as if distance itself could keep the past at bay. Yet the isolation also fed
her sense that she had been cut off from her people and culture, and that loss deepened her
conviction that nothing was truly safe or stable.

By the time she sought help, Leila was worn down by a life organized around scanning, holding
herself back from thought, and replaying what should have been different. She feared that if she let
her guard down, even briefly, something terrible would break in again—on deck, at home, or in her
sleep. What brought her to treatment was the realization that she was no longer merely remembering
the abuse; she was living as though it were still waiting outside her door, and she was afraid that
if this continued she would lose even more of the calm, connected life she once thought she was
meant to have.
