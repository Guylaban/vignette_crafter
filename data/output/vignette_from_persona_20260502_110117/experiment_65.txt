orderly and solved problems quietly, but after years of physical abuse from her late partner, that
sense of herself began to fray. The violence had not been a single incident but a pattern of being

unsettled her most was not only the memory of his hands on her, but the conviction that something in
her must be fundamentally wrong for having stayed, for not stopping it, for seeming so helpless
while it was happening. She had come to believe that her reactions since then showed she was a lousy
coper, and the belief settled into a broader sense that she did not know herself anymore.

In the months and years afterward, Mereana’s life narrowed around ways of keeping herself from
feeling exposed. She avoided eye contact with other people, especially in the office when a
colleague approached her desk or when a neighbor greeted her in the corridor, because meeting

avoided being touched in any form; a hand on her arm, a brief hug from family, even a passing brush
in a crowded lift could make her go rigid and pull away. At night, she stayed up until very late,
telling herself she preferred the quiet, but really because sleep felt like surrender and because
the darkness gave her too much space to notice every ache in her chest and every sudden memory. She
worked as a database administrator from home several days a week, and the isolation made it easier
to manage these habits, though it also made her world smaller. When she did go into the office, she
chose seating that would keep others from coming too close, and she arranged her day so she could
leave before social invitations began.

As those patterns hardened, they began to feed the very beliefs she was trying to outrun. Each time
Mereana pulled away from a colleague’s friendly touch or looked down instead of meeting someone’s

before. Staying awake into the early hours left her tired and irritable the next day, and she
interpreted that fatigue as further evidence that she was failing at ordinary life. The more she
withdrew, the more convinced she became that her reactions showed she was a lousy coper, and the
more she noticed herself thinking, with a kind of dull certainty, that there must be something wrong
with her as a person. She had expected time to make her feel more settled, but instead she found
that her own strategies for surviving had become part of what confirmed her worst judgments about
herself.

That loop became especially clear in the small humiliations of daily life. At work, a younger

through harmless tasks and avoiding sleep because she could not bear the blankness that came with
lying down. By the following morning she was exhausted and more likely to keep her gaze fixed on the

flash of alarm, yet each also seemed to confirm her private verdict that she was no longer the self
she once had been.

Mereana finally sought help when the strain began to interfere with the work she still took pride in

around the fear of touch, the avoidance of people’s faces, and the long nights that left her drained
before morning. What brought her to therapy was the uneasy sense that she could not keep living as
if every ordinary human contact were a threat, yet what she feared most was that treatment would
prove her right—that there really was something wrong with her, and that she had already lost
herself for good.
