Nabil, a 38-year-old Middle Eastern hairdresser living alone, came to understand his life as having
split into “before” and “after” the day his body first betrayed him. The frightening event had been
a sudden, life-threatening illness that struck while he was still at work in the salon, standing
behind a client with scissors in hand when his chest tightened, sweat broke out across his back, and
the room seemed to tilt. He remembered the metallic smell that seemed to cling to the air, the panic
of being rushed through medical assessments, and the humiliation of lying under bright lights while
strangers examined him as if he were already half gone. Since then, certain things had become loaded
with danger in a way he could not shake: waking from a nightmare with his heart hammering and no
immediate sense of where he was; seeing photos connected to the illness or the hospital period; and
any medical examination or procedure. Each of these moments hit him with a wave of fear and horror
so sudden that he felt pulled back into the same helplessness, his whole body tensing as if it had
been warned that danger was immediate.

The memories did not come to him like ordinary recollection. Sometimes it was a smell that arrived
first — a sharp, clinical odor that belonged to the hospital and would fill his mind before he could
name where he was. At other times, the event returned as pain in the same places his body had hurt
during the illness, making him clutch at his chest or stomach as though the illness were returning
in real time. There were also moments when no clear picture came at all, only the full emotional
force of that period: terror, shame, and the sense of being trapped inside a body he could not
control. Those surges of memory were enough to drive his threat system into overdrive. Once the
feelings hit, he became more vigilant, scanning himself for signs that something was wrong, and the
tension in his muscles never fully settled. He had come to believe that his body kept acting as if
he were still in danger even when he was safe, and that frightened him almost as much as the illness
itself. He wondered, with growing alarm, whether these reactions meant he was going crazy, and
whether he could still trust his own body at all.

That belief changed the way he moved through ordinary life. After one nightmare left him gasping
awake and shaky for the rest of the morning, he found himself dreading sleep, but it was the daytime
reminders that were hardest to avoid. In the salon, a client once brought in a photograph from a
family gathering that happened to include a hospital bracelet on someone’s wrist, and Nabil felt his
stomach drop before he had even processed what he was seeing. The sight pulled up the remembered
smell, the bodily pain, and the same raw fear all at once, and he excused himself to the back room,
shaking and ashamed. He began turning away from photos on customers’ phones and making sure he never
lingered over anything medical. Even routine physical exams became a source of mounting distress; a
recent appointment, meant to be simple, left him so activated that he left convinced the exam itself
had confirmed his vulnerability. The more he noticed his body reacting, the more certain he became
that his body was the enemy, and that certainty fed the fear again. He would catch himself checking
his pulse, bracing against another wave, and those sensations would in turn bring the illness back
into his mind.

Over the months, the memory of the illness and the fear of its return began to chase each other. A
smell, a photo, or a medical setting could summon the full force of the event; once the memories
flared, they sharpened his sense of threat and made the next trigger feel even closer. Nabil’s work
suffered in quiet but concrete ways. He kept his schedule lighter, avoided conversations about
health, and hesitated when clients mentioned hospitals, family illnesses, or photos from the time he
had been sick. He had once been known for being relaxed and chatty in the chair, but now he stayed
on guard, monitoring his own body for the first sign of distress. By the time he sought help, he was
exhausted by the cycle and afraid that one more strong reaction would prove he could no longer trust
himself. He wanted to feel safe in his own body again, but what finally brought him to therapy was
the fear that, without help, he would keep living as though the illness were still happening.
