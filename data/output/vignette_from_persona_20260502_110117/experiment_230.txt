away from his assailant. Being alone with men, especially unfamiliar men, became intolerable; if he
found himself in an elevator with a male coworker or cornered in a narrow corridor at work, his body
reacted before he could reason with it. He started to avoid military and police uniforms, and even

event happened, or even driving past the street near it, became nearly impossible. Because those
cues could appear without warning, his world shrank to a few places he considered safe, and he
arranged his routines around staying inside them.

Nader came to believe that the danger was not only in those obvious reminders but in his own mind,
which seemed to misread ordinary life as threat. He often reacted as if he were in danger even when
nothing was actually wrong, and that constant sense of alarm kept him scanning his surroundings for
signs of danger wherever he went. The more he scanned, the more proof he seemed to find that he was
not safe, and the more he searched for exits, suspicious faces, or the glint of authority uniforms,
the more certain he became that the world could turn on him without warning. That heightened

less he could test whether his fear was justified, and the stronger the feeling became that danger

unresolved wrong sharpened his resentment each time he had to change his own behavior to avoid

he had spoken that night, and this self-blame made his avoidance feel necessary, almost deserved.

to admit: each cancelled errand, each refusal to be around uniforms or authority figures, seemed to

might tip into threat.

sought help, he was exhausted by the vigilance, the restricted routine, and the sense that his life

accidental proximity to a man would send him back into the same dread. He wanted relief, but more
than that, he wanted to know whether he could ever feel safe enough to move through the world again
without constantly bracing for the worst.
