Two years earlier, on the subway ride home after a late shift, armed men had forced passengers down,
shouting political slogans as they detonated an explosive in the car ahead. Minh survived by
crawling beneath a seat and later stumbling out through smoke and broken glass, but what stayed with
him was not only the blast itself. He remembered pushing past another injured passenger to reach the
door, hearing someone call for help, and then freezing and leaving. Since then he had carried the
sense that he was no longer the man he had been, that in the moment that mattered he had acted in a
way that went against who he was. He had once seen himself as steady, dependable, the one who could
be counted on in his family and at work; now he was certain he could not rely on himself, and that
he was no longer capable of doing the things that had defined him.

After the attack, Minh began working longer and longer hours at the bank, taking extra shifts and
volunteering for opening and closing duties so he would not have time alone with his thoughts. The
constant busyness gave him brief relief, but it also narrowed his life. He stopped seeing friends,
kept conversations with coworkers short, and avoided getting emotionally close to anyone, especially
after his divorce, because intimacy felt dangerous when he already distrusted his own reactions. At
night, once he finally returned to his apartment, the silence made his mind drift back to the subway
car. He tried hard not to think about it, but the effort itself seemed to keep the memory near the
surface. When he was alone, images of smoke, shouting, and the injured passenger returned with a
force that made his chest tighten.

Minh’s appraisal of himself and his actions had become part of the fear that followed him. He
believed the attack had revealed something broken in him, and that belief made ordinary sensations
feel charged. A sudden bang in the bank lobby, a train screeching outside, or even a voice raised
sharply on the street could bring him back to the moment of the blast. When that happened, he became
irritable and snappish, sometimes acting aggressively toward customers or snapping at coworkers, and
afterward he interpreted his own temper as more proof that he was failing. He would tell himself
that someone who could not keep calm or trust himself was not really safe anywhere. That thought

was safe, sitting up in bed to listen for sounds in the hallway or to glance at the lock on the
door. He frequently looked behind him when walking to the station or crossing the parking lot after
work, scanning for someone following him, even on familiar routes near the bank. These checks
brought only momentary reassurance. The next day he would be more tense, already expecting his body
to betray him with another surge of alarm, and that anticipation fed back into his conviction that
he was not capable of steadying himself. The more exhausted he became from broken sleep and constant
vigilance, the more he saw his irritability and jumpiness as evidence that he had become someone
unreliable. The less he trusted himself, the more he watched the world for signs of threat.

Over months, this pattern reshaped Minh’s days. He stayed late at the bank because work was easier

would only expose how unsafe and damaged he felt. Even at home he found little relief, because the

himself he had been before. What brought Minh to treatment was the fear that he was becoming
permanently altered by what happened on the train, and what he most hoped for was some way to feel
safe without having to keep working, checking, and bracing himself every waking hour.
