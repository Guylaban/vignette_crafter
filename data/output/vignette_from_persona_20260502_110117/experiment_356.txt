Amina, a 77-year-old African American woman who had spent much of her life in public service as a
politician, came to treatment after years of carrying the effects of a violent relationship that had
ended long ago but never felt fully over. She described the original abuse as happening in the very
place that was supposed to be private and safe: in close quarters with her partner, where there was
nowhere to put distance between herself and his rage. What stayed with her most was not a single
complete scene but scattered pieces — the flash of blood, the sense of being pinned in by his body,
the sound of anger in his voice — and even now she found herself cutting off the memory before it
could become whole. When she tried to recall it, she would feel a surge of guilt, as if remembering
meant she had failed in some way, and the missing parts only made her more certain that something in
her had been permanently altered for the worse. She had come to believe that people could not be
trusted, and that belief was not abstract to her; it shaped how she read faces, how she judged tone,
and how quickly she withdrew when she sensed even mild tension.

In her daily life, Amina noticed how tightly those old cues still held her. If someone stood too
close to her in a hallway, or if a colleague or family member expressed anger — even in a calm,
controlled way — she felt her body brace before she could reason with herself. At night, especially,
her world narrowed; she said it was harder to feel safe after dark than during the day, and she
often found herself alert to every sound in the house. She would check behind her when walking from
her car or through the building where she still did occasional political consulting, scanning for
someone following her. The proximity of another body, a raised voice, or the sight of blood could
pull her attention sharply back to what had happened, and in those moments her mind would fragment
again, the missing parts returning as a blankness that felt ominous rather than comforting. Each
time this happened, she became more convinced that the abuse had left her permanently damaged and
that she would never feel normal emotions again, which in turn made her more watchful and more
easily startled.

That sense of being changed for the worse fed back into the way she remembered the past. When a
memory surfaced — perhaps a partial image during the night, or a fragment triggered by someone
standing too close at a campaign event — the guilt was immediate and intense, and she would push the
recollection away before it could fully form. Yet the very act of pushing it aside left her with
only broken pieces, and those broken pieces made her more suspicious of what had happened and of
other people around her. If she could not hold the memory together, she reasoned, then she could not
trust her own mind, and if she could not trust her own mind, she could not trust anyone else either.
That mistrust made ordinary encounters feel dangerous. A hand placed too near her shoulder, a driver
who stepped too close while speaking sharply, even the sight of blood on television could make her
flinch and tighten her guard, as if the old danger might be beginning again.

Over months, these reactions had narrowed Amina’s life. She had become more selective about where
she sat in meetings, how late she stayed out, and which social events she attended, especially in
the evening. At work, she could still perform with composure, but she found herself listening for
anger in people’s voices and watching exits as much as faces, and this constant scanning left her
exhausted. The more she stayed on alert, the harder it became to settle her mind, and the more her
memories arrived in fragments that confirmed how unsafe she felt. Those fragments, in turn, deepened
her conviction that she had been permanently altered and that people were not to be trusted. That
belief made her more sensitive to closeness, to anger, and to blood, and each new jolt reinforced
the sense that the world remained dangerous in the same old way.

She finally sought help because the pattern was no longer something she could manage quietly. Amina
was troubled by how often she was checking behind her, how easily she startled, and how much
nighttime had become a period of dread rather than rest. She was also frightened by the way the
memory still came only in pieces, with guilt attached to every fragment, and she hoped that speaking
about it might help her feel less permanently changed than she now feared.
