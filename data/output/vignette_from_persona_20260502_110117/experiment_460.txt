though his life had narrowed around a single assault that he had never been able to put fully into
the past. The event had happened after work, when he had been drinking with men he thought were
mates; he remembered the smell of alcohol on one of them, the blur of a motorcycle pulling up
outside, and then the humiliation and violation of being sexually assaulted when he was too stunned
to fight effectively. Since then, he had found that ordinary news reports about assaults or violence
could catch him off guard and leave him abruptly tense, as if the story on the radio were not a
story at all but a doorway back into that night. The same happened with the hard mechanical whine of
a motorcycle outside his home or down on the street near the building sites where he worked. Those
sounds had become potent because they seemed to arrive with the force of the original moment, and
the smell of alcohol could do the same, bringing back the assault with a blunt immediacy that made
him feel exposed and trapped.

What distressed Rangi most was the way his memory of the event did not behave like a normal
recollection. He described it as more real and vivid than his memories of ordinary life, as if it
sat close to the surface and could be entered without warning. Sometimes, when a trigger hit him, he
did not get a clear picture at all but instead felt the full emotional intensity of the
assault—shame, fear, and a heavy grief that seemed to come from nowhere—while the scene itself
stayed blurred. At other times, a flash of the episode would feel so immediate that he could almost
smell the alcohol again and hear the motorcycle in the background. When he remembered what had
happened, the grief was overwhelming; he would go quiet, look away, and feel as though he had lost
something that could never be recovered, including the easy confidence he had once had in other men
and in his own body.

In the months that followed, Rangi began building habits around control, partly because he felt his
body had betrayed him and partly because he wanted to avoid anything that might leave him physically
stressed and therefore vulnerable. He stopped pushing himself at work when the roof pitch was steep
or the weather made the job punishing, and he tried to arrange his day so he would not have to
strain too hard. At home, he became strict about eating, telling himself that if he could control
what went in, he could at least control something. He also worked hard at keeping busy with other
things, filling any empty time with chores, errands, and extra tasks for friends and family. These
strategies brought only brief relief. The more tightly he managed himself, the more his body felt
like something he had to supervise, and the more likely it seemed that a remembered sensation—a
burst of exertion on the roof, the taste or smell of alcohol at a gathering, even a news clip
playing in the background—would pull him back into the event. He found that the very effort to stay
in control made the assault feel closer, because it kept his attention fixed on how easily he might
be overwhelmed.

That same pattern worked in the other direction as well. Because the memories felt so vivid and
emotionally charged, Rangi became more determined to avoid situations that reminded him of them. He
steered clear of social drinking, declined invitations where alcohol would be present, and altered
his routine so he would not have to pass places where motorcycles congregated. At work he preferred
jobs that kept him away from particularly taxing physical demands, and he would make excuses if a
task seemed likely to leave him winded or sore. Yet the more he avoided strain and the more he
filled his days with busyness, the smaller his world became, and the more startling the reminders
felt when they did break through. Even a news item on the television in the lunchroom could catch
him because he had been trying so hard not to think about what happened. The pattern left him
increasingly isolated from his coworkers and more withdrawn with his wife, who noticed that he was
always occupied but never at ease.

By the time he sought help, Rangi was exhausted by the constant effort to outrun what kept
returning. He was afraid that if he let up on control, even briefly, the memories would flood back
with the same force and leave him overwhelmed with grief and shame; at the same time, he was hoping
that therapy might help him feel less ruled by those moments when a sound, a smell, or a news report
seemed to throw him straight back into the assault.
