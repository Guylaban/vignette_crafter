Huy Nguyen had spent most of his adult life in courtrooms and detention centers as a public
defender, a man accustomed to staying composed while other people unraveled. After his wife died, he
was already living with a quiet loneliness, but the event that changed him happened on an ordinary
winter afternoon when he was driving home from work and saw the wreck that had taken her life: a
crumpled car at an intersection, broken glass glittering on the pavement, the shriek of a passing
siren, and her face only half-visible before the ambulance doors shut. Since then, when he tried to
remember that day, the memory would not come back as a full story. It arrived in disconnected
fragments — the sound of metal, the smell of fuel, his own hands gripping the steering wheel, the
fluorescent light inside the ambulance, and, most painfully, the sudden wave of grief that seemed to
break over him all at once. On bad days he said it was as if he could hear the siren again inside
his head, even when the room was silent.

Those broken pieces made him feel less certain of what had happened and less able to put the event
behind him. Because the memory kept returning in fragments and with that intrusive sound, he found
himself scanning the world for danger, especially for anything that resembled the accident: traffic
piling up outside his apartment, flashing lights reflected in a shop window, the wail of emergency
vehicles in the distance. At the office, where he spent long hours reading case files and meeting
clients in the public defender’s cramped, fluorescent-lit waiting room, he became vigilant for
dangerous people, studying men’s faces too long, startled by raised voices, and reacting as if he
were in danger even when nothing was actually wrong. A slammed door in the hallway could make his
chest tighten before he had time to think. He began checking his front door, the windows in his
small apartment, and the lock on his car again and again, convinced that if he did not stay on guard
something terrible could happen to the people left in his life.

That constant threat feeling fed the other habits he had fallen into. He would avoid television
programs and films about crashes, hospitals, or violent loss, and he stopped reading the newspaper
features that might contain similar stories, because even a brief scene could bring back the broken
memory and the hearing of that siren in his mind. After particularly bad evenings, he found himself
eating more than usual, not because he was hungry but because the food gave him something immediate
to focus on when the images and fear would not let go. Yet the more he tried to distract himself,
the more alert he became to every sound in the building and every passerby on his street, as if his
own efforts to settle himself had only taught his body that danger was always near. When he was
especially keyed up, the fragments of the accident felt closer and less contained; the grief rushed
back, and the memory seemed to sharpen around the same sights and sounds he was trying not to think
about.

Over the following months this pattern narrowed his life. He started taking longer routes home to
avoid the intersection where the crash had happened, and he declined social invitations that might
require him to drive after dark. Colleagues noticed that he ate lunch alone in his office more often
and seemed jumpy when anyone came up behind him. He was ashamed of how much fear and anger he
carried, and he hated that he could not seem to trust his own senses anymore. The more he checked,
avoided, and tried to distract himself, the more convinced he became that danger could appear
anywhere and that he had to remain ready for it. That sense of being on alert also made the memories
come back in harsher, more broken flashes, especially at night, when the siren sound seemed to cut
through the silence and leave him with the same overwhelming grief all over again.

By the time he sought help, Huy was exhausted by how much of his day was organized around staying
safe from feelings and images he could not control. He came to treatment because he feared he was
becoming isolated and because he worried that one more jolt — one more siren, one more sudden sound,
one more glimpse of a wrecked car — would throw him back into the same terror and grief he had been
fighting alone. What he hoped for most was not to forget his wife, but to be able to remember her
death without feeling as if he were reliving it, and to stop living as though danger were waiting
just beyond every locked door.
