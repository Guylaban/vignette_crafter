Minh was 24, a South Eastern Asian man working as a painter, when he first came to treatment after
months of feeling as if his own life had become arranged around one brutal stretch of time with an
ex-partner. The abuse had happened behind closed doors in the apartment they once shared, in the
ordinary spaces of a relationship that had looked stable from the outside. He described one night
when an argument escalated and his partner shoved him hard against a wall, then struck him
repeatedly while he froze, unable to move or speak. What stayed with Minh was not a clean sequence
but fragments: the edge of a table, the sound of his own breathing, his partner’s face looming and
then disappearing. Some moments were painfully clear, almost lit up in his mind, while other parts
were blank. When he tried to tell the story, he could not put it in order, and that confusion made
the whole event feel unfinished, as if it could happen again at any point.

Since then, certain moments had become charged with the same terror. If he found himself thinking
unwanted thoughts about that night, or if a room felt uncertain in a way he could not name, he would
immediately start scanning for exits as soon as he entered a building. At work, in clients’ homes or
half-empty apartments where he was painting walls, he found himself checking doorways and hallways
before he even set down his brushes. He became especially uneasy when he felt watched or observed,
as if someone were standing behind him and judging whether he was alert enough. Those moments pulled
him back toward the memory itself: the scene would return with a rush of bodily fear, and he would
feel as if it were happening right now rather than something that had ended. The more the memory
broke through, the more he interpreted it as evidence that he was unsafe, and that he was somehow
the sort of person to whom violence happened because he was inadequate and did not belong anywhere.
That belief made him more vigilant, more suspicious of every pause or glance, and it also made the
triggers themselves broader; even uncertainty at the end of a conversation, or the thought that
someone might be looking at him, could set off the same alarm.

As the weeks passed, Minh’s life narrowed around trying not to be caught off guard. He listened for
sounds that might mean someone was entering his home, especially at night, and if a noise came
through the walls or from the front door, he would lie rigid, waiting. He began avoiding the dreams
he knew tended to come on at certain times of day or when he allowed himself to rest, so he stayed
up late, scrolling on his phone or keeping the television on low, hoping to outrun sleep. When he
felt threatened, even by a raised voice or a sudden movement, he froze completely, and afterward he
hated himself for not reacting. Each time he froze or avoided a reminder of the person who harmed
him, the memory seemed to lose its place in time and become even harder to organize; he would recall
a hand, a shout, the pressure of being pinned, but the order remained confused, and the gaps made
him trust his own mind less. In turn, that uncertainty deepened his belief that he was inadequate,
and the shame sharpened his sense that he no longer belonged anywhere—not in the relationship he had
left, not in his family’s home, and not even in the rooms where he worked. Because he expected
danger in delays, he grew impatient and panicked if a client was late or if a call went unanswered,
assuming something bad was happening. His whole body stayed poised for threat, and that constant
scanning fed back into the same unwanted thoughts and watched feeling that had first opened the
spiral.

By the time he sought help, Minh was exhausted by the cycle of checking, avoiding, and bracing
himself. He was still in a relationship, but he found himself struggling to stay present with his
partner when ordinary silence or a long pause made him feel cornered. He had begun to worry that if
he kept living this way, he would lose his work, his sleep, and eventually the ability to trust
anyone at all. What brought him in was the fear that the memories were becoming stronger instead of
fading, and that his own sense of where he belonged was slipping further away each week. He hoped,
with some shame and urgency, that treatment might help him feel safe enough to sleep without dread
and to stand in a room without immediately searching for the door.
