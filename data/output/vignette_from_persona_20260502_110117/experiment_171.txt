while repairing a damaged section of an older building, left him suddenly overwhelmed and frightened
in a way he had never experienced. What he remembers most is not a single dramatic image but the
details that lodged in him: the sharp hospital smell when he was taken for treatment, the dark of
the ambulance and later the way nighttime in the ward seemed to close in around him, and the
sickening, related chemical smell that seemed to cling to his clothes and skin. Since then, those
same smells and any waking moment in the dark have become potent reminders; when they appear,
Somchai immediately pulls back from the feeling, tries hard not to think about what happened, and
clamps down on his reactions, because he fears that if he lets himself stay with it he will lose
control.

Yet the more he tries to shut it out, the more the event returns. He describes the memories as a
film that plays over and over, vividly detailed, with the same sequence of events arriving uninvited
and, at times, without any clear picture at all—just the full emotional intensity flooding him back
as if his body remembers before his mind does. Those recollections have fed the sense that he cannot
rely on himself, that he failed to protect his own body once and may do so again. He has come to
believe that his future is already damaged, that there is no real future left to plan for, and that
the world is a dangerous place that can turn hazardous without warning. Those beliefs have shaped
the way he lives. He avoids unfamiliar places because he cannot assess the danger there, and he has
become careful in the extreme never to disagree or assert himself, even with clients or family,

how he used to be before the event—stronger, calmer, less guarded—and that comparison leaves him
more convinced that the man he was is gone for good.

In daily life, these patterns keep the alarm going. When a faint hospital-like odor drifts through a
clinic waiting room, or when a smell related to the chemicals on the job appears in a garage,

the ambulance, and he then redoubles his efforts to avoid thinking about it. The attempt to suppress
the memories often leaves him more keyed up, unable to relax even when he knows he is in a safe
place, and his concentration slips at work and at home. On the job he has begun staying close to
familiar houses and routes, turning down unfamiliar assignments, and letting others make the
decisions so he does not have to face anything uncertain; at home, he becomes more withdrawn and

it. That bracing keeps his attention fixed on possible threats, and the constant scanning only seems
to pull up more fragments of the event, as if the mind cannot stop rehearsing what happened.

night. Waking in the dark now brings the whole chain back into motion, and he lies tense and
watchful, certain that he cannot relax and afraid that the next unfamiliar place, the next smell, or
the next memory will overtake him again. What finally brought him in was the fear that his shrinking
world—work narrowed to what feels safe, conversation softened to avoid disagreement, thoughts

that the future, even at 66, is not already lost.
