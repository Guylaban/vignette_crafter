Marianne, a 51-year-old European American civil servant, had been working for years in a municipal
office where the routine of paperwork and public enquiries usually suited her. The event that
changed that sense of order happened at a workplace storage facility, when a sealed container of
toxic chemicals was opened unexpectedly and a sharp, choking odour spread through the room. People
started speaking at once, voices overlapping in a way she could not make sense of, and although no
one seemed to know exactly what was happening, she remembers the confusion more than any clear
sequence. In the first days afterward, whenever someone talked about the incident or mentioned the
facility, Marianne found herself going blank. She would start to explain what had happened and then
lose the thread completely, as if the details were there but unreachable, and even when she tried
deliberately to call the memory up, she could not get hold of it in words. What remained was
fragmentary and nonverbal: the smell, the sense of danger, and the feeling that her body had
understood something her mind could not yet say.

That inability to explain the event became part of what frightened her. Marianne noticed that when
colleagues asked her what had happened, she would rehearse her answers in her head before speaking,
and still the moment of telling often collapsed into hesitation or silence. Because the memory
stayed so hard to access and so difficult to put into language, she began to avoid any situation
that might expose that gap: she kept away from hospitals and ambulances, and even a routine physical
examination made her tense, as if medical settings might force her back toward the thing she could
not fully describe. At the same time, the event’s uncanny qualities made ordinary unpredictability
feel dangerous. A sudden change in plan, a noisy room, or incomprehensible chatter in a corridor
could bring back the same sense of being thrown into confusion, and she would immediately feel on
edge. She became especially watchful when people approached without warning, including strangers in
ordinary places, because her body had come to expect that danger could arrive just as abruptly as it
had that day. Unexpected or loud sounds kept her on high alert, and she described feeling safe only
in a very limited number of places, usually her own home or a few familiar routes she had mapped out
carefully.

Over time, Marianne’s efforts to manage this fear began to shape the very situations that made it
worse. She rehearsed conversations before nearly any interaction, from a brief exchange at work to a
greeting at the door, trying to prevent herself from freezing or losing her words. But the more she
relied on rehearsing, the more she noticed that any unscripted moment felt risky, and that
heightened vigilance made her scan for danger in unfamiliar voices, approaching people, and sudden
noises all the more intensely. Physical contact became another source of strain: she would pull back
from a handshake, stiffen if someone brushed past her, and avoid being touched in almost any
context, which left her feeling more separate and more exposed when she was out in public. Even in
the civil service office, where she had once moved comfortably among colleagues and visitors, she
now sat rigidly at her desk, dreading interruptions and trying to keep her world narrow enough to
feel manageable. The very habits she used to protect herself were gradually feeding the sense that
the world beyond those few safe places was unsafe, and the more she narrowed her life, the more
threatening ordinary contact and everyday movement seemed.

By the time she sought help, Marianne was distressed by how tightly the fear, the fractured memory,
and the avoidance had become linked. Talking about the incident was still enough to make the memory
fragment, and the fragmenting memory in turn kept the event vivid and unfinished, as though it might
break through at any moment in a smell, a sound, or a stranger’s sudden approach. She was exhausted
by the constant rehearsing, the vigilance around noise and people, and the way she kept herself
protected by staying in only the places that felt familiar. What finally brought her to treatment
was the realization that she was no longer simply avoiding the toxic exposure itself, but also
shrinking away from the ordinary parts of life that reminded her of it. She hoped that therapy might
help her make sense of what had happened and find a way to speak about it without losing the thread,
though what she feared most was that any fuller reminder would once again leave her flooded by the
same helpless sense of threat.
