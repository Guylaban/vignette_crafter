Daniel, a 35-year-old special education teacher of European background, came to treatment describing
a mugging that had left him feeling as though ordinary life had become threaded with danger. It had
happened late one winter evening after he stayed after school to finish paperwork and call a parent
back before driving home. As he walked to his car, a man stepped out from between two parked
vehicles, shouted at him, and demanded his wallet. The noise, the suddenness of the confrontation,
and the stranger’s proximity were overwhelming; Daniel later said that the memory of the man’s face,
the angle of the streetlight, and the feel of his own body pinned in place came back “like
photographs,” each image fixed and unnervingly clear. In the days that followed, anything that
echoed that moment could pull him back into it. A student shouting across the classroom, the sharp
ache in his neck after a tense day, or the sight of police in the hallway outside his school office
would jolt him with the same tight fear he had felt that night, and the images would return in a
rush, vivid and exact, as if the event were unfolding again.

What had been most distressing to Daniel was not only the robbery itself but the meaning he had made
of it afterward. He had come to believe that somebody else would not have gotten into that
situation, that he should have been more alert, more capable, more in control. He saw himself as
inadequate, especially because he worked in a role that required him to be composed and steady with
vulnerable children. That self-judgment sat close to his body. He felt he had to be on guard all the
time, and even when he was home in his apartment or sitting in the staff room at school, he could
not relax in any place that should have felt safe. His shoulders stayed raised, his neck tight, and
he found himself scanning exits as soon as he entered any room or building, automatically checking
where people stood and how quickly he could leave. The more he watched for danger, the more the
robbery seemed to come back to him; a flash of memory, often sparked by shouting or by the sudden
sight of a uniformed officer, would break through and make him feel as if he were there again.

Those recollections did not come as distant stories but as immediate, bodily episodes. Daniel would
remember the attacker’s voice and his own helpless stillness with such force that rage surged
through him, a hot and humiliating anger at being targeted and at himself for feeling frozen. At the
same time, guilt followed close behind, because he replayed every detail and believed he should have
done something different, perhaps made a quicker choice, noticed the man sooner, or protected
himself better. Once those images and emotions took hold, his sense of present safety collapsed. He
became even more unable to relax, and the heightened watchfulness made the world seem full of
possible repeats. In turn, that constant state of alarm sharpened his response to triggers: shouting
from students in his classroom, the stiffness in his neck after leaning over desks, and the sight of
police near the school now carried a different weight because he was already primed to expect
threat. Each encounter seemed to confirm that danger was close by, and each confirmation fed his
belief that he had to stay on guard and that he was inadequate if he did not.

Over the months, Daniel began organizing his life around this pattern. He arrived early to school so
he could choose a seat with a clear view of the doors, and he avoided late meetings when he could.
Even then, he found himself tracking every raised voice in the building and every tension in his own
body, as though either might signal that he was about to be caught off guard again. What finally
brought him to seek help was the sense that the vigilance was spreading into everything: he was
exhausted, increasingly isolated, and frightened by how quickly a shout or a sudden look from police
could make him feel transported back to the street corner. He said he wanted to stop living as if he
were always one step away from being mugged again, but he was most afraid that if he let his guard
down, he would discover he really was too inadequate to protect himself.
