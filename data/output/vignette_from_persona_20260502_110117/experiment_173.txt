through habit, pride, and the expectations of family. The assault had happened years earlier, but

one painfully exact, with empty spaces between them that she could not fill in no matter how hard
she tried. Those snapshots returned most forcefully when something in the present resembled the

narrow aisles at work after the store had quieted, her body would tense before she could think. The
same happened when she saw people misbehaving in public, pushing past others or acting
disrespectfully; it stirred the same sense that boundaries could be crossed without consequence.
Smoke or a fire smell could also catch her abruptly, carrying her back to the sensory edge of that
evening and making the images arrive with unnerving immediacy.

Over time, Asha had come to believe that what happened to her was deeply unfair and that nothing had
been done about it. The injustice of it sat with her as much as the assault itself. She felt that
the world had simply moved on while she remained the one carrying the damage, and that thought made
her feel that nothing in her life felt meaningful anymore. She also judged herself harshly, saying
she could not deal with even the slightest upset, as if the smallest problem proved how fragile she
had become. Those beliefs made the triggers heavier. When she saw men misbehaving or found herself
alone with men at work, she did not just feel alarm; she felt the old wrongness rise in her mind,
proof to her that people could do harm and face no real consequences. A whiff of smoke in the
neighborhood or from a nearby cooking accident could produce the same bitterness, because it
reminded her of how quickly danger had entered her life and how helpless she had been to stop it.
Each reminder seemed to confirm that what happened was not only terrifying but profoundly unjust,
and that she had been left to live in a world that no longer felt worth much.

The memory also tightened the grip of the triggers. Asha said the pictures in her mind came first
and made everything else sharper: once one fragment arrived, the rest of the world seemed to line up
around it. If a customer at the store raised his voice, if a man lingered near her when she was
stocking shelves, or if she caught that sharp smell of smoke from a passing truck or someone’s burnt
food, the snapshots would flash up and she would feel as though she were back in those few moments
again. The unclear stretches of the assault were almost worse in some ways, because the blankness
left room for dread to fill in what she could not remember. She found herself scanning men’s faces,
watching for threat, and becoming increasingly unwilling to be alone in the back rooms or during

would not have to turn out onto the floor alone with male customers or staff. At home, she avoided
community gatherings where men might crowd the doorway or stand too near. The more she withdrew, the
more isolated she felt, and the more her life seemed stripped of meaning.

In the months before seeking treatment, these patterns had settled into her daily routine. Asha kept
functioning, but only by narrowing her world. She hurried through shifts, avoided certain aisles
when the store was quiet, and left social visits early if she sensed disrespect or heard an
argument. Even minor upsets now felt intolerable to her, not because they were objectively large,
but because they seemed to confirm her belief that she had no capacity left. That belief fed back
into her vigilance: if she was so easily overwhelmed, she reasoned, she had to stay alert all the
time. And the more alert she became, the more often she noticed the very things that unsettled her —
the rough behavior of strangers, a man standing too close, a trace of smoke drifting through the air
— each one reviving the photographs in her mind and the conviction that she had never been made
whole after what was done to her. By the time she finally sat down in the clinic, she was exhausted
by the constant checking, ashamed of how small her world had become, and afraid that if she did not
get help she would keep living as if the assault were still happening. What she most hoped for was
some way to feel that her life could matter again, and what she feared most was that the images, the
injustice, and the sense of being unable to cope would never let her go.
