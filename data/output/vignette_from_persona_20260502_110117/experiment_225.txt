Daniel Vega was 25, a single Latin American American man working as a data scientist, and by the
time he came for help he had become known at work for being calm, efficient, and hard to read. That
steadiness had been forged three years earlier, when his infant daughter’s fever had turned into a
cascade of hospital admissions, alarms, and late-night consultations that seemed to erase any
ordinary sense of time. He remembered the emergency department as a blur of fluorescent light and
hurried footsteps, his daughter limp in his arms while he was told she might not make it through the
night. The memory still did not sit in order for him; he could not put the sequence of tests,
decisions, and phone calls together properly, and parts of it felt so extreme that he sometimes had
to stop and remind himself it had happened to him at all. Yet when he tried to recall it, the same
terror returned in full, as if his body had been dropped back into that room.

Certain moments had become potent in a way he found hard to explain to others. Sirens on the street
outside his apartment, the sharp smell of sweat or body odour on the subway, or hearing a child cry
in the distance could all jar him into an instant sense that something was wrong. Those cues pulled
him back toward the hospital and, at the same time, into the present; his chest tightened, his pulse
spiked, and he jumped at ordinary sounds that would not bother most people. Because the reminders
hit so quickly and so vividly, he had come to brace himself for them everywhere. He avoided
hospitals, ambulances, and physical examinations whenever he could, crossing streets to keep away
from the entrance of his building’s urgent care clinic and inventing reasons not to go to medical
appointments.

Daniel’s efforts to stay away from reminders gradually shaped his days. At work, he would mute his
phone alerts, keep the office door closed, and ask to work from home after any incident in the
neighbourhood that brought an ambulance nearby. He never allowed himself to feel or express anger,
telling himself that any anger would be selfish or dangerous, and he tried hard not to think about
the trauma at all. That deliberate pushing away made the memory more fragmented rather than less;
when it intruded, it returned in pieces, and the missing order made the whole episode feel even less
believable. The less he wanted to think about it, the more abruptly the images broke through,
usually attached to the first thing that resembled the original scene.

The trouble was that his vigilance never really switched off. He had difficulty falling asleep
because he felt he needed to stay alert, as if sleep would leave him unprepared for another
emergency, and he noticed strong physical reactions whenever something reminded him of what had
happened. A siren outside his apartment could send him to the window before he realized he was
moving; a crying child in a café could make him freeze, shoulders rigid, scanning for signs of
danger. Those bodily surges reinforced the sense that threat was still nearby, and the more his body
reacted, the more he searched for the next reminder. Even the smell of sweat on a crowded train
could make him feel the hospital was close again, which was enough to send him avoiding the train on
future days or choosing routes and schedules that made unexpected encounters less likely.

Over time, Daniel’s attempt to manage the fear by suppressing anger and thought began to narrow his
world. He found that when he shut down his reactions and refused to dwell on the illness, the
memories came back less as a story than as raw terror, and that terror seemed to make every sound,
smell, and cry feel more dangerous. He became more alert to those cues, and his alertness made it
easier to notice them, which kept the cycle moving. At work he was still functional, but he had
stopped staying late if it meant walking home after dark when sirens seemed more frequent, and he
had declined invitations to visit colleagues’ families at the hospital after births or surgeries. He
knew these choices protected him in the short term, but they also left him feeling cut off and on
guard, as though he were still waiting for the next emergency.

What finally brought Daniel to therapy was not a single dramatic collapse but the accumulation of
nights spent lying awake, listening for sounds he could not control, and days spent jumping at
harmless noises while trying to look unbothered in meetings. He was most afraid that if he let
himself feel anger or think clearly about what happened to his daughter, the terror would become
overwhelming again; at the same time, he hoped that if he could understand why the memory still
arrived in fragments and why sirens, sweat, and crying kept pulling him back, he might finally sleep
without feeling he had to stand guard over the world.
