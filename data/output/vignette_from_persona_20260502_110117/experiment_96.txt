violence. He had survived the bombing of a relief convoy years earlier, an attack that still came

metal of the truck, the dust hanging in the air, the faces of people he had been speaking with only
seconds before. When he tried to describe it, he would often stop and say it seemed hard to believe
it had happened to him at all, as if the scene belonged to someone else’s life. What remained most
vividly were pictures and images, and sometimes, when the memory surfaced, he would hear the same
sounds in his head that had cut through the explosion—sirens, shouting, the high, metallic ringing
that followed the blast.

Those fragments were enough to keep Minh turning the event over and over in his mind. Because the
memory felt so extreme and unreal, he kept searching it for what he had done wrong, repeatedly
blaming himself for not noticing danger sooner, for continuing down the route, for failing the
people who had trusted him to get them through safely. The more he replayed it, the more certain he
became that ordinary life could turn suddenly catastrophic. He was increasingly watchful in a way
his wife recognized immediately: scanning entrances, watching the road before crossing, sitting
where he could see people coming and going. Over time, he came to believe that safety existed only
in a very small number of places—his home, a particular corner of the living room, the quiet back
seat of his own house—not in the wider world where he once moved so freely.

tension. He told himself that keeping peace was safer than standing his ground, and the effort to

searching for what might happen next. The sounds did not merely startle him; they pulled him back
toward the attack, and because of that he became even more convinced that he needed to keep watch
