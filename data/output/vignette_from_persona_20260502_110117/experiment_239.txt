Raúl Mendez was a 70-year-old widowed dentist who had spent most of his life in a busy Latin
American immigrant neighborhood, treating generations of families in the same small office near a
downtown bus line. The terrorist attack that changed him happened years earlier while he was
crossing a public square after work, still in his white coat, when a vehicle and armed men turned a
crowded evening into chaos. He remembered the sudden crack of gunshots, the shouting, the uniforms
moving through the smoke, and the terrible confusion of not knowing who was protecting whom. Even
now, when the memory returned, it did not come to him as a clear story; it hit him in fragments so
vivid that he would have to stop speaking, his chest tightening as if he were back among the noise,
the panic, and the sight of bodies diving for cover. He said he could not seem to put the
frightening event into words, and that was part of why it kept coming back with such force. When it
did, the whole scene overwhelmed him emotionally, as if the terror were happening again through all
his senses at once.

Over time, certain things around him became dangerous in a way that seemed obvious to him. A man in
a police uniform entering his building, a television discussion about military action, or the sharp
pop of a car backfiring outside the clinic could drop him into the same state of alarm. Those
moments often carried him straight back to the attack, and he would feel the memory surge up before
he could steady himself. He became intensely watchful when he was out in the waiting room or walking
to his car, scanning the faces of other people for signs that they might mean harm, and checking
exits as soon as he entered a room or building. In the office, where he had once moved with
practiced ease, he now positioned himself near the door and noticed every sudden sound in the
hallway. He described feeling on high alert whenever he heard something unexpected or loud, as
though another blast could happen without warning. The triggers and the memory fed one another: a
uniform, a loud noise, or even a conversation about soldiers would revive the sensory force of the
event, and once the memory took hold, it made those same cues feel even more charged the next time
they appeared.

Raúl tried to protect himself by managing the fear in ways that seemed sensible at first. He began
avoiding news reports about attacks or conflicts that reminded him of what had happened, and he
carried a weapon more often than before, telling himself that if he stayed prepared he might not be
helpless again. Yet these habits came with a cost. The more he replayed the event in his mind, the
more he blamed himself for not having done enough that day, and that self-accusation kept the scene
active rather than settled. At night he would go over the square, the gunfire, and the uniforms
again and again, searching for the moment he believed he should have acted differently. Those
periods of rumination often ended with the same wave of sensory recollection, and once the memory
returned so vividly, he was left too overwhelmed to think in a calmer way. Afterward he would notice
that he was more likely to avoid the news again, more likely to keep the weapon close, and more
certain that danger was always near. His efforts to control the fear also seemed to teach him to
look harder for it: carrying the weapon and replaying the event made him watch people more closely,
scan every doorway, and stay braced for loud sounds, which in turn kept the attack feeling present.

In the clinic and in the neighborhood, this pattern had narrowed his life. He no longer stayed for
coffee after work and rarely attended community gatherings where military topics might come up or
where uniformed officers were present. He had become more isolated since his wife died, and his
adult daughter worried that he seemed to be living as if the attack were still waiting outside the
office door. What finally brought him to seek help was the growing burden of constantly being on
guard, especially after he startled badly at a sudden bang in the dental lab and spent the rest of
the day watching every person who walked past the front window. Raúl said he was tired of carrying
fear everywhere and tired of the way it kept pulling him back into the same images and the same
blame. What he feared most was that he would never feel safe enough to let his guard down, but what
he hoped for was simpler: to walk into a room, hear an unexpected sound, and not have his whole body
tell him that the terror was starting again.
