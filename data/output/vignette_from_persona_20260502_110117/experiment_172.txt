Daniel had built a life around language, ritual, and the steady rhythm of synagogue days, but at 63
he still found himself returning in his mind to the years when he was a refugee and the world had
taught him that safety could vanish in a matter of seconds. He had fled as a young man from an armed
crackdown, moving through checkpoints and crowded stations where shouting meant danger and the flash
of a weapon could change a room instantly. He remembered the humiliation of being grabbed from
behind in a crush at a border crossing, unprepared hands on his shoulders while men yelled and
rifles were raised, and he had never quite shaken the sense that his body had been made for alarm.
Even now, if someone touched him without warning in the hallway of the synagogue or at a crowded
Shabbat table, he would jolt and go rigid before he could think. A raised voice in a meeting, even
when it was only someone’s excitement, could pull him back to the old fear so sharply that he would
lose his place mid-sentence. When he saw a police officer’s holster, a hunting knife at a family
gathering, or even a tool that looked weapon-like, his attention narrowed and his chest tightened,
as if the next second might be the one that mattered.

Those moments did not end with the startle itself. After an unexpected touch or the sight of
something weapon-like, Daniel would immediately pull inward and begin punishing himself in private,
telling himself he deserved to suffer for surviving when others had not. He would distract himself
with study, prayer books, email, or paperwork, staying busy so he would not have to sit with what
had been stirred up, and he had increasingly avoided emotional closeness with his partner, fearing
that intimacy would expose how fragile he really was. At first these habits seemed like ways to get
through the day, but they had become part of how he lived: a reflex to numb, withdraw, and deny
himself comfort whenever the old fear was touched.

What Daniel believed about himself sat close to the center of his distress. He was convinced that
his body had become a traitor, that it kept reacting as if he were still in danger even when he was
safe in his own study or walking home from synagogue. He could feel his concentration fray during
sermons, staff meetings, and even while reading Torah portions, and this made him worry that he
could no longer trust himself to remain steady in the role he had spent decades occupying. He often
thought that somebody else would have stopped the event from happening, that a braver or more
capable person would not have been swept up and powerless in the same way. Those appraisals fed his
sense that he was unsafe in the world and incompetent within it, and they left him scanning
constantly for where to sit, choosing the edge of the room, the wall seat, the aisle seat, any place
where he felt more protected. His world had narrowed to a handful of places that felt tolerable, and
anywhere else felt uncertain enough that he could not relax.

The narrowing itself made the symptoms harder to escape. Because Daniel was so watchful for
shouting, sudden contact, and anything that resembled a weapon, he began noticing them everywhere: a
congregant calling across the room, a hand landing on his back before he had time to brace, a
ceremonial object that briefly looked like a blade under bright light. Each time, his alarm spiked
and he recoiled into distance or busied himself with something else, and afterward he was harsher
with himself than before, which only deepened his belief that his body was unreliable and that he
had failed in some basic way. That self-criticism kept the old image of himself as weak and
undeserving close at hand, and it made ordinary closeness feel risky, because intimacy invited being
seen in that state. Over months, he found himself sitting apart at dinners, leaving conversations
early, and taking extra routes through the building to avoid crowded hallways where a careless touch
might catch him off guard. His avoidance brought short-lived relief, but it also kept his world full
of reminders that he was not at ease anywhere except in a few tightly controlled spots.

By the time he came for help, Daniel was exhausted by how much effort it took to keep himself
composed. In his relationship he had become emotionally distant, and at work he worried that his
concentration slips and constant seat-checking were beginning to show. He sought treatment because
he no longer wanted to live as if danger were waiting in every room, and because the old conviction
that he deserved suffering had begun to feel less like discipline and more like a prison. What
frightened him most was the possibility that his body would never stop sounding the alarm, and what
he hoped for, quietly but sincerely, was to trust himself enough to remain present with the people
and places that still mattered to him.
