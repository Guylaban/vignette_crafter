Ibrahim had spent nearly forty years around explosives, and it was that expertise that made the day
of the chemical exposure feel so incomprehensible. He was working as a bomb disposal technician,
moving through a secured industrial site in heavy protective gear, when a ruptured container
released a toxic chemical cloud into the work area. The alarm went up, men shouted, and the sharp
sting of the air made his throat burn before he could get fully clear. He remembered the metallic
echo of voices through the mask, the rush of boots on concrete, and later the antiseptic smell of
the emergency department as he was checked over and decontaminated. Since then, those same sensory
fragments had become dangerous in their own right. A loud bang from a cart in the corridor, the
smell of disinfectant at a clinic, or even walking into his own workplace now pulled him back into
the moment of exposure with a force that felt immediate and bodily, as if the danger were already in
the room again.

In the months that followed, Ibrahim began organizing his life around the expectation of threat. He
was constantly scanning his surroundings for signs of danger, reading doorways, windows, parked
vehicles, and the spacing between people with a vigilance that never fully switched off. He always
identified escape routes before he felt comfortable anywhere, whether he was sitting in a staff
meeting, entering a restaurant, or waiting in the hallway outside an appointment. If someone touched
him unexpectedly, he reacted with alarm, his body jerking before he could stop it, embarrassed by
how quickly he went from composed to braced for impact. He had come to believe that if he was not
watching carefully enough, if he failed to anticipate where he could get out, something terrible
could happen again without warning. Even the workplace that had once given him confidence now fed
that fear: every time he returned to it, the routines of the job and the industrial surroundings
brought the exposure back to mind, and that renewed his certainty that danger could arrive anywhere,
without a clear signal.

Those same triggers also shaped what he began to do to keep himself steady. Ibrahim started avoiding
being alone in a room or building, telling himself that solitary spaces made it too easy for his
mind to drift back to the chemical release and the hospital. He avoided hospitals, ambulances, and
physical examinations, refusing routine care whenever he could and making excuses if the appointment
sounded even slightly invasive. At the same time, he began working excessively, taking on extra
shifts and lingering at the depot long after tasks were finished because staying busy was the only
way he knew to keep from being alone with his thoughts. The strategy helped briefly, but it also
left him exhausted and more keyed up by the end of the day, and that exhaustion made the world feel
even less manageable. The more he used work and constant company to stay away from quiet moments,
the more he arrived home already tense, already watching, already convinced that he had to stay one
step ahead.

Over time, that constant self-protection deepened the sense of danger rather than easing it. Because
Ibrahim spent so much effort avoiding the places and moments that reminded him of the incident, he
had fewer chances to feel settled in them again, and the alarms in his body stayed loud. He found
himself scanning more intensely whenever he was alone, and the moment he heard a sudden noise or
caught the antiseptic smell of a medical corridor, his whole attention narrowed to danger again. It
was in those moments that his startle response felt most humiliating to him, and he became even more
reluctant to go into hospitals or sit by himself in a quiet room where the sensations might catch
him off guard. At work, a loud noise from equipment or a shouted instruction could set the same
cycle in motion, leaving him jumpy, checking exits, and trying to outrun the feeling by burying
himself in another task.

By the time he sought help, Ibrahim was 62, still married, and increasingly worried that the life he
had built around control was shrinking. His wife had begun noticing how often he chose extra work
over sitting at home, how carefully he positioned himself near doors, and how quickly he tensed when
she touched his shoulder without warning. He came to treatment because he was tired of living as if
every room needed an exit plan and every unfamiliar smell or sound might mean catastrophe. What
frightened him most was the fear that he would never again feel safe enough to rest, and what he
hoped for, more quietly, was to be able to sit in a room with his wife, hear a sudden sound, and not
feel driven immediately back into danger.
