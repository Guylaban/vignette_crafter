Darnell, a 47-year-old North American bus driver who lives alone, came to treatment after months of
feeling as if his life had narrowed around one frightening night with police. He described being
pulled over late after his shift, when blue lights flashed in his mirrors and an officer ordered him
out of the bus while passengers watched. The officer’s tone was sharp, the scene public, and Darnell
said that what stayed with him most was not only the fear in the moment but the humiliating sense of
being trapped in front of strangers, unable to explain himself fast enough. Since then, even the
sight of police vehicles parked near his route, or hearing officers talk at a roadside stop, had
been enough to jolt him back into that moment. Talking about what happened in intake was almost
worse; as soon as he started to recount it, his thoughts would jump, his chest would tighten, and he
would suddenly cover his face with his hands as if he could hide from the memory by hiding from the
room.

Over time, Darnell began arranging his days around those reactions. When the memory came to mind
without warning, especially while he was driving past a station or seeing an officer on the
sidewalk, he would turn the radio up loud or leave the television blaring at home so that he would
not have to sit with the images in his head. He also started avoiding people more broadly, not just
police, because he had come to believe that once other people saw him afraid, they could judge him,
corner him, or use whatever they learned against him. That mistrust fed a darker view of the world:
he told the clinician that the world felt dangerous now, and that people could not be trusted. At
the same time, he felt he had lost his connection to his community and culture, saying that he no
longer felt like the man who used to know everyone on his route or stop to chat with neighbors after
work. The more he pulled back and covered his face, the more he sensed himself becoming separate
from the people and places that had once grounded him, and those feelings made the old police scene
feel newly potent whenever it was mentioned or unexpectedly brought to mind.

Darnell’s threat-related symptoms had also become part of his daily routine. He was irritable in a
way that startled him, snapping at dispatchers, cutting people off in traffic, and once slamming a
bus door hard enough that a passenger complained. He could not concentrate for long, especially on
unfamiliar routes, because part of his attention was always scanning for trouble. At home he
repeatedly checked the doors and windows, sometimes doubling back several times before bed,
convinced that if he missed something he would pay for it. He understood these behaviors as
necessary vigilance, because he had come to believe the world was dangerous and that trust was a
mistake. That belief, in turn, kept his body on edge. Small reminders of the police incident—a siren
in the distance, a marked car near his apartment, even the sound of someone saying “officer” on a
radio segment—could make him feel instantly more watchful and more sure that danger was near. When
his temper shortened and his attention scattered, he found himself avoiding people even more,
pulling the curtains shut, retreating behind loud music, and covering his face whenever he felt
exposed.

The cycle was wearing him down. His attempts to manage the fear by blocking it out or staying away
from others only seemed to make the old memory more intrusive and the outside world more
threatening. Avoiding people and drowning out the silence gave him brief relief, but it also
deepened his sense that he no longer belonged anywhere, which sharpened his mistrust and made every
police presence feel like proof that he had been right all along. The more he checked locks and
braced for confrontation, the more tired and irritable he became, and the more likely he was to
interpret ordinary encounters as unsafe. By the time he sought help, Darnell was exhausted by the
feeling that he was constantly on guard, afraid that a memory could hit him out of nowhere, or that
one more encounter with police or even one conversation about the event would send him right back
into the same panic and anger. What brought him in was his fear that he was becoming isolated and
unrecognizable to himself, and what he most hoped was that he could learn how to be around people
again without feeling that the world, and everyone in it, was waiting to turn on him.
