Leonie Varga, a 41-year-old firefighter, had always thought of herself as someone who ran toward
danger rather than away from it, but the illness that brought her close to death at the age of 39
changed the way her own body and surroundings were experienced. It began with what she first
dismissed as a stubborn flu, then escalated into a terrifying collapse that ended in an ambulance, a
hospital bed, and a blur of drips, needles, antiseptic wipes, and examination lights that seemed to
strip her of any sense of control. What stayed with her most was not a clear story she could tell,
but fragments: the sting of a needle, the cold metallic gleam of medical equipment, the sharp
hospital smell clinging to her clothes and hair, and the helpless knowledge that people were working
on her body while she could barely speak. Even now, when she tried to remember it, the experience
came back less as a narrative than as a bodily fact. She would freeze in a posture that felt the
same as lying in bed with her chest tight and her limbs heavy, and she would find herself unable to
put the event into words. The memory brought back the same terror she had felt then, so vividly that
she felt as if she were back in that room, waiting for something terrible to happen again.

In the months that followed, Leonie began to live as if danger might be hiding in ordinary places.
Her mind started scanning constantly, not only in hospitals but anywhere a bright corridor, a
trolley, or a white coat might appear. A whiff of antiseptic in a clinic hallway, the sight of a
drip stand in a television drama, or the feel of a doctor preparing for a physical examination could
jolt her body into immediate alarm. Those cues did not simply remind her of what happened; they made
her body tense and ready to react, her heart pounding and muscles braced before she had fully
registered what she was seeing. She was especially thrown by the smell of hospitals when she
accompanied her husband to a follow-up appointment, because it seemed to come with the same
helplessness she had felt during her illness, and once that bodily alarm took hold she began
searching harder for signs that something was wrong. The more her surroundings felt threatening, the
more intensely those medical cues stood out to her, as if her own vigilance was pulling them from
the background and fixing them in place. At work in the fire station, she found herself noticing
exits, checking faces, and watching for any sign that someone might suddenly become unwell, as
though keeping herself ready could prevent being caught off guard again.

Leonie came to dread any strong emotion because it seemed to open the door to the memory. If she
felt frightened, overwhelmed, or even too moved by a conversation, she would quickly redirect her
thoughts to anything else and try to flatten the feeling before it could gather strength. She
avoided talking about the illness, avoided thinking through what had happened, and steered clear of
hospitals, ambulances, and physical examinations whenever she could. Yet the very effort to shove
the memory away seemed to keep it alive in another form. When a reminder did break through, it
returned with the same terror and the same physical sensation of being trapped in the position she
had been in during the illness, and because she had never found words for it, the memory remained
raw and hard to organize. The more she pushed it aside, the less contained it felt; it stayed close
to the surface, surfacing in flashes whenever her body detected something even remotely similar. She
began declining visits to injured colleagues in hospital, then made excuses not to accompany her
husband to appointments, and over time those absences narrowed her world and strained the ease of
their life together. Leonie did not see this as avoidance in a simple sense. In her mind, she was
keeping herself from becoming overwhelmed, and from having the terror surge back so strongly that
she would lose control in front of others.

By the time she sought help, the pattern had become exhausting. She felt constantly tense and ready
to react, and the strain of scanning for danger had started to follow her into sleep, into meals,
and into quiet moments at the station when there was no immediate call to answer. She was not only
afraid of hospitals anymore; she was afraid of the feeling that any strong physical sensation or
sudden smell might send her body straight back into that frightening place. What finally brought her
to treatment was the realization that she could no longer keep working the same way without becoming
increasingly distant from her colleagues and her husband, and that her efforts to manage the fear by
thinking of something else had not made it smaller. Leonie hoped for relief from the constant alarm,
but she was most afraid that if she stopped guarding herself, the terror of the illness would rush
back in full force and leave her trapped in her body all over again.
