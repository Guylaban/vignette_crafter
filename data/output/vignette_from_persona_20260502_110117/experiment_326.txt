Ravi had spent most of his adult life as a firefighter in a large American city, but the event that
later defined his treatment had taken place years earlier, when he was deployed as a relief worker
in a war zone after shelling had torn through a residential district. One afternoon, while helping
pull survivors from a collapsed building, an explosion went off nearby with a force that seemed to
shake the street itself. In the confusion he heard men shouting, then the sudden silence that
followed, and he saw the aftermath of violence that made ordinary rules feel useless. What he has
never been able to fully settle in his mind is his own role in those hours; he came away feeling
that he had acted in ways that went against who he was. That conviction settled quickly into the
belief that his life had been destroyed by the trauma and that he no longer belonged anywhere, not
with the local families he had tried to help, and not later among the people back home who could not
understand what he had seen.

In the months that followed, Ravi began to react sharply to anything that resembled the war zone in
miniature. A bang from a dumpster lid slamming in the station bay, a mortar-like backfire on the
street, or even a sudden pop from a construction site could bring the event back with frightening
force. At those moments, the memory did not return as a clean story but as fragments he could not
put in the right order: the wall buckling, someone crying, his own hands moving before he could
think. He could not find words to describe what had happened, and when the memory came on, he felt
physical pain in the same parts of his body as during the event, as if the strain had been stored
there. The same was true when he encountered comments about sexual violence on the news or heard
someone making light of people’s suffering, or when he watched people misbehaving in ways that
struck him as cruel or reckless; those moments reopened the same scene and made him feel that the
world was again full of danger and disgrace. Each reminder fed his sense that he had crossed a moral
line and that his life had been irreparably altered, and he began to read even ordinary encounters
through that lens, seeing himself as cut off from others and permanently marked.

Those appraisals soon shaped his attempts to get through the day. Ravi would try hard not to think
about the trauma, forcing himself away from any image or thought that might surface. When the
feelings became too strong, he sometimes hurt himself physically just enough to release the
overwhelming pressure, later describing it as the only thing that made the inner noise stop for a
moment. He also performed private mental rituals, repeating phrases and checks in his head to
prevent bad things from happening again, as though enough precision might keep the past from
returning. Yet these efforts never stayed contained. The more he tried to suppress the memories, the
more they seemed to slip back in, jagged and incomplete, and the more his body registered them as
pain. In turn, the return of those fragments made him feel more threatened, more ashamed, and more
certain that he was not safe in his own mind. His fear and shame made him even more determined to
control his thoughts, and that control only kept the event alive.

Over time Ravi also became increasingly on guard. Strong negative feelings such as fear, horror,
anger, guilt, and shame came on quickly, and he became irritable, snapping at people at work and at
home, sometimes with an aggression that left him embarrassed afterward. Unusual silence was
especially hard for him; if a room went quiet, he felt something dangerous was about to happen, as
if the stillness itself were the prelude to another blast or another deadening loss. Those states of
alarm made his belief that he had ruined his life feel even more convincing, and they sharpened his
sense that he belonged nowhere. They also pushed him back toward the same attempts to manage himself
through silence, suppression, ritual, and, at the worst moments, self-harm. Because he expected
danger and felt so contaminated by what he remembered, he became more sensitive to every potential
cue: every bang near the firehouse, every cruel remark on television, every sign of moral failure in
other people.

Ravi’s work and daily life had slowly contracted around these reactions. At the station he could not
relax when the apparatus floor went quiet, and at home after his divorce he found himself alone with
the very thoughts he most wanted to avoid. He finally came for help when his irritability and
physical self-injury began to frighten him, and when he realized that the rituals and suppression
were taking more from him than they were protecting. What he wants most now is to stop feeling that
he is still back in that war zone, and what he fears most is that if the memories break through
again, they will prove that he has already become someone he does not recognize.
