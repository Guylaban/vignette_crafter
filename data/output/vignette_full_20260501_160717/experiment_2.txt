uprooted by conflict that forced his family to flee their homeland. He remembered the terrifying
crush of people in crowded spaces, the desperate struggle to move through chaotic border crossings,

physical locations, once symbols of temporary safety, became imprinted with a deep sense of
vulnerability.

Years later, living in the bustling city and navigating his demanding career, Rohan found himself in
a constant state of hypervigilance. His work often required him to visit construction sites, which,
with their throngs of workers and confined spaces, could instantly become overwhelming. When
confronted with these crowded spaces, Rohan’s body reacted before his mind had time to process, a
visceral jolt of panic that left him breathless and disoriented. He had come to believe, deep down,
that there was something fundamentally wrong with him as a person, a defect inherited from the past
that made him uniquely susceptible to harm. This pervasive sense of being a defective self was a
heavy burden, frequently leading him to imagine getting revenge on the nameless people responsible
for his family’s suffering, fantasies that offered a fleeting sense of control but ultimately left
him feeling more isolated.

His negative appraisals about himself and the world profoundly shaped his coping. The conviction
that people were not what they seemed, that others harbored hidden intentions, made him distrust
others instinctively. This deep-seated suspicion, coupled with his belief that there was something
wrong with him, drove his maladaptive strategies. Rohan found himself working excessively, pouring
all his energy into complex architectural designs, often staying late at the office long after his
colleagues had left. This overwork served as a desperate attempt to suppress thoughts about the
trauma, to keep the intrusive memories at bay by sheer exhaustion. Yet, the constant effort of
suppressing thoughts only reinforced his sense of being defective, making him feel as though he was
constantly failing to be normal, while also keeping his mind churning with a low-level hum of
anxiety.

His belief that he was exposed to others, that they could somehow see what had happened to him just
by looking at him, fed directly into a persistent sense of threat. He felt threatened by strangers
approaching him even in ordinary situations, his perception of danger amplified by the conviction

him, telling him he was safe, Rohan found it impossible to believe it; his body’s immediate,
overwhelming response to perceived danger meant that his mind struggled to override the primal sense
of alarm. This constant feeling of being under threat, triggered by the most mundane interactions,
only solidified his negative appraisal that he could not trust safety, eroding any sense of
security.

The cycle tightened. His maladaptive strategies, particularly his relentless overwork, left him
perpetually exhausted and isolated. This exhaustion and the constant emotional burden of suppressing
thoughts made him more susceptible to feeling threatened, his defenses lowered, his body even
quicker to react before his mind. One evening, during a design review held in a bustling open-plan

sensitivity to relationship reminders, echoing comments from an ex-partner who had once accused him

untrustworthy and that he was perpetually exposed.

came to therapy feeling trapped, exhausted by the constant vigilance and the endless internal
battle, still clinging to the hope that perhaps, just perhaps, someone could help him understand why
he felt so fundamentally broken and so perpetually unsafe.
