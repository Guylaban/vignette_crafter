meeting later than planned and walked to her car in a dim municipal lot behind the community center.

his coat, the shock of seeing how quickly a routine errand had turned dangerous, and the
helplessness of standing still while he rifled through her bag and demanded her wallet and keys.
Even after he ran off, she found herself staring at the empty lot, unable to gather her thoughts or
trust that the silence meant she was safe.

In the weeks that followed, Marilyn found that ordinary moments in the city could carry that night
back with them. The scrape of footsteps behind her in a hallway, a man in a hooded jacket near the
council building, or the sight of a dark parking lot after a meeting would make her body tense
before she had fully registered what she was seeing. She began checking over her shoulder more
often, scanning entrances and cars with a vigilance that felt sensible to her, not exaggerated;
after all, she was certain that the attack had shown her how quickly someone could be singled out
when she let her guard down. In her own mind, the event had taught her that public spaces were no
longer predictable, and that she had to stay alert if she wanted to avoid another humiliation or
worse. What troubled her most was not only the memory of the mugging itself but the sense that it
had changed the rules of ordinary life, making each trip to a meeting, a store, or her parking space
feel like a test of whether she would be caught unaware again.

At work, Marilyn began altering the way she moved through her days. She asked staff to escort her to
her car after evening sessions, parked closer to entrances when she could, and sometimes declined
community events that would end after dark. She told herself these choices were practical for a
woman her age, though privately she knew they were also a way of keeping the memory from returning
with full force. Even so, the more she reorganized her routine around avoiding the places and times
that reminded her of the assault, the smaller her world became. Colleagues noticed that she left
meetings early and seemed distracted when discussion turned to neighborhood safety, and Marilyn
found herself less willing to linger in the very public spaces that had once defined her role. She
still wore the polished composure of an elected official, but beneath it she was always watching:
for movement in the corner of her eye, for a stranger who stayed too close, for the instant when an
ordinary evening might tip into danger.

Over time, that constant scanning wore her down. Marilyn slept lightly, replaying the shove and the
demand for her purse whenever she heard a door slam outside her apartment or a car engine idle too
long in the street. She had come to believe that being caught off guard was what had made her
vulnerable, and that if she stayed attentive enough she might prevent another attack, yet the effort
of staying vigilant left her exhausted and more irritable with her daughter and with staff. Her
divorce had already taught her to manage on her own, and now she was reluctant to ask anyone to go
places with her for fear of seeming weak or frightened. The city still mattered to her, and she
disliked the way the mugging had started to narrow her life into routes she thought were safe and
routes she no longer trusted.

By the time Marilyn sought help, the fear had not settled on its own. She was still showing up for

place full of threats she could not afford to ignore, and that her efforts to stay safe were no
longer giving her the freedom they once promised. What she hoped for was simple but hard won: to be
able to leave a meeting, cross a parking lot, and return home without feeling as though she had to
brace for an attack at every turn.
