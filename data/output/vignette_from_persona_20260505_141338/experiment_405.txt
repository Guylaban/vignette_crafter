Kian, a 31-year-old married judge of South Eastern Asian descent, presented with symptoms consistent
with post-traumatic stress following a devastating natural disaster three years prior. He carried
himself with a quiet intensity, a trait he found increasingly difficult to maintain as the demands
of his high-pressure profession clashed with an ever-present internal turbulence.

The typhoon had struck without much warning, a sudden, furious assault on his coastal hometown. Kian
remembered the deafening roar of the wind, the way the old building he was in groaned and shuddered,
and then the sickening lurch as a section of the roof collapsed. He had been lucky, escaping with
only minor cuts, but the hours that followed were etched into his mind as a series of horrifying,
vivid pictures: the sight of injured neighbors pulled from the rubble, the desperate cries for help,
and the makeshift triage station in the town square. He could still see the pale, drawn faces, the
bloodied bandages, and hear the frantic sirens of the few emergency vehicles that managed to
navigate the debris-strewn streets. The pervasive, cloying smell of antiseptic mixed with damp earth
and something metallic – blood, he later realized – had clung to everything.

In the years since, Kian found that these fragmented, visual memories would ambush him without
warning. They weren’t just images; they were full sensory assaults, making him feel entirely
transported back to those terrifying moments. He would suddenly taste the metallic tang in the air,
feel the tremor of the ground, and hear the distant, wailing sirens as if they were right beside
him. What frightened him most was the feeling that these moments were completely real, as if time
had folded in on itself.

His daily life, once meticulously ordered, had become a minefield of triggers. Being a judge meant
spending long hours in the courthouse, a building often frequented by cleaning crews whose
disinfectants brought back the pungent, clinical odor of the makeshift hospital, leaving Kian
feeling suddenly disoriented and hyper-alert. On days when the weather mirrored the oppressive
humidity and heavy skies of the typhoon, a deep sense of dread would settle over him, making it hard
to concentrate on the complex legal arguments before him. Perhaps the most jarring were the
ambulances. Their piercing wail, a sound he heard almost daily from his chambers overlooking the
city streets, would instantly plunge him back into the chaos of the disaster’s aftermath, the urgent
flashing lights behind his closed eyelids.

To cope, Kian had developed a rigid set of rules for himself. He tried hard not to think about the
typhoon, pushing away any intrusive image or sensation with fierce determination, believing that
engaging with it would somehow open a floodgate he couldn't close. He also found himself
increasingly avoiding going out, preferring the controlled environment of his home or office to the
unpredictability of the outside world. Social invitations with his wife, even simple dinners with
friends, became sources of anxiety, leading to quiet arguments and a growing distance between them.
Even at home, true relaxation felt dangerous. The idea of letting his guard down, of allowing his
mind to wander, filled him with an almost physical apprehension. He had come to believe that
constant vigilance was the only way to protect himself from being caught off guard again, leaving
him in a perpetual state of low-grade tension.

The cumulative effect of these strategies had left Kian exhausted, perpetually on edge, and
increasingly isolated. His wife, worried by his withdrawal, had gently but firmly encouraged him to
seek help. He sat in the therapy room, a man accustomed to presiding over the fates of others,
feeling profoundly vulnerable, yet also desperate for some semblance of the peace he once knew. He
hoped that by speaking about it, he might finally find a way to escape the prison of his past, but
he was deeply afraid of what might happen if he stopped trying so hard to keep it all contained.
