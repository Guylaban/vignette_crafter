Stefan, a 49-year-old fashion designer, presented for therapy at the insistence of his wife, Maria.
For the past year, his life had been overshadowed by the aftermath of a severe car accident in which
Maria was critically injured. Stefan had been driving, and though he escaped with minor physical
injuries, the emotional scars ran deep. He remembered the sickening screech of tires and the jarring
impact with chilling clarity, the metallic smell, the distorted shape of the passenger door. What
often returned to him in a flash was the sight of Maria, unconscious and bleeding, but the moments
just before the crash—what he was doing, what he might have missed—remained a terrifying blank.
Similarly, the period between the ambulance arriving and her being whisked away felt like a hazy,
fragmented dream, making the entire event feel both starkly real and profoundly incomplete.

When these vivid fragments of the accident broke through, Stefan was immediately overwhelmed by a
crushing wave of emotion. He would feel an intense, gut-wrenching guilt that forced him to stop
whatever he was doing, whether he was sketching a new design or simply trying to read a book. He was
certain that he was solely responsible for Maria’s pain and the long, arduous road to her recovery.
This pervasive sense of guilt was often accompanied by a torrent of other negative emotions –
profound fear for Maria's continued well-being, horror at the images he couldn't banish, and a
simmering anger at himself.

To cope, Stefan had developed a relentless strategy of mental distraction. He kept his mind occupied
all the time, throwing himself into his work in his bustling design studio. He would stay late,
endlessly tweaking patterns, researching fabrics, or managing production schedules, often until the
early hours of the morning. Even at home, he would immerse himself in documentaries or complex
puzzles, anything to prevent his mind from drifting. He tried hard not to think about the trauma,
actively pushing away any thoughts or memories that threatened to surface, finding temporary relief
in the sheer volume of his tasks.

Despite his efforts at distraction, the trauma continued to intrude. Stefan found himself plagued by
persistent sleep problems; he would lie awake for hours, his mind racing, or wake frequently
throughout the night, often drenched in a cold sweat. Certain triggers in his daily life could
instantly ignite strong physical reactions. The sudden blare of a car horn in the street outside his
studio, the sight of a flashing emergency light on a news report, or even the smell of antiseptic
that reminded him of the hospital, would send his heart racing, his palms sweating, and leave him
feeling breathless, as if the accident were happening all over again.

Over the past year, Stefan’s avoidance extended beyond just thoughts and memories. He found himself
unable to make any plans for the future. He couldn't commit to long-term design projects, which
began to strain his professional relationships and stall his career. He also avoided discussing
future holidays with Maria, or even simple weekend outings, always finding an excuse, telling her it
was "too much right now." He had come to believe that making plans was tempting fate, and that any
attempt to envision a future would only lead to further disaster, a belief that left him feeling
trapped in a perpetual present. This avoidance had put a significant strain on his marriage, leaving
Maria feeling isolated and confused. It was her growing concern, her quiet desperation, that finally
prompted Stefan to seek professional help. He came to therapy not knowing what to expect, but
desperate for some reprieve from the relentless guilt and the constant fear that had become his
everyday reality.
