her husband’s passing, finding solace in her work at the local grocery store. Her world was
irrevocably shattered one late night, years ago, as she walked home from her evening shift. Taking a

seared into her memory alongside the terror. The assault itself was a blur of stark, disconnected
images. Some moments, like the glint of a belt buckle or the rough texture of the brick wall against

episode felt like a few distinct pictures, each a frozen tableau of violation and powerlessness.

In the aftermath, Meena's world began to shrink dramatically. The once familiar path home became a
gauntlet of potential threats, and her belief solidified that the world was an inherently dangerous
place. What happened to her was a profound injustice, a wrong that, despite her reporting it, had
gone unaddressed by a system she now felt had utterly failed her. This deep-seated feeling of
injustice festered, making her feel increasingly isolated and resentful.

landscape of anxiety. Whenever a male customer was the last one in an aisle, or if she found herself

pound, her breath would quicken, and she would become hyper-aware of every sound, every movement,
convinced that the past was about to repeat itself. She began to subtly refuse evening shifts,
citing fatigue, a decision that inadvertently strained her relationships with younger colleagues who
had to cover for her.

The smell of smoke, even from a distant barbecue, a passing car’s exhaust, or a colleague’s
cigarette break, would instantly transport her back to that alley. The air would thicken, the
metallic tang would return, and she would be overwhelmed by the same terror and helplessness she had
experienced that night. She found herself meticulously avoiding areas of the store where delivery
trucks idled, often emitting smoky fumes, even if it meant taking a longer, less efficient route to
retrieve items for customers.

Beyond specific sensory triggers, Meena became acutely sensitive to any form of "misbehavior." A
customer raising their voice at a cashier, a colleague gossiping in the breakroom, or even a child
throwing a tantrum near the produce aisle—these seemingly minor transgressions would fill her with a
profound sense of moral outrage and deep unease. She perceived them as further evidence that the
fabric of decency had been torn, just as it had been on that terrible night. Often, she would
retreat to the staff room, unable to tolerate the perceived chaos and disrespect.

that she "can't deal with even the slightest upset." A misplaced item at home, a forgotten errand,

completely overwhelmed and incapable of handling life's ordinary challenges. She would frequently

rich with family connections and the quiet joy of tending her small garden, now felt profoundly
empty. "Nothing in my life feels meaningful anymore," she would sigh, looking out at the wilting
plants she no longer had the energy or will to tend. Her widowhood, already a source of deep grief,
was compounded by the trauma, leaving her feeling utterly alone and without purpose. The simple
pleasures she once enjoyed faded, replaced by a pervasive sense of futility.

Meena finally sought help after her manager, noticing her increasing absences and withdrawal, gently

she was becoming a shadow of her former self, trapped by an unrelenting fear and a profound sense of
unacknowledged injustice. She yearned for a return to the quiet peace she once knew, but was
terrified that it was permanently lost to her.
