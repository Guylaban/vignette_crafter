Chanh, a 66-year-old roofer from a South Eastern Asian background, found himself in an unfamiliar
therapist’s office, feeling the weight of the past year pressing down on him. For decades, Chanh had
been known for his meticulous work and steady hands, scaling roofs with a confidence born of long
experience. His life with his wife, Mai, had been one of quiet routine and hard-earned comfort until
a late-night job changed everything. He had been contracted to work on the roof of an old industrial
plant, replacing corroded ventilation ducts. As he was finishing up, a sudden, unexpected release of
toxic fumes from a lower vent engulfed him. He remembered the burning in his lungs, the dizzying
disorientation, and the desperate scramble before he collapsed, his last conscious thought the
metallic, acrid smell that clung to everything.

His memories of that frightening event were like a film that played over and over in his mind, vivid
and detailed. He could still see the greenish haze, feel the sting in his eyes, hear the distant
sirens. But sometimes, even without a clear image, a wave of full emotional intensity would wash
over him—pure, unadulterated terror and nausea—triggered by something as simple as a whiff of
disinfectant. The antiseptic smell of the hospital, where he spent several days recovering, had
become a potent reminder, alongside any related smells that vaguely resembled the chemical. As the
weeks turned into months, the darkness of night, or even waking up in the dark, brought back the
vulnerability and fear of that evening on the roof.

Life for Chanh became a careful navigation around these triggers. He found himself unable to relax,
even when he knew he was in the safety of his own home. He would sit on the sofa with Mai, his body
tense, his eyes scanning the room as if assessing an unseen danger. His concentration, once sharp
and focused, had deteriorated, making even simple tasks feel overwhelming. He tried hard not to
think about the trauma, pushing the memories away whenever they surfaced, but they always returned,
often unbidden and relentless. This constant mental effort left him exhausted. He also started
avoiding going to unfamiliar places, explaining to Mai that he simply couldn't assess the danger
there, feeling a profound unease in environments he didn't know well.

Over time, Chanh had come to believe that the world was now a dangerous place, a belief reinforced
by the suddenness of his accident. He found himself dwelling on how he used to be before the
event—the capable, confident roofer, the calm husband. Now, he was certain he couldn't rely on
himself anymore; the incident had shattered his self-trust, making him question every decision. This
new sense of unreliability seeped into his interactions; to avoid any potential conflict or further
strain, he would never disagree or assert himself, even with Mai, preferring to let others make
choices. This withdrawal and his inability to relax created a growing distance between them.

His work, his lifelong passion, became a source of anxiety. He started turning down evening jobs,
and even during daylight hours, his difficulty concentrating made him slower and more prone to
errors. He feared he might make a mistake that could injure him or someone else, deepening his sense
that he was no longer competent. A profound sense of hopelessness had settled over him, leading him
to conclude that he had no future, at least not the future he had once envisioned. Mai, noticing his
increasing isolation and quiet despair, finally urged him to seek help. Chanh came to therapy not
with hope, but with a weary resignation, primarily because he felt he had nothing left to lose, and
perhaps, a faint, unarticulated wish that he might find some way back to the man he used to be.
