Asha Devi, at sixty-six years old, carried the quiet weight of a life irrevocably altered. Married
for over four decades, she now worked as a security guard at a sprawling suburban mall, a job that
offered both routine and an unexpected array of challenges to her fragile peace. The turning point
in her life had been five years ago, a day etched into her memory with harrowing clarity. She had
been driving her beloved grandson, Rohan, then just seven, to a weekend cricket match. A sudden,
violent impact from a distracted driver had shattered their journey, and though Asha herself escaped
with only minor injuries, Rohan had been critically hurt. He succumbed to his injuries days later,
leaving Asha with an unshakeable conviction that the event happened because of the way she acted;
she should have protected him, and she failed.

In the immediate aftermath, the memory of the accident would ambush Asha, feeling not like a past
event but as if it were happening right now. The screech of tires, the crumpling metal, Rohan’s
small, still form – these images would flood her mind with such intensity that she would feel so
overwhelmed by emotion that she had to stop whatever she was doing, her breath catching in her
throat. These memories felt more real and vivid than anything else in her life, eclipsing the
mundane details of her days.

Over time, daily life at the mall became a minefield of triggers. She would be patrolling the
parking lot, her eyes constantly scanning for danger, when the sight of two cars with even minor
dents or scratches would send a jolt through her, her body feeling a strong physical reaction, a
familiar tightening in her chest. Overhearing colleagues in the breakroom discuss a minor fender
bender outside the food court would plunge her back into the terror of that day, her mind replaying
the crash imagery. Even the mention of funerals, cemeteries, or any grief-related event, perhaps a
customer talking about a recent loss, would make her heart pound, a stark reminder of Rohan's small
casket. Her body felt constantly tense and ready to react to danger, a perpetual state of vigilance
that exhausted her.

To cope, Asha developed a careful strategy of avoidance. She tried hard not to think about the
trauma, pushing away any intrusive thoughts or images with a desperate internal plea for silence.
She also started to avoid anything that reminded her of Rohan, tucking away his photographs,
changing the channel if a children's show he loved came on, even subtly steering conversations away
from shared memories with her husband. This avoidance of grief reminders, while intended to shield
her, had created a quiet chasm between them. At work, her reluctance to engage in any situation that
might involve disagreement or confrontation meant she often let minor infractions slide, rather than
risk the emotional upheaval of a potential argument, even if it meant compromising her duties. She
worried that any conflict could spiral out of control, echoing the sudden, uncontrollable chaos of
the accident.

This meticulous self-preservation had come at a cost. Asha had come to believe that she would never
be able to feel normal emotions again, that the capacity for true joy or peace had died with Rohan.
The constant scanning for danger made her perpetually on edge, and her refusal to engage in conflict
meant she was increasingly isolated, both professionally and personally. Her husband, grieving in
his own way, felt her withdrawal, though neither spoke of the growing distance.

A few weeks ago, a particularly graphic news report about a multi-car pile-up flashed across the TV
in the security office. The images had been too much, too real, and Asha had collapsed into a chair,
overwhelmed by a wave of physical reactions – dizziness, nausea, and an uncontrollable tremor. It
was the first time her colleagues had seen her so visibly distressed, and their concerned questions
had finally prompted her to acknowledge the depth of her suffering. She was certain that her careful
strategies were failing, and the fear that she would forever live trapped in this state of constant
alert, haunted by the past and unable to feel, was what finally brought her to seek help. She
desperately hoped for a way out of the perpetual shadow of that day, to find some semblance of
peace, even if she couldn't yet imagine what that might feel like.
