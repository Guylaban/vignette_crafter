Anya, a 35-year-old South Eastern Asian woman, had always prided herself on her empathy and the
gentle strength she brought to her work as a physical therapist. But that was before. Before the
night her then-partner, in a fit of rage, had held her down, pressing her face against the cold,
tiled floor of their bathroom. The chilling sensation of the ceramic against her cheek, the
unyielding pressure of his hand on her back, the terrifying awareness that she was utterly
powerless, unable to move or speak as he berated her – these were moments etched into her memory,
not as something from the past, but as if they were happening right now, in the very present. Even
now, the memory returned with such a flood of terror and shame that she would often have to stop
whatever she was doing, her breath catching in her throat, her hands trembling. When she had tried
to explain it to her husband, Mark, or even to a close friend, the words would tangle, the thread of
the narrative would vanish, and she would find herself unable to finish, leaving her feeling even
more isolated and misunderstood.

In the months that followed, Anya found herself constantly dwelling on how she used to be before
that night – the confident, unburdened woman who could freely connect with her patients and enjoy
intimacy with Mark. Now, she was haunted by a gnawing belief that the event had happened because of
the way she had acted, replaying every moment, every word, every gesture, convinced that if she had
just done *this* or *not done that*, she could have prevented it all. This relentless rumination
solidified a deeper, more insidious conviction: there was something fundamentally wrong with her as
a person, a flaw that had invited such cruelty. The humiliation of what she had endured felt like a
permanent stain, making her wary of others and deeply self-conscious.

Her professional life, once a source of deep satisfaction, had become a minefield. As a physical
therapist, her job inherently involved physical touch, and the simple act of placing her hands on a
patient’s shoulder or guiding their limb through an exercise could trigger a wave of panic. The
tactile sensation of skin-on-skin contact, especially if a patient’s hand brushed hers unexpectedly,
would send a jolt through her, instantly transporting her back to the feeling of being trapped. What
frightened her most was any situation where she felt pressured or had to comply with something she
didn't want to do. If a patient was resistant to an exercise, or a colleague pushed her to take on
an extra shift, she would experience an intense, almost physical, revulsion, convinced that she was
once again losing control and being forced into something against her will. She found herself
constantly vigilant, scanning the faces and movements of her patients and co-workers, searching for
any sign of potential aggression or dominance, always on guard for dangerous people.

Over time, this hypervigilance and the effort to avoid triggers had taken a significant toll. She
had started subtly delegating tasks that required prolonged physical contact with patients, often
inventing excuses about needing to focus on paperwork or other administrative duties. Her once-fluid
interactions with patients became stilted, marked by a noticeable distance, leading to some patients
requesting other therapists. At home, her marriage was strained. Mark’s touch, once a comfort, now
often felt alarming, and she would flinch or stiffen without meaning to, leaving him confused and
hurt. The pervasive feeling of humiliation made her withdraw from social gatherings, fearing that
others could somehow see the "damaged" part of her. The constant internal battle, the emotional
overwhelm, and the increasing isolation had become unbearable. Anya had finally sought help,
desperately hoping to find a way to escape the prison of her past and reclaim the life she
remembered, before the cold tile and the crushing pressure had changed everything.
