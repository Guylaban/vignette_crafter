details remain elusive. She had been deployed with a medical unit, working in a makeshift clinic not
far from the front lines when the sirens began to wail, a sound that sliced through the night and

shattering of glass, the terrified screams of patients and colleagues. Amidst the chaos of treating
the wounded, her own heart had begun to pound furiously in her chest, a frantic drumbeat against her
ribs, convinced she was about to die. She had somehow moved through the night, tending to injuries,
but the memory itself remains a fragmented, terrifying whirlwind. When the memory returns, it is not
a coherent narrative but a surge of pure, unadulterated rage, an overwhelming wave of emotion that
forces her to stop whatever she is doing, gasping for air. She knows what happened, the broad
strokes of the attack, but when she tries to articulate the specifics, the words simply won't come;
it’s as if they are trapped behind a wall of unspeakable horror.

Since returning home, Leilani has found that the world is a minefield of potential threats. The
sudden wail of an ambulance siren, a common sound in the city where she now works, sends a jolt of
ice through her veins, her body instantly tensing, ready for impact. Even the mundane loud noises of
her hospital workplace – a dropped tray, a sudden shout in the hallway – cause her to startle
violently, her heart leaping into a frantic rhythm. What frightens her most is when her own heart
starts racing from something benign, like climbing a flight of stairs; the sensation immediately
transports her back to that night, convincing her that danger is imminent, that she is about to be
overwhelmed by a flashback, suddenly feeling and acting as if the mortar attack were happening
again, right here, right now. This constant fear of her own physiological responses has led her to
avoid exercise, fearing that any exertion that raises her heart rate will inevitably trigger the
panic she so desperately tries to outrun.

Her days are characterized by an exhausting vigilance. Whether she is on a busy ward or walking
through a grocery store, Leilani constantly scans her surroundings, her eyes darting from face to
face, corner to corner, searching for any sign of threat, any anomaly that might signal danger. This
hyper-alertness leaves her perpetually on edge, making her irritable with colleagues who seem
oblivious to the hidden dangers she perceives, and prone to angry outbursts when startled or
challenged. Her partner has noticed her withdrawal, her reluctance to engage in activities they once
enjoyed. She finds herself avoiding situations that might make her feel happy or hopeful, as if
allowing herself to feel joy would somehow make her vulnerable, a target for another devastating
blow. The thought of feeling intense positive emotion is almost as terrifying as the prospect of
feeling intense negative emotion, as if both are just different paths to the same overwhelming
vulnerability.

Weeks have turned into months, and the cumulative effect of her avoidance and constant threat
monitoring has begun to take a significant toll. Her nursing performance, once exemplary, has

and distant demeanor. Her relationship, once a source of comfort, now feels strained, as she

scanning, terrified of the rage and overwhelm that accompany her memories, and desperate for a way
to quiet the internal alarm bells that ring incessantly. She has come to therapy with a quiet
desperation, hoping to find a way to live without the constant shadow of danger, to reclaim the
parts of herself that feel lost to that terrible night.
