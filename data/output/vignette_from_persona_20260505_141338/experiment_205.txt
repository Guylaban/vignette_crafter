Amina, a woman of 55, carried the weight of her past like a constant, unseen burden. Her journey
from her homeland had been etched into her memory not as a narrative, but as a series of terrifying,
fragmented sensations. She remembered the suffocating night on the overcrowded, rickety boat, the
relentless slap of waves against the hull, and the chilling cold of the water seeping through the
floorboards. The rough, damp texture of the canvas tarp they huddled under, meant to offer
protection, had instead become a symbol of her vulnerability. The worst, though, was the utter
uncertainty of it all – not knowing if they would reach safety, if they would be caught, if the boat
would simply capsize in the vast, unforgiving darkness. She had often dwelled on the actions of the
smugglers who promised safe passage only to put them in such peril, a bitter rumination on what
others had done to her and her family.

Years later, in her new life as an HVAC technician in the bustling suburbs of America, these
fragments would ambush her. When the memory of that night returned, it often felt so overwhelming,
so viscerally real, that Amina would have to stop whatever she was doing, her hands shaking, her
breath catching in her throat, until the wave of terror subsided. Sometimes, the sheer extremity of
it made the whole experience feel unreal, as if it were a story she’d heard rather than lived. A
profound shame would often wash over her too, a deep humiliation tied to the helplessness she felt
during the escape.

Her daily work, navigating the complex systems of buildings, was a constant negotiation with her
internal world. Entering an unfamiliar commercial building or a client's home, Amina found herself
immediately scanning her surroundings, her eyes darting to exits, windows, and potential hiding
spots. It was an automatic, ingrained habit; she always had to plan an escape route before she could
even begin to assess a faulty furnace. A sudden, unusual silence in an empty office building or a
quiet basement would instantly trigger a jolt of fear, making her feel certain that something
dangerous was about to happen. Her heart would pound, her senses on high alert, constantly scanning
for signs of danger, an invisible threat she was convinced was always lurking.

Working with water systems, a common part of her job, was particularly challenging. A leaking pipe
in a cramped utility closet, the sight of a puddle spreading across a basement floor, or even the
sound of rushing water from a large boiler could suddenly transport her back to that night on the
boat. She would feel herself acting as if the stressful experience were actually happening again,
her body tensing, her mind racing, for a few disorienting moments. The specific texture of damp,
rough insulation, so similar to the tarp she remembered, could provoke the same intense reaction.

Over the years, Amina’s constant vigilance and her sudden emotional shifts had taken their toll. Her
divorce, several years prior, had been a painful consequence, as her ex-husband struggled to
understand the invisible war she was fighting. She had become increasingly withdrawn, finding solace
in the predictable routines of her work, even as it presented its own challenges. But lately, the
flashbacks had become more frequent, the shame more pervasive, and the exhaustion from her constant
scanning and planning was draining her. It was a particularly bad incident at work last month, when
a burst pipe in a building’s sub-basement left her paralyzed with fear, unable to complete the
repair, that finally prompted her to seek help. She hoped, perhaps, to find a way to quiet the
constant alarm bells in her mind, to finally believe that the danger was truly in the past.
