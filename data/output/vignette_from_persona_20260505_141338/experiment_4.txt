Omar, a nineteen-year-old environmental scientist, found himself sitting in the waiting room, his
back pressed firmly against the wall, observing every person who entered or exited. Even here, in a
place meant for healing, he felt an insistent pull to ensure his surroundings were safe, scanning
faces for any hint of threat. This hypervigilance had become an almost unconscious habit, a silent
ritual he performed wherever he went.

His earliest memories were steeped in a pervasive sense of unpredictability. As a young child, his
home was often a volatile space, marked by the sharp, escalating tones of arguments between his
caregiver and other family members. These disagreements frequently spiraled, the air thick with
tension, until someone, often Omar himself, would begin to cry. It was in these moments of distress,
when his small body was wracked with sobs, that the caregiver’s frustration would sometimes erupt
into sudden, unexpected physical contact—a rough grab, a push, a swift, open-handed strike that left
not just a sting, but a profound, visceral terror. He learned quickly that crying was a dangerous
signal, and that touch, particularly when he hadn’t braced for it, could signify an imminent threat.

Years later, these early experiences cast long shadows over Omar’s life. Now married, he found
navigating the natural disagreements inherent in a close relationship to be an immense challenge. If
his wife, Layla, expressed frustration or raised her voice during a discussion, a cold dread would
seize him. He would immediately shut down, often agreeing to whatever she wanted, however
unreasonable, just to extinguish the potential for escalating conflict. He had come to believe that
any disagreement carried the potential for chaos, and his primary goal was to prevent its eruption
at all costs. This avoidance of conflict, however, had begun to create distance between them,
leaving Layla feeling unheard and frustrated.

His professional life as an environmental scientist, which often required collaborative fieldwork or
team meetings, also became a minefield. During project discussions, if a colleague became animated
or passionate, Omar would find himself scrutinizing their face, searching for the subtle micro-
expressions that, to him, once preceded danger. He meticulously selected his seat in the lab or
conference room, ensuring his back was always against a wall, allowing him to survey the entire
space without feeling exposed. He often volunteered for solitary tasks, preferring to analyze data
alone rather than engage in group brainstorming, which he feared might lead to arguments. The
thought of being alone in the large, quiet office building after hours was unbearable, so he would
often linger until a colleague was leaving, or rush home to Layla, unable to tolerate the silence
and perceived vulnerability.

The unexpected touch was another persistent torment. If a colleague lightly tapped his shoulder to
get his attention, or if Layla affectionately brushed his arm while they were cooking, Omar would
flinch, his heart leaping into his throat. He would quickly rationalize it away, but the internal
alarm bells were deafening, transporting him back to those moments of childhood terror. He was
certain that he needed to be constantly vigilant, that any unannounced contact was a breach of his
fragile safety.

The cumulative weight of these strategies had begun to take its toll. Omar’s marriage felt strained
by his inability to engage in normal disagreements and his constant need for company. At work, while
competent, his avoidance of collaborative projects and his quiet, watchful demeanor made it
difficult for him to connect with colleagues or advance in roles requiring leadership. It was Layla,
noticing his increasing isolation and the subtle ways he navigated every room for a "safe" spot, who
gently suggested he seek help. What frightened Omar most was the growing realization that the world,
through his eyes, was a place of constant potential threat, and he was exhausted by the relentless
effort to stay safe. He longed for a life where he didn’t have to fight an invisible war every
single day.
