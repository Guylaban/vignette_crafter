Anil, a 64-year-old South Asian man, found himself seeking help after months of living under a
constant, invisible threat. The ordeal that shattered his sense of safety occurred over a year ago,
during a routine evening delivery for the warehouse where he had worked for decades. He remembered
the chill in the air as he secured the last pallet, the dimming light of the late afternoon casting
long shadows across the industrial park. That was when the van pulled up, two men emerging to
overpower him, dragging him into the vehicle. He was blindfolded and driven to a remote, derelict
building. The hours that followed were a blur of terror, punctuated by the rustle and slither of
something unseen in the dark corners of his confinement – a sound that his captors gleefully
identified as snakes, a constant, menacing reminder of his vulnerability. The worst of it, however,
was the complete darkness that descended with nightfall, making every sound amplify, every shadow
feel like a monster. He was eventually released, disoriented but physically unharmed, just as dawn
broke.

In the months since, the world had become a minefield of reminders. As the clock approached the time
of day he was abducted, a cold dread would grip Anil, his heart beginning to pound as if reliving
those first terrifying moments. At work, if a colleague mentioned snakes in passing, or if a
documentary played on a breakroom TV, he would feel a surge of panic, his breath catching in his
throat. But it was the night that held the most terror. He had come to believe that he could not
stop bad things from happening to him, a conviction solidified by his helplessness during the
captivity. This belief made the arrival of darkness an unbearable burden. He found it harder to feel
safe at night than during the day, convinced that the cover of dark would once again bring danger.

To cope, Anil developed a meticulous strategy of evasion. He would do everything in his power to
avoid going to sleep, or even lying in the dark. He’d stay up late, watching television, reading, or
just sitting in the brightly lit living room, often retreating to the sofa rather than the marital
bed. This constant sleep avoidance left him perpetually exhausted, his mind foggy. He also developed
a habit of constantly checking his body for signs of danger or injury, his fingers often finding
their way to his pulse, his breathing becoming shallow as he monitored every ache or flutter,
convinced they were harbingers of another threat. Whenever his wife, Leela, noticed his distress, he
would keep asking her to reassure him that he was okay, that everything was fine, needing her words
to momentarily quell the rising tide of fear.

His vigilance extended beyond his own body. He was constantly superalert and watchful, scanning his
environment wherever he went. At the warehouse, he’d frequently look behind him, even when walking
down empty aisles, certain that someone might be following him, that danger lurked just out of
sight. This hypervigilance made simple tasks arduous and exhausting, and his colleagues had begun to
notice his distractedness and his constant need for reassurance. Leela, too, was weary from his
nightly rituals and endless questions. Anil knew he was pushing her away, but the alternative –
confronting the dark, the memories – felt like an insurmountable task. He was certain that if he
allowed himself to think about the event, he would not be able to handle it, that the memories would
consume him entirely.

The cumulative effect of these changes had been devastating. He had stopped enjoying his walks in
the park, his evenings with Leela, even his work, which had once been a source of quiet pride. There
was a pervasive sense of despair, a quiet resignation that he was not sure his life was worth living
anymore, not with this constant shadow hanging over him. It was Leela, seeing the life drain from
his eyes, who finally persuaded him to seek help. He came to therapy not with hope, but with a deep-
seated fear that even this would not be enough, that he was too broken, too vulnerable, to ever
truly feel safe again.
