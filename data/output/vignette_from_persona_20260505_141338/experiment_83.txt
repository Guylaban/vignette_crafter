behind the wheel, navigating the busy streets of her city with quiet confidence. That changed nine

reckless driver ran the red light, T-boning another car directly in front of her bus. The screech of
tires, the sickening crunch of metal, and the shattering glass were instantaneous. Sofia saw a blur
of motion, then the horrifying sight of a pedestrian caught in the chaos, flung violently to the
pavement. Blood bloomed rapidly on the asphalt. Trapped in her driver’s seat, she slammed on her
brakes, but could do nothing else. The sheer lack of control, the inability to intervene, burned
into her mind as she watched, horrified, as paramedics worked on the crumpled cars and the still
figure on the ground.

Since that day, Sofia’s life had narrowed considerably. The memory of the accident felt less like a
past event and more like a live presence, ready to ambush her at any moment. Sometimes, a sudden
glimpse of a dented fender on the road, or even just the sight of a red stain on the sidewalk, would
plunge her back into the scene. She would feel the shuddering impact of the crash, hear the screams,
smell the burning rubber – a full sensory reliving that was so vivid, it felt like it was happening
right now. Other times, the full emotional intensity of the event would wash over her without any
clear image or memory, a wave of terror and despair that left her gasping, convinced that something
terrible was about to happen again.

The guilt was a constant companion. Sofia was certain she should have protected others, that she had
failed in some fundamental way. “I will never forgive myself for not doing more to stop what
happened,” she’d repeat to herself, her mind replaying the moments leading up to the crash,
searching for an intervention she could have made. This profound sense of failure led her to
question the very value of her existence, whispering to herself in the quiet hours, “I am not sure
my life is worth living anymore.”

To cope, Sofia developed a series of intricate rituals. Before each shift, she would perform mental
checks, visualizing her route and mentally "clearing" it of any potential dangers, a form of magical
prevention she hoped would keep bad things from happening again. If she forgot a step, she'd feel a
surge of dread, sometimes even pulling over to restart the ritual. Her fear of the world’s
unpredictable dangers extended to personal contact. She began flinching away from her husband,

"too tired" or "not in the mood." Any form of physical contact with others, even a brush against a
passenger on her bus, now felt profoundly unsettling. She also meticulously avoided anything that
reminded her of someone she had lost – not just the pedestrian, but the loss of her former sense of
safety and self. She stopped watching the news, avoided certain routes, and even changed her grocery
store to avoid passing the intersection where the accident occurred.

desperately hoping to find a way out of the constant loop of fear and self-reproach.
