burning thatch as homes were set ablaze. He had been forced to flee into the dense, unfamiliar bush,
leaving behind his ailing grandmother who was unable to keep pace. For days, he hid, cold and
hungry, witnessing horrors from afar, the crackle of distant fires and the cries of victims echoing

very nature he now patrolled, and by suppressing the urge to intervene when he saw others in
distress, knowing it would mean his own certain death. He had come to believe that his survival was
a profound betrayal, a deep stain on his character; he felt an overwhelming guilt for living when so
many others, including his grandmother, had not. He was also haunted by the memory of a brief, harsh
exchange with another desperate refugee during his escape, who had castigated him for a decision
he’d made, cementing a deep-seated fear of being judged or criticised.

In the years following his immigration to the United States and his eventual career as a park
ranger, the world began to feel like a minefield. The tranquil forests and trails he maintained,
though beautiful, were a constant, unsettling echo of the dangerous wilderness where he had hidden.

transport him back to those terrifying days, filling him with a visceral fear he struggled to
articulate. Even the sight of a campfire, a controlled burn to manage undergrowth, or the distant
glow of a wildfire on the horizon would send a jolt of panic through him, the flames bringing back
the searing image of his burning village. He found himself increasingly unable to relax, even when
off duty in the safety of his own home. His body remained in a state of hyper-alertness, making it

regulations, Samuel would interpret their words as harsh criticism, his heart pounding, his hands
trembling, convinced he was being found wanting in the same way he felt he had been years ago.

Over time, Samuel began to withdraw. He systematically cut off all contact with his few friends and
remaining family, convinced that his experiences had made him fundamentally different, isolated and
set apart from others. He believed no one could truly understand the horror he had witnessed or the

judgment. He started to avoid wearing his full park ranger uniform whenever possible, finding the

cope with the relentless hypervigilance and the crushing weight of his memories, he began drinking

became a solitary ritual, further cementing his isolation. He developed a pervasive sense that when
things took longer than expected—a delayed response from a colleague, a slow-moving car on the
road—it was not just an inconvenience, but a sign that something bad, something catastrophic, was
about to happen. This constant threat monitoring made his job, which often involved unpredictable
events and remote locations, increasingly difficult to manage.

his increased isolation and irritability. He had come to feel that he had acted in ways that went
against his very core values, choices he had made to survive that now made him feel unworthy and
deeply ashamed. Samuel finally sought help not because of any single dramatic event, but because the
weight of his guilt, the persistent fear, and the suffocating loneliness had become too heavy to
bear. He walked into the clinic feeling utterly exhausted, desperate for relief, yet deeply fearful
that his story was too dark, his guilt too profound, for anyone to truly help him.
