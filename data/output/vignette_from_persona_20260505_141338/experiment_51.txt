Layla, a 24-year-old tailor, first sought help after nearly two years of living with a pervasive
sense of dread that had slowly but surely reshaped her entire world. The catalyst for this profound
shift was a life-threatening motor vehicle collision she endured one rainy evening. She had been a
passenger in a ride-share, heading home after a long day at her shop, when a speeding truck ran a
red light, T-boning their vehicle with brutal force. The world had dissolved into a cacophony of
screeching metal, shattering glass, and her own choked scream, followed by a terrifying stillness
where she was trapped, gasping for breath, convinced she was moments from dying. The paramedics had
to cut her out of the wreckage, and even now, the memory of the twisted metal pressing against her,
the acrid smell of burning oil, and the helpless terror of being utterly at the mercy of forces
beyond her control felt as though it were happening right now, bringing back the exact same terror
she had felt at the time. Her memories of that frightening event were like a film that played over
and over in her mind, often triggered by the sudden blare of a horn, the screech of tires, or even
the scent of exhaust fumes.

After the accident, Layla found herself increasingly on edge. She had come to believe that the world
was a dangerous place, and that she had to be especially careful because you never knew what could
happen next. This conviction fueled a constant, exhausting vigilance. When she was at her tailor
shop, meticulously working on a garment, she would instinctively position her cutting table so her
back was always against a wall, allowing her to see the entire room and the entrance. Even then, she
found herself constantly scanning her surroundings for signs of danger, her eyes darting to every
customer, every shadow, every movement outside the window. She had grown deeply wary of others,
concluding that people were not what they seemed, and that their carelessness could easily lead to
catastrophe. This distrust extended to the drivers on the road, the strangers in the street, and
even the seemingly benign individuals who entered her shop.

Her sleep, once a refuge, had become another arena for her relentless watchfulness. She had immense
difficulty falling asleep because she felt an overwhelming need to stay alert, convinced that
lowering her guard, even in slumber, would leave her vulnerable. Her nights were often spent tossing
and turning, her mind replaying the accident, or mentally rehearsing worst-case scenarios for
various potential threats, hoping that by anticipating them, she could somehow feel prepared. This
constant mental preparation, however, only left her more exhausted and drained during the day.

The most significant and debilitating change in Layla's life was her complete avoidance of vehicles.
The thought of driving herself, or even being a passenger in a car, was utterly paralyzing. She
would walk for miles to get to her shop, or rely on expensive ride-shares, which she endured with
white knuckles and shallow breaths, her eyes constantly checking her surroundings for any hint of
danger, every sudden brake or turn sending a jolt of panic through her. This avoidance had severe
consequences for her business. She could no longer easily visit fabric suppliers across town, nor
could she offer home fittings to elderly or less mobile clients, significantly limiting her reach
and income. Her social life had dwindled to almost nothing, as meeting friends often involved
navigating transportation she could no longer tolerate.

Layla’s breaking point came when a major fabric order, crucial for a large bridal party, was delayed
because she couldn't bring herself to pick it up from a distant warehouse. The financial strain and
the profound sense of isolation finally made her realize she couldn't continue this way. She yearned
for a life where she wasn't constantly scanning for danger, where her memories weren't a looping
film of terror, and where the simple act of leaving her home didn't feel like a perilous expedition
into a world she had come to believe was inherently hostile. She hoped that therapy might offer some
way to reclaim the freedom and peace she had lost.
