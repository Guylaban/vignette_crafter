Ramesh, an eighty-year-old widower, carried the weight of a moment from his long career in the Coast
Guard. Decades ago, during a fierce storm off the New England coast, his cutter was responding to a
distress call from a small fishing vessel. In the chaos of the rescue, as a fisherman was being
brought aboard, a sudden, violent swell caused a piece of heavy equipment to swing wildly. Ramesh,
in his haste to secure it, misjudged the force, and the davit arm struck the fisherman, causing a
severe head injury. The man survived but was permanently disabled, a fact that etched itself into
Ramesh’s soul.

In the immediate aftermath, Ramesh found his memories of the incident were a jumble. When he tried
to deliberately remember what happened, he found it difficult to access the memory; it was like
trying to grasp smoke. Instead, his memories of the event came in disconnected pieces that did not
fit together – a flash of the man’s face, the cold spray of the ocean, the metallic clang, but never
a coherent sequence. Yet, sometimes, without warning, the memory would return, feeling completely
real across all his senses at once: the smell of the salty air, the roar of the wind, the sickening
thud, and the desperate shouts. To cope, Ramesh had developed a habit of trying to push these
thoughts out of his mind. He would tell himself the event did not really happen or was not that bad,
a desperate attempt at magical thinking. He would force himself to think only positive thoughts, but
the relief was always fleeting.

Over the years, a profound sense of self-blame had taken root. He had come to believe there was
something fundamentally wrong with him as a person, that the event happened to him because of the
sort of person he was—flawed, prone to making catastrophic mistakes. This belief fueled a constant
need to be on guard all the time, as if his inherent defectiveness might manifest again at any
moment.

Now, in his retirement, his world had grown small. He lived in a quiet coastal town, but being in a
natural environment similar to where it happened, particularly near the sea or in open, windswept
areas, would bring a wave of unease and dread. He avoided the scenic coastal paths he and his late
wife used to enjoy, opting instead for the enclosed safety of his small home. The piercing wail of
sirens, whether from an ambulance or a police car, would instantly transport him back to the frantic
urgency of that day, his heart pounding with the echoes of old panic. Even talking about the future
became a source of anxiety. When his grandchildren spoke of their plans, he would grow quiet,
certain that his presence might somehow taint their prospects, or that his own defective nature
would inevitably lead to more harm.

He found himself avoiding going to unfamiliar places because he felt he could not assess the danger,
a lingering fear that he would once again fail to protect himself or others. When he did venture
out, he would always choose where to sit based on how safe or protected he felt, often with his back
to a wall, facing the exit. In a strange, almost contradictory way, he also found himself taking too
many risks or doing things that could cause him harm, perhaps a subconscious form of self-punishment
or a desperate attempt to feel something other than the crushing weight of his past. The once
vibrant social life he shared with his wife had dwindled. He declined invitations to community
events, particularly those held outdoors or in new venues, leaving him increasingly isolated.

The recent passing of his wife had intensified his solitude, leaving him with more time for his
thoughts to circle. He had finally sought help, not out of hope for a cure, but out of a quiet
desperation. He was tired of the constant vigilance, tired of the fragmented memories, and most of
all, he was afraid of dying with this heavy burden still weighing on his soul, believing himself to
be fundamentally defective to the very end.
