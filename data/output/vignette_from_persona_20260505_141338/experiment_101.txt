William, a 67-year-old widower and retired military medic, presented for therapy, his face etched
with a weariness that went beyond his years. The reason for his distress was a life-threatening
motor vehicle collision he experienced six months prior. He had been on his way home from
volunteering at the local VA hospital, a familiar drive he had made countless times, when a drunk
driver swerved into his lane, hitting him head-on. The impact was violent and immediate, pinning him
in the crumpled metal of his vehicle. William remembered the sickening crunch of steel, the sharp
pain, and then, most vividly, the acrid scent of burning rubber and gasoline mingling with the
unmistakable smell of alcohol emanating from the other driver’s vehicle. Though severely injured
himself, his medic training had instinctively kicked in, and he found himself trying to assess the
scene, hearing the other driver’s panicked cries, feeling utterly helpless and furious that he
couldn't physically move to render aid.

In the immediate aftermath, William found it nearly impossible to speak about the accident. When
family or police officers tried to get him to recount what happened, he would lose his train of
thought, the narrative dissolving into fragments. He would start a sentence, then trail off, feeling
as though the terrifying images and sounds were playing out in his mind, but the words to describe
them simply wouldn't come. At times, the entire memory felt profoundly alien to him, as though it
belonged to someone else entirely, a story he had merely overheard rather than lived. He would
sometimes hear the other driver's distressed cries or the screech of tires in his head, even when no
such sound was present.

As weeks turned into months, William found his world shrinking. The once-familiar scent of alcohol,
whether from a passing stranger in the grocery store or a cleaning product, would instantly trigger
a jolt of alarm through his body, his heart racing, his muscles tensing. Seeing images of damaged
cars, even in news reports, would bring a wave of nausea and a sudden surge of adrenaline, his mind
replaying the sight of his own crushed vehicle. The burnt smell, even of something as innocuous as
toast, would send him back to the scene, his breath catching in his throat. He had become profoundly
jumpy, startled by ordinary sounds that never used to bother him – a dropped plate, a car horn, the
sudden ring of his phone – his body reacting with a visceral, uncontrolled tremor.

To cope, William started avoiding anything that might remind him of the crash. He stopped driving
altogether, and even being a passenger became an unbearable ordeal. He would refuse rides, opting
instead to walk or rely on expensive taxi services, which quickly became unsustainable. This
decision had significant consequences for a widower living alone; he found himself increasingly
isolated, unable to visit friends, attend his weekly poker game, or even get to his volunteer shifts
at the VA. He also tried desperately not to think about the accident, pushing the memories away
whenever they surfaced, but they would often return with greater force, unbidden and overwhelming.
Alongside this, he began to constantly monitor his own thoughts, scrutinizing every fleeting image
or distressing idea for signs that he might be "going crazy," a terrifying prospect for a man who
had always prided himself on his mental fortitude.

A profound sense of self-blame had settled over William. He replayed the moments leading up to the
collision countless times, convinced that if he had swerved differently, or reacted faster, or
somehow been more vigilant, the entire event could have been prevented. "It happened because of the
way I acted," he would tell himself, a thought that brought a deep ache to his chest. This
conviction eroded his confidence, leading to a pervasive self-doubt; if he couldn't trust himself to
make the right decision in a life-or-death situation, how could he trust himself with anything now?
He had come to believe he was a weak person, a stark contrast to the capable, resilient medic he had
once been.

The cumulative weight of his isolation, the constant vigilance, and the overwhelming self-reproach
finally brought William to seek help. He felt trapped in his own home, haunted by memories and
terrified by his own mind. He yearned for a return to some semblance of his old life, to be able to
drive himself to his volunteer work, to feel like the strong, reliable man he once was, and to
escape the terrifying certainty that he was slowly losing his grip on reality.
