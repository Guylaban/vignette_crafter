Alana, a 31-year-old professor of Oceanic heritage, remembers the night of the motor vehicle
collision with a frustrating blend of stark clarity and bewildering blanks. She had been driving
home from a late faculty meeting, the streetlights blurring in the rain. One moment, the rhythmic
hum of her car, the next, a deafening crunch of metal, the shattering sound of glass, and the
sickening sensation of her world spinning out of control. Then, a profound, ringing silence, broken
only by the smell of burning rubber and gasoline. She remembers the paramedics cutting her out of
the wreckage, the cold night air on her exposed skin, the urgent, muffled voices. But when she tries
to explain what happened, the narrative often dissolves into disconnected pieces that do not fit
together. Some moments are extremely clear – the glint of chrome just before impact, the feeling of
being lifted onto a stretcher – while others, like the immediate aftermath or conversations with
first responders, are completely blank. She often loses the thread, unable to finish her story,
leaving her feeling frustrated and misunderstood by her husband, David, and her doctors.

Months of physical recovery slowly gave way to a new kind of struggle. Alana returned to her
university, but her internal landscape was irrevocably altered. Her once-vibrant academic life,
steeped in lively philosophical debates, now felt overshadowed by a pervasive sense of dread. Simple
daily occurrences could send a jolt of terror through her. Walking across campus, she might overhear
students discussing a recent accident on the news, or catch a glimpse of a damaged car in a film
clip playing on a monitor. In these moments, a sickening sensation would wash over her, a feeling of
beginning to feel detached or unreal, as if she were floating outside her own body, disconnected
from the very ground beneath her feet. It was a terrifying echo of the disorienting moments after
the crash.

Her sleep offered little respite. Alana often found herself waking suddenly in the dead of night,
her heart pounding, her body tense and alert, compelled to check the locks, peer out the window,
consumed by a desperate need to confirm she was safe. During the day, this hypervigilance persisted.
In the bustling university corridors, she was constantly on guard, scanning faces, alert to every
sudden sound, every unexpected movement. One afternoon, while reviewing student papers in her
office, a sudden, sharp bang from a nearby construction site brought her crashing back to the moment
of impact. For a few horrifying seconds, she was convinced she was back in the car, the crunch of
metal deafening, the smell of gasoline in her nostrils. She could feel the seatbelt tightening, the
sensation of impact, before slowly, painfully, she came back to her office, gasping for breath, her
hands shaking.

The woman she used to be felt like a distant memory. Alana had always been known for her infectious
laugh and zest for life, but now she had come to believe that she was always miserable, the
happiness she once felt irrevocably lost. Her profound sense of disconnection extended beyond her
personal feelings; she was certain that nothing in her life felt meaningful anymore. Her academic
pursuits, once a source of immense satisfaction, now seemed hollow, and she felt a growing
conviction that she no longer belonged anywhere – not among her colleagues, not even within the
comfort of her own home.

To cope, Alana found herself increasingly avoiding situations that might trigger these terrifying
sensations. She stopped driving altogether, relying on David or public transport, which limited her
ability to attend evening lectures or conferences and made her feel professionally isolated. She
carefully curated her social interactions, steering clear of any discussion that veered towards
current events or personal anecdotes involving accidents. This meant she spent more time isolated in
her office, missing out on the casual collegiality that had once been a hallmark of her department.
Her once-active social calendar dwindled, and even simple conversations with David became strained
as she struggled to articulate her inner turmoil without losing her thread or feeling the familiar
rush of unreality.

It was David’s quiet concern, and the growing chasm she felt between them, that finally prompted
Alana to seek help. She walked into the clinic feeling utterly exhausted, trapped in a cycle of fear
and detachment. What frightened her most was the thought that this fractured, joyless existence was
all that remained for her, that the vibrant woman she once was had been irrevocably lost to the
wreckage of that single, catastrophic moment. She hoped, with a fragile thread of desperation, that
someone could help her find her way back, or at least help her understand what had become of her.
