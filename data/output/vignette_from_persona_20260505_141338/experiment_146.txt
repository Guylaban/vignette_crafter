Omar, a 30-year-old tax inspector, sits in the clinic, his posture rigid, eyes scanning the room, as
he recounts the night, just over a year ago, when his life irrevocably shifted. He had been driving

own car, a dark grey sedan he'd been proud of, was utterly demolished. He remembers the paramedics,
the blinding lights, and the pervasive, acrid smell of alcohol clinging to the air, emanating from
the other driver's vehicle. He had been lucky, they told him, to survive with only a broken arm and
a concussion.

But luck felt like a cruel joke. Since the accident, Omar’s world had shrunk. The initial shock gave
way to a persistent, gnawing fear. Now, any loud, unexpected sound – a car horn blaring, a dropped
file in the office, even the sudden clang of a pan in his kitchen – sends a jolt of terror through
him. His body tenses, his heart races, and he jumps, startling at ordinary noises that would never
have bothered him before. He finds himself constantly on guard, his senses heightened, perpetually
superalert and watchful, as if danger lurks around every corner. This hypervigilance is exhausting,
leaving him drained even before his workday begins.

The triggers are relentless. He finds himself frozen, staring, whenever he sees images of cars hit
by accident on the news or online, the mangled metal mirroring the wreckage of his own vehicle. The
faintest whiff of alcohol, whether from a passing pedestrian or a cleaning product, instantly
transports him back to the scene, the smell a potent key unlocking the terror. Even seeing a car
similar to his old sedan on the street can send a wave of nausea through him, forcing him to avert
his gaze quickly.

To cope, Omar has developed a series of strategies that have become habits. He has completely
stopped driving, relying on public transport or expensive ride-sharing services to get to his
various audit sites. This has made his job as a tax inspector incredibly challenging; what used to
be a quick drive to a business across town now involves multiple transfers and unpredictable delays,
significantly reducing the number of inspections he can complete in a day. He’s noticed his
performance reviews are slipping, and the fear that he is no longer capable of doing the things that
defined him – not just his job, but his independence – weighs heavily on him. He also spends long
hours on his phone, playing games or endlessly scrolling the internet, using the screen as an
escape, a way to avoid the intrusive thoughts and the crushing anxiety that rise when he’s alone
with his mind. Food, too, has become a casualty. He often avoids eating or restricts his food
intake, finding a strange sense of control in this self-denial when so much else feels out of his
hands.

Underneath it all, a deep sense of despair has settled. Omar has come to believe that he can't rely
on himself anymore, that his own body and mind have betrayed him, leaving him vulnerable and broken.

distant. He is certain that he will never be able to feel normal emotions again, that the accident

accident, seems to him now like a prophecy of his current emotional barrenness. The constant
