Nia, a 42-year-old train conductor, sat rigidly in the waiting room, her gaze sweeping over the
other faces, searching for any flicker of an unfamiliar intention. Her journey to this point had
been long and fraught, beginning years ago during a period of intense political upheaval in her home
country. She had been unjustly detained, held in a small, windowless room where the air was thick
with the acrid smell of smoke – sometimes from distant fires, sometimes from the cheap cigarettes of
her captors. The worst moments were often the sudden silences, when the muffled sounds from outside
would vanish completely, leaving only the terrifying stillness of her confinement, broken only by
the throbbing pain of her own injuries, which she could feel, but barely see in the gloom. Those
memories, vivid and fragmented, were mostly a series of terrifying images – the glint of metal, the
shadow of a figure, the stark lines of a wound. Even now, she found herself unable to construct a
clear, coherent narrative of exactly what had happened, even for herself. It was a jumble of
sensations and disconnected snapshots that felt as though they were happening right now, in this
very room.

In the years since immigrating to the States and building a new life with her husband, David, Nia
had found a certain rhythm in her work as a train conductor. The steady rumble of the locomotive,
the predictable schedule, the constant flow of people – it all provided a structure that, for a
time, kept some of the past at bay. Yet, the echoes of her captivity were never far. A sudden,
unexpected silence on the tracks, perhaps when the train stopped unexpectedly between stations,
would plunge her back into that dark room, her heart hammering. The faint smell of smoke from a
passenger’s clothing or a distant brush fire seen from the window could instantly transport her,
making her hands clammy on the controls. She was also acutely sensitive to injuries; a passenger
with a visible cut or bruise, even a minor one, would immediately put her on guard, an instinctive
alarm bell ringing in her mind.

At home, Nia found herself unable to truly relax, even in the safety of her familiar living room.
Her senses were constantly on high alert, her ears straining to catch any sound that might indicate
someone was entering her home without her knowledge. David’s sudden movements, a quick turn or an
unexpected gesture, would make her flinch, her body tensing automatically. She had developed a habit
of constantly checking her own body, running her hands over her arms and legs, searching for signs
of danger or injury, a silent ritual that offered a fleeting sense of control. To cope with the
relentless anxiety, she had started smoking, finding a strange comfort in the ritual of lighting a
cigarette, a brief moment of distraction. She also found herself mentally rehearsing worst-case
scenarios, playing out every imaginable catastrophe on her train route, believing that if she could
visualize the worst, she might somehow be prepared for it, might avoid being caught off guard again.

These coping mechanisms, however, were taking their toll. David had grown increasingly concerned by
her constant vigilance, the way she seemed to be always scanning her surroundings, even at their own
dinner table. He’d noticed her jumping at small sounds and had expressed worry about her increasing
smoking habit. At work, her intense focus on potential dangers, combined with the sudden jolts of
reliving the past, had led to a few near-misses with schedules, causing her to fear for her job
security. The thought of losing this steady anchor in her life, of being cast adrift without its
structure, was what finally propelled Nia to seek help, though she remained deeply apprehensive,
uncertain if anyone could truly understand the fragmented terror that still lived within her.
