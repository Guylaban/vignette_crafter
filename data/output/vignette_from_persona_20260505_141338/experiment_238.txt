Thomas, a 49-year-old City Council Member, sits rigidly in the chair, his gaze fixed somewhere
beyond the wall, occasionally darting to the door. He recounts the incident that brought him here,
haltingly at first, as if picking through broken glass. He was deployed overseas, a lifetime ago it
feels, when their convoy was ambushed. He remembers the sudden, deafening roar, the immediate
impact, and then a chaotic blur of dust, shouts, and gunfire. He tries to explain the sequence of
events, but the details are fragmented; certain moments are sharp, almost painfully vivid, while
others are entirely missing, leaving large, unsettling gaps. When he attempts to connect these
pieces, the narrative unravels, and he loses his train of thought, unable to finish a sentence. It’s
not just the words that fail him; trying to piece it together brings back the same visceral terror
he felt at the time, a gripping fear that makes his chest tighten and his breath catch.

After returning home, the world felt fundamentally altered. He had come to believe, with an
unshakable certainty, that the world was a dangerous place, a belief reinforced by every sudden
noise or unexpected movement. His body, he felt, had betrayed him, constantly reacting as if he were
still in the thick of combat, even when he was safe in his own home. He was always on guard, his
senses heightened, scanning every room he entered, every crowd he navigated. This relentless
hypervigilance left him utterly exhausted. He struggled to fall asleep, his mind racing, and if he
did drift off, he would often wake abruptly, his heart pounding.

His public role as a City Council Member, which once energized him, had become a minefield. At town
hall meetings or community events, a well-meaning constituent might place a hand on his arm or back,
and he would flinch violently, reacting with alarm to the unexpected contact. He found it impossible
to trust assurances of safety, even from his wife, Sarah, or his closest colleagues; the internal
alarm bells were always ringing. To cope, Thomas had developed a series of habits. When driving home
from a particularly stressful council meeting, he would blast loud music, or keep the television on
constantly at home, the noise a shield against the silence that allowed his thoughts to spiral. What
frightened him most was the persistent feeling that he was losing his mind; he found himself
constantly monitoring his own thoughts, searching for any sign that he was truly "going crazy"
because of his body's uncontrollable reactions.

The most challenging aspect, both professionally and personally, was his inability to tolerate
physical contact. Handshakes at public events became an agonizing ordeal he tried to avoid, often
crossing his arms or keeping his distance. At home, his marriage to Sarah, once close and physically
affectionate, had grown distant. He found himself stiffening whenever she tried to touch him,
creating a chasm between them that he didn't know how to bridge. This growing isolation, combined
with the relentless internal siege and the fear that he was losing himself, finally prompted him to
seek help. He worried that if he continued down this path, he would completely alienate everyone he
cared about, and lose the sense of purpose he once found in his work.
