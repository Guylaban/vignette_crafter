Samuel, a 58-year-old African American man, had been a pillar of his community for decades, known
for his steady hands and warm conversation at "Sam's Styles," his barbershop. His life took an
abrupt turn one late Tuesday evening, about eight months ago. He had just locked up for the night,
the familiar clinking of keys echoing in the quiet street as he walked home. As he turned down a
less-lit side street, a figure emerged from the shadows, smelling distinctly of stale alcohol. The
man was immediately aggressive, not just demanding money but verbally abusive, his words sharp and
demeaning, accusing Samuel of being slow and stupid as he rifled through his pockets. Samuel
remembered the glint of something metallic in the mugger's hand, the way the man’s eyes darted
around, and the overwhelming sense of helplessness as he was pushed against a wall. The entire
encounter felt like it lasted an eternity, a series of vivid, terrifying pictures flashing before
his eyes: the mugger's sneering face, the dimly lit brick wall, the pavement beneath his feet.

In the months that followed, those visual memories became a relentless internal film, playing over
and over in Samuel’s mind, unbidden and unwelcome. He would be in the middle of a haircut, his
clippers buzzing, and suddenly, he’d see the mugger’s face, feel the push against the wall, hear the
harsh accusations. When his wife, Deborah, gently tried to get him to talk about it, he often found
himself frustrated. He knew exactly what had happened, the precise images were so clear, yet he
struggled to string together words to describe the fear, the indignity, the full emotional weight of
it. It was all pictures, a silent, looping movie.

The world outside his head also became a minefield of triggers. The faint scent of alcohol on a
client’s breath, or even the cleaning solution he used in the shop, could make his stomach clench,
instantly transporting him back to that alley. What truly unnerved him, however, was how acutely
sensitive he had become to criticism. A client might gently suggest, "Could you just trim a little
more off the sides, Sam?" and the words would hit him with the force of the mugger’s verbal assault.
He would feel a sudden flush of anger and shame, his hands trembling slightly, convinced he was
being judged, found wanting, just as he had been that night. He also found himself constantly on
edge, scrutinising everyone who walked past his shop window, every new face that entered. He was
certain that something terrible was about to happen again, his eyes darting to the door, scanning
for any hint of someone acting suspiciously, just as the mugger had seemed to emerge from nowhere.

To cope, Samuel developed a few strategies. He would try incredibly hard not to think about the
trauma, immersing himself in intricate haircutting or listening intently to clients' stories, hoping
to drown out the intrusive images. But the effort was exhausting, and the images always broke
through. He also found himself dwelling endlessly on what he had done wrong. *Why did I take that
shortcut? Why didn't I just hand over the money faster? I should have fought back, I should have
been stronger.* This self-blame was a constant, gnawing presence. Slowly, almost imperceptibly at
first, he began to avoid people. He used to enjoy chatting with other barbers at the local
association meetings, but now he’d make excuses, preferring to stay home. He started declining
invitations from his extended family, finding the effort of feigning normalcy too much. At the
barbershop, he found himself scheduling fewer appointments, particularly in the late afternoons, and
often kept the blinds drawn, preferring the solitude to the constant scrutiny he felt from the
street. This quiet withdrawal began to strain his relationship with Deborah, who missed the
easygoing, sociable man she married. The cumulative weight of the looping memories, the constant
vigilance, and the growing isolation finally brought Samuel to seek help, weary of the film that
played behind his eyes and the fear that dictated his every move.
