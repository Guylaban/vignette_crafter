that felt both too real and utterly detached. His wife, Thuy, had passed many years ago, but it was
the ghost of their son, Minh, that truly haunted him. When Minh was born, tiny and fragile, a severe
congenital heart defect had plunged their lives into a relentless cycle of hospital visits, urgent
procedures, and the suffocating scent of antiseptic. Bao remembered the sterile gleam of the
operating theatre lights, the hushed, urgent voices of doctors, the rhythmic beeping of machines
that had been Minh’s constant companions for the brief, agonizing months of his life. Every memory
of that time was like a film that played over and over in his mind, vivid and relentless, never
truly fading into the past. It felt, to Bao, as if those frantic moments were happening right now,
the terror and helplessness as fresh as they had been decades ago.

In the years that followed, this internal film began to project onto his external world. The smell
of disinfectant, even a common household cleaner, could trigger a sudden, overwhelming physical
reaction – his heart would pound, his breath would catch, and he would suddenly feel as if he were
back in the hospital corridor, waiting for news, the fluorescent lights buzzing overhead. These were
the moments when the past truly became the present, and he would briefly lose touch with his
surroundings, transported entirely back to Minh’s bedside. What frightened him most was the
unpredictability of these episodes, the way they could seize him without warning.

His work as a cybersecurity analyst, a field demanding meticulous attention and clear communication,
became a particular struggle. When an intrusive memory struck, or a subtle antiseptic scent from the

colleague asked him to explain a complex network vulnerability he’d identified, he would often lose
the thread, his words becoming muddled, unable to finish his explanation coherently. He had come to
believe that if he tried too hard to articulate the raw details of his experience, he would simply
fall apart, and the shame of that potential breakdown often kept him silent.

Over time, Bao developed a careful architecture of avoidance. He stopped visiting his younger
colleagues who had recently become parents, unable to bear the sight of healthy infants or the
casual talk of pregnancies and childbirth, which evoked a profound sense of grief and injustice. He
began to decline social invitations, preferring the solitude of his home, where he could control his
environment. More perilously, his own health began to suffer. Annual check-ups became a source of
intense anxiety. The thought of lying on an examination table, the sterile instruments, the doctor’s
questions, all felt like an unbearable echo of Minh’s suffering. He was certain that undergoing any
medical procedure himself would re-open the wound entirely, unleashing a torrent of the strong
negative feelings—the fear, horror, and profound guilt he still carried—that he fought so hard to
keep at bay.

The accumulation of these avoidances, the increasing isolation, and the constant battle against the
relentless intrusion of the past had begun to weigh heavily on Bao. His friends urged him to see a
doctor about his persistent cough, but the mere suggestion filled him with dread. He recognized that
his life had narrowed, constrained by invisible walls built of fear and memory. It was this creeping
realization, the fear that he was slowly disappearing behind these walls, that finally prompted him
to seek help. He came to therapy not knowing if he could ever truly escape the shadow of Minh’s
illness, but hoping, perhaps, to find a way to live without the past feeling so acutely present.
