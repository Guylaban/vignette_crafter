Koa had been an interior designer for over forty years, a profession he loved for its blend of
creativity and precision, but his world began to shrink dramatically after the harrowing experience
of being taken captive during a project abroad. He had been consulting on a large hospitality
development in a remote region when he was seized by an armed group. For weeks, he was held in a
small, windowless room, the air thick with the smell of dust and fear, the only sounds often the
harsh, guttural shouts of his captors speaking in Arabic. The constant sense of being trapped,
exposed, and utterly helpless etched itself deeply into his mind.

Years later, Koa found that the memory of the event would intrude suddenly, often without warning,
plunging him back into the terror of that small room. He might be making a cup of coffee, or simply
sitting in his living room, and the scene would flash before him, vivid and disorienting. What often
accompanied these sudden intrusions, or even sometimes triggered them, was sound. If he heard
shouting in Arabic, whether from a news report on TV or a snippet of conversation in a bustling
market, his heart would instantly pound, his muscles would tense, and his breath would catch. The
feeling of emotional vulnerability, too, had become a potent trigger. Any situation where he felt
exposed or emotionally raw, perhaps during a difficult conversation with a friend, could send a jolt
of fear through him, reminding him of his powerlessness during captivity.

To cope, Koa had developed a series of careful strategies. Before any interaction, even a simple
phone call, he would find himself compulsively rehearsing what to say, meticulously planning every
word and possible response. This made even casual conversations feel like high-stakes performances,
exhausting him and often leading him to avoid social contact altogether. He also began to actively
avoid enclosed spaces. Elevators became unthinkable, small client meeting rooms caused him intense
anxiety, and even his own home felt too confining at times, leading him to spend more time outdoors,
regardless of the weather. The most significant change, however, was his increasing avoidance of
work itself. The focused attention required for design, the need to meet clients in their offices,
and the simple act of sitting at his desk for hours in what felt like a confined space, all became
overwhelming. Projects languished, emails went unanswered, and his once-thriving business slowly
dwindled.

The world, for Koa, felt like a constant threat. He often experienced strong negative feelings—waves
of fear, anger, and a pervasive sense of shame about what he had endured. He found himself unable to
relax unless his back was against a wall, a strategic position that allowed him to see the entire
room, a habit he unconsciously adopted in restaurants, waiting rooms, and even at home. He would
notice his body reacting to danger before his mind had even had time to process it; a sudden loud
noise, a shadow moving unexpectedly, and his heart would be racing, his muscles coiled, before he
could consciously register what had happened. It was as if his body had its own, faster warning
system.

When he tried to talk about his experience, Koa often struggled. Sometimes, he could recall what
happened, but it felt strangely distant, as if it had happened to a stranger, leaving him feeling
completely numb. Other times, when he tried to explain the details to a sympathetic friend or a new
doctor, he would lose the thread, his narrative becoming disjointed, and he would simply give up,
unable to finish. During these attempts at recall, or even when a memory surfaced unbidden, he would
sometimes distinctly hear things in his head that he had heard during the frightening event—the
muffled shouts, the clang of a door, the shuffle of footsteps outside his cell.

Koa’s life had become a quiet, solitary existence, marked by an ever-present vigilance and the
weight of unspoken trauma. His wife had passed away some years prior, and in his widowhood, he found
himself with fewer anchors to the outside world. The increasing isolation due to his avoidance of
work and social interactions, combined with the relentless internal struggle, had finally prompted
him to seek help. He arrived at the clinic, exhausted and apprehensive, hoping for some relief from
the constant fear, but also deeply afraid that he might never truly escape the shadow of that small,
dark room.
