Nia, a 42-year-old African American woman, presents with a history of profound distress following an
experience of torture several years prior. She was abducted late one night after leaving her factory
job, dragged into a vehicle, and taken to a desolate, windowless room. The air was thick with the
smell of damp earth and stale sweat, and outside, a relentless storm raged, its drumming against the
flimsy roof the only sound apart from her captors’ voices. They remained unnervingly close, their
physical proximity a constant, suffocating threat, and repeatedly used a specific, chilling code
name during their interrogation. Nia remembers the sheer terror of being confined and completely at
their mercy.

When Nia attempts to recall the events of that night, the memory often feels fragmented and
dislocated, as if it belongs to someone else rather than to her. The sheer brutality of what
transpired sometimes makes the story feel unreal, too extreme to have possibly happened within her
own lived experience. Yet, the memory is also intensely sensory; occasionally, the distinct smell of
damp earth and a metallic tang will flood her awareness, even when no such scent is physically
present, pulling her back into the horror of that room.

The world Nia inhabits now is fundamentally different. She has come to believe the world is a
profoundly dangerous place, a conviction that colors every interaction and decision. Her body, once
a dependable tool for her physically demanding factory work, now feels utterly unreliable, prone to
sudden, overwhelming surges of fear that bypass conscious thought. She is certain that she is no
longer capable of the resilience and independence that defined her before the event, a feeling
exacerbated by her recent divorce.

This pervasive sense of threat manifests in her daily life. At home, every creak of the old house or
distant siren sends her heart racing. Nia constantly listens for sounds that might mean someone is
trying to enter her home, often checking and re-checking the locks on her doors and windows multiple
times before she can even attempt to rest. Her body often reacts to perceived danger before her mind
has even had time to register what is happening; a sudden jolt, a rapid intake of breath, a pounding
pulse, all occur before she consciously identifies a potential threat. Ordinary sounds, like a
dropped tool on the factory floor or a car backfiring outside her apartment, make her jump
violently, leaving her shaken and hyper-alert for minutes afterward.

Nia has developed several strategies to manage her internal turmoil. She applies heavy makeup each
morning, a thick, protective mask she feels can hide the fear in her eyes and the exhaustion etched
on her face, making her feel less vulnerable to the scrutiny of others. She also makes a concerted
effort to keep her mind occupied at all times, listening to loud music during her commute, focusing
intensely on repetitive tasks at the factory, or watching endless television shows at home, anything
to prevent her thoughts from drifting back to the past. Furthermore, she meticulously avoids wearing
any clothes that resemble what she was wearing during the traumatic event, discarding items that
even vaguely remind her of that night, even if it means significantly reducing her wardrobe options.

Her factory job, with its constant noise and repetitive tasks, provides a double-edged sword. The
steady hum of machinery can be a form of mental distraction, helping her to focus away from
intrusive thoughts. However, the close proximity of co-workers, especially when they brush past her
in the narrow aisles, can trigger an intense surge of panic, bringing back the suffocating feeling
of confinement and unwanted physical closeness. Similarly, the sudden clang of metal or a loud shout
from a supervisor can instantly make her jump, leaving her breathless and disoriented. She finds
herself meticulously planning her movements to avoid accidental bumps or touches from colleagues,
often taking longer routes around the factory floor.

Over weeks and months, these coping mechanisms have taken a toll. Her avoidance of certain clothing
and her constant need for mental distraction have turned getting ready for work into a prolonged,
exhausting ritual, often leaving her sleep-deprived. Her hypervigilance and jumpiness have impacted
her efficiency at the factory, leading to a recent warning from her supervisor about her declining
focus and increased errors. The cumulative exhaustion and the fear of losing her job—her last
bastion of independence, even if she feels increasingly incapable—finally pushed her to seek help.
Nia is tired of living in a state of constant alert, haunted by a memory that feels both intimately
hers and utterly alien, desperate to find a way to trust her own body and the world again.
