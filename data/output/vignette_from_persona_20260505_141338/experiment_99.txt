Leilani, at 71, carried the weight of decades of service as a police officer, but it was the trauma
of a single night that had reshaped her world. It had been three years since her son, Manu, was
critically injured in a head-on collision caused by a drunk driver. Leilani, off-duty but ever
watchful, had been driving home when she saw the flashing lights in the distance, a familiar beacon
of distress. A cold dread had seized her as she recognized the stretch of road, a shortcut Manu
often took. She had pulled over, a seasoned officer’s instinct overriding the rising panic, only to
find the wreckage, the twisted metal of her son’s beloved car, a horrifying silhouette against the
night. The smell of gasoline and something acrid, unmistakably alcohol, had permeated the air, a
scent that still clung to her memory.

From that night onward, Leilani’s world had narrowed. Her memories of the frightening event were not
fleeting; they were intensely vivid, playing out in her mind as a series of clear and detailed
pictures. She would see the crushed vehicles, the frantic movements of the first responders, the
flashing blue and red lights painting the scene in stark, urgent strokes. Sometimes, the images were
accompanied by auditory recall, the distinct sounds of crunching metal, urgent shouts, and the wail
of sirens echoing in her head, as real as if she were back on that roadside.

These intrusive memories were easily triggered. Driving past any scene where cars had been hit in an
accident, even a minor fender bender, would instantly transport her back, the crash imagery so
potent it felt like a physical blow. The smell of alcohol, whether from a passing pedestrian’s
breath or a spilled drink at a family gathering, would send a jolt of alarm through her, a visceral
reminder of the driver who had nearly taken her son. Even casual conversations among her colleagues
about traffic accidents, discussing the details of a recent incident, would cause her to withdraw,
her mind racing with unwanted recollections.

Leilani had come to believe that the event happened because of the way she acted. If only she had
insisted Manu take the longer, safer route that night. If only she hadn't been so preoccupied with a
difficult case earlier in the day, she might have given him a stronger warning. The conviction that
she should have protected others, especially her own child, and that she had failed, weighed heavily
on her. As a police officer, she was sworn to protect, and the inability to shield her own son from
harm felt like a profound personal and professional failing. This sense of personal responsibility
intertwined with a pervasive feeling of helplessness; she was certain now that she couldn't stop bad
things from happening to her or those she loved, no matter how vigilant she was.

Over the past three years, Leilani’s coping strategies had begun to take a toll. At work, she found
herself increasingly avoiding assignments that involved traffic accidents or DUI investigations. She
would invent reasons to stay at the station, delegating responsibilities to younger officers, a
stark contrast to her once proactive approach. This avoidance had led to her being passed over for
promotions and had created a subtle but growing distance between her and her colleagues, who noticed
her increasing withdrawal but didn’t understand its depth. At home, she had stopped attending the
weekly potlucks at her church, fearing the presence of alcohol and the inevitable chatter about

was his unwavering support, and the quiet realization that the woman she once was had become a
stranger, that finally brought Leilani to therapy, hoping to find a way to reclaim herself from the
relentless grip of that night.
