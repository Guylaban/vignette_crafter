Tariq Al-Hamad had always prided himself on his calm demeanor and his ability to navigate the
demanding world of publishing with a steady hand. But that was before. One late evening, just as he
and his wife, Layla, were deep in sleep, an intruder broke into their home. Tariq had woken
instantly, disoriented and utterly exposed, feeling the cool air on his naked skin as he
instinctively tried to shield Layla. The sharp crack of the forced door, Layla's choked scream, the
heavy thud of footsteps – these sounds now echoed in his mind with chilling clarity, replaying
themselves at unexpected moments. He remembered the silhouette of the intruder in the dim light, the
terror in Layla’s eyes, and his own overwhelming sense of helplessness, compounded by the profound
exhaustion he had felt that night after a grueling work week.

In the months that followed, Tariq found his world shrinking. His memories of the event were like a
fractured film, playing over and over in his mind but never in a coherent sequence. He’d see
disconnected pieces: a flash of a hand, the glint of something metallic, Layla’s terrified face, the
bedroom door ajar. He’d often hear the intruder’s heavy breathing or Layla’s cry as if they were
happening in the room with him, even when he knew he was alone. This constant, fragmented replay
made it impossible for him to truly process what had happened, leaving him perpetually on edge.

The feeling of vulnerability he had experienced that night, particularly the acute awareness of
being naked and unprepared, became a potent trigger. Now, even in the privacy of his own bedroom,
undressing for bed or waking up in the middle of the night could plunge him into a cold sweat, his
heart pounding with the memory of that utter helplessness. The exhaustion he’d felt then also became
a significant source of anxiety; feeling very tired or exhausted now sent a jolt of fear through
him, convincing him that he would be unable to protect himself or Layla if something were to happen
again. Public transport, specifically bus riding, also became unbearable. The confined space, the
unpredictable nature of fellow passengers, the feeling of being trapped with no immediate escape
route, all stirred up a visceral dread that mirrored the powerlessness he felt when his home was
invaded. He started taking longer, more expensive taxi rides to avoid the bus routes he once used
daily.

Tariq's daily life transformed into a constant exercise in vigilance. As a publisher, he frequently
met new people, but now, every interaction was a meticulous examination. He found himself studying
people's faces and body language, searching for any subtle sign of aggression, any flicker of threat
in their eyes or a tense set of their jaw. He was certain that if he could just detect the danger
early enough, he could prevent history from repeating itself. This hyper-focus extended to his
surroundings; his eyes constantly darted around, noticing potential weapons or dangerous objects in
any environment – a heavy stapler on a desk, a loose brick on the street, a discarded bottle in the
park. He mentally cataloged them, always aware of his escape routes.

This pervasive fear led him to avoid anything unfamiliar. He used to enjoy exploring new
neighborhoods for independent bookstores or scouting unique venues for book launches, but now, he
avoided going to unfamiliar places altogether. He explained to Layla, and to himself, that he simply
"couldn't assess the danger" in new environments, making them too risky. This avoidance started to
impact his work; he turned down opportunities to attend industry conferences in other cities and
delegated meetings with new authors if they were in unfamiliar parts of town. His colleagues noticed
his withdrawn nature, and Layla felt the growing distance between them, his preoccupation a constant
barrier.

The cumulative toll of this constant vigilance and avoidance was immense. Tariq was perpetually
exhausted, not just from lack of sleep but from the mental burden of his unrelenting fear. He
finally sought help at Layla’s insistence after a particularly bad episode on a crowded subway
platform, where he’d nearly had a panic attack, convinced a stranger was about to attack. He came to
therapy feeling trapped, his life defined by fear and avoidance, desperate to reclaim some semblance
of his former self, but deeply afraid that letting his guard down would only open the door to
another catastrophe. What frightened him most was the idea that he might never truly be safe again,
and that he would forever be unable to protect the person he loved most.
