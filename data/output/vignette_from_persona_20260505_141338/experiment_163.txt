Aisha, a 47-year-old architect, still found it difficult to deliberately piece together the details
of the accident that had occurred almost a year ago. When she tried to access the memory, it felt
like trying to grasp smoke – fragmented and elusive. Yet, in moments she least expected, vivid
sensations would pierce through. She could still taste the metallic tang of fear and dust in her
mouth, just as she had the moment the steel beam, dislodged by a last-minute structural change she
had approved, swung wildly and struck a worker on the site. The sickening *thud* and the worker's
scream, followed by the blare of sirens, often replayed in her mind, an auditory recall that felt as
real as if they were happening again.

In the weeks and months that followed, Aisha found herself dwelling incessantly on what she had done
wrong. She would replay every decision, every conversation leading up to that moment, certain that
if she had just checked one more detail, or pushed back harder on the accelerated timeline, the
accident would have been averted. It was a relentless self-blame rumination that consumed her
thoughts. To cope, she would sometimes remind herself that it could have been worse, that the worker
had survived, a downward comparison she hoped would bring some relief, but the weight of her
perceived culpability remained.

Her daily life, once ordered and purposeful, had become a minefield of potential threats. Working in
her downtown office, surrounded by the constant hum of city life and the occasional clang of nearby
construction, had become a source of constant distress. Ordinary sounds, like a sudden car horn or
the distant rumble of machinery, would make her jump or startle, reacting as if she were in
immediate danger even when nothing was actually wrong. These false alarms left her perpetually on
edge, making it incredibly difficult to concentrate on the intricate designs and blueprints that
were once her passion. She found herself staring blankly at her computer screen, unable to focus,
her mind racing with a vague sense of dread.

Aisha had developed a series of mental rituals, a form of magical prevention. Before every client
meeting, before signing off on any drawing, she would silently repeat a specific sequence of numbers
in her head, convinced that this internal incantation would prevent another catastrophe. This
constant vigilance, coupled with her struggle to concentrate, began to affect her work. Deadlines
were missed, and her once impeccable attention to detail wavered, leading to minor errors that she
would then obsess over, convinced they were precursors to another disaster. She started to delegate
site visits, finding increasingly elaborate excuses to avoid the very places she needed to be to
oversee her projects, fearing that her presence might somehow invite misfortune.

The person Aisha had become felt utterly unrecognisable to her. The confident, decisive architect
who had once thrived on complex challenges had been replaced by someone anxious and withdrawn. She
often thought, "Somebody else would not have gotten into this situation," comparing herself
unfavourably to an imagined, perfect version of herself who would have prevented the tragedy. This
deep sense of shame and inadequacy led her to feel increasingly isolated and set apart from others.
She pulled away from her colleagues, no longer engaging in the easy banter that once characterised
her workplace, and even her relationship with her husband felt strained by the unspoken burden she
carried.

The toll on her career and her marriage was becoming undeniable. Her husband had gently, but firmly,
suggested she seek help, expressing concern for her profound change in demeanour. Aisha finally made
the call, exhausted by the relentless internal battle and the constant fear of making another
mistake. She came to therapy hoping to find a way back to the person she once was, or at least to a
place where she could trust herself again, where the world didn't feel like a constant threat, and
where she could concentrate on her work without the shadow of that terrible day looming over every
decision. Most of all, she longed for a quiet mind, free from the echoes of the past and the fear of
a future she felt powerless to control.
