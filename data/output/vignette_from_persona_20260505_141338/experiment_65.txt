Leilani, a 67-year-old woman of Oceanic descent, found herself sitting in the quiet waiting room,
the hum of the air conditioning a stark contrast to the persistent clamor in her mind. Widowed for
several years, she had built a life around her work as a Database Administrator, a role that allowed
for a degree of solitude she had come to crave. Yet, even in the structured world of data and code,
the echoes of a past trauma continued to shape her present reality.

The abuse had occurred years ago, at the hands of a romantic partner. She remembered one evening
with visceral clarity: a heated argument escalating into a terrifying physical confrontation in
their living room. He had grabbed her by the shoulders, his fingers digging into her flesh, shaking
her until her teeth rattled. What stayed with her most profoundly was the intensity in his eyes as
he yelled, his face inches from hers, and the feeling of being utterly powerless under his grip. She
had felt every muscle in her body tense, her breath catching in her throat, the world narrowing to
the terrifying pressure of his hands and the demanding, unblinking stare that seemed to strip her
bare.

In the aftermath, a profound shift began to take hold. The simple act of going to bed became a
source of dread. Leilani found herself staying up later and later, often until well past 2 AM. She
would sit at her computer, meticulously organizing files, reading articles, or sometimes just
staring blankly at the screen, anything to postpone the moment she had to turn out the lights. She
described it as an unspoken pact with herself: if she could just delay sleep long enough, she might
avoid the vulnerability of the night, the intrusive memories, or the unsettling sense of being
exposed. This habit, formed out of a desperate need for control, left her constantly fatigued, her
days a blur of exhaustion.

Her interactions with others also changed dramatically. The memory of her partner’s grip made any
form of physical contact deeply unsettling. A friendly pat on the shoulder from a colleague at the
office, a hug from a well-meaning friend, or even an accidental brush against someone in a grocery
aisle would cause her to flinch, a sudden, involuntary recoil that she tried to mask with a quick
apology or a feigned distraction. She explained that it wasn't about the person touching her, but
the feeling of being touched itself, a sensation that immediately transported her back to that
moment of powerlessness. She found herself subtly maneuvering to create distance, keeping her arms
crossed, or walking slightly ahead of companions.

At work, her role as a Database Administrator often required collaboration, but Leilani found
herself increasingly avoiding eye contact during meetings or one-on-one discussions. Instead, her
gaze would drift to her keyboard, a desk calendar, or even the wall behind a colleague’s head. She
knew it likely made her appear disengaged or aloof, but the thought of meeting someone’s gaze,
especially for an extended period, brought a surge of anxiety, a faint echo of the intense,
demanding stare she had once endured.

As these patterns solidified, Leilani’s internal world grew increasingly critical. Observing her own
reactions – her inability to sleep, her aversion to touch, her social withdrawal – she had come to
believe, with a heavy heart, that her reactions since the event showed that she was a lousy coper.
She would often think, "Why can't I just get over this like other people seem to?" This self-
judgment was accompanied by a profound sense of loss. "I feel like I don't know myself anymore," she
confided, explaining that the vibrant, outgoing woman she once was had been replaced by someone
cautious, fearful, and perpetually on edge. Deeper still, a pervasive and painful thought had taken
root: "There is something wrong with me as a person." This belief that she was fundamentally
defective, perhaps even broken, colored every aspect of her self-perception.

Over the months and years, these avoidance behaviors and negative appraisals had concrete
consequences. Her chronic fatigue began to impact her focus at work, making complex problem-solving
more arduous. While she remained competent, she rarely took on new challenges or engaged in the
collegial banter that built team cohesion, leading to a quiet isolation in the office. Socially, her
grandchildren, once quick to offer hugs, had gradually stopped, sensing her discomfort, which
deepened her sorrow and reinforced her belief that she was somehow unlovable.

Leilani finally sought help after a particularly lonely holiday season. The quiet of her home,
usually a comfort, had become oppressive, amplifying her sense of isolation and the persistent
belief that she was fundamentally flawed. What frightened her most was the thought that this was
simply her life now, an unchangeable reality. Yet, a small part of her still harbored a fragile hope
that she might, somehow, rediscover the woman she used to be.
