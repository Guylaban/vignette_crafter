Emily, a 35-year-old counselor, sat rigidly in the waiting room, her gaze fixed on a distant point
on the wall, carefully avoiding the eyes of anyone who might pass by. It had been nearly a year
since the accident, but the memory, or rather the fragmented pieces of it, still felt as fresh and
agonizing as the moment it occurred. She had been driving home late after a particularly draining
session, feeling overwhelmed and tired, when she inexplicably ran a red light. The screech of tires,
the sickening crumple of metal, and the sound of shattering glass were vivid, but the actual impact
remained a blurry, disjointed mess in her mind. What she remembered most clearly was the face of the
driver in the other car, contorted in agony, and the piercing wail of approaching sirens, followed
by the flashing red and blue lights of ambulances and police cars. She couldn't tell a clear story
of what happened even to herself, let alone to her husband or anyone else who asked. When those
disconnected pieces of memory did surface, they brought with them an overwhelming wave of grief, not
just for the injured driver, but for the life she felt she had lost.

From that day forward, Emily had come to believe, with unwavering certainty, that the event happened
because of the way she acted. She felt a profound sense of failure, convinced that she should have
protected others and she simply failed. This conviction eroded the very foundation of her identity.
As a counselor, her life's work had been about helping and protecting, yet now nothing in her life
felt meaningful anymore. The joy she once found in her practice, in connecting with clients, had
vanished, replaced by a hollow emptiness.

Her days became a constant battle against the intrusion of painful thoughts and reminders. She tried
hard not to think about the trauma, immersing herself in administrative tasks at her office or
meticulously cleaning her home, anything to keep the memories at bay. Yet, the world around her
seemed determined to pull her back. The sound of an ambulance siren, even faintly in the distance,
would send a jolt of terror through her, making her freeze or pull over to the side of the road
until it passed. Hospitals, physical examinations, and any mention of medical procedures were
meticulously avoided. She had even started to take a longer route to work to bypass the main
hospital entrance, a small detour that had become a significant ritual of avoidance.

Her professional life, once a source of pride, was now a source of immense anxiety. Sitting across
from clients, Emily found it almost impossible to maintain eye contact. She would focus on their
hands, their hair, or a point just over their shoulder, struggling to appear engaged while feeling a
deep sense of shame. This avoidance, coupled with her constant state of hypervigilance – always on
guard, watchful, scanning her surroundings for any perceived threat or reminder – made her feel like
a fraud. She was perpetually exhausted, plagued by trouble falling or staying asleep, her mind
racing with fragments of the accident or hypothetical scenarios of what might have been. The
constant presence of strong negative feelings such as fear, horror, guilt, and shame weighed heavily
on her every waking moment.

Her husband had noticed her withdrawal, her increasing quietness, and the way she flinched at sudden
noises. He tried to reach out, but Emily found herself incapable of sharing the full depth of her

feeling of being trapped in a life devoid of meaning, and the realization that she could no longer
effectively do the work she once loved, that finally brought her to seek help. What frightened her
most was the thought that she might never escape the shadow of that day, that she would remain
forever broken, unable to connect, unable to heal, and unable to find purpose again.
