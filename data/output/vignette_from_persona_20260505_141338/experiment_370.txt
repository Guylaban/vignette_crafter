every breath, the cold, insistent pressure of needles piercing his skin for IV lines and blood
draws, and the constant procession of medical personnel performing procedures, their faces grim. For
days, he hovered on the brink, his body a battlefield, a puppet to the doctors and the disease. He
survived, but the experience left an indelible mark, a constant, low hum of anxiety beneath the
surface of his daily life.

Now, months later, Liam finds his world irrevocably altered. He had come to believe that his body,
once a reliable vessel for his ambitions, was fundamentally unreliable, a ticking time bomb waiting
to betray him again. He often felt as though his very being was his enemy, constantly reacting as if
he were still in danger, even when he was safe in his apartment or at work. This profound shift in
his relationship with himself had also stolen much of the joy and purpose he once felt; nothing in
his life, not even his chosen path as a counselor, felt as meaningful as it once did.

The echoes of his illness were everywhere. The faint, clinical scent of a new cleaning product in
his counseling office, or the sharp, sterile smell of antiseptic in a public restroom, could send a
chilling wave through him, his heart pounding in his chest. A client shifting unexpectedly in their
chair during a session, or a colleague turning a corner too quickly in the hallway, would make him
jump, his muscles tensing, his whole system reacting as if he were bracing for an imminent threat.
He would experience strong physical reactions – a racing pulse, clammy hands, a sudden rush of heat
– whenever something, however innocuous, reminded him of his time in the hospital. He’d be walking
home and catch sight of an ambulance rushing past, lights flashing, and the image of the gurney, the
IV bags, the faces of paramedics, would flash before his eyes, leaving him breathless and
disoriented.

cues. He would take circuitous routes to avoid driving past the local hospital, and the thought of a
routine physical examination filled him with such dread that he repeatedly postponed it, despite his
partner’s gentle urgings. He knew, intellectually, that he needed to process what had happened, but
the mere idea of thinking about anything discussed in therapy, especially his own trauma, felt
overwhelming. He’d change the subject, deflect, or simply shut down internally whenever his partner
or friends tried to inquire about his well-being, creating a subtle but growing distance.

this, Liam had developed a habit of delaying sleep, staying up until very late, scrolling through
his phone, watching movies, anything to keep his mind occupied and avoid the moment he’d be alone
with his thoughts. This, coupled with the persistent anxiety, meant he had trouble falling and
staying asleep, often waking exhausted and irritable. The fatigue bled into his work, making it

What finally brought Liam to seek help was a growing sense of isolation, coupled with a terrifying
realization: he was becoming a less effective counselor, the very thing he had always wanted to be.

betrayal, and that the vibrant, hopeful person he was before his illness was gone for good. He
longed to reclaim a sense of purpose, to trust his own skin again, and to find a way to live without
the constant, exhausting vigilance that had become his unwelcome companion.
