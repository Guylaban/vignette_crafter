into a ‘before’ and ‘after’ the terrifying weeks when her son, Mateo, then just a toddler, was
struck by a sudden, severe illness. The memory of that time was fragmented yet intensely vivid in
specific ways. She remembered the sterile, metallic tang of the hospital air, a taste that now often
returned to her mouth unbidden, sharp and acrid, whenever a memory of Mateo’s small, still form in
the ICU surfaced. She could recall the hushed, almost reverent tones of the doctors, their voices
low and careful, delivering updates that felt like a slow-motion unraveling of her world. What
frightened her most was the silence, the profound, heavy silence of Mateo’s room when he was
sedated, the only sounds the rhythmic, quiet beeps of machines. This silence had come to signify an
impending disaster, a precursor to bad news. Then there were the agonizing delays: endless hours
spent in waiting rooms for test results, minutes that stretched into an eternity when she waited for
a nurse to respond to a call, each passing second fueling her certainty that something terrible was
happening, or about to happen.

In the aftermath, Sofia found herself unable to fully grasp the event. When she tried to think about
it, to make sense of the blur of fear and exhaustion, her mind would slam shut, pushing the memory
away before she could fully recall it. She found herself unable to put the frightening event into
words; it was a jumble of images and sensations, too overwhelming to articulate to friends or family
who asked how she was coping. This struggle to communicate, coupled with the profound shift in her
own internal landscape, left her feeling utterly isolated and set apart from others. She had once
been a vibrant, happy person, but that version of herself seemed to have vanished, replaced by a
constant, low thrum of misery. She believed, with a crushing certainty, that she was helpless,
unable to stop bad things from happening to her or those she loved.

These beliefs and the shadow of Mateo’s illness began to seep into every corner of Sofia’s daily
life. As an accountant, her days were often spent in a quiet office, meticulously poring over
figures. But the quietude that once brought focus now filled her with dread. A lull in office
chatter, the hushed sound of her colleagues focusing, or the unexpected silence when her computer
briefly froze, would make her heart pound, her palms sweat, and her breath catch in her throat –
strong physical reactions that screamed at her that something dangerous was about to happen.
Similarly, any delay became a source of intense anxiety. Waiting for a large file to upload, for a
client to respond to an email, or for her manager to approve a report, would trigger the familiar
surge of panic. She'd find herself tapping her foot incessantly, checking her watch, her mind racing
with catastrophic scenarios, convinced that these delays were harbingers of disaster, just like the
delays in the hospital.

To cope, Sofia developed habits that eventually alienated her further. She started bringing
headphones to work, playing music constantly to drown out the silence, making her seem less
approachable. She became obsessively punctual, arriving early, leaving late, and completing tasks
far ahead of deadlines, driven by a desperate need to avoid any potential 'delay' that might trigger
her anxiety. While her efficiency was noted, her colleagues found her increasingly withdrawn and
perpetually on edge. She stopped socializing after work, preferring to be alone, unable to
articulate the profound misery that had become her constant companion. This self-imposed isolation,
fueled by her belief that no one could truly understand her, only deepened her unhappiness. The
cumulative toll of these changes – the constant vigilance, the inability to find peace in quiet
moments, the pervasive sense of helplessness – eventually brought Sofia to seek help. She was
exhausted, a shadow of her former self, desperately hoping to find a way back to a life where
silence didn't feel like a threat and happiness wasn't just a distant memory.
