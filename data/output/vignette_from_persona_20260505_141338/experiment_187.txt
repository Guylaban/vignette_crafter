Koa, a 63-year-old man with a kind but weary face, sat across from the desk, his hands clasped
tightly. For years, he had been a pillar of his community, his passion for organizing local
initiatives a defining part of his identity. But lately, that identity felt like it was slipping
away, eroded by the lingering shadow of a life-threatening accident he had survived over a decade
ago. He had been driving home late one night after a particularly contentious community meeting, his
mind still buzzing with the day’s discussions, when another driver, running a red light, broadsided
his vehicle.

The collision itself was a blur of screeching metal and shattering glass, but certain moments
remained terrifyingly vivid in Koa’s mind: the sickening lurch, the smell of burning rubber, the
immediate, searing pain, and the feeling of being utterly trapped in the mangled wreckage. Other
parts of the event, however, were simply gone, entire stretches of time he couldn't access no matter
how hard he tried. He knew what had happened, understood the sequence of events intellectually, but
when asked to recount the full experience, he found himself unable to find the words, the language
for the terror and the helplessness eluding him. He would often describe it as feeling like a movie
reel with crucial scenes cut out, leaving him with a fragmented narrative.

In the aftermath, Koa had come to believe that the accident was his fault. He constantly replayed
the moments before impact, convinced that if he had been more alert, if he hadn't been so tired from
the long meeting, if he had taken a different route home, he could have prevented it. This
relentless self-blame gnawed at him, a silent companion that intensified as the years passed.

Over time, his body developed a mind of its own. Even when he was intellectually certain he was
safe, a sudden loud noise or an unexpected movement would send his heart racing, his palms sweating,
his breath catching in his throat. He felt as though his body was keeping him in a perpetual state
of danger, betraying his conscious knowledge of safety. What frightened him most was how his nervous
system would flare up whenever he encountered police or military uniforms. He remembered the stern
faces of the officers at the accident scene, their official presence, the way they had questioned
him, making him feel scrutinized and responsible even as he lay in pain. Now, seeing a police car or
an officer walking down the street would trigger a surge of adrenaline, his body reacting as if he
were once again caught in the chaos of that night.

His work as a community organizer, which once brought him so much purpose, had become increasingly
challenging. Any expression of anger from others, even a calm but firm disagreement during a
meeting, would send a jolt of anxiety through him, instantly transporting him back to the angry
shouts of bystanders at the accident scene, the raw frustration in their voices. He found himself
withdrawing from debates, unable to mediate conflicts as effectively as before, fearing any
escalation of emotion. He also struggled with intrusive thoughts, flashes of the twisted metal, the
smell of gasoline, the feeling of being trapped, which would invade his mind without warning, often
when he was trying to focus on a task.

The emotional toll had been immense. Koa described feeling an pervasive sense of emotional numbness,
as if a thick glass wall had separated him from the world and his own feelings. He had been divorced
for several years, and while the reasons were complex, he knew this internal deadness had
contributed to the growing distance between him and his former wife, and now, even his adult
children. He struggled to feel joy, sorrow, or even genuine connection, often saying he felt "dead
inside," a mere observer of his own life.

Recently, the cumulative effect of his avoidance had become undeniable. He found himself making
excuses to skip community meetings where conflicts were likely, or where he knew police liaison
officers might be present. He would take long, circuitous routes to avoid areas known for police
patrols, meaning he was less visible and accessible to the very community he was meant to serve. He
recognized that his professional life, his passion, was slowly atrophying. He came to therapy
because he was tired of feeling like a ghost in his own life, hoping to find a way to reclaim the
man he once was, before the accident stole his sense of safety and left him feeling so profoundly
disconnected.
