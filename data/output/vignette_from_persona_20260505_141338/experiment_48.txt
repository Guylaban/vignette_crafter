Koa, a 53-year-old photographer, lived with a constant internal hum of readiness, a legacy of the
weeks he spent captive in a remote, unfamiliar land. He had been on assignment, documenting a
little-known community, when he was seized by a small, unpredictable group. Confined to a cramped,
windowless room, Koa’s world shrank to the four walls around him and the erratic movements of his
captors. They would enter without warning, their gestures often sudden and unsettling, their moods
volatile. He spent every waking moment of his captivity observing, planning, and failing to find an
escape route, the knowledge that he was utterly trapped etching itself deep into his psyche.

After his eventual release and return home to his wife, Leilani, Koa’s body never truly relaxed. His
muscles were perpetually coiled, his jaw often tight, and a low thrum of anxiety resonated beneath
his skin. He felt constantly braced, poised to react, even though the immediate danger was long
past. This persistent physical tension, a “body on alert,” was a new, unwelcome companion.

The world outside his home, once a source of inspiration for his photography, now felt fraught with
hidden perils. Any sudden movement by others would immediately put him on guard. A colleague turning
abruptly in the studio, a pedestrian stepping unexpectedly into his camera frame, even Leilani
reaching quickly for a cup in the kitchen – each swift motion would send a jolt through him, his
heart quickening, his gaze sharpening as he instinctively assessed the perceived threat. He had come
to believe that these sudden shifts were precursors to danger, a silent warning he could not afford
to ignore.

This conviction fueled a profound hypervigilance towards people. When out on an assignment in public
spaces, Koa found his eyes constantly scanning crowds, scrutinizing faces, searching for any flicker
of malevolence or unpredictability. He’d note who was standing where, who was looking at whom, his
internal radar perpetually sweeping for potential threats in every human interaction. He was certain
that danger would re-emerge, and it would come from people.

Entering any new place, whether a bustling restaurant for a client meeting or a quiet art gallery
opening, triggered an automatic sequence of protective behaviours. His first priority was always to
find a “safe” spot to sit. He would gravitate towards seats with his back firmly against a wall,
ideally near an exit, where he could observe the entire room without anyone being able to approach
him unseen from behind. He needed to feel protected, to have a clear line of sight, to know he could
react if necessary. This “safe seat seeking” was an unconscious ritual, a small act of control in a
world that felt increasingly uncontrollable.

Simultaneously, his mind would engage in meticulous “escape route planning.” Upon entering any
building – a new studio, a café, even a friend's house – Koa’s internal map would immediately
highlight all possible exits: doors, windows, stairwells. He would mentally rehearse potential
escape routes, evaluating their feasibility, noting obstacles. It was a silent, internal exercise
that played out dozens of times a day, a constant readiness for an emergency he hoped would never
come but was certain could.

At home, the persistent internal alarm often manifested outwardly. He found himself frequently
turning to Leilani, asking, "Are we okay?" or "Is everything alright?" He needed her affirmations,
her calm presence, to momentarily quiet the pervasive uncertainty that gnawed at him, even though
the effect was often fleeting. This constant "reassurance seeking" became a quiet strain on their
loving marriage.

The cumulative effect of these changes had taken a heavy toll. His photography work, once his
passion and livelihood, became increasingly challenging. He found himself declining assignments that
required him to be in crowded, unpredictable public spaces, or those that involved working closely
with new teams of people. His creativity felt stifled by the relentless vigilance, and the sheer
energy he expended on threat monitoring left him perpetually exhausted. Leilani, though patient and
understanding, had begun to express concern about his constant need for reassurance and his
increasing withdrawal from social activities. It was her gentle but firm insistence, coupled with
his own profound weariness of living in a perpetual state of alert, that finally brought Koa to seek
help. What he hoped for most was to reclaim the joy he once found in his art and to feel truly safe
again, to finally let his body and mind rest.
