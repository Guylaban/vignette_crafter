Priya, a 32-year-old chaplain, found her life irrevocably altered by the harrowing experience of
being captured and tortured while on a humanitarian mission abroad. She remembered the stark, cold
floor beneath her, the smell of damp concrete, and the constant, unsettling clang of metal doors
echoing through the confined space. What seared into her memory most vividly was the feeling of
rough ropes biting into her wrists and ankles, holding her immobile for what felt like an eternity.
Figures in dark, unfamiliar uniforms moved in and out of her periphery, their presence a constant,
suffocating threat. Each time they approached, the specific guttural sounds of their language, sharp
and aggressive, would pierce through the haze of fear, leaving her in a state of terror. Even after
her eventual release and return to the United States, the echoes of that time continued to haunt
her.

In the initial weeks and months following her return, Priya found herself struggling to articulate
the unspeakable. When well-meaning friends and family asked what had happened, she would try to
explain, but the words simply wouldn’t come. She knew what had happened, the terror was palpable
within her, but she was unable to find words to describe it. As she attempted to piece together a
coherent narrative, her thoughts would scatter, and she would lose the thread, unable to finish. It
was as if the frightening event couldn't be put into words; she experienced it more as a jumble of
sensations, images, and raw emotion rather than a linear story. This inability to communicate her
experience deepened her sense of isolation.

Her work as a chaplain, which once brought her profound solace and purpose, became a minefield of
triggers. The sterile, brightly lit hospital corridors, with their wide-open spaces, often left her
feeling exposed and vulnerable, as if she were once again in an interrogation room with nowhere to
hide. She found herself instinctively avoiding the hospital’s expansive main lobby, opting for
circuitous routes through less-trafficked service hallways, even if it meant being late for a
patient visit. The sight of security guards in their dark uniforms, or even police officers visiting
patients, would send a jolt of ice through her veins, reminding her viscerally of the figures who
had held her captive. Even the mundane clatter of a food cart or the distant, muffled sound of a
pager could instantly transport her back, triggering a wave of intense fear and a racing heart.

When these intrusive memories and sensations became overwhelming, Priya had developed a desperate
coping mechanism. She would hurt herself physically, a sharp scratch or a pinch, finding that the
immediate, acute pain served as a potent distraction, a way to stop thinking about the event and the
unbearable feelings it brought. This private ritual became a grim comfort, a momentary reprieve from
the internal chaos. Her social life, once vibrant, had withered. Her marriage had ended in divorce,
partly due to her increasing withdrawal and her inability to share the depth of her suffering. She
started actively avoiding social contacts, particularly friends she had socialized with before the
event, convinced that they wouldn’t understand the person she had become, or that her presence would
only burden them. She had come to believe that it was safer, for herself and for others, to remain
in the periphery.

Now, Priya found herself at a crossroads. The constant vigilance, the self-harm, and the profound
isolation had taken their toll. Her ability to connect with her patients, a core part of her
chaplaincy, was diminishing, and she feared losing the last vestige of her former self. What
frightened her most was the prospect of this internal prison becoming her permanent reality, and it
was this fear, coupled with a desperate longing for some semblance of peace, that finally led her to
seek help. She hoped, perhaps against hope, that someone could help her find a way out of the
shadows that had consumed her life.
