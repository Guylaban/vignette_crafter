Marek, a 21-year-old public defender, was 15 when he and his family fled their war-torn homeland, a
journey that still casts a long shadow over his life. He remembers vividly the days spent hiding in
the skeletal remains of an abandoned building, the pervasive silence broken only by the distant
thrum of artillery or, more terrifyingly, the sudden, sharp crack of a nearby gunshot. One night,
the silence was shattered by the violent kick of a door downstairs, followed by the sound of
splintering wood as soldiers searched the building. Marek recalled pressing himself against a cold,
damp wall, his muscles rigid, holding his breath until his chest ached, the absolute quiet that
followed the terrifying sounds almost worse than the noise itself. The soldiers eventually moved on,
but the memory of that moment, of his body tensed and still, remains etched in him.

Even now, six years later, these memories often resurface, typically as fleeting visual images: a
flash of splintered wood, the grimy texture of the wall, the dark, dust-filled air. Sometimes, the
memory isn't a picture at all, but a powerful wave of pure, unadulterated terror, a suffocating
feeling that washes over him without any clear image or context. And when the memories do come, he
often feels his body re-enact that moment, his muscles tensing, his shoulders hunching as if he's
still pressed against that wall, holding his breath.

Marek has come to believe, deep in his bones, that the world is a fundamentally dangerous place. He
feels he has to be on guard all the time, a constant vigilance he maintains because, as he often
tells himself, you never know what can happen next. This heightened state of alert means he startles
easily; a sudden slam of a door in the courthouse or the unexpected ring of a phone makes him jump,
his heart instantly racing. Paradoxically, unusual silence also makes him feel something dangerous
is about to happen, transporting him back to those terrifying moments of quiet during his flight.
These reactions often leave him feeling as if he’s in danger even when nothing is actually wrong,
his body responding with fight-or-flight readiness to benign stimuli.

In his demanding role as a public defender, Marek finds his vigilance both a perceived asset and a
source of constant strain. He constantly scans his surroundings in the busy courtroom, always
assessing potential threats, though this hyper-awareness exhausts him. His professional environment
also presents daily challenges to his coping strategies. He actively avoids talking with colleagues
or his partner about his past experiences as a refugee, deftly changing the subject or offering
vague answers when his upbringing is mentioned. He also finds himself constantly monitoring his own
thoughts, fearful that these intense, unpredictable reactions mean he is going crazy, a terrifying
prospect he tries desperately to suppress. This avoidance of discussing the event or its aftermath
has created a subtle but persistent distance in his relationship, leaving his partner wondering
about his guardedness.

The cumulative toll of this constant vigilance and internal struggle has become immense. He came to
therapy not after a single dramatic event, but because the exhaustion and the pervasive sense of
unease had become too much to bear. He worries deeply about the impact on his relationship and his
ability to truly connect with others. What frightens him most is the persistent thought monitoring,
the fear that his mind is somehow breaking, and he desperately hopes to find a way to regain some
sense of safety and control in his own head.
