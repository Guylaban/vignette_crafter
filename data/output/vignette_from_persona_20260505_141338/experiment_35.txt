Eleanor, at eighty years old, carried the weight of a lifetime marked by a traumatic betrayal that
had occurred decades ago. She remembered the evening with her former partner, Arthur, in their
shared apartment, not as a coherent story, but as a series of disjointed, vivid snapshots: the
sudden, unexpected shift in his expression, the sharp sting of impact, the cold floor against her
cheek. The sequence of these moments often felt confused, jumbled in her mind, yet each picture
brought with it an overwhelming wave of grief that felt as fresh as if it had happened yesterday.

The abuse had shattered Eleanor’s youthful beliefs about the world. She had once thought people were
inherently good, that love offered safety, but Arthur’s violence had irrevocably convinced her that
people simply couldn’t be trusted, especially those closest to you. A deep, pervasive feeling of
being dirty or contaminated by what had happened settled within her, a stain she had never been able
to wash away, regardless of how much time passed or how much distance she put between herself and
the event.

This deep-seated mistrust and the lingering memory of danger shaped Eleanor’s daily life. As a
dedicated social worker, her profession required constant interaction, yet being alone with men,
whether clients in her office or colleagues in a quiet hallway, always triggered a subtle but
intense unease. She would find herself instinctively angling her body towards the door, or ensuring
another person was within earshot, a constant, low hum of alertness beneath her professional
demeanor. Encounters with known people, particularly those who stirred echoes of that time or the
relationship’s dynamics, would send a familiar tightening through her chest, a stark reminder of
vulnerability. Even the most innocuous sounds could be startling; a door opening unexpectedly or a
sudden slam would make her heart pound, a jolt of fear running through her. In her office, she often
kept her door slightly ajar, a compromise between her need to avoid the jarring sound of it being
opened and her fear of feeling exposed.

Eleanor lived in a perpetual state of hypervigilance. As soon as she entered any room or building –
be it a client’s home, a community center, or even a grocery store – her eyes would immediately scan
for exits, mapping out potential escape routes, a habit so ingrained she barely noticed she was
doing it. This constant watchfulness bled into her nights, making sleep elusive. She struggled to
fall asleep, her mind racing, replaying fragments of memories or anticipating unseen threats. She
often woke frequently, her sleep disrupted. Eleanor had come to associate the quiet hours before bed
with vivid, frightening nightmares, leading her to adopt a strategy of avoiding dreams altogether by
staying up late, occupying herself with books or television until exhaustion finally claimed her.

For decades, Eleanor had actively tried to erase the memory of the event, pushing it deep into the
recesses of her mind, avoiding any reminders, any photographs, any discussions about that painful
chapter. Yet, paradoxically, a part of her mind was consumed by meaning seeking. She would endlessly
ruminate on *why* it had happened, replaying the snapshots in her mind, searching for a reason, a
sign she had missed, something that could explain the inexplicable violence and perhaps restore some
order to her shattered beliefs.

At eighty, the cumulative toll of these strategies and symptoms had become immense. Her social work
career, while providing a sense of purpose, was also a constant drain, requiring her to manage her
internal state while being present for others. While she had helped countless individuals, her deep-
seated mistrust meant she had remained single, having never fully recovered from the profound
betrayal. The constant effort to manage her internal world, coupled with the increasing physical
demands of aging, had left her profoundly weary. Eleanor came to therapy not with a clear goal, but
with an overwhelming sense of exhaustion, perhaps a desperate hope for some peace in her final
years, yearning for a night of undisturbed sleep, a moment where she didn’t have to scan for exits,
or feel the phantom dirtiness of contamination.
