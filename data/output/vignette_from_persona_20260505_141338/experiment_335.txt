Nia, a twenty-year-old pharmacist, carried the weight of a past trauma that felt both intimately
hers and strangely detached. She had been visiting family abroad when she was abducted and held in a
desolate, makeshift detention center. The most vivid and horrifying memories revolved around a
small, perpetually damp room where she was repeatedly subjected to waterboarding, the sensation of
suffocating, icy water filling her lungs. Her captors often engaged in volatile, shouting arguments
amongst themselves, their raised voices echoing through the thin walls, instilling a deep, visceral
terror. She was also forced to comply with humiliating and degrading demands, acts that stripped
away every shred of her will, leaving her with an enduring sense of powerlessness.

In the immediate aftermath of her release, Nia found herself grappling with a fragmented
recollection of the ordeal. She could not seem to put the frightening event into words, the full
narrative eluding her grasp. Instead, her memory came in disjointed, nonverbal fragments, often
accompanied by a terrifying bodily reenactment. When a memory of what happened would surface, she
would suddenly feel her body contorting into the same position as when the frightening event
occurred, her muscles tensing, her breath catching, as if she were reliving the suffocation and
restraint. She felt like she was only remembering parts of what happened, the rest a terrifying,
inaccessible blank. This incompleteness, she had come to believe, was a sign of her own weakness, a
failing to properly process the horror.

A pervasive sense of guilt settled over Nia. She felt guilty for surviving when others she had seen
in captivity had not, a heavy burden that whispered she didn't deserve to be free. This survival
guilt was often accompanied by a harsh, internal voice telling herself she deserved to suffer
because of what happened, a form of punishment thinking that fueled her reluctance to seek comfort
or joy. She had become convinced that she had to be on guard all the time, a hypervigilance that
extended to every aspect of her life. The thought of revisiting the traumatic event, of piecing
together the missing parts, was overwhelming; she firmly believed that if she thought about the
event, she would not be able to handle it, fearing she would completely break down.

Now, working as a pharmacist in a busy urban clinic, Nia’s past shadows constantly bled into her
present. The mundane elements of her daily life often became potent triggers. A customer spilling a
large cup of water in the waiting area, or the sound of heavy rain drumming against the pharmacy
windows, could instantly plunge her back into the terror of the flooded room, leaving her breathless
and disoriented. Arguments, whether between two impatient customers or a brief, raised-voice
disagreement between colleagues, made her heart pound, her hands tremble, transporting her to the
chaotic, fear-filled exchanges of her captors. Even a simple, firm directive from her manager, or a
demanding patient insisting on a specific action, could trigger a profound sense of losing autonomy,
a suffocating feeling that she was being forced to do something against her will, making it
difficult for her to respond calmly.

Nia constantly scanned her surroundings for signs of danger, her eyes darting between faces in the
waiting area, monitoring the movements of every person who entered the pharmacy. Before she could
feel comfortable anywhere, she always identified escape routes, mentally mapping out the fastest way
out of the pharmacy, her apartment, or any restaurant she visited. Sleep offered no true respite;
she had difficulty falling asleep because she needed to stay alert, her mind racing, listening for
any unusual sound outside her apartment, effectively avoiding dreams by depriving herself of deep
rest. She also found herself subtly avoiding certain customers or delivery personnel who bore a
passing resemblance, however slight, to her former captors, even if it was just a similar build or a
particular mannerism.

Over the months, these strategies took their toll. Her constant exhaustion from sleep vigilance made
it harder to concentrate on complex prescriptions, increasing her anxiety about making a critical
error. Her subtle avoidance of certain individuals at work led to misunderstandings and
occasionally, complaints about her detached demeanor, impacting her professional reputation. In her
relationship, her partner grew increasingly concerned by her withdrawn nature and her inability to
truly relax, often feeling shut out by her relentless need to be on guard. What finally brought Nia
to seek help was a particularly intense argument with her partner, which, combined with a sudden
downpour outside her apartment, had triggered a full-blown bodily reenactment, leaving her curled on
the floor, gasping for air. In that terrifying moment, she understood she could no longer manage the
fragments of her past alone.
