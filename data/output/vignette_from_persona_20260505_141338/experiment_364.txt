Sione Taufa, a man whose 63 years were etched with the quiet dignity of an airline pilot, sat in the
consulting room, his hands clasped tightly in his lap. For decades, he had navigated the world's
skies, a career that once brought him immense pride, but now felt like a constant test of endurance.
His journey into this persistent state of unease began abruptly during his youth, when he served as
a co-pilot on a Medevac helicopter in a distant conflict zone. He vividly remembered the mission: a
desperate attempt to extract wounded soldiers from a scorching hot landing zone under a hail of
enemy fire. The helicopter had taken a direct hit, lurching violently before crash-landing with a
sickening thud. Sione had been thrown against the console, his world dissolving into a chaos of
grinding metal and screams. He could still feel the gritty, rough texture of the sand and debris
that had filled the cockpit, the acrid smell of hydraulic fluid mixing with something metallic and
sweet. He remembered the incessant, terrifying thumping of the rotors before they abruptly died,
plunging the scene into an eerie, dangerous silence. It was just as dusk was settling, around six in
the evening, when he had to help pull his injured comrades from the wreckage, his hands brushing
against the rough, blood-soaked fabric of a soldier's uniform.

In the years that followed, Sione had tried to bury the experience, to leave it in the past. Yet,
the past refused to stay put. The memories themselves often felt strangely detached, like watching a
harrowing scene unfold on a screen, "like it belongs to someone else rather than to me." But then,
without warning, a specific sensation or sound would instantly shatter that fragile detachment,
bringing back "the full memory" with the same overwhelming "terror" he had felt during the crash.

His life as an airline pilot, a profession he had once loved, became a minefield of these unexpected
triggers. The distant thrum of a helicopter, often heard near airports or over cities, would send a
jolt through his entire body, his heart instantly racing. He’d find himself gripping the controls
tighter, a cold sweat breaking out on his brow, even as he consciously reminded himself he was in a
commercial jet, thousands of feet above the ground. The tactile memory of the rough sand and
bloodied fabric would sometimes resurface in unexpected ways; the coarse weave of a new uniform, the
texture of a seatbelt, or even the rough grip on a control stick could unexpectedly transport him
back to the wreckage. Most insidiously, as six o’clock in the evening approached, regardless of
whether he was mid-flight, preparing for a departure, or at home with his wife, Mele, a profound
agitation would settle over him, making him irritable and intensely watchful.

This constant internal battle had reshaped Sione’s perception of the world. He had come to believe
that "the world is a dangerous place," and that safety was an illusion. This conviction manifested
in his daily routine. As soon as he entered any room or building, whether it was an airport lounge,
a restaurant during a layover, or even a friend’s living room, his eyes would instinctively sweep
the area, "scanning for exits" and potential threats. At home, "night vigilance" had become his
unwelcome companion; he found it "harder to feel safe at night than during the day," often lying
awake for hours, straining to hear every creak and rustle, convinced that danger lurked just beyond
the walls. Any "sudden movements by others," whether a flight attendant reaching for an overhead bin
or Mele turning quickly in the kitchen, would put him "immediately on guard," his muscles tensing,
ready to react.

He often reflected on his younger self, thinking, "I used to be a happy person, but now I am always
miserable." A deep sense of shame had settled over him, a pervasive feeling that "there is something
wrong with me as a person." He felt defective, broken, unable to simply "get over" what had
happened. This belief led him to withdraw, to avoid burdening Mele with his constant anxiety and
jumpiness. He started taking longer routes to the airport, sometimes missing the familiar helicopter
sounds. He found excuses to decline social invitations from colleagues, preferring the solitude of
his hotel room on layovers. What had once been a vibrant marriage began to fray under the weight of
his unspoken fears and his increasing emotional distance. Mele noticed the change, the perpetual
tension in his shoulders, the way his eyes never quite settled. Their once easy conversations were
replaced by strained silences, and their connection, which had always been his anchor, felt
increasingly tenuous.

The breaking point came after a particularly intense flight where a sudden, unexpected helicopter
sound from a ground crew during landing had triggered a momentary disorientation, almost leading to
a procedural error. Coupled with a night where his night vigilance had kept him awake until dawn,
convinced of an unseen threat, Mele had finally insisted he seek help. Sione arrived at the clinic
feeling utterly exhausted, a deep weariness in his bones. He confessed that a part of him believed
he was too far gone, that this misery was simply his lot in life. Yet, another, smaller part, the
part that still remembered the man he used to be, held a fragile hope that he might, somehow, find a
way back to a life where he didn't constantly feel on the brink of disaster, a life where he could
simply feel safe again.
