that once brought him immense satisfaction. A few years prior, his world had been irrevocably
altered by a brutal assault. He had been leaving a community fundraiser, a little later than usual,

the sickening smell of stale alcohol on his attacker, and the terrifying sensation of being utterly
powerless as he was repeatedly struck and left unconscious. He awoke days later in a hospital bed,
the pervasive, clinical scent of antiseptic assaulting his senses, a stark reminder of his profound
vulnerability and severe injuries.

Since that night, Robert’s memories of the assault were not a continuous narrative but rather a
series of vivid, terrifying snapshots: the flash of a dark shape, the sudden pain, the metallic
taste of blood, the sterile white of the hospital ceiling. These fragments would appear suddenly,
without warning, each bringing back the exact terror he had felt in those moments, a visceral,
overwhelming fear that pulsed through his body before vanishing as quickly as it came. What
frightened him most was how real the terror felt each time, as if he were reliving the trauma anew.

strength cleaning products, particularly around the nurse’s office, could instantly transport him
back to the overwhelming antiseptic smell of the hospital, leaving him feeling disoriented and
exposed. The smell of alcohol, even a residual whiff from a student’s breath or an alcohol-based
hand sanitizer, would send a jolt of alarm through him, making him acutely aware of his
surroundings, his heart pounding. But perhaps most challenging in his role as a counselor was the
trigger of emotional closeness. When a student would lean in, confiding a personal struggle, or when
his wife, Mary, sought intimate conversation, Robert would feel an immense unease. He had come to
believe that allowing himself to be emotionally close to others was inherently dangerous, a
precursor to betrayal or harm.

time, had begun to isolate him. When a trigger would strike, particularly during a counseling
session or a conversation with Mary, he would deliberately try to leave his body, to blank out, his

avoid people more generally, taking his lunch alone in his office, declining invitations to staff
gatherings, and making excuses to skip social events with friends. The most painful avoidance,

The consequences of these changes had slowly accumulated. At school, students, who once sought him
out for his warm, approachable demeanor, now seemed to find him distant and less available. His

for his work. At home, Mary had grown increasingly confused and hurt by his withdrawal, leading to a
palpable tension in their marriage. It was the growing silence between them, the look of

by people, that finally compelled Robert to seek help. He was terrified of losing the most important
relationships in his life, and he desperately longed for a way to reclaim the peace and connection
he once knew.
