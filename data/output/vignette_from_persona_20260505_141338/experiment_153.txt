Zara, a 22-year-old South Asian woman, found herself navigating a life increasingly constricted by
the echoes of a traumatic night six months prior. She had been on her final delivery run, a late-
night shift in an unfamiliar part of the city, when two figures emerged from the shadows near her
car. A rough voice had demanded her keys and phone, a cold, metallic glint briefly visible in the
low light. The sheer terror of being forced to comply, the feeling of absolute powerlessness as her
car was driven away, leaving her stranded and shaking, had imprinted itself deeply. The subsequent
arrival of the police, while necessary, had only heightened her sense of vulnerability, the flashing
lights and stern questions making her feel like a suspect in her own ordeal, cementing a new, uneasy
relationship with authority.

In the aftermath, Zara found her memories of the event fractured and disorienting. She rarely saw a
full, coherent picture of what happened; instead, her mind often presented the frightening event as
a few distinct moments, each a vivid, still picture – the glint of metal, the receding taillights of
her stolen car. Other times, the full emotional intensity of the event would wash over her without
any clear image or memory at all, a sudden, overwhelming wave of terror and despair. Sometimes, when
these feelings arose, she would hear things in her head that she heard during the frightening event:
the low, menacing growl of the voice, the click of the car door as it was pulled open. She often
found herself telling herself that the event did not really happen or was not that bad, a desperate
attempt to push away the overwhelming reality of her experience, especially the gnawing thought that
she should have done more to stop what happened. She would never forgive herself for her perceived
inaction.

These days, Zara’s life as a delivery driver had become a constant, exhausting battle. The very act
of driving or being a passenger in a vehicle, once her means of livelihood and independence, now
brought a suffocating wave of anxiety. She found herself taking fewer shifts, especially avoiding
night deliveries, and constantly rerouting to avoid streets that felt too dark or isolated, a
significant reduction in her income. She was certain that she couldn't stop bad things from
happening to her, a belief that colored every interaction. When a customer was demanding or a
supervisor gave an instruction, the subtle pressure to comply would trigger a profound sense of
panic, reminiscent of being forced to hand over her keys. Any glimpse of a police car, even parked
on a routine patrol, would send a jolt of fear through her, making her heart pound and her hands
tremble. Even more pervasive was the constant vigilance for people acting suspiciously – a group of
teenagers loitering, someone walking too close to her car – turning every urban landscape into a
potential threat.

Zara’s days were now defined by an exhausting hyper-alertness. She was constantly identifying escape
routes before she felt comfortable anywhere, scanning exits in restaurants where she picked up
orders, or even planning how she would get out of her own apartment building if danger struck. Sleep
offered little respite; she would wake during the night to check whether she was safe, her mind
replaying potential threats. Even when she knew she was in a safe place, like her own bed or a
friend’s apartment, she was unable to relax, her body tense, her mind racing. To quiet the
relentless fear and the emotional intensity that flared without warning, she had started taking
drugs, a desperate attempt to numb the incessant dread. This had only made her feel more isolated
and set apart from others, a secret burden she couldn't share. The combination of reduced work,
financial strain, and the overwhelming sense of constant threat had finally pushed her to seek help,
hoping for a way to reclaim some semblance of her former self, but terrified that the world would
forever remain a dangerous, unpredictable place she was powerless to navigate.
