Isabella, 38, presented for therapy after months of struggling with an unrelenting sense of dread
and despair. A single woman working long hours on an assembly line in a bustling factory, her life
had been irrevocably altered a year and a half prior by the sudden, unexpected death of her mother,
Elena. Isabella vividly recalled the day as a slow-motion nightmare: her mother, vibrant and full of
life one moment, collapsed without warning in their kitchen. Isabella, the only one home, had
frantically called 911, her heart pounding against her ribs as she listened to the dispatcher's calm
instructions. The wail of approaching sirens had been deafening, a harbinger of doom that now echoed
in her memory. She remembered the blur of paramedics, the desperate attempts to revive her mother,
and the final, crushing pronouncement that Elena was gone. The funeral itself was a haze, a
necessary ritual she endured through a fog of disbelief and profound shock.

Since that day, Isabella’s world had shrunk. The sound of sirens, whether from an ambulance on the
street or a police car passing her factory, would instantly transport her back to that kitchen, her
breath catching in her throat, her hands trembling. She found herself increasingly avoiding anything
that might remind her of her loss. This meant steering clear of cemeteries, turning off the radio if
a somber song played, and even changing her route home to avoid the funeral home she used to pass.
At work, the loud, repetitive hum of the machinery often provided a backdrop for her mind to wander,
and it was in these moments that she would find herself going over and over the details of her
mother’s collapse. She scrutinised every second, replaying her actions, searching for a sign she
might have missed, a different choice she could have made that might have changed the outcome.
*Could I have called sooner? Did I explain the symptoms clearly enough?* These questions circled
endlessly, offering no answers, only deeper distress.

What frightened Isabella most was the profound shift she felt within herself. She had always been a
cheerful, optimistic person, the one who found joy in small things, but that version of herself felt
utterly lost. "I used to be a happy person, but now I am always miserable," she confided, her voice
flat. This pervasive misery was accompanied by a deep conviction that "my life has been destroyed by
the trauma." She no longer found pleasure in activities she once loved, and even simple social
interactions felt draining. She had started to believe that "I no longer belong anywhere," a feeling
amplified by her reluctance to engage in conversations about grief or loss. If a co-worker in the
breakroom mentioned a relative’s illness or a friend’s recent bereavement, Isabella would quietly
gather her things and retreat, sometimes feigning a sudden task on the assembly line. This
avoidance, over time, led to a growing isolation. Her siblings, who had initially tried to support
her, found her increasingly distant and unwilling to talk about their mother, leading to a strain in
their relationships. Isabella, in turn, felt an urge to constantly seek reassurance from her closest
friend, asking, "Am I okay? Do you think I'm handling this wrong?" or "Was it my fault?" hoping for
a definitive answer that never truly settled her unease.

The relentless cycle of rumination, avoidance, and the crushing weight of her negative beliefs had
begun to take a significant toll. Her concentration at the factory had suffered, leading to minor
errors and a noticeable dip in her usual efficiency, though her supervisors had yet to address it
directly. The constant internal turmoil left her exhausted, and the thought of facing another day
filled her with dread. It was the feeling of being utterly consumed by her past, coupled with the
growing chasm between her and her family, that finally led Isabella to acknowledge she needed help.
She came to therapy hoping to find a way back to the person she once was, or at least to a life that
didn’t feel perpetually destroyed by the shadow of her mother’s death.
