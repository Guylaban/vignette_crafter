to the day that irrevocably altered the landscape of his inner world. It had been a complex vascular
procedure, and an unexpected electrical malfunction in a piece of critical cautery equipment had
surged, causing a sudden, acrid burst of smoke and intense heat within the sterile confines of the
operating room. He remembered the sickening, sweet smell of something burning, a smell that had
clung to his mask and skin, and the immediate, searing heat he felt on his exposed forearms as he
instinctively shielded his patient. The incident, though quickly contained, had left a junior nurse
with a severe burn and a lingering, metallic tang in the air that Minh could still conjure with
terrifying clarity. In the chaotic moments that followed, the sequence of events had blurred into a
jumbled, disorienting mess, and even now, trying to piece it together felt like trying to grasp
smoke. The memory itself often felt alien, as if he were observing a scene from a film rather than
reliving a profound personal trauma.

In the weeks and months that followed, Minh found himself wrestling with a pervasive sense of
unease. He would often feel the full, crushing weight of the incident’s emotional intensity – the
shock, the terror, the helplessness – without any distinct visual image to accompany it, a
disembodied dread. He tried desperately to push these thoughts away, actively suppressing any
flicker of the memory that dared to surface, convinced that dwelling on it would only make him less
capable. His constant vigilance became a new, exhausting baseline; he had come to believe he had to
be especially careful because you never knew what could happen next. His own body, once a reliable
instrument in his precise profession, now felt like an enemy. It kept reacting as if he were still
in danger even when he knew he was safe, jumping violently at unexpected or loud sounds in the
hospital corridors, or startling at the sudden clang of an dropped instrument.

The world outside the operating room became a minefield of triggers. A burnt smell from a
colleague's forgotten toast in the breakroom could send a jolt of panic through him. The specific
warmth of a hot cup of coffee or even a warm shower on his skin would sometimes trigger a rush of
anxiety, a phantom echo of the heat from that day. The inherent stress of his demanding surgical
practice, once a familiar challenge, now felt like a suffocating weight, activating his internal
alarm system. He began to avoid watching any TV programmes or films that depicted medical
emergencies or fires, finding even fictionalized accounts too close for comfort. His wife, Mei,
noticed his increasing detachment. He would actively push away moments of shared joy or tenderness,
finding positive emotions unsettling, almost dangerous, as if allowing himself to feel good would
somehow make him vulnerable or less prepared for the next unforeseen catastrophe.

Paradoxically, alongside this hypervigilance, Minh found himself taking too many risks, doing things
that could cause him harm. He would drive a little too fast on his commute, disregard minor aches
and pains, and push himself to take on more complex cases, often working late into the night without
adequate rest. He rationalized it as staying sharp, but deep down, a part of him felt there was
something fundamentally wrong with him as a person, a defect he could only atone for through
relentless, almost reckless, dedication. His work, once a source of immense pride, was now tinged
with a constant, simmering anxiety. The smell of cautery in the OR, a common occurrence, would
sometimes momentarily freeze him, his mind flashing to that day, only for him to quickly suppress it
and force himself back into focus.

It was Mei’s quiet, persistent worry, coupled with a particularly vivid emotional flashback during a
routine check-up where the warm stethoscope on his chest brought an overwhelming, disembodied wave
of terror, that finally prompted Minh to seek help. He sat in the therapist’s office, exhausted,
weary of the constant internal battle, and most afraid that he might never truly feel safe again,
that his body would forever betray him, and that the person he once was had been irrevocably lost.
