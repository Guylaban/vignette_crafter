Karim, a 40-year-old paramedic, had always prided himself on his calm under pressure, his ability to
bring order to chaos. That changed irrevocably two years ago when his young son, Omar, was involved
in a severe car accident. Karim was off-duty, enjoying a rare day off, when the call came from his
wife. He rushed to the scene, arriving moments before the first ambulance. The sight of Omar’s
crumpled car seat, the mangled metal, and his son’s small, still form, imprinted itself onto Karim’s
mind with a horrifying clarity. He was a paramedic, trained for this, but seeing his own child like
that, he was just a father, overwhelmed and utterly helpless. He felt a profound, sickening terror
as he watched his colleagues work on his son, a feeling that still grips him with the same intensity
whenever the memory surges back.

Now, when Karim tries to recall the details of that day, the memory often returns as a full sensory
reliving. He can smell the burnt rubber and gasoline, hear the frantic shouts of bystanders, feel
the rough texture of the asphalt under his knees, all as if he is standing there again. Yet,
strangely, there are frustrating memory gaps; he simply cannot remember the exact moments he pulled
Omar from the wreckage, nor the first words he spoke to his wife when she arrived. It's a blur of
raw emotion and fragmented images, punctuated by that recurring terror.

The aftermath of the accident and Omar’s long hospital stay left Karim with a pervasive sense of
failure. "I should have protected him," he often thinks, the words a bitter mantra. "I’m a
paramedic, I save lives, but I couldn't save my own son from that." This gnawing belief that he had
failed to protect his child led him to conclude that he couldn't rely on himself, especially when it
truly mattered. He had come to believe that he was permanently changed for the worse, a broken
version of his former self.

These beliefs permeated his daily life. At work, the very environment that once defined him became a
minefield of triggers. The visible injuries or wounds on others, particularly children, can
instantly transport him back to Omar’s bedside, making his hands tremble as he tries to maintain his
professional composure. The sharp, clean hospital or antiseptic smell, so ubiquitous in his
profession, often brings a wave of nausea and a tightness in his chest, a visceral reminder of the
sterile, terrifying days spent waiting for news of his son. Even casual reminders of victims, like a
news report about a child injured in an accident, can send a jolt of anxiety through him.

Karim found himself constantly on edge. A sudden, unexpected touch from a colleague on his shoulder
would make him jump, his heart racing, adrenaline surging. His concentration suffered; he struggled
to focus on patient reports or team briefings, his mind often drifting, unable to hold a coherent
thought. In public spaces, he instinctively sought out seats with his back to a wall, where he felt
safer, able to see everyone entering and exiting, a small, unconscious ritual he performed for a
sense of protection.

To cope, Karim developed a series of maladaptive strategies. He threw himself into work, picking up
extra shifts, telling himself it was to provide for his family. In reality, it was a desperate
attempt to keep his mind occupied, to avoid the quiet moments when the traumatic memories threatened
to ambush him. "If I just keep busy, I won't have to think about it," he reasoned, but the constant
overworking only left him exhausted and irritable. He also tried hard not to think about the trauma,
pushing away any intrusive images or thoughts whenever they surfaced, often muttering "No, not now"
under his breath. This relentless avoidance of his own thoughts, coupled with his physical absence,
created a growing chasm between him and his wife, who felt he was emotionally distant and
unreachable. Their once close relationship now felt strained, marked by silence and unspoken
resentment. He also started making excuses to avoid specific training sessions, particularly those
involving pediatric trauma simulations, knowing he simply couldn't face those scenarios, which began
to impact his professional development.

The increasing distance from his wife, the constant exhaustion, and the gnawing fear that his
diminished concentration might lead to a mistake at work finally brought Karim to seek help. He
dreaded the prospect of talking about the accident, fearing the renewed terror that would inevitably
resurface, but the thought of losing his family and his career, combined with the relentless
internal battle, had become unbearable.
