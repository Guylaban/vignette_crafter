reliance. That was before the sudden, terrifying onset of acute respiratory distress, which landed
her in the intensive care unit, fighting for every breath. The memory of those days remained vivid:
the sudden dizziness that had seized her while she was working under a sink, quickly followed by an
overwhelming sensation of suffocation. She remembered the frantic rush to the emergency room, the
sterile, metallic tang of antiseptic permeating everything, and the feeling of utter helplessness as
tubes and wires became extensions of her failing body. What chilled her most was the terrifying
sense that her body, which had always served her faithfully, had utterly betrayed her. She had been
surrounded by medical professionals, yet in her most vulnerable moments, she felt profoundly alone,
a deep-seated conviction forming that she truly couldn’t rely on other people.

Since being discharged, Priya’s world had shrunk considerably. The physical sensations that had
heralded her illness—a slight lightheadedness, a momentary difficulty breathing—were no longer
benign. Now, even a brief spell of dizziness while climbing a ladder or a fleeting shortness of
breath after lifting a heavy pipe could instantly transport her back to the ICU bed, triggering a
wave of intense fear. These moments, often encountered in the demanding environment of her plumbing
work, were particularly distressing. She had come to believe that she had to be especially careful
because you never knew what could happen next, constantly vigilant for any sign that her body might
once again turn against her.

This constant monitoring of her internal state had begun to dictate her life. The once-familiar
scent of disinfectant, common in many of the homes she worked in or even just passing by a hospital
on her way to a job, would send a shiver down her spine, bringing with it the vivid memories of her
hospitalisation. To cope, Priya had slowly started to implement changes. She found herself taking
circuitous routes to avoid driving past hospitals, and she would quickly change the radio station if
an ambulance siren pierced the air. What troubled her most was her growing reluctance to engage with

at inventing excuses to avoid them, terrified that any doctor’s visit would lead her back into the
very environment that had become a symbol of her deepest fears.

required working in cramped, poorly ventilated spaces, fearing the breathlessness these conditions

dreaded the physical stress it placed on her body. Priya, who had always been the first to roll up
her sleeves, found herself avoiding situations that put her body under any perceived strain, leading
to a noticeable drop in her workload and, consequently, her income. Her once bustling schedule now
had large, empty gaps, which she often spent alone, caught in the cycle of monitoring her symptoms
