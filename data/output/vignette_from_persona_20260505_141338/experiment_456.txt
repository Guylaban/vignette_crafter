terribly watched.

In the weeks and months that followed, the echoes of that day began to reshape Nia’s entire
existence. The sound of a car backfiring, a firecracker, or even a sudden drop of a heavy object
would send a jolt of terror through her, instantly transporting her back to the park. What
frightened her most was the insidious belief that she had not done enough, that as a spiritual
leader, she *should* have been able to prevent it, or at least respond more effectively. "I will
never forgive myself for not doing more to stop what happened," she often found herself thinking, a
heavy weight settling in her chest. This self-reproach fueled a pervasive sense of dread, a

had to be especially careful because you never know what can happen next, a constant, low thrum of
anxiety underlying every interaction.

sermons with her former peace. Even when her husband, David, would reassure her that they were safe
at home, she found it impossible to believe him, the words feeling hollow against the certainty she

vulnerability, making it harder to feel safe than during the day, her senses on high alert for any
unusual sound.

had changed. Nia came to therapy not with hope, but with a desperate, quiet fear that she might
