Elena, a 57-year-old Latina physical therapist, vividly remembered the day her world fractured. It
was during Hurricane Irma, when her quiet neighborhood in South Florida, usually bathed in sun,
became a terrifying inland sea. The water rose with shocking speed, engulfing her small home on
Calle del Sol, the very street name now a cruel irony. She had been trying to help her elderly
neighbor, Marisol, whose house was lower-lying. As the murky, debris-filled water surged into
Marisol’s living room, Elena remembered the sickening lurch in her stomach, a profound nausea that
wasn't just fear but a visceral reaction to the smell of sewage and the violent churning of the
floodwaters around their legs. The sound of Marisol’s frantic cries, mixed with the relentless roar
of the wind and the crashing waves that shouldn't have been there, still echoed in her mind. They
were eventually rescued, but that feeling of absolute helplessness and the persistent nausea during
the ordeal had etched itself deeply.

In the months that followed, Elena found her life shrinking. The memory of the flood itself often
felt strangely detached, like a scene from a film she had only half-watched, not something that had
happened to *her*. At other times, a sudden trigger would unleash the full, raw intensity of the
terror and despair without any clear image to accompany it – just a crushing wave of emotion that
left her breathless. When a memory did surface, even one she couldn't fully grasp, it brought with
it an overwhelming grief, not just for what she had lost, but for the person she used to be.

Now, almost two years later, her days were a careful navigation of a landscape fraught with internal
and external threats. The slightest feeling of nausea, perhaps from an upset stomach, could plunge
her back into the visceral panic of the flood. Hearing the name "Marisol" or even the mention of
"Calle del Sol" would cause a jolt of anxiety, her mind immediately replaying fragments of that
horrifying afternoon. Any encounter with water, whether it was a heavy rainfall, a full bathtub, or
even the vast expanse of the ocean she once loved, would trigger a powerful wave of distress. She
had developed an almost reflexive habit of "blanking out" or trying to "leave her body" when these
feelings became too intense, a deliberate attempt to escape the overwhelming sensations.

Her work as a physical therapist, which once brought her immense satisfaction, had become a source
of constant stress. She used to visit patients in their homes across the county, but now the thought
of "going out" filled her with dread. The world outside her small apartment felt unpredictable and
dangerous. She had drastically reduced her caseload, insisting that the few patients she still saw
come to her home office, a space she meticulously controlled. Even there, unexpected or loud sounds
– a car horn from the street, a patient dropping a crutch, the sudden whir of an air conditioner –
sent her heart racing, her body tensing, convinced that some new disaster was imminent. She only
felt truly safe within the walls of her apartment, a self-imposed prison she had meticulously
created.

Sleep offered little respite. Night after night, Elena struggled to fall asleep, her mind replaying
snippets of the past or racing with anxious predictions about the future. When she did drift off,
she rarely stayed asleep for long, waking at the slightest noise, her senses on high alert. She
would lie awake for hours, dwelling on how active and carefree she had been before the hurricane,
lamenting the vibrant woman she felt she had lost. This constant vigilance and avoidance had taken a
toll on her relationships and her financial stability, leaving her feeling increasingly isolated.
The cumulative weight of these changes, the persistent grief, and the ever-present fear had finally
driven Elena to seek help, though she wasn't entirely sure what could be done to bring back the "old
Elena" she so desperately missed.
