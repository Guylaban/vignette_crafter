Amina, a 63-year-old auditor, had always prided herself on her meticulous nature and pragmatic view
of the world. That changed irrevocably two years ago during what was meant to be a relaxing family
picnic at a state park. Her daughter, Layla, had wandered a short distance to admire a patch of
wildflowers when a man emerged from the trees, swift and brutal. Amina, frozen by a terror she had
never known, watched in horror as Layla was dragged into a dilapidated, locked shed nearby. The
memory of Layla’s terrified eyes, her muffled screams, and Amina’s own utter powerlessness to
intervene, became the defining moment of her life. The scent of damp earth and pine needles, the
rustling of leaves in the breeze, and the metallic clang of the shed door as it locked, were seared
into her mind.

In the weeks and months that followed, Amina found her world shrinking. The traumatic event itself
felt less like a memory and more like a recurring nightmare that could erupt without warning. A
sudden breeze carrying the scent of pine could instantly plunge her back into that moment, and the
memory would flood her senses completely. She would see the assailant’s face, hear Layla’s pleas,
and feel the rough texture of the ground under her knees as if she were still there, paralyzed. Each
time, the terror on recall was as profound and visceral as the original experience, leaving her
gasping for breath, heart pounding. She had come to believe that her body was her enemy, constantly
reacting as if she were still in danger even when she was safe, betraying her with its
uncontrollable panic.

Her professional life, once a source of stability, began to suffer. As an auditor, her work often
required her to visit various client sites, sometimes involving small, windowless rooms for document
review. Now, being in a locked or confined room, even a perfectly ordinary office, would trigger a
wave of intense anxiety. The click of a closing door, the feeling of being enclosed, would bring
back the image of Layla trapped in the shed, and Amina would find herself sweating, her breath
shallow. She started to avoid going to unfamiliar places altogether, convinced that she could not
assess the danger in new environments. This meant turning down lucrative contracts that required
travel or visiting new businesses, significantly impacting her income and career prospects. She
stayed mostly within a small, familiar radius of her home, her routes predictable and her
interactions limited.

The hypervigilance became a constant companion. Amina was superalert, watchful, and on guard even in
her own home. Every creak of the floorboards, every distant siren, would send a jolt of alarm
through her. This perpetual state of readiness made trouble falling or staying asleep a nightly
struggle. She would lie awake for hours, replaying the event, or bolt upright in bed from a
nightmare, the terror of victim memories still fresh. What frightened her most was the pervasive
feeling of helplessness. She couldn’t stop bad things from happening to her daughter, and she was
certain she couldn't stop them from happening again. This conviction led to a profound loss of faith
in people, in the inherent goodness of life, and in any higher power that might protect them. The
world, she now believed, was an unpredictable and dangerous place where safety was an illusion.

After nearly two years of living in this constant state of dread and isolation, a particularly vivid
flashback triggered by a news report about a missing person, combined with the realization that she
had not left her neighborhood in weeks, finally prompted Amina to seek help. She felt trapped, not
just by the memories, but by the walls of fear she had built around herself, desperately hoping to
find a way to reclaim some semblance of the life she had lost.
