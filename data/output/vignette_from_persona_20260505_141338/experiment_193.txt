shared with her beloved husband, Arthur. That life shattered one quiet Tuesday morning, a little
over a year ago. Arthur had been in the kitchen, making their usual morning coffee, when a sudden,
guttural gasp had drawn Margaret from the living room. She found him slumped against the counter,
his eyes wide and unseeing. The paramedics arrived swiftly, but it was too late. The doctors later
spoke of a massive, unexpected cardiac event, but for Margaret, the medical explanation offered
little solace. In the agonizing moments she had spent trying to rouse him, she had come to believe,
with a certainty that gnawed at her, that she should have protected him, that she had somehow failed
to intervene, to notice a sign, to act faster. This conviction, "I should have protected others and
I failed," became a heavy cloak she wore constantly.

In the weeks and months that followed, Margaret found herself in a relentless pursuit of activity.
Her home, which had once been a sanctuary, now felt imbued with the memory of that morning, and she
could not bear to be still within its walls. She "worked hard at keeping busy with other things,"
meticulously reorganizing every closet, repainting rooms, and volunteering for every committee her
church offered. The busyness was a shield, a way to keep her mind from lingering on the image of
Arthur, or on the pervasive feeling that she was unable to relax even when she knew she was in a
safe place. Even sitting in her favorite armchair, a restless tension hummed beneath her skin, her
muscles often aching from an unconscious clenching. Her body felt constantly tense and ready to

bracing for another blow.

difficult. She felt a profound sense of estrangement, a belief that she no longer belonged anywhere.
