once defined by sharp analysis and calculated risks. Now, almost three years since the sudden death

his existence into a before and an after. Marcus, just 28, had been involved in a drunk driving
accident. Samuel remembered the sterile scent of the emergency room, the hushed tones of the
doctors, and the crushing weight of the words spoken, but when he tried to deliberately remember the
exact moment he was told Marcus was gone, the details felt elusive, like trying to grasp smoke. He
could vividly recall the cold metal of the gurney, the slight tremor in his own hands, and the dull
ache in his chest, yet the full picture remained incomplete, a collection of sudden pieces that
would flash into his mind without warning – the flash of an ambulance light, the specific texture of
the hospital wall – only to disappear just as quickly.

In the aftermath, Samuel found himself caught in an endless loop of rumination, going over and over
why this had happened to Marcus, to him. What if he had insisted Marcus stay home that night? What
if he had called him five minutes earlier? The questions gnawed at him, relentless and unanswerable,
fueling a profound sense of guilt and anger. He tried hard not to think about the trauma, burying
himself in work, in the complex dance of market fluctuations, hoping that sheer mental effort could
suppress the intrusive thoughts. But the strategies were fleeting; the moments of Marcus’s final
hours, however incomplete, would return, unbidden, often triggered by the most innocuous things. A
sudden screech of tires from the street below his high-rise office could plunge him back into the
terrifying cacophony of the accident scene he’d only pieced together from reports. He would suddenly
feel as if the stressful experience were actually happening again, his heart pounding, a wave of
horror washing over him, even as he sat before his trading screens.

To cope, Samuel began to avoid anything that reminded him of Marcus. He packed away all his son’s
belongings, storing them in a remote corner of his divorced home’s attic. He stopped frequenting the
downtown coffee shop where they used to meet for breakfast, and politely but firmly changed the
subject whenever old friends mentioned Marcus. This avoidance extended to his own grief, making it
difficult to fully process the profound loss. He found himself increasingly isolated, his once
vibrant social circle shrinking. At work, the avoidance of emotional reminders inadvertently led to
a different, more concerning pattern. He started taking too many risks, making increasingly
aggressive trades that went against his usual cautious instincts. There was a recklessness to his

no longer held the same weight. He had come to believe that if fate could snatch away his son, then
what was the point of careful planning anyway?

his memory and the echoes of his grief, without losing himself entirely.
