Koa, a 63-year-old publisher, sat quietly, his gaze sweeping the room before settling on the wall
behind him. The catastrophic structural failure at an industry gala two years ago had irrevocably
altered his world. He had been celebrating a new book release, the air thick with the buzz of
conversation, the clinking of glasses, and the distinct aroma of various spirits being served. Then,
without warning, a section of the grand old building’s ceiling had buckled, followed by a wall,
plunging the room into a cacophony of screams, dust, and collapsing debris. He remembered the
sickening lurch, the immediate, overwhelming darkness, and the acrid smell of plaster, sweat, and
spilled alcohol that permeated everything as he lay trapped, surrounded by the moans of the injured
and the terrifying silence of others.

Since that day, Koa’s life had become a careful choreography of avoidance and hypervigilance. The
memories of the frightening event were vivid and clear, mostly a series of visual images – the
crumbling ceiling, the terrified faces, the twisted metal. These visual memories forced their way
into his mind at the most inconvenient times, flashing like a series of grim photographs, regardless
of what he was doing. Even waking from a nightmare could trigger a fresh wave of terror, the
lingering sensation of the dream’s horror making his heart pound as if he were back in the
collapsing building. The smell of alcohol, even a faint whiff from a passing stranger or an open
bottle, would instantly transport him back to the dust-choked air and the feeling of utter
helplessness. He had learned to avoid places where he might encounter such smells, which meant many
social gatherings were now off-limits.

What frightened him most was the pervasive feeling that danger could strike again, anywhere, at any
time. He felt on guard even inside his own home, scrutinizing shadows, listening intently to every
creak of the old house. His startle response was hair-trigger; he would jump at ordinary sounds that
would not bother most people – a car backfiring, a book dropped in the next room, the sudden ring of
the phone. This constant alertness meant he was perpetually scanning his environment for signs of
threat, his eyes darting to emergency exits, structural supports, and the faces of strangers.
Whenever he found himself in a public space, like a restaurant or a meeting room, he would
instinctively choose a seat with his back against a wall, believing it gave him a strategic
advantage, allowing him to see everyone and everything. This became such a habit that his wife would
automatically guide him to such a spot without a word.

His once vibrant social life, deeply intertwined with his publishing career, had significantly
shrunk. His friends, many of whom were industry colleagues, often wanted to discuss the disaster or
invite him to events. Koa found himself making excuses, increasingly avoiding seeing friends. The
thought of navigating crowded rooms, or the potential for a conversation to veer into the traumatic
event, felt unbearable. He knew he was missing out on important networking opportunities and that
his colleagues were starting to notice his absence from launches and conferences. His wife had
gently suggested he seek help, but he had resisted, convinced that his strategies kept him safe. It
was only when his most recent manuscript review was riddled with errors, clear evidence of his
inability to concentrate amidst the constant internal intrusions and external vigilance, that Koa
realized his carefully constructed world was crumbling around him. He came to therapy, still
scanning the room, hoping for a way to quiet the relentless internal alarm, to reclaim a life that
felt less like a perpetual vigil.
