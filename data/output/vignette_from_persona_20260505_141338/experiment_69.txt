Samuel Johnson had always prided himself on his steady nerves, a necessary trait for a man who spent
his career as a bomb disposal technician. But that was before the incident. He remembered the call-
out vividly: a suspected chemical leak in an abandoned industrial complex, a routine assessment that
turned catastrophic. As he approached a corroded storage container, a sudden, violent breach
erupted, spewing a cloud of acrid, toxic gas. His protective suit was compromised, and for agonizing
moments, he was engulfed, gasping for air as the chemicals seared his lungs and skin. The frantic
evacuation, the sirens, the agonizing burn, and the overwhelming smell of the chemicals mixed with
the antiseptic sting of the emergency room had etched themselves into his memory.

Now, two years later at 62, Sam found himself a stranger to the calm he once possessed. His once
familiar workplace had become a minefield of potential triggers. The metallic clang of equipment,
the sudden hiss of a pneumatic tool, or even the distinct, sharp scent of cleaning solvents used in
the depot could send a jolt of pure panic through him, his heart hammering against his ribs as if he
were back in that contaminated space. He’d often find himself tensing, muscles coiled, ready to
react to a threat that wasn't there.

His home life had changed too. Maria, his wife of forty years, often worried about him. He was
rarely still. What frightened Sam most was the quiet; being alone in a room or building with his own
thoughts was unbearable. The silence allowed the intrusive images of the gas cloud, the burning, the
fear of suffocation, to flood his mind. To avoid this, Sam worked excessively. He'd volunteer for
extra shifts, take on meticulous paperwork that could easily wait, or spend hours in his workshop
tinkering with projects he didn't truly need to complete. He told Maria he was just being
productive, but the truth was he was desperately trying to outrun the echoes of the past, to fill
every moment with activity so there was no room for the terror. This relentless overwork meant Maria
often ate dinner alone, and their evenings, once filled with shared stories, were now quiet and
separate.

His vigilance extended to every aspect of his day. In any public space, be it the grocery store or
his grandson's school auditorium, Sam’s eyes would ceaselessly scan his surroundings for signs of
danger, categorizing people, assessing exits, mentally mapping out escape routes before he felt
comfortable enough to sit down. He was certain that if he dropped his guard, even for a second,
disaster would strike again. This constant scanning meant he was rarely truly present, his mind
always on high alert. Maria had learned not to touch him unexpectedly, as a gentle tap on his
shoulder would often result in him flinching violently, his body recoiling as if struck, a reaction
he couldn't control and always regretted immediately after.

The hospital smell, a potent reminder of the aftermath of his exposure, was another powerful
trigger. He actively avoided medical cues: he hadn't had his annual physical in over a year,
something unprecedented for him. The sight of an ambulance siren, the smell of antiseptic in a
store, or even a television commercial for a hospital could make him feel nauseous, his chest
tightening with a familiar dread. He knew Maria was concerned about his persistent cough, but the
thought of stepping into a doctor's office, with its sterile scents and hushed tones, filled him
with an almost insurmountable anxiety.

The cumulative toll of these past two years had become unbearable. The constant vigilance, the
exhaustion from overwork, and the growing distance from Maria had finally pushed him to seek help.
He felt like he was trapped, perpetually bracing for an attack that never came, yet always felt
imminent. He simply longed for a moment of genuine peace, a return to the man he was before the gas
cloud, before the world became a place of constant threat.
