Rohan, a forty-two-year-old tax inspector, found his life irrevocably altered one rainy Tuesday
afternoon a year and a half ago. He had been driving home, tired after a long day of auditing, when
a moment of distraction led to his car hydroplaning. He remembered the sickening screech of tires,
the metallic groan as his vehicle slammed into the rear of another sedan, and the subsequent chain
reaction involving a third car. What haunted him most was the sight of the crumpled passenger side
of the car he hit, and the agonizing screams that followed. He later learned the driver had
sustained severe, life-altering injuries.

In the immediate aftermath, Rohan’s memory of the event was a jumble of fragmented sensory input. He
often found himself replaying the crash in his mind, and when he did, he sometimes heard the
distinct, horrifying sound of shattering glass and the injured driver’s cries as clearly as if they
were happening in the room with him. Yet, despite these vivid auditory flashes, he felt like he was
only remembering parts of what happened; the full picture remained frustratingly out of reach, like
a puzzle with crucial pieces missing. Some moments, like the sight of the mangled steel, were
extremely clear, almost painfully so, while others, particularly the moments leading up to the
impact, were completely blank.

Since the accident, Rohan had come to believe that his world had been completely destroyed. He had
always seen himself as a careful, responsible man, a pillar of his community, but the accident
shattered those beliefs entirely. He felt a deep, pervasive sense of humiliation, convinced that he
was permanently marked by his failure. What frightened him most was the crushing weight of
responsibility; he was certain he should have protected the other driver, and he felt he had
profoundly failed them. This internal narrative of failure and shame became a constant companion.

His days were now punctuated by sudden jolts of fear. Driving to his office, a modern glass-fronted
building in the city center, became an ordeal. The sight of cars hit by accident, even minor fender-
benders on the news, sent a jolt of anxiety through him, triggering vivid, unwanted images of his
own crash. He found himself taking longer, circuitous routes to work, trying to avoid busy
intersections where he might witness such scenes. Funerals, cemeteries, or any grief-related events,
even a distant obituary in the newspaper, would plunge him into a profound despair, reminding him of
the precariousness of life and the suffering he had caused. He also went to great lengths to
suppress thoughts about the trauma, actively pushing away any intrusive memories or images, which
only seemed to make them stronger. He would quickly change the TV channel if a car accident was
reported, or abruptly end conversations if they veered towards discussions of loss or tragedy.

This avoidance extended to personal conversations. His wife, Priya, had tried to talk to him about
the accident, sensing his distress, but Rohan would shut down, making it clear he didn't want to
revisit the event. He saw talking about the event as a dangerous trigger, one that would only
exacerbate his internal turmoil. He had also developed a rigid internal dialogue, telling himself he
deserved to suffer because of what happened, a form of punishment thinking that dictated many of his
choices. He would often deny himself small pleasures, refusing to buy new clothes or go out with
friends, convinced he didn't warrant happiness. Occasionally, he would try to find some relief by
reminding himself it could have been worse, that at least no one had died, but this downward
comparison offered little solace and quickly evaporated under the weight of his guilt.

Over the past year, these patterns had solidified into habits. His long hours at work, once a sign
of dedication, became a form of self-flagellation, a way to occupy his mind and punish himself. He’d
stay late, meticulously poring over tax returns, the precise, ordered nature of the work a stark
contrast to the chaos in his mind. This dedication, however, had come at a cost to his marriage;
Priya felt increasingly isolated, sensing a growing distance and a wall of silence between them.
Rohan's emotional withdrawal and his constant state of internal turmoil had eroded their intimacy,
leaving him feeling more alone than ever.

The cumulative toll of his suffering finally pushed Rohan to seek help. He had woken up one morning
feeling utterly exhausted, the weight of his unaddressed guilt and the relentless suppression of his
thoughts having become unbearable. He knew he couldn't continue living this way, constantly bracing
himself against the next trigger, trapped in a cycle of self-punishment and fear. He came to therapy
hoping for some reprieve, but also deeply afraid that confronting the memories would only confirm
his darkest fears about himself.
