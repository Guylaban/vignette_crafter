Michael, a 38-year-old married teacher, first noticed the changes in himself shortly after the
mugging eight months ago. He had been walking home from an evening class he was teaching, cutting
through a busy pedestrian underpass near the university campus, when a group of three men had
cornered him. The memory was relentlessly vivid, forcing its way into his mind at unwanted moments,
bringing with it the sickening taste of metallic blood from where his lip had split against the
concrete wall. He could still feel the press of bodies, the sudden, deafening shout of one of his
assailants, the jarring sensation of being slammed backwards. It felt completely real across all his
senses at once, as if he were back there, trapped in the narrow passage.

In the weeks and months that followed, Michael found himself increasingly on edge. The world, he had
come to believe, was a fundamentally dangerous place, a belief reinforced by the fact that the
police had never caught his attackers, leaving him with a profound sense that what happened to him
was deeply unfair and that the institutions meant to protect him had failed. This conviction fueled
a constant vigilance. As soon as he entered any room or building, his eyes would immediately dart
around, scanning for exits, mapping potential escape routes. He had also developed a habit of
intensely studying people's faces and body language, searching for any subtle cue that might
indicate a threat, a holdover from the terrifying moments when he’d been pinned, trying to read the
intentions of his attackers. Unexpected movement nearby, even a student suddenly standing up in
class or a colleague turning quickly, would cause him to react as if he were in immediate danger,
his heart leaping, muscles tensing.

His work as a history teacher at the local high school, once a source of quiet satisfaction, had
become a minefield. The school’s crowded hallways between classes, filled with jostling teenagers,
now felt suffocating. The sheer volume of students, the unexpected bumps and shoves, often triggered
a wave of anxiety, reminding him of being overwhelmed and cornered. Even the lively chatter of his
classroom, particularly when students became boisterous, could escalate into a loud noise that set
his teeth on edge, making him jumpy and irritable. He found himself standing near the classroom door
more often than not, his gaze constantly sweeping the room, less engaged with his lesson plan and
more focused on monitoring every child.

Driving, too, had become fraught. The mugging hadn't involved a car directly, but the memory of a
loud car horn blaring nearby during the attack had become inextricably linked to the terror. Now, if
his wife, Sarah, was in the passenger seat, he felt an intense, almost unbearable pressure. The idea
of driving with others, of being responsible for someone else's safety while simultaneously feeling
trapped and hyper-aware of every surrounding vehicle and noise, was overwhelming. He started making
excuses to avoid driving when Sarah needed a lift, or insisted on taking separate cars, creating a
subtle but growing distance between them. Sarah had noticed his withdrawal, his increased jumpiness,
and the way he flinched at sudden sounds, but he hadn't yet articulated the full extent of his
internal struggle.

Lately, the constant strain had become too much to bear. The intrusive memories were more frequent,
the sense of threat ever-present, and his avoidance of social situations and even simple drives with
Sarah was starting to impact their relationship. He was exhausted, perpetually on guard, and deeply
afraid that he would never feel safe again. It was the growing chasm between him and Sarah, more
than anything else, that finally prompted Michael to seek help, hoping to find a way to reclaim some
semblance of his former self and the sense of security that had been so brutally stolen from him.
