whose classroom buzzed with curiosity and hands-on learning. That was before the incident. It was a
Tuesday afternoon, during a routine science experiment involving a small, contained chemical
reaction. A sudden, unexpected bump from an over-eager student sent a flask toppling from its stand,
spilling a corrosive liquid onto another child’s arm. The acrid, metallic smell of the chemical, the
child’s piercing scream, and the sight of the liquid spreading on their skin were seared into
Priya’s mind. She reacted instantly, rushing to help, but the damage was done. In the aftermath, a
heavy blanket of self-blame descended upon her; she was certain the event happened because of the
way she acted, convinced that if she had been more vigilant, if she had arranged the lab stations
differently, it would never have occurred.

Now, months later, the memory of that afternoon often feels like it is happening right now rather
than something that is over. It doesn’t play out as a continuous narrative, but as a series of
sharp, painful snapshots: the flask tipping, the splash of the liquid, the child’s face contorted in
agony, her own hands fumbling for the emergency wash. Sometimes, a faint chemical scent from a
cleaning product in the school hallway, or even the smell of certain hand sanitizers, can instantly
transport her back to that classroom, the smell of the spill overwhelming her.

The incident irrevocably altered her sense of self. Priya had come to believe she was no longer
capable of doing the things that defined her, especially in her role as a teacher. The vibrant,
confident educator who once loved guiding her students through exciting, hands-on projects was gone.
She now shied away from any activity that carried even a remote risk, delegating tasks she once
relished and opting for safer, less engaging lesson plans. This shift in her teaching style was
noticeable, and she felt a deep sense of inadequacy, as if she was failing her students. What
frightened her most was the feeling that she was permanently an outsider now, separated from her
colleagues and even her husband, Rohan, by the burden of her guilt. She felt they couldn't possibly
understand the weight she carried.

Her daily life had become a minefield of triggers. When a parent would praise her teaching during a
parent-teacher conference, or a colleague would compliment a lesson plan she’d put together, Priya
would feel a profound sense of unease, almost confusion, rather than pride. The praise felt
undeserved, a stark reminder of her perceived failure, making her retreat further into herself.
Similarly, any positive emotions she experienced, whether from a funny story Rohan told or a
pleasant family gathering, were tinged with a strange discomfort, as if she didn't deserve to feel
happy. Conversations about accidents, even minor ones mentioned on the news or among her colleagues
discussing a child’s scraped knee on the playground, would immediately bring back the vivid imagery
and the acrid smell of that Tuesday afternoon.

Priya’s withdrawal had begun to strain her marriage. Rohan would try to comfort her, to praise her
resilience, but his efforts often backfired, triggering her sense of undeservingness and making her
feel even more like an outsider to his attempts at connection. She would often pull away, unable to
articulate the confusion and shame that praise ignited within her. The cumulative effect of these
struggles, the growing distance in her marriage, and the constant fear of the memories resurfacing
in her beloved classroom, finally prompted Priya to seek help. She longed to reclaim the vibrant,
engaged teacher she once was, but feared that part of herself was irrevocably lost.
