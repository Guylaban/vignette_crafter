Isabella, a 26-year-old school counselor with a warm smile that rarely reached her eyes, carried a
heavy secret from her childhood. She was only nine when she witnessed an argument between her mother
and her mother's then-partner escalate violently in their small living room. The memory was a
chaotic blur of raised voices, a sudden shove, and her mother falling against the coffee table, a
sharp cry escaping her lips. Isabella remembered freezing in the doorway, her small hands covering
her mouth, paralyzed by fear. She had stood there, unable to move, unable to call for help, even as
her mother struggled to get up. The partner had stormed out, leaving a stunned silence and her
mother weeping on the floor. In the immediate aftermath, Isabella had come to believe that the event
happened because of the way she acted – or, more accurately, the way she *didn't* act. If only she
had screamed, or run for help, or done *something*, she reasoned, maybe it wouldn't have happened.

Years later, the memory of that night continued to haunt her, often returning without warning.
Sometimes, the smell of a particular cleaning product, reminiscent of her childhood home, or the
sound of an unexpected loud bang in the school hallways, could instantly bring back the full, vivid
memory. When this happened, a wave of intense shame and fear would wash over her, so overwhelming
that she would have to stop whatever she was doing, her mind racing, her heart pounding. She had
tried to talk about it before, in bits and pieces to a trusted friend, but found herself losing the
thread, unable to explain clearly what had happened, the words catching in her throat as the
emotions became too much.

Now, as a school counselor, Isabella found her professional life increasingly complicated by her
internal world. She felt a constant need to be vigilant for dangerous people, scanning faces in the
corridors, always on edge. She had come to believe deeply that people couldn't be trusted, a
conviction that made it hard to truly connect with colleagues or even some of the students she was
meant to support. Even inside her own home, a small apartment she carefully curated for comfort, she
felt on guard, hyper-alert to every creak and distant siren. She found herself habitually
positioning herself with her back against a wall during meetings or even when having coffee with a
friend, needing to see the entire room, needing to be aware of every entrance and exit. The thought
of someone touching her unexpectedly would send a jolt of alarm through her, making her flinch
violently, a reaction that had made casual interactions with students and colleagues awkward and
strained.

Her childhood home, though now occupied by another family, was still in the same city. Isabella went
to great lengths to avoid going to or near the place where the event happened. This meant taking
long, circuitous routes to work, avoiding entire neighborhoods, and declining invitations to social
events if they were in that part of town. This avoidance had slowly but surely narrowed her world,
making her feel increasingly isolated. She spent countless hours ruminating about how the event
could have been prevented, replaying the scene in her mind, imagining different scenarios where she
intervened, where she was brave. This constant mental re-enactment, far from providing a sense of
closure, only reinforced her belief that she had failed, and that the people or institutions that
should have protected her – her mother, the adults around her – had ultimately let her down.

The cumulative effect of these internal struggles had begun to manifest externally. Her colleagues
noticed her guardedness, her jumpiness, and her tendency to keep to herself. She had turned down
several opportunities for professional development that would have required travel or more intensive
social interaction. The constant hypervigilance and rumination were exhausting, leaving her feeling
drained and irritable, impacting her ability to fully engage with her students. She had finally made

deeply cared about. She hoped therapy might offer some relief, though she was deeply skeptical that
