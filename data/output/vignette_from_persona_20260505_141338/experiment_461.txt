Minh, now 77, carries the weight of a lifetime marked by a single, shattering event that occurred
when he was a young man in his village. He remembers the day with unsettling clarity: a sudden,
deafening *bang* that tore through the air, the unmistakable sound of a mortar shell striking
nearby. In the immediate chaos that followed, he saw his younger sister, Lien, near the impact zone,
her name echoing through the panicked screams of their neighbors. The image of the blast, the acrid
smell of dust and smoke, and the subsequent wails of the injured are etched vividly into his mind.
He recalls the frantic scramble, the desperate attempts to help, and the eventual arrival of
military vehicles, their sirens piercing the air as they collected the wounded. Even now, the memory
forces its way into his thoughts, unbidden, often when he is trying to focus on something else
entirely. Sometimes, he finds himself looking at these detailed recollections and feeling as though
they belong to someone else, a story he merely observed, not lived.

For decades, Minh has navigated a world filled with potential reminders of that day. The sound of
any loud *bang* or explosion, even a car backfiring or fireworks on the Fourth of July, sends a jolt
of terror through him, instantly transporting him back to the village. The wail of an ambulance
siren, a frequent occurrence in his work as a firefighter, triggers a similar visceral dread,
conjuring images of the wounded and the helpless. Most acutely, hearing names that sound similar to
"Lien" can stop him in his tracks, his heart pounding with an irrational fear that history might
repeat itself.

Minh had become a firefighter years after immigrating to the United States, a profession he believed
would allow him to protect others, perhaps a quiet penance for the helplessness he felt as a young
man. Yet, his daily environment constantly pits him against his past. At the station, the blare of a
fire truck’s siren can cause him to flinch, a reaction he tries to hide from younger colleagues.
When responding to calls, the heightened emergency context, the shouts of names, and the occasional
sharp crack of collapsing structures become intense challenges. He has learned to constantly scan
his surroundings, his eyes darting across the scene, assessing every shadow, every movement, for a
sign of danger that might not be there. It’s a habit that has extended to his home life; even
sitting in his living room, he finds himself meticulously checking the windows and doors.

The traumatic memory has cast a long shadow over his life, shaping his internal world. He is certain
that if he truly allows himself to think about the event, to delve into the details of Lien's fate,
he will not be able to handle it; the emotional toll would be too great. This conviction drives his
attempts to push the memories away, though they always return. He also harbors a deep-seated belief
that somebody else, someone stronger or smarter, would not have gotten into that situation, or would
have acted differently, a quiet self-reproach that has festered over the years. This feeling of
powerlessness, of being caught in circumstances beyond his control, has led him to feel like an
object, not a person, reduced to a mere casualty of fate. In quieter moments, he finds himself
consumed by elaborate revenge fantasies, imagining retribution against those he holds responsible
for the war and its devastating impact on his family and village.

These internal battles have taken their toll. For years, Minh has struggled with sleep, dreading the
moment he has to go to bed or lie in the dark. He often stays up late, watching television or
reading, anything to avoid the quiet stillness where the vivid memories are most likely to intrude.
This chronic sleep deprivation has left him perpetually fatigued, affecting his concentration and
making him more irritable with his wife, who has grown accustomed to his late nights and the

Now, at 77, Minh finds that the strategies he developed to cope are no longer sufficient. The

triggers than ever. A recent incident at the station, where a new recruit's name, "Lien," was called
out during a drill, caused a full-blown panic attack that he could no longer dismiss as mere stress.
His wife, noticing his increasing withdrawal and the dark circles perpetually under his eyes,
finally urged him to seek help. He comes to therapy now, not entirely sure what to expect, but with
a quiet desperation, hoping to find some relief from the constant war being waged within him, a war
that has lasted far longer than the one that started it all.
