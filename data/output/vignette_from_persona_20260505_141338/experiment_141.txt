Robert, a fifty-three-year-old social worker, had always prided himself on his unwavering dedication
and his ability to connect with people from all walks of life. His work often took him into
unfamiliar neighborhoods and various homes, a reality he embraced until a little over two years ago.
He had been conducting a routine home visit in a somewhat run-down apartment building, a new client
referral in a part of the city he hadn't frequented much. The client, a man in his late twenties,
had seemed agitated but cooperative. Robert was asked to step into a small, windowless back room to
review some paperwork, a common enough request. It was in that confined space, the door clicking
shut behind him, that the world shifted irrevocably. The assault was sudden, brutal, and
overwhelming, leaving him disoriented and numb.

In the immediate aftermath, a profound sense of terror clung to him, a cold, visceral dread that
resurfaced whenever he revisited the memory. Remembering the event brought back the same terror he
felt at the time, a heart-pounding, breath-stealing re-experience. Compounding this was an intense
shame that washed over him whenever the details intruded, a burning humiliation that made him recoil
from his own thoughts. He knew what had happened, the sequence of events, the actions taken, yet
when he tried to articulate it, to put words to the experience, he found himself utterly unable. The
words simply wouldn't come, a jumble of raw feeling and fragmented images, leaving him frustrated
and isolated in his struggle to convey the depth of his ordeal.

Over the following months, Robert’s world began to shrink. The memory of that small, enclosed room
became a potent trigger. He started actively avoiding enclosed or confined spaces, opting for the
stairs over elevators in his office building, making excuses to hold meetings in larger, open-plan
areas, and finding himself increasingly uncomfortable in client homes with narrow hallways or small
rooms. His once adventurous spirit, which had allowed him to navigate the city's diverse landscape
for his job, was replaced by a pervasive caution. He now avoided going to unfamiliar places,
convinced he could no longer accurately assess the danger, which significantly hampered his ability
to take on new client referrals outside his established routes.

A new vigilance settled over him. When walking to his car after a late appointment, or even just
through the office parking lot, he frequently looked behind him to check if someone was following
him, a constant, low-level hum of anxiety accompanying his movements. This hyper-awareness extended
to his expectations of others; when something took longer than expected—a colleague’s delay in
responding to an email, a client running late for an appointment—he would immediately assume
something bad was happening, his mind leaping to worst-case scenarios involving danger or harm.

In an attempt to regain a sense of control, Robert began to ruminate about how to get even with his
assailant, replaying scenarios in his mind, imagining confrontations, devising elaborate plans for
retribution. These thoughts offered a fleeting sense of power, but ultimately left him exhausted and
consumed. Simultaneously, he fiercely tried to push the intrusive thoughts and memories of the
assault out of his mind, a constant internal battle to suppress the images and feelings that
threatened to overwhelm him. He would try to distract himself with work, with household chores,
anything to keep the memories at bay, but they always returned.

His professional life, once a source of deep satisfaction, became increasingly fraught. His
reluctance to enter new homes or small consultation rooms meant he was subtly shifting his caseload,
favoring clients in familiar settings, and turning down opportunities for professional growth that
would require him to venture into new environments. His supervisor had begun to notice his decreased
flexibility, though Robert offered vague explanations. At home, his wife noticed his increasing
preoccupation and withdrawal, the way he would suddenly go quiet, lost in thought, or the way he now
insisted on taking specific, well-lit routes, even if they were longer. The constant internal
struggle and the relentless threat monitoring left him perpetually on edge, making it difficult to
fully engage with her.

It was the realization that his professional effectiveness was severely compromised, coupled with a
growing sense of isolation from his wife, that finally led Robert to seek help. He knew he couldn’t
continue living this way, constantly scanning for danger, trapped by memories, and consumed by
thoughts of revenge or desperate suppression. What frightened him most was the thought of losing
himself entirely to this pervasive dread, of becoming a shell of the social worker and husband he
once was.
