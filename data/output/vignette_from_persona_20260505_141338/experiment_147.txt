menacing. For five harrowing days, Rohan was held captive, bound and interrogated, accused of

images flashed unbidden into his mind, he would feel a phantom "pain recall" in his wrists and
ankles, where the ropes had chafed, or a dull ache in his jaw from a blow. Other times, the memory
would surface with a chilling "emotionless recall," as if he were recounting a story that happened
to a stranger, a detached narrative that felt profoundly unsettling.

Since his release, Rohan’s world had shrunk, governed by an invisible threat. He found his
occupation, once a source of peace, now riddled with triggers. Any "talks about military" topics,
whether from a visitor casually mentioning their service or a news report playing in the ranger
station, would make his heart pound, a cold dread washing over him as if his captors were once again
nearby. The sight of "people in uniform," even his own colleagues or a state trooper on patrol, sent
a jolt of anxiety through him, a primal fear that authority could turn menacing at any moment. He
was acutely aware of "being seen"; patrolling open trails or even sitting in his office with the
blinds open made him feel exposed, vulnerable, constantly scanning his surroundings for potential
threats.

or a hiker appearing quickly around a bend would cause him to "startle to movement," reacting as if
in grave danger, his body tensing, ready to flee. At times, the past would overwhelm the present
entirely; a particular scent or sound would plunge him into a "flashback," suddenly feeling and
acting as if he were back in that damp, claustrophobic tent, the terror palpable and all-consuming.

to believe" her, his mind always anticipating danger, checking locks, peering out windows, unable to
truly relax.

was certain that "somebody else would not have gotten into this situation," blaming himself for not
being more vigilant, stronger, or smarter. This self-reproach fed into a deep sense of "isolation,"

shame or the constant vigilance he endured.

experienced during captivity. At night, he would lie awake, consumed by "revenge fantasies" against

thoughts were dark and exhausting but offered a temporary, potent release from his feelings of
helplessness. In his interactions, especially at work or with people he perceived as powerful, he
became overtly agreeable, often engaging in "people pleasing," going along with whatever others
wanted to "feel safe" and avoid conflict.

Over the months, these patterns took a heavy toll. His "restrict eating" left him constantly
fatigued, making the physical demands of his park ranger duties increasingly difficult. His "people
pleasing" meant he was overworked, taking on extra shifts and tasks to avoid confrontation, leaving
him little time for himself or Priya. The "isolation" he felt, despite living with Priya, created a
growing chasm between them, as he struggled to articulate the depths of his internal suffering,
convinced she wouldn't understand his profound humiliation.

The breaking point arrived when a minor dispute with a group of campers escalated. Rohan, paralyzed
by his "people pleasing" tendencies and the fear of confrontation, froze, unable to assert his

continue living this way, haunted by his past, always on edge. He came to therapy hoping to find a
way to reclaim his life, but what frightened him most was the lingering certainty that he might
never truly feel safe again, that the humiliation would forever define him.
