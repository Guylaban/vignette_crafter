Amina, a fifty-year-old social worker, carried the weight of a past that had never truly receded.
When she was a young woman, civil war had erupted in her home country, forcing her and her family to
flee their village under the cover of night. The journey was etched into her memory: the constant
fear of discovery, the desperate need to remain unseen, the nights spent huddled in ditches while
distant gunfire echoed and the whispers of unseen patrols drifted too close. She remembered one
particularly harrowing night, hiding with her younger sister in a dense thicket, the crunch of boots
on gravel nearby, the guttural shouts of soldiers searching. She had been certain they would be
found, her heart a frantic drum against her ribs. Even after they found refuge in America, a
profound sense of vigilance had taken root within her, a silent, unyielding sentinel.

In the decades that followed, this vigilance had become an intrinsic part of Amina’s daily
existence. Each morning, she would carefully select her clothing, always opting for loose, muted
garments that offered no particular shape or color to draw the eye. It was a habit born of necessity
during her flight, and it had persisted, a quiet ritual of self-preservation. She had come to
believe that if she remained inconspicuous, she would be safer, less noticed, less vulnerable. This
extended to her movements in the world; whether navigating the bustling streets of the city for her
social work appointments or simply walking through a grocery store, Amina was constantly scanning
her environment for signs of threat. Her eyes darted from face to face, checking reflections in shop
windows, assessing exits, always mapping potential dangers, even when she knew intellectually there
was no immediate peril.

Her work as a social worker, a profession she had chosen out of a deep desire to help others
navigate their own hardships, often felt like a tightrope walk. Her clients, many of whom faced
profound challenges, frequently brought their own intense emotional distress into her office. Amina
found herself instinctively avoiding situations that made her feel strong emotions, subtly steering
conversations away from raw grief or explosive anger, focusing instead on practical solutions or
resource provision. It wasn't that she lacked empathy; it was that the intensity of others' feelings
felt too close to the raw, uncontrolled fear she had once known, threatening to overwhelm her
carefully constructed defenses.

At home, the quiet of her apartment offered little respite. Amina often reacted as if she was in
danger even when nothing was actually wrong. A sudden car backfiring outside her window could send a
jolt of terror through her, her body tensing, her breath catching, as if the sound heralded an
immediate threat. Sleep offered no escape either. She frequently woke during the night, her mind
instantly alert, her body rigid. She would lie there, listening intently to the silence, her senses
straining, before inevitably getting up to check that her doors were locked, her windows secured.
Even after satisfying herself that her apartment was safe, she found herself unable to relax, her
muscles remaining tense, her mind replaying fragmented images of past dangers, the sense of
impending doom refusing to dissipate.

Over the years, this pervasive inability to truly relax, coupled with her constant vigilance and
emotional guardedness, had taken a heavy toll. Her marriage had slowly disintegrated, her ex-husband
eventually unable to penetrate the wall of anxiety that enveloped her. He had often told her she was
"always on edge," a state she couldn't explain or control. Now divorced, she lived alone, her days a
relentless cycle of perceived threat and exhaustion. The chronic fatigue from her restless nights
impacted her concentration at work, making it harder to engage fully with her clients, and she often
felt herself withdrawing from colleagues, too drained to participate in casual conversation.

The decision to seek help had been a slow accumulation of these burdens. Just last week, during a
routine home visit in a quiet suburban neighborhood, a dog barking unexpectedly behind a fence had
triggered such a violent startle response that Amina had nearly dropped her case files, her heart
pounding uncontrollably for minutes afterward. The sheer intensity of her reaction, in such an
innocuous setting, had brought a wave of despair. She was tired of living in a perpetual state of
readiness for a danger that rarely materialized, tired of the exhaustion, and deeply afraid that
this constant, unyielding tension would eventually consume her entirely. She hoped, with a fragile
flicker of hope, that someone might finally help her find a way to truly feel safe.
