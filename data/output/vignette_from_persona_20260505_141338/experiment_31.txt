Linh, a twenty-four-year-old urban planner, carried the weight of a childhood trauma that continued
to shape her world. Growing up, her home had been a place of unpredictable fear, particularly during
moments when her caregiver’s anger erupted. She vividly recalled the chilling sensation of being
held down, her small body pinned, the pain sharp and disorienting. During these episodes, a profound
nausea would often rise in her throat, an overwhelming physical reaction to the helplessness and the
violation. What lingered most acutely was the feeling of being utterly exposed, an object to be
controlled, not a child deserving of care. This deep-seated violation had left her with a conviction
that what happened to her was deeply unfair, a profound injustice for which nothing had ever been
done, and a persistent, gnawing feeling that she was a weak person, easily overpowered.

Years later, Linh found that specific sensations and situations could plunge her back into that
state of terror. Undergoing any medical examination or procedure was a particular ordeal. The simple
act of a doctor’s touch, the sterile environment, or the instruction to lie still could trigger a
wave of panic, a visceral re-experience of being restrained, making routine check-ups almost
impossible to endure. Similarly, an unexpected wave of nausea, perhaps from a bad meal or motion
sickness, would immediately put her on high alert, a direct echo of her body’s reaction during the
abuse. She had also come to believe that being seen, truly seen, made her vulnerable. Whether in a
large crowd or a small group, the sensation of others’ gazes on her could evoke a deep unease,
making her feel like an object, not a person with agency.

These sensitivities profoundly impacted her work as an urban planner. Her job often required site
visits to bustling construction zones or public consultations in crowded community halls. In these
environments, sudden movements by others – a colleague turning abruptly, a passerby rushing past –
would immediately put her on guard, her muscles tensing, her heart racing as if preparing for an
unseen threat. This constant vigilance made it incredibly difficult for her to concentrate on
blueprints or project specifications, her mind frequently pulled away by perceived dangers. To cope,
Linh had gradually narrowed her world. She only felt safe in a very limited number of places: her
apartment, a specific quiet corner of her office, and a small park near her home. Navigating other
areas felt fraught with peril.

When people, even well-meaning friends, tried to bring up her past or talk about difficult
childhoods, Linh would go to great lengths to avoid these reminders. She’d change the subject
abruptly, make excuses to leave, or simply tune out, finding it easier to pretend these
conversations weren’t happening. In her social interactions, she often found herself going along
with whatever others wanted, agreeing to plans she didn't enjoy or holding back her own opinions,
believing that this people-pleasing approach would keep her safe from conflict or disapproval. She
often caught herself lost in counterfactual thoughts, replaying moments from her past, thinking
about how life would have been different if the event had not occurred, a relentless mental exercise
that offered no solace.

Her memory of the abuse itself was fragmented. When she tried to deliberately remember what
happened, she found it difficult to access the memory, as if a part of her mind had walled it off.
Yet, when fragments did surface, they often brought with them an intense rage, a burning anger at
the injustice. Sometimes, even without any clear image or coherent memory, she would be overwhelmed
by the full emotional intensity of the event – the fear, the shame, the helplessness – leaving her
shaken and disoriented, unsure of what had triggered such a powerful internal storm.

The cumulative effect of these struggles had begun to take a toll on her career. The avoidance of
public spaces and her difficulty concentrating meant she was falling behind on projects that
required fieldwork or collaborative meetings. Her manager had recently noted her increasing
isolation and missed opportunities, a consequence of her shrinking "safe zone" and her avoidance of
reminders. This growing professional struggle, coupled with the persistent feeling of being
fundamentally weak and dehumanized, finally prompted Linh to seek help. She arrived at therapy
feeling a profound weariness, uncertain if anyone could truly understand the invisible chains that
bound her, yet desperate for a way to feel like a whole person again, rather than just an object of
fear.
