Yasmin, a 44-year-old prosecutor, had always been known for her sharp mind and unwavering resolve.

her early twenties. She had been living with her family in a bustling city when war descended,
turning everyday life into a landscape of unpredictable terror. One harrowing night, as the sounds
of shelling grew dangerously close, Yasmin and her family huddled in the cramped, damp basement of

ground, their language harsh and guttural. What etched itself into her memory was the chilling
command of a particular voice, raspy and arrogant, giving orders to search the houses. She
remembered the suffocating fear, the absolute certainty that their hiding place would be discovered,
the feeling of being hunted, meticulously tracked, as if unseen eyes were scrutinizing every crack
in the foundation, every breath they dared to take. The world outside felt like a relentless,
predatory gaze.

In the years that followed, as Yasmin built a new life in America, a subtle but pervasive shift
began. The ambitious, joyful woman she once was seemed to recede, replaced by a constant
undercurrent of dread. “I used to be a happy person,” she would reflect, “but now I am always
miserable.” The world, she had come to believe, was an inherently dangerous place, a belief that
colored every interaction and every news headline. Even the smallest upset, a minor disagreement
with her husband, a challenging moment at work, now felt insurmountable. “I can’t deal with even the
slightest upset,” she’d confess, overwhelmed by what others seemed to navigate with ease.

of a defendant’s voice, particularly if it carried a certain resonant quality or an aggressive tone,
could send a jolt of ice through her veins, instantly transporting her back to that basement. In the
busy courthouse corridors, the feeling of someone walking behind her, even a colleague, would
trigger an intense, visceral panic. She would quicken her pace, her heart pounding, convinced she
was being followed or tracked. In the courtroom, under the gaze of the jury or the public gallery,
she often felt an overwhelming sense of being watched, a pervasive scrutiny that made it difficult
to focus, her mind racing with old fears of discovery.

At home, the vigilance continued. Yasmin found herself waking during the night, her eyes snapping
open, compelled to check the locks on the doors and windows, to listen intently for any unusual
sounds, needing to verify that she was safe. Her sleep was fractured, restless, offering little true
respite. Her sense of security had shrunk to an alarmingly small circle. She only felt truly safe
within the walls of her own home, or in her private office, a familiar space she could control.
Venturing beyond these limited safe zones felt like stepping into enemy territory. Any unexpected or
loud sound – a car backfiring outside, a dropped plate in the kitchen, even a sudden clap of thunder
– would make her jump, her body tensing, instantly on high alert as if danger were imminent.

Over time, this constant state of hypervigilance began to take its toll. She started declining
invitations to social events with her husband, preferring the perceived safety of their apartment.
At work, she found herself avoiding cases that required extensive public interaction or aggressive
cross-examination, unconsciously steering away from scenarios that might intensify her sense of
being watched or confronted by threatening voices. Her once vibrant professional life felt
increasingly constrained, and her husband, though patient, grew concerned about her increasing
withdrawal and pervasive unhappiness. It was the realization that her life had become so small, so
dictated by an invisible threat, that finally brought Yasmin to seek help. She longed for a return
to the person she used to be, but what frightened her most was the conviction that such happiness
was lost to her forever, and that the world would always remain a place of lurking danger.
