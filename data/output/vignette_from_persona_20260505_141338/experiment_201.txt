life. A renowned fashion designer, she had been on a site visit to a textile factory in a bustling
city abroad, overseeing the intricate production of her latest collection. The air was thick with
the hum of machinery and the scent of dyes when, without warning, a catastrophic fire erupted. A
spark, a faulty wire, and then an inferno. Elena remembered the suffocating, searing heat on her
skin, a physical assault that permeated every pore as flames consumed the building with terrifying
speed. She saw workers, many of whom she had just shared tea with, trapped and injured, their pleas
echoing amidst the chaos. The overwhelming feeling of utter helplessness, of being utterly powerless
to control the unfolding disaster, had rooted itself deeply within her. In the fire’s devastating
aftermath, the factory owners faced minimal repercussions, their negligence largely unpunished, a
stark injustice that solidified Elena’s belief that the people responsible had faced no consequences
for their actions. She was certain that she should have protected others, that she had somehow
failed those workers, and the haunting thought that the event happened because of the way she acted,
that her presence or pressure for production might have contributed to the lax safety, became a
constant, corrosive whisper in her mind.

From that day forward, Elena’s world began to shrink. The memory of the fire was not a distant
recollection but a living entity that forced its way into her mind when she least expected it. A
sudden flush of heat from a steam iron in her studio, or the oppressive warmth of a summer
afternoon, could instantly bring back the full sensory horror of the blaze. She would be back in the
factory, the smell of burning fabric, the screams, the intense heat on her skin, all completely real
across all her senses at once, without warning.

To cope, Elena developed a meticulous system of avoidance. She stopped watching the news entirely,
her heart pounding at the mere mention of a building collapse or industrial accident, desperate to
avoid news reports about events similar to what had happened to her. She meticulously curated her
environment, avoiding anything that reminded her of the profound loss of life she had witnessed,
like certain areas of the city or conversations about tragedies. This extended to her own emotional
landscape; she found herself pushing away positive emotions. A wave of joy, a moment of pride in her
work, would be met with a pang of guilt, a deep-seated fear that if she allowed herself to feel too
much happiness, something terrible would inevitably follow. She had come to believe that feeling
good was unsafe, a dangerous indulgence given the suffering she had witnessed.

Over the years, these strategies took a toll on her once vibrant career. Elena, who used to
personally oversee every stage of her designs, now sent her assistants to factories, even local
ones, unable to face the industrial environments that now felt fraught with danger. Her once daring
and experimental collections became more subdued, reflecting her internal emotional landscape. Her
husband, Miguel, noticed her increasing emotional distance, her tendency to retreat into herself,
and her steadfast refusal to engage with current events. He would often find her staring blankly,
lost in a memory, only to snap back with a forced smile.

from the pervasive fear that she would never truly escape the fire, that she would forever be
trapped in the memory of the day she failed to protect others.
