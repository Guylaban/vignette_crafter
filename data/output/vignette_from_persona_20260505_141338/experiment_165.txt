her once vibrant and outgoing nature. The shift began six months ago, after an accident that forever
altered her sense of self and safety. She had been driving home from her hairdressing salon late one
evening, tired but content, when a momentary lapse in judgment led to a collision. The screech of
tires, the deafening crunch of metal, and the subsequent sight of the other driver, seriously
injured and trapped, were seared into her mind.

The memory of the crash often returned to Linh in vivid, almost photographic stills: the shattered
windshield, the deployed airbag, the contorted metal of the other car. These visual memories would
flood her awareness, yet sometimes, in an attempt to distance herself from the overwhelming horror,
her mind would render the scene with a strange, emotionless clarity, as if she were watching a film
about a stranger. But the numbness was always fleeting, quickly replaced by an overwhelming wave of
grief – a deep, guttural sorrow for the harm she had caused. She was certain she had failed to
protect the other person, a burden that weighed heavily on her conscience. A new, unsettling belief
had taken root: she could no longer trust her own body; it had betrayed her, leading to such a
catastrophic mistake.

Now, every loud noise, whether a sudden bang from a dropped scissor at the salon or a car horn
blaring outside, would trigger a jolt of physical reactivity, her heart pounding, her breath
catching. The sound would instantly conjure an intrusive thought, an unwanted replay of the crash’s
impact, sometimes accompanied by the terrifying crash imagery of cars hitting violently.
Unsurprisingly, Linh had completely stopped driving. The thought of getting behind the wheel, or
even being a passenger, filled her with such dread that she would walk long distances or rely on her
boyfriend, even when it was inconvenient for him. This avoidance had started subtly, but over weeks,
it had become a rigid rule in her life, isolating her further.

Her salon, once a place of creative joy, had become another arena for her hypervigilance. She found
herself scanning exits as soon as she entered any room or building, a habit that extended to the
bustling hair salon where she worked. Even in the seemingly safe environment of her own home, she
found she was unable to relax, her muscles perpetually tensed, her mind constantly on alert for an
unseen threat. This constant state of readiness was exhausting, reinforcing her belief that she
couldn't rely on other people to keep her safe; she had to be constantly vigilant herself.

Her relationship with her boyfriend, once easy and supportive, now felt strained. When she felt even
a hint of threat or conflict, even a raised voice, Linh would find herself adopting a fawn response,
becoming overly compliant and agreeable, desperate to appease and avoid any further tension. She
worried he would leave her, a fear that intertwined with her growing conviction that she couldn't
truly rely on anyone. The upcoming anniversary of the accident loomed large, a date she planned to
meticulously avoid acknowledging, hoping that by ignoring it, she could somehow escape the pain it
represented. This strategy, however, only made the dread more pronounced as the date drew nearer.
Her once flourishing career as a hairdresser, which required steady hands and a calm demeanor, was
also suffering. Her clients noticed her jumpiness, her distracted air, and her difficulty making eye
contact. The constant internal battle made it harder to focus on intricate styles, and she found
herself making more small errors, further eroding her already fragile self-trust.

The accumulated weight of these changes, the constant anxiety, and the pervasive sense of guilt
finally led Linh to seek help. She was tired of feeling like a prisoner in her own mind, exhausted
by the relentless vigilance and the crushing grief. What frightened her most was the growing feeling
that the accident had irrevocably broken her, and she desperately hoped that someone could help her
piece herself back together, to feel safe and trusting again, both of the world and of her own body.
