Sarah, a 28-year-old kindergarten teacher, had lived in a state of constant apprehension since the
months-long ordeal of being stalked by a former acquaintance. It had begun subtly with persistent
messages, but escalated to his physical presence near her apartment, a creeping dread that permeated
her every waking moment. The most terrifying night came when she heard heavy, ragged breathing just
outside her bedroom window, a sound that lingered for what felt like an eternity before being
swallowed by an unnerving, absolute silence. The police were called, but their inability to find
him, coupled with what felt like a dismissive tone, left her with a profound sense of shame and
helplessness.

The memory of that period was a fragmented tapestry. Some moments, like the exact sound of that
laboured breath or the specific shadow she glimpsed, were seared into her mind with piercing
clarity. Yet, other crucial details, such as her actions immediately after calling for help, were
completely blank. She knew, intellectually, what had happened, but when she tried to articulate the
specific sensations or the sequence of events, she often found herself unable to find the words, a
frustrating void where language should have been. Without warning, sudden pieces of the memory would
erupt into her consciousness – a snippet of a text, the chilling creak of a floorboard, the visceral
thumping of her own heart – only to vanish just as quickly, leaving her disoriented and breathless.

Now, any heavy breathing sound, even from a child with a sniffle during a quiet reading session in
her classroom, sends a jolt of panic through Sarah. Her heart pounds, and a cold dread spreads
through her chest, her mind instantly transported back to that terrifying night. The sight of a
police car or an officer in uniform immediately makes her stomach clench, not just in fear, but with
a renewed sense of exposure and profound shame. She constantly feels as though she is being followed
or tracked, a pervasive unease that compels her to scan her surroundings relentlessly. This feeling
is particularly intense when she walks from her classroom to her car after school, or when
navigating crowded grocery store aisles. She finds herself jumping at shadows, her gaze darting to
strangers, certain that something dangerous is about to happen, especially when an unusual silence
descends in the school hallway after the children have left for the day. That quiet, rather than
offering peace, now feels pregnant with menace, a signal that she needs to be on high alert.

Sarah had come to believe, deep in her core, that people couldn’t be trusted. The world felt like an
inherently dangerous place, and she was certain that her life, as she knew it, had been irrevocably
destroyed by the trauma. As a kindergarten teacher, a profession she once loved for its openness and
connection, her days were now a constant battle against her own fear. She kept her classroom door
locked, even during school hours, much to the mild annoyance of her colleagues. She avoided parent-
teacher conferences whenever possible, delegating to her co-teacher, because approaching strangers,
even familiar parents, filled her with an overwhelming sense of threat. She felt she had to be on
guard all the time, constantly monitoring her environment, checking windows, listening intently to
every sound. This relentless hypervigilance was utterly exhausting, leaving her perpetually drained
and irritable. It strained her relationship with her partner, Mark, who often felt shut out by her
constant anxiety and inability to relax. She used to enjoy walks in the park, but now the open
spaces and the presence of other people felt too exposed, too risky.

The cumulative effect of this constant vigilance, the fragmented memories, and the pervasive sense
of dread finally brought Sarah to seek help. She was tired of feeling this way, tired of the
exhaustion, the fear, and the gnawing belief that her life had been permanently derailed. What
frightened her most was the thought that she would never feel safe again, that this pervasive fear
would continue to define every aspect of her existence, preventing her from truly living or trusting
anyone, even Mark, who was trying so hard to understand. She desperately hoped to find a way to
reclaim some semblance of the peace and safety she had lost.
