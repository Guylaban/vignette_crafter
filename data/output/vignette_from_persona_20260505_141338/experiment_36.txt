Margaret, a 65-year-old retired military medic, found her world irrevocably altered on a clear
Tuesday afternoon six months ago. She had been driving home from a volunteer shift at the local
clinic, her mind on the quiet evening ahead, when the screech of tires and a thunderous crash
erupted just ahead of her on the highway. In an instant, she was thrust into a scene of mangled
metal and shattered glass. Her medic training, honed over decades, kicked in immediately. She pulled
over, grabbed her first-aid kit, and ran towards the chaos, her heart already pounding with a
familiar sense of duty. While she was attending to a young woman trapped in a crumpled sedan, a
secondary impact occurred – another car, careening out of the pile-up, slammed into the back of a
stationary truck nearby, sending debris flying and causing a terrifying shudder through the ground
beneath her. It was the visceral sound, the sight of a vehicle identical to her own reduced to
scrap, and the overwhelming feeling of helplessness amidst the destruction that imprinted itself
most deeply.

Since that day, Margaret has found her once-predictable life consumed by an insidious sense of
unease. The memory of the accident often returns unbidden, a sudden, vivid burst of sensation where
she feels like the frightening event is happening right now. She might be washing dishes or reading
a book, and suddenly she’s back on that highway, the smell of burning rubber in her nostrils, the
screams echoing in her ears. These moments are so overwhelming that she often has to stop whatever
she is doing, her body trembling. When she tries to talk about it, to articulate the horror, she
finds herself unable to find the words; the emotions are too intense, too raw, and she simply shuts
down.

Her daily environment, once a source of comfort, has become a minefield of potential triggers.
Driving, something she used to enjoy, is now fraught with anxiety. The sight of cars hit by accident
on news reports or even in films sends a cold dread through her, bringing back the images of twisted
metal and the sounds of impact. Even more unsettling is encountering similar cars to those involved
in the pile-up; a specific model, a particular dent, or even a certain colour can instantly
transport her back, leaving her feeling profoundly uncomfortable and on edge. She’s become acutely
aware of every sound around her. Unexpected or loud noises – a car backfiring, a sudden bang from a
construction site, even a dropped pot in her kitchen – make her jump, her heart racing as if danger
is imminent. Her body feels constantly tense, a persistent knot in her stomach, ready to react to a
threat that she knows intellectually isn't there, but emotionally feels very real.

To cope with this relentless internal pressure, Margaret has developed several strategies. When the
intrusive thoughts or flashbacks begin to surface, she tries hard not to think about the trauma,
pushing the images and sensations away with all her might. If that doesn't work, she distracts
herself with something else – endless tidying, watching television, anything to shift her focus. She
has also noticed she eats excessively to manage her distress, finding a temporary solace in food
that helps quell the churning anxiety. This habit, formed over weeks, has led to a noticeable weight
gain and a decline in her overall energy, making her feel even more isolated. She has stopped
driving on major highways altogether, opting for longer, more convoluted routes on back roads, which
means she rarely visits her old friends who live across town. Her volunteer work, which used to be a
cornerstone of her retirement, has become sporadic, as the effort of managing her internal state
often leaves her too exhausted to face the outside world.

What finally brought Margaret to seek help was a particularly vivid flashback that occurred while
she was watching a documentary about road safety. The crash imagery was brief, but it was enough to
make her feel as if the stressful experience were actually happening again, right there in her
living room. She spent the rest of the day in a state of profound agitation, unable to shake the
feeling that she was no longer safe, that the world was a dangerous place where disaster could
strike at any moment. She arrived at the clinic feeling utterly depleted, expressing a desperate
hope to reclaim some semblance of her former self, and most of all, to escape the constant,
exhausting vigilance that had become her life. What frightened her most was the thought that this
feeling of being perpetually on alert, of constantly reliving the worst day of her life, might never
truly end.
