Leilani, 33, a network engineer, carried the weight of a past that felt perpetually present. Her
life had irrevocably fractured three years ago when her son, then a toddler, was suddenly struck by
a severe, life-threatening respiratory illness. The memory of those weeks in the pediatric intensive
care unit was a raw wound. She could still smell the antiseptic, hear the incessant beeping of
machines, and feel the cold dread as doctors, their faces grim, discussed ventilation and prognoses.
The arguments, too, were etched into her mind: desperate pleas to medical staff for more aggressive
treatments, and hushed, then furious, disagreements with her then-husband about choices, blame, and
the terrifying fragility of their child’s life. Every disagreement felt like a battle for survival,
every decision a potential death sentence.

Though her son eventually recovered, Leilani did not. The world, once a place of relative safety,
now felt inherently precarious. Her home, which should have been a sanctuary, became another place
where she felt perpetually on guard. She found herself constantly scanning the environment,
listening intently for any unusual sound, her muscles subtly tensed, as if an unseen threat lurked
just beyond her peripheral vision. Even inside her own four walls, a pervasive sense of unease clung
to her, a silent alarm bell that never truly quieted.

This constant vigilance manifested acutely in her professional life. Working as a network engineer
often required focused concentration, but a sudden, unexpected movement nearby – a colleague walking
past her desk, or a technician moving equipment in the server room – would cause her to jump, her
heart immediately seizing as if she were in immediate danger. These intense physical reactions, a
racing pulse and a tightness in her chest, were also triggered by less direct reminders. The faint
wail of an ambulance siren in the distance, or even the sight of a small child coughing in the
grocery store, could send a jolt of terror through her, bringing back the suffocating fear of her
son’s illness. She had come to believe that any moment of relaxation was an invitation for disaster,
that she needed to be constantly ready to protect herself and her child from the next unforeseen
catastrophe.

Her experiences had profoundly reshaped her interactions. Arguments, once a normal part of life, now
felt like immediate threats. The mere hint of a disagreement, whether with a colleague about project
architecture or with a family member over trivial plans, would trigger a cascade of anxiety, often
leading her to withdraw or concede quickly to avoid confrontation. This impact extended to her
relationships with related people. While she adored her son, every interaction with him, every time
he coughed or complained of a minor ache, brought a surge of the old terror. Her ex-husband, who she
still co-parented with, was another potent reminder, their shared history of the crisis making every
conversation fraught with unspoken fear and resentment. She found herself carefully managing these
interactions, keeping emotional distance to protect herself from the overwhelming feelings they
evoked.

The thought of medical examinations or procedures for herself or her son filled her with dread. She
had delayed her own annual physical for over a year, inventing excuses, and she meticulously vetted
every detail of her son’s routine check-ups, often canceling or rescheduling at the last minute, the
sterile environment of the clinic and the sight of medical instruments triggering intense
flashbacks. This pattern of avoidance had not only strained her relationships, contributing to the
dissolution of her marriage, but also created a quiet isolation. At work, her colleagues found her
increasingly reserved, her reluctance to engage in robust discussions seen as disinterest, slowly
impacting her opportunities for advancement.

What finally brought Leilani to seek help was a simple, yet devastating, question from her son. He
had asked, with the innocent directness of a seven-year-old, "Mommy, why do you always look so
scared?" His words had pierced through the protective shell she had built, revealing the profound
cost of her constant vigilance. She was tired of living in a state of perpetual alarm, exhausted by
the effort of keeping the past at bay. What frightened her most was not just the possibility of
another catastrophe, but the certainty that if she continued this way, she would miss out on truly
living, truly connecting, truly being present for the healthy, thriving child she had fought so hard
to save. She hoped therapy could help her find a way back to a life where safety was felt, not just
desperately sought.
