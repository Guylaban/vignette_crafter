helplessness as the disaster unfolded around him.

In the weeks and months that followed, Mateo found himself haunted by the event. The memory itself
often felt distant, as if it had happened to someone else, a strange, detached quality to his
recollection that made it feel unreal, even as it consumed his thoughts. Any casual "crash
discussion" among his colleagues at the tech firm, even about a minor software failure or a
metaphorical system crash, would jolt him. He’d feel the familiar knot in his stomach, his mind
instantly replaying fragments of the disaster, and a wave of the same dissociative feeling would
wash over him, making his office feel strangely distant, as if he were observing himself from
outside his own body.

and he had failed. This conviction was a constant, gnawing presence, driving him to endlessly replay
the scene in his mind. He’d spend hours, particularly late at night, engaging in counterfactual
thinking, constructing elaborate mental scenarios: *What if I had pushed harder? What if I had
chosen a different exit? Life would have been different if the event had not occurred.* This
relentless rumination offered no comfort, only deepening his sense of guilt and responsibility.

was certain that you could never truly know who would harm you, or when disaster might strike again.

developed a strong aversion to anything that might trigger a reminder. He actively avoided police,
taking circuitous routes to work to bypass areas where he knew officers were often present,

perceived failure. Media consumption became impossible; watching TV programmes or films, or reading
about similar events, felt like an unbearable assault. He cancelled his news subscriptions and muted
keywords on social media, creating a carefully curated digital bubble, but even then, a stray
headline or a snippet of conversation could pierce through his defenses.

His avoidance strategies had tangible consequences. As a QA engineer, his job demanded focus and
problem-solving, but his mind was frequently elsewhere, lost in anxious rumination or hyper-vigilant
scanning. He found it difficult to engage in team discussions, especially if they veered even
slightly into topics of failure or accidents, leading him to isolate himself more at work. His
social life dwindled, as avoiding media meant he was out of touch with current events, making casual
conversations awkward and strained. He started declining invitations, preferring the solitude of his
apartment where he could control the inputs. A deep pessimism had settled over him, a quiet
conviction that nothing good could happen to him anymore, not truly. He was stuck, trapped in a
cycle of fear and regret. The constant exhaustion from his internal battles, coupled with the
growing isolation, finally led Mateo to recognize he couldn’t continue this way. He sought help,
hoping to find a way to quiet the relentless voice of self-blame and navigate a world that felt
perpetually on the brink of collapse.
