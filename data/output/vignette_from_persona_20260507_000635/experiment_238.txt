John, a 49-year-old European-American man and married City Council Member, has been experiencing
persistent difficulties since his return from combat. His trauma left him with a fragmented sense of
self and a pervasive feeling that the world is a dangerous place. John’s memories of the traumatic
event remain incomplete, making it difficult for him to piece together what happened during those
critical moments. When he attempts to recount his experiences, he often loses track of his thoughts
and struggles to provide a coherent narrative. The day the trauma occurred was vividly etched in
John's memory but with gaps that left him feeling uncertain about many aspects of the event. The
chaos and violence were overwhelming; bullets whizzed past him, explosions echoed through the air,
and the fear of being hit at any moment kept his senses on high alert. Despite surviving, he carries
a deep-seated belief that his body continues to react as if it is still in danger, no matter how
safe he truly is. John's hypervigilance has become an integral part of his daily life. He constantly
monitors his surroundings and remains on guard at all times, convinced that another threat could
arise unexpectedly. This constant state of alertness has taken a toll on his ability to relax or
engage in leisure activities without feeling anxious. Even the simplest things, like sitting in his
office at City Hall or attending council meetings, can trigger intense feelings of unease. John’s
hyperarousal manifests in other ways as well. He struggles with sleep problems, finding it difficult
to fall asleep and often wakes up several times during the night, each time feeling a surge of
anxiety that he must push through until dawn. This lack of rest only exacerbates his hypervigilance
and leaves him exhausted. His avoidance behaviors have grown more pronounced over time. Any form of
physical contact with others is particularly distressing for John. When someone touches him
unexpectedly, such as a handshake from a colleague or an affectionate pat on the back from his wife,
he reacts with alarm, his body tensing up instantly and his mind racing to prepare for potential
danger. This has created significant strain in both his professional and personal life. In his role
at City Hall, John often finds himself avoiding social interactions that might lead to physical
contact or close proximity to others. His colleagues have noticed a change in him; he is more
distant and less willing to engage in team-building activities or informal gatherings after work.
This has affected his ability to collaborate effectively with others and has created some friction
within the council. The emotional toll of these symptoms has also taken its toll on John’s marriage.
He finds it challenging to open up about what he is going through, partly because of his fear that
talking about it will make him relive the trauma and partly due to a belief that no one can truly
understand or provide comfort. His wife has expressed concern over his increasing isolation and
reluctance to seek help. John’s maladaptive strategies have only compounded these issues. He often
turns to sensory distractions like playing loud music or keeping the TV on to drown out intrusive
thoughts, but this has not alleviated his distress. In fact, it has led him to feel more
disconnected from others, as he retreats into a world of noise rather than facing his fears. What
frightened John most was the feeling that no matter how many times he told himself he was safe, he
found it impossible to believe it. The terror on recall of the event was so intense that it
overshadowed any attempts at healing or moving forward. He had come to believe that the world would
always be a dangerous place and that his body’s reactions were a permanent reminder of that fact.
Finally, after months of struggling in silence, John decided he could no longer bear the weight of
these symptoms alone. The strain on his relationships and his inability to function effectively at
work had become too much to handle. He sought help, hoping to regain some semblance of normalcy and
find a way to reconcile with his past without it constantly haunting him. John was most afraid that
he would never be able to fully trust the safety of his surroundings again. At the same time, he was
hopeful that with therapy, he could learn strategies to manage his symptoms and perhaps even begin
to rebuild his sense of security in the world around him.
