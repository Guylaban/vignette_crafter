# Marcus

Marcus sits in the therapy office with his shoulders drawn inward, a posture that has become
habitual over the past two years. At sixty-eight, he carries himself with the careful deliberation
of someone who has learned not to trust his own body. His work as the director of a community health
nonprofit has defined much of his adult life—decades spent building programs for vulnerable
populations, advocating for those without voices. Now, ironically, he finds himself unable to speak
about what happened to him.

The assault occurred during a solo camping trip in the mountains, something Marcus had done annually
for thirty years. He had gone to clear his head after a difficult board meeting. A man approached
him at dusk near the trail, asking for directions. Marcus, always helpful, always attuned to others'
needs, stopped to assist. What followed was a violent attack that left him physically injured and
psychologically shattered. He was found by another hiker the next morning.

In the weeks immediately following, Marcus did what he had always done—he functioned. He went to the
hospital, gave a police statement, returned to work. His ex-wife, whom he remained cordial with,
offered support that he politely declined. His adult children knew only the bare facts: their father
had been assaulted during a camping trip. He didn't want to burden them with details. This
compartmentalization worked for a while. Then, around month four, everything fractured.

Now, two years later, Marcus describes feeling like he exists behind glass. He goes through his
workdays mechanically, attending meetings and reviewing grant proposals, but finds himself unable to
engage with the human connection that once fueled his passion for the work. During staff meetings,
he sometimes dissociates entirely, coming to awareness mid-sentence without knowing what he's been
saying. His colleagues have noticed his withdrawal, though they attribute it to aging or burnout.

Sleep has become a battlefield. Most nights, Marcus lies awake until three or four in the morning,
his mind cycling through intrusive images of that evening. When he does sleep, he wakes gasping, his
sheets soaked with sweat. He's taken to sleeping in his living room chair rather than his bed, a
change he doesn't fully understand but feels necessary. The darkness of his bedroom has become
unbearable.

His body has become a stranger to him. Certain physical sensations—the feel of fabric against his
skin, unexpected touch, even the pressure of his own hands—trigger waves of panic. He's lost fifteen
pounds without trying, having difficulty with appetite and finding eating itself an act requiring
conscious effort. He avoids the gym where he once exercised regularly, unable to tolerate the
vulnerability of changing clothes or showering in public spaces.

What troubles Marcus most is the shame, a corrosive feeling that contradicts everything he
intellectually understands about trauma. He works with trauma survivors; he knows the statistics,
the neurobiology, the fact that assault is never the victim's fault. Yet he carries a deep, wordless
conviction that somehow he should have known better, should have been more careful, should have been
stronger. He finds himself hypervigilant in ways that exhaust him—scanning crowds, noting exits,
feeling his heart race at unexpected sounds.

Marcus continues to show up to work, to answer emails, to maintain the facade of functionality. But
underneath, he feels himself fragmenting, caught between the man he was and someone he no longer
recognizes. He has finally, reluctantly, come to therapy, though he struggles to articulate why he's
here or what he hopes might change.
