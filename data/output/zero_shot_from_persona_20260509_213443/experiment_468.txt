# Marcus

Marcus is a twenty-year-old doctor working in a busy urban hospital in his home country. He carries
himself with a quiet competence that belies the weight he bears. Three years ago, he was conscripted
into military service during a period of civil unrest. What was meant to be a routine two-year
deployment became something far more harrowing when his unit was ambushed during a supply run to a
remote clinic.

He describes the day with clinical precision at first—the time of day, the weather, the composition
of his unit—as though recounting a patient's medical history. Then his voice changes. The convoy was
attacked without warning. Gunfire erupted from buildings on either side of the road. Marcus was in
the second vehicle. He watched the first vehicle ahead catch fire. He remembers the smell—burning
fuel and something else he doesn't name. His sergeant ordered them to return fire and take cover.
For hours, they were pinned down. Marcus saw two of his closest friends killed. He performed first
aid on a third, pressing gauze to wounds while bullets struck the concrete around them. The soldier
died anyway.

When the rescue team finally arrived, Marcus was numb. He completed his remaining service on
autopilot, obtained his discharge, and immediately enrolled in medical school. He threw himself into
his studies with an intensity that impressed his professors. But the ground beneath his feet never
quite solidified again.

Now, working as a junior doctor, Marcus finds himself hypervigilant in ways that don't fit the
hospital environment. He startles at loud noises—the crash of a dropped instrument tray sends his
heart racing and his hands trembling for minutes afterward. He's taken to arriving at the hospital
forty minutes early, needing time to walk the corridors and confirm that exits are clear, that he
knows where things are. His colleagues attribute his meticulousness to conscientiousness. They don't
know he's mapping escape routes.

Sleep is elusive. Most nights he lies awake, his mind replaying fragments he cannot control.
Sometimes he's back in the convoy; sometimes he's in the hospital and the patients on his ward are
his fallen comrades. He's stopped trying to explain this to friends. When he does manage to sleep,
he wakes gasping, drenched in sweat, his body convinced danger is immediate.

What troubles Marcus most is the intrusive guilt. He survived when others didn't. He performs
surgery now, saves lives, and yet he cannot shake the feeling that he doesn't deserve to be alive,
that his survival was arbitrary and unearned. When he loses a patient despite doing everything
correctly, something in him fractures. He replays the case obsessively, searching for where he
failed, conflating this loss with the losses he couldn't prevent in the convoy.

He's withdrawn from his peers at the hospital, eating lunch alone, declining social invitations.
Dating feels impossible—how could he explain the nightmares, the way his body sometimes goes rigid
with panic for no reason his conscious mind can identify? He feels fundamentally separated from
people who haven't experienced what he has, as though they're speaking a language he no longer
understands.

Marcus continues to work, continues to show up, continues to save lives. His performance reviews are
excellent. But inside, he exists in a state of constant vigilance, his nervous system unable to
accept that the danger has passed, that he is safe. The skills that kept him alive during the
ambush—hyperawareness, emotional numbness, the ability to compartmentalize—now feel like a prison he
cannot escape.
