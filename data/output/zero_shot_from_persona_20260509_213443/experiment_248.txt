# James

James sits in the leather chair across from the office, his posture rigid despite the chair's
invitation to sink back. At sixty-nine, he maintains the bearing of someone who has spent four
decades in the structured world of financial analysis—shoulders squared, tie precisely knotted, pen
clipped in his shirt pocket. But his hands tell a different story. They rest on his thighs, and his
fingers move in small, repetitive motions, as if working through an invisible calculation he cannot
quite complete.

His wife, Margaret, died eighteen months ago. Not suddenly, not in the way one might expect for a
man his age. She was hit by a delivery truck while crossing the street near their home on a Tuesday
afternoon in October. James was at the office when it happened, reviewing quarterly earnings
reports. He remembers the quality of light that day—how it fell across his spreadsheets in precise,
measured rectangles. He remembers the phone call. He remembers the hospital.

What he cannot seem to stop remembering is what he saw when he arrived at the scene. Margaret was
still there, though they had already called the ambulance. The way her body looked against the
asphalt. The way her purse lay open several feet away, her wallet visible. These images return
without warning—while he is making coffee, while he is reviewing a client's portfolio, while he is
trying to sleep. They arrive with the same insistent quality as a notification on his phone,
demanding his attention whether he wants to give it or not.

Since Margaret's death, James has stopped attending the office. He took early retirement, though he
had planned to work another three years. He tells himself it was practical—the commute felt
pointless, the conversations with colleagues felt performative. But the truth is that being around
people who offered condolences felt unbearable. Their sympathy seemed to confirm something he cannot
accept: that Margaret is truly gone, that the life they had built together for forty-three years has
simply ended.

He spends most of his days in the house they shared. He has not changed much about it. Margaret's
reading glasses still sit on the nightstand. Her winter coat still hangs in the closet. He finds
himself unable to move these objects, as if doing so would constitute a final acknowledgment of her
absence. Instead, he has developed a routine of checking the locks on the doors and windows multiple
times each night—a habit that began as reasonable caution and has become something else entirely. He
checks them at nine o'clock, at midnight, at three in the morning. He cannot explain why, except
that when he does not check them, a sense of dread builds in his chest until he must get up and
verify that everything is secure.

Sleep has become unreliable. He lies awake for hours, and when sleep finally comes, it brings dreams
where he is back at the hospital, or back at the scene, or searching for Margaret in crowds of
people who all look like strangers. He wakes gasping, his heart racing as if he has been running.
Some mornings, he lies in bed for an hour after waking, unable to generate the motivation to rise.

His daughter calls twice a week. She worries, though she tries to hide it. She has suggested he
might benefit from talking to someone, and after months of resistance, he has agreed. He is not
certain what talking will accomplish. The facts remain unchanged: Margaret is dead. He was not
there. He cannot undo any of it. But his daughter's concern has worn through his reluctance, and so
he has come, sitting in this chair with his hands moving their invisible calculations, trying to
find the formula that might restore some equilibrium to a life that no longer balances.
