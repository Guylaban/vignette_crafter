# Maria

Maria sits in the chair with her hands folded in her lap, a habit that seems to calm her. At forty-
four, she carries herself with the careful precision of someone who has spent decades training her
hands to be steady and sure. Her studio—a clean, well-lit space in the city's arts district—has been
her sanctuary for over twenty years. She built it from nothing, earned a reputation for intricate,
meaningful work. Her clients trust her with their skin, their stories. Now she struggles to trust
her own body.

The accident happened on a Tuesday morning last October. Maria was preparing pigments in the back
room, a ritual she'd performed thousands of times. She'd always been meticulous about ventilation,
safety protocols. But that morning, a container of concentrated iron oxide solution had been left
uncapped on the shelf—someone else's carelessness, though Maria still blames herself. When she
reached for it, the bottle tipped. The chemical splashed across her neck, shoulders, and down her
right arm.

She remembers the burning. She remembers running to the sink, the water too hot, then too cold. She
remembers her apprentice's face, pale and frozen. She remembers the ambulance ride, though the
details are fragmented—sirens, an oxygen mask, a paramedic's voice asking her name repeatedly. The
hospital visit revealed chemical burns on fifteen percent of her body. The doctors said she was
lucky. She spent three weeks in treatment, watching her skin blister and peel, wondering if she
would ever look like herself again.

The physical wounds healed faster than anyone expected. The scarring is minimal—faint pink marks
across her shoulder and upper arm that fade a little more each month. But Maria can't stop seeing
that moment. She's back in the studio dozens of times a day, even when she's sitting at home beside
her husband, even when she's lying in bed at night. The bottle tips. The chemical comes. Every time,
her body floods with adrenaline as if it's happening for the first time.

She hasn't returned to the studio since November. Her husband, a gentle man who works in software
development, has been patient, but she can feel the weight of her absence affecting him too. He
worries. He hovers. Sometimes she catches him watching her, and she knows he's thinking about the
hospital, about how close he came to losing her.

The smell of chemicals now triggers an immediate panic response. She can't walk past cleaning supply
aisles in grocery stores. She took a bath last week and had to leave halfway through when she caught
the scent of something unfamiliar in the water. Her heart raced for hours afterward. She's developed
a habit of checking and rechecking bottles, containers, anything that might spill. At night, she
lies awake cataloging dangers—the cleaning products under the sink, the paint thinner in the garage,
the pesticides the neighbor uses on his lawn.

What troubles her most is the loss of her hands. Not physically—they work fine, the scars barely
visible. But psychologically, she's terrified to use them the way she always has. The thought of
holding a tattoo gun again, of leaning close to a client's skin, of being in that studio where it
happened—her chest tightens just imagining it. She was someone who created beauty for a living. Now
she's someone who's afraid.

Her husband suggests she could redesign the studio, hire more staff, change her routine. Practical
solutions to practical problems. But Maria knows the problem isn't practical. It's that her nervous
system no longer believes she's safe, even though logically, she knows she is.
