# Priya's Story

Priya has worked the evening shift at the shopping center for nearly twelve years. She knows the
rhythms of the mall—when the crowds thin, which stores stay open late, the names of the security
guards who make their rounds. She's good at her job as a retail associate, patient with customers,
meticulous about inventory. Her manager trusts her to close the store most nights.

Three months ago, on an ordinary Tuesday evening, everything fractured.

It was around 8:45 p.m. when the shots came. Priya was restocking shelves in the back when she heard
what she thought was something falling in another store. Then came screaming. Real, terrified
screaming that made her freeze. She emerged from the stockroom to see people running past her
store's entrance, their faces twisted in panic. Her body moved before her mind caught up—she locked
the gate, pulled down the security shutter, and huddled behind the counter with two customers who'd
been browsing near the front.

What she witnessed through the metal slats of the shutter was fragmented, dreamlike. A figure moving
through the mall corridor. Security guards running. A woman on the ground, her leg at an unnatural
angle, crawling backward. The sound of more shots. The smell of something acrid and wrong. It lasted
perhaps five minutes, though Priya couldn't say for certain. Time had become unreliable.

The police arrived. The shooting ended. One person was killed—a teenage boy who worked at the food
court. Two others were injured. Priya gave her statement that night, her hands shaking so badly she
could barely hold the officer's pen.

She returned to work two weeks later, thinking she was ready. She wasn't. Now, when she closes the
store, her heart races as she pulls down the security shutter. The sound of it descending—a metallic
scraping she never noticed before—sends electricity through her chest. She finds herself checking
the corners of the stockroom, listening for footsteps that aren't there. Some nights she calls her
husband Rajesh to stay on the phone while she locks up, needing the sound of another voice, though
she doesn't explain why.

At home, she lies awake beside Rajesh, her body tense beneath the covers. She'll suddenly jolt awake
at 3 a.m., her heart pounding, unsure what woke her. The sound of a car backfiring outside sends her
into a state of hypervigilance that takes hours to settle. She's stopped going to the mall on her
days off—even walking past the entrance makes her nauseous.

What troubles her most is how the trauma has colonized the ordinary. She startles at the beep of the
register. Crowded aisles feel suffocating. She finds herself scanning her coworkers' faces,
searching for signs of danger she can't articulate. She's become someone who flinches, who holds her
breath, who cannot quite believe she's safe even when logic insists she is.

Rajesh has been patient, but she can see the worry in his eyes. She's called in sick twice in the
past month, unable to face the drive to work. She wonders if she can keep doing this job, this work
she's done so competently for so long. The mall that was familiar has become a place of threat, and
Priya moves through it now like a stranger in her own life.
