# Margaret

Margaret sits with her hands folded in her lap, her posture still maintaining the careful composure
that has defined her professional life for the past thirty-five years. At sixty, she has delivered
thousands of babies, guided countless women through their most vulnerable moments, and built a
reputation as someone unfailingly calm under pressure. That steadiness now feels like a performance
she can no longer sustain.

The incident happened fourteen months ago on an ordinary Tuesday morning at the private maternity
clinic where Margaret worked. A cleaning contractor had mixed industrial-strength bleach with
ammonia in one of the labor rooms—a catastrophic oversight. Margaret was the first to enter, and the
chemical fumes hit her immediately. She remembers the burning sensation in her throat, the way her
eyes watered so intensely she could barely see, and the metallic taste that seemed to coat her
mouth. She called for help, guided herself out of the room, and spent the next six hours in the
emergency department being monitored. The doctors said she was fortunate—her exposure was brief, her
lungs had cleared, her bloodwork was normal. She was discharged with reassurance and told she would
be fine.

But Margaret was not fine.

Within weeks, she began experiencing what she describes as "phantom reactions." At unpredictable
moments throughout her day, she would smell the chemical again—that acrid, suffocating odor—even
though no one else could detect anything. Her heart would race. She would feel the burning in her
throat return, though her physical examinations remained normal. The first time this happened while
she was attending a delivery, she had to excuse herself, breathing into her hands in the supply
closet, terrified that her hands were shaking too much to safely support her patient.

Margaret attempted to return to work after two weeks off. She told herself she was being ridiculous,
that her body was simply playing tricks on her. But the maternity ward, the place she had found
meaning and purpose for nearly four decades, had become a minefield. The smell of antiseptic now
triggers a cascade of anxiety. She finds herself hypervigilant about cleaning products, checking
labels obsessively, sometimes asking colleagues to verify that something is "safe" before she enters
a room. She has taken to arriving earlier than necessary, walking through each space methodically,
as though she can somehow prevent another accident through sheer force of will.

Her sleep has deteriorated significantly. She lies awake for hours, her mind replaying the moment
she opened that door, imagining worse outcomes—what if she had been pregnant at the time, what if
she had inhaled more deeply, what if the clinic had been full of laboring women. These "what-ifs"
loop endlessly. When she does sleep, she often wakes gasping, uncertain whether she has been
dreaming about the chemical exposure or about something else entirely.

Her partner has noticed her withdrawal. Margaret, who once loved hosting dinner parties and spending
weekends at their cottage, now prefers to stay home. She finds social interaction exhausting. Even
conversations with colleagues feel strained; she worries they see her as unreliable now, someone who
had a "reaction" and couldn't handle it professionally.

She has cut her hours at the clinic to part-time, though she hasn't fully admitted to herself that
this might be permanent. She still loves the work—the privilege of witnessing new life, the trust
women place in her hands. But something fundamental has shifted. The safety she once felt in her own
competence and in her familiar environment has fractured, and she cannot yet imagine how to rebuild
it.
