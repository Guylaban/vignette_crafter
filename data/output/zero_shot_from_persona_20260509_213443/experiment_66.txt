# Marcus

Marcus is a fifty-five-year-old painter who has spent the last thirty years transforming spaces with
color and precision. His hands know the weight of a brush, the rhythm of a roller, the discipline
required to cut a perfect line. He takes pride in his work—the way he can visualize a room's
potential and bring it to life. His wife, Amara, often teases him about how he notices the paint on
every wall they pass, assessing quality and technique like other people read newspapers.

Eighteen months ago, Marcus's youngest son, David, died suddenly of a heart attack. David was
thirty-two, a healthy man with a wife and two young children. There had been no warning, no illness,
no time to prepare. Marcus was at a job site when he received the call. He remembers standing in an
unfinished living room, roller in hand, the afternoon light streaming through bare windows, when
everything became unreal.

The funeral happened in a blur. Marcus moved through it mechanically, speaking to relatives,
accepting condolences, his body present but his mind somewhere else entirely. He returned to work
two weeks later because staying home felt worse. The empty house amplified the silence.

But something has shifted in the months since. Marcus finds himself unable to concentrate on jobs
that once brought him satisfaction. He'll be painting a bedroom, and suddenly his chest tightens.
His breathing becomes shallow. He sets down his brush and has to sit, sometimes for an hour, waiting
for the sensation to pass. He's started making mistakes—missed spots, uneven coverage—things that
never happened before. His clients have noticed. One asked if he was feeling alright.

At night, Marcus lies awake beside Amara, his mind replaying moments he wasn't even present for. He
imagines David's final moments, the confusion, the fear. He wonders if David called out for someone.
He wonders if it hurt. These thoughts come uninvited, vivid and relentless, usually between two and
four in the morning. When he does sleep, he sometimes wakes gasping, his heart racing as if it's his
own body betraying him.

Crowds have become difficult. A trip to the hardware store can leave him feeling exposed and
panicked. He avoids his usual suppliers, preferring to order online. He's stopped attending his
weekly poker game with friends. Amara invited her sister to visit last month, and Marcus found
himself irritable and withdrawn, unable to engage in normal conversation. He apologized afterward,
but the effort of being around people feels monumental.

What troubles Marcus most is the guilt. He was not there when David needed him. He was painting
someone else's walls. This thought loops endlessly. He knows intellectually that he couldn't have
prevented anything, but the knowledge doesn't penetrate the feeling. He's also angry—at his body for
surviving, at God or fate or whatever took his son, at the randomness of a heart that simply
stopped.

Amara has suggested he talk to someone. She's worried about him. He's lost weight. He drinks more
than he used to. The house where he and Amara have built their life together now feels like a place
where something essential is missing. He walks past David's old bedroom sometimes and cannot bring
himself to open the door.

Marcus still goes to work. His hands still know what to do. But the joy has drained from it, leaving
only the mechanical repetition of a man trying to paint over something that cannot be covered.
