# Maria's Story

Maria has been working as a live-in housekeeper for the same family for nearly eight years. She
takes pride in her work—the way she organizes their home, the care she puts into preparing meals,
the trust they've placed in her. At forty-two, she's built a quiet, structured life around this job.
She sends money back to her sister in the Philippines each month and has learned to navigate the
rhythms of an American household with practiced efficiency.

Three years ago, everything changed. A man began appearing at the house when the family was away.
First, she noticed small things—the gate left open when she was certain she'd locked it, her work
shoes moved from the porch. Then came the notes. Handwritten messages left on the kitchen counter,
slipped under her bedroom door. They were never explicitly threatening, but they were intimate in a
way that made her skin crawl. He knew her schedule. He knew when she went to the market on
Thursdays. He knew the color of her car.

When she finally told her employers, they called the police, but by then the man had disappeared.
The investigation went nowhere. No fingerprints. No clear identity. Just the unsettling knowledge
that someone had been in their home, watching her.

Now, three years later, Maria still locks every door and window multiple times before bed. She
checks them again at 2 a.m., and once more at dawn. Her hands shake as she does this. Her mind knows
the checking doesn't change anything, but her body demands it anyway. The ritual has become as
necessary as breathing.

She no longer goes to the market alone. Even with her employer accompanying her, she feels exposed
in open spaces. Her eyes scan faces in crowds, searching for something she couldn't describe to
anyone. In the supermarket, she stays close to aisles with mirrors so she can see behind her. In the
car, she watches the vehicles behind them. Sometimes she's convinced someone is following, and her
heart hammers against her ribs for miles until the car turns off and she can breathe again.

At night, sleep is fragmented. She lies awake listening to the house settle, interpreting every
creak as footsteps. When she does sleep, she dreams of the gate opening, of footprints in the
hallway, of finding him standing at the foot of her bed. She wakes gasping, her nightgown soaked
with sweat.

The worst part is the feeling of being watched even now, even in the safety of the house where
nothing has happened in three years. It's a constant low-level electricity running through her body,
a hypervigilance that exhausts her. She finds herself irritable with the family, snapping at their
children over small things, then immediately feeling guilty. She's withdrawn from the few
friendships she'd made, declining invitations to church gatherings and community events. The thought
of being around people she doesn't know intimately triggers a panic she can't quite control.

Maria still shows up to work each day. She still cooks and cleans and maintains the home. But
something in her has fractured. She moves through her days in a fog of watchfulness, her nervous
system permanently tuned to a frequency of threat. She tells herself it's been long enough, that she
should be over this by now. But her body hasn't received that message.
