# A Shepherd's Burden

Mama Grace sits in the worn leather chair across from me, her hands folded in her lap with the
practiced composure of someone accustomed to holding space for others' pain. At eighty, she carries
herself with the dignity of a woman who has spent fifty-three years in ministry, first in her
homeland and then here, building a congregation from nothing in a city that initially felt as
foreign as the moon. Yet beneath the composed exterior, I notice how her fingers worry the fabric of
her cardigan—a gesture that contradicts the stillness of her face.

She came to see me because her daughter insisted. "Mama is not sleeping," her daughter had said on
the phone, concern threading through her voice. "She wakes at three in the morning, every morning,
and she doesn't come back to bed." Grace had resisted at first. Pastors counsel others; they do not
seek counsel themselves. But her husband, Samuel, had gently suggested that perhaps even shepherds
need tending, and so here she sits.

The trauma that brought her to my office happened forty-seven years ago, though she speaks of it as
though it happened last week. In 1977, when she was thirty-three years old and already an ordained
minister, the political violence in her country escalated without warning. Grace and Samuel had been
running a small church and school in their community. One afternoon, soldiers came. They were
looking for "subversives"—anyone who had spoken against the regime, anyone who had sheltered
refugees, anyone who had preached about justice. Grace's church had done all three.

She remembers the sound of boots on the concrete floor. She remembers Samuel being pulled away from
her, his hand slipping through her fingers. She remembers the officer's face, unremarkable and
blank, as he told her that her husband was being taken for questioning. She remembers not knowing,
for three days, whether he was alive.

Samuel returned, changed in ways that took years to understand. But Grace never fully returned from
those three days. The waiting had fractured something essential in her.

For decades, she pushed forward. She rebuilt their church after they emigrated. She preached sermons
about faith and resilience. She counseled parishioners through their own traumas—many of them
refugees like herself—and she did this work with genuine compassion and grace. But at night, in the
hours when the world quiets, her body remembers what her mind has tried to transcend.

Now, at eighty, the hypervigilance that once served her—the constant monitoring of her environment,
the inability to fully relax—has become a prison. She wakes at three in the morning, heart racing,
convinced that something terrible is about to happen. Some mornings she cannot remember why she is
afraid; the fear simply exists, lodged in her chest like a stone. During the day, loud noises make
her flinch. The sound of a car door slamming sends her back to that afternoon. Crowds at church
gatherings, which once brought her joy, now feel suffocating. She finds herself scanning faces,
assessing threats, even though she knows intellectually that she is safe in her own country, in her
own church, surrounded by people who love her.

The worst part, she tells me quietly, is the guilt. She is alive. Samuel is alive. They built a good
life. They have grandchildren who will never know the fear that shaped their parents. And yet, the
nightmares persist. The hyperarousal continues. Her body has not received the message that the
danger has passed. Part of her remains in that church in 1977, waiting for boots on concrete,
waiting for her husband to be returned to her, waiting for the next terrible thing.
