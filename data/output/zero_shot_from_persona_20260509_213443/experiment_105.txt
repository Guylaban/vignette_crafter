# Margaret's Hands

Margaret sits in the studio where she has spent the last fifty years, her weathered hands moving
across the clay with the same deliberate grace they've always known. But something has changed. The
rhythm is different now—more cautious, interrupted by tremors that weren't there before.

Six months ago, Margaret was leaving her pottery studio late one evening, carrying a bag of finished
pieces to her car. She had been doing this same walk for decades, through the quiet streets of her
neighborhood, past the same storefronts and lampposts. That night, a man emerged from between two
buildings. What happened in the next few minutes exists in her memory as fragmented images: his face
inches from hers, his hands gripping her arm, the sharp pain when she fell against the concrete. He
took her purse and left her there, bruised and shaking.

The physical injuries healed relatively quickly. But Margaret's relationship with her body and her
surroundings has fractured in ways that don't show on X-rays.

Now, when Margaret works at the wheel, she finds herself unable to fully concentrate. Her mind
drifts to the assault—not in a linear way, but in sudden, intrusive moments. She'll be centering a
piece of clay, feeling the familiar resistance beneath her palms, and suddenly she's back on that
street, her heart racing as though the threat is immediate and present. Her hands tense. The clay
becomes difficult to control. Sometimes she stops working entirely and sits very still, waiting for
the feeling to pass.

The studio, which once felt like sanctuary, now feels unsafe after dark. Margaret used to work into
the evenings, finding peace in the solitude and the meditative quality of her craft. Now she leaves
before sunset, rushing through the last hour of daylight to close up and get to her car. The drive
home, which takes fifteen minutes, feels endless. Every car that pulls up beside her at a red light
makes her breath catch. She checks her doors three times before she can relax enough to sit down.

Sleep has become difficult. Margaret lies awake at night, her mind replaying the assault in vivid
detail—details that seem to sharpen rather than fade with time. Sometimes she jolts awake in the
dark, disoriented and afraid, convinced that someone is in her bedroom. Her daughter, who lives
across town, has started calling more often, sensing something is wrong though Margaret hasn't told
her the full story.

What troubles Margaret most is the way her own mind has betrayed her sense of agency. She has always
been a capable woman—a skilled artist, a widow who managed her life independently after her husband
died fifteen years ago. Now, at seventy-eight, she finds herself feeling helpless in unexpected
moments. A knock at the door sends her heart racing. A stranger's raised voice in a store makes her
palms sweat. She avoids going to the grocery store at certain times, avoids walking to the mailbox
when it's getting dark.

The clay still calls to her. Some mornings, Margaret arrives at the studio with genuine hope that
today will be different, that her hands will remember their old confidence. And sometimes they
do—she'll create a piece that satisfies her, something with the fluidity and intention that has
always defined her work. But increasingly, these moments are interrupted by hypervigilance, by a
body that seems to be preparing for a threat that isn't there. Margaret sits at her wheel,
surrounded by the tools of a lifetime, and wonders if the woman who knew how to lose herself in her
art is still inside her somewhere.
