# Clinical Vignette: Officer Layla

Layla has been a police officer for thirty-four years. She grew up watching her father work in law
enforcement in a small community, and she inherited both his sense of duty and his quiet
determination. For most of her career, she has been known as steady—the kind of officer other cops
trust to have their back, the one who stays calm when situations escalate. She has two adult
children and has been married to Hassan for thirty-two years. They have a routine: she works her
shifts, comes home, and they share tea on the back porch most evenings.

Eighteen months ago, everything changed during what seemed like a routine call.

Layla responded to a domestic disturbance at a residential address she'd been to before. The
situation appeared contained when she arrived. The suspect was cooperative, compliant, standing in
the living room with his hands visible. She had made the scene assessment—a skill she'd honed over
three decades—and believed the immediate danger had passed. As she turned to speak with the victim,
the man produced a knife from behind a couch cushion and attacked her with sudden, explosive
violence.

The blade caught her shoulder and arm. She fought back instinctively, her training taking over even
as shock flooded her system. A backup officer arrived within minutes. The suspect was subdued. Layla
was transported to the hospital with deep lacerations requiring surgery and extensive stitching.

The physical wounds healed. The scars on her shoulder are now pale, faded lines that she notices
when she showers. But something else has not healed.

Now, when Layla responds to calls, her body operates in a state of constant hypervigilance. Her
heart rate elevates before she even exits her patrol car. Every person she approaches could be
hiding something; every hand movement registers as a potential threat. She finds herself checking
and rechecking her weapon, making sure it's accessible. She's been written up twice for being overly
aggressive during pat-downs—she searches more thoroughly now, more roughly than she used to, driven
by a fear she cannot fully articulate.

At night, she wakes suddenly, her shirt soaked with sweat, her mind replaying the moment the knife
appeared. Hassan reaches for her in the darkness, and sometimes she flinches at his touch. She has
always been the protector in their relationship; now she feels like she is breaking apart in ways
she cannot explain to him. How do you tell your husband of thirty-two years that you're afraid? That
you no longer trust your own judgment? That the skill she relied on—her ability to read a scene, to
assess danger—failed her in the most fundamental way?

She has taken two medical leaves in the past year. The first time, she told herself she just needed
rest. The second time, she couldn't bring herself to return to patrol. Now she works administrative
duties, filing reports and answering phones, and the shame of this is almost worse than the fear.
Her colleagues don't say anything directly, but she feels their eyes on her, the subtle shift in how
they treat her. The strong one has become fragile.

Layla sits in the sessions and talks about the night in measured tones. She describes the attack
with clinical precision, as if reporting a case. But her hands tremble as she speaks, and sometimes
she stops mid-sentence, lost in a memory that has pulled her away from the present moment. She wants
to return to work—real work, the work that has defined her entire adult life. But she is terrified
of what might happen if she does.
