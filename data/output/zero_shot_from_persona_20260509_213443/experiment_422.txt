# Marcus

Marcus sits in the waiting room with his laptop open, though he isn't working. The screen has been
dark for ten minutes. He's a tax consultant—a good one—but lately, the spreadsheets and client
meetings blur together into something that feels almost unreal.

Twelve years ago, Marcus was a different person. He was a sergeant in the Army, stationed overseas,
where he spent eighteen months managing logistics for his unit. The work was routine,
administrative, nothing like what the movies show. Then, one afternoon in April, his convoy was hit
by an IED. Marcus was in the second vehicle. He remembers the sound first—not an explosion exactly,
but a compression of the world, a moment where everything became too loud and too bright at once.
His best friend, Daniel, was in the vehicle ahead. Marcus remembers running toward it. He remembers
what he found.

He came home six months later. He told people he was fine. He meant it at the time.

For years, Marcus functioned well enough. He went to college on the GI Bill, became a tax
consultant, married Sarah, a kind woman who worked in nonprofit administration. They bought a house
in the suburbs. From the outside, his life looked exactly as it should. But somewhere around the
eight-year mark, things shifted. The dreams started—not every night, but often enough that Marcus
began to dread sleep. In them, he's back in that moment, but the details change. Sometimes he's the
one trapped. Sometimes it's Sarah. Sometimes it's his five-year-old daughter, Emma.

He wakes gasping. Sarah has learned not to touch him when he's disoriented. Once, she did, and he
nearly hit her before recognizing where he was.

The hypervigilance is constant now. When Marcus drives, he scans the roadside for disturbed earth,
for anything that doesn't belong. He notices every car that stays behind him too long. In the
office, he positions himself where he can see the door. His colleagues think he's just particular
about his workspace. During tax season last year, a colleague dropped a heavy binder on the floor
near his desk, and Marcus found himself under the table before he understood what happened. He told
his boss he had a migraine and went home.

What troubles him most is the emotional numbness. Sarah will tell him about her day—a win with a
client, something Emma did that was funny—and he hears the words, but they don't land. He feels like
he's watching his own life from behind glass. He loves his wife and daughter; he knows this
intellectually. But the feeling doesn't match the knowing. When Emma runs to hug him after work, he
hugs her back, but it feels performative, like he's remembering how a father should respond rather
than actually feeling it.

The irritability has gotten worse. Last month, he snapped at a junior consultant over a minor filing
error—something that would have been routine annoyance before. Instead, he felt a rage so sudden and
hot it frightened him. He left the office for the rest of the day.

Sarah finally insisted he come to therapy. She said it gently, but Marcus heard the desperation
underneath. He knows she's exhausted. He knows she's afraid of him, even though he's never been
violent outside of that one half-conscious moment. He knows she's grieving the man he was before
April, the one who could be present with her, who could simply enjoy a dinner without his mind
cataloging exits and threats.

Marcus came because his marriage depends on it. But sitting here now, he's not sure anyone can help
him feel like himself again. He's not sure he remembers who that was.
