# Maria's Story

Maria has been a paramedic for thirty-eight years. She takes pride in her steady hands, her ability
to remain calm when others panic, her knowledge of protocols that have saved countless lives. She
arrives at work early most days, checks her equipment with ritualistic precision, and knows the
neighborhoods of her city better than the streets of her own childhood home in Puerto Rico. This
identity—the competent, unflappable paramedic—has sustained her through decades of difficult calls.

Four years ago, everything fractured.

She and her partner were called to a residential break-in on the south side. The police had already
cleared the scene, or so dispatch said. When they entered the house, a man with a knife appeared
from the kitchen. Maria's partner ran. Maria froze—a moment of hesitation that still haunts her—and
the man grabbed her, pulling her into a back bedroom and locking the door. For seventeen minutes, he
held the knife to her throat while police negotiators worked outside. She remembers the smell of his
sweat, the tremor in his hand, the sound of her own breathing. She remembers thinking she would die
there, that her husband Miguel would get a phone call instead of coming home to dinner.

The police got her out. She was not physically harmed. This detail—the absence of physical
injury—somehow makes everything worse.

The first months after, Maria tried to return to work. She would suit up, check her equipment, and
make it maybe two calls before her chest would tighten so severely she couldn't breathe. One
afternoon, responding to a routine welfare check, she found herself unable to exit the ambulance.
She sat in the driver's seat with her hands shaking, convinced that opening that door would lead her
back into that bedroom. Her supervisor sent her home.

Now, a year into medical leave, Maria struggles with the ordinary architecture of her days. She no
longer sleeps more than three hours at a time. When she does sleep, she dreams of the knife, of her
throat closing, of being unable to call for help. She wakes gasping, sometimes with her hands
pressed against her neck. Miguel has learned not to touch her suddenly; twice she has struck out at
him in those first disoriented seconds after waking, and the guilt from those moments compounds
everything else.

She avoids driving past the house where it happened, which means avoiding entire neighborhoods of
the city she once knew intimately. She's stopped going to the grocery store, preferring to order
delivery rather than navigate the vulnerability of being in public spaces. When she does venture
out, her eyes scan constantly for exits, for threats. A car door slamming sends her heart racing. A
raised voice in a restaurant makes her dizzy.

What troubles her most is the loss of herself. She was never someone who was afraid. She was the
person other people relied on. Now she's afraid of her own mailbox, afraid of her kitchen where
Miguel might move too quickly, afraid of the person she's becoming. She tells herself she should be
"over it" by now—seventeen minutes shouldn't define her, shouldn't steal her career, shouldn't make
her a burden to the man who loves her. But the fear doesn't listen to logic. It lives in her body
now, permanent as bone.

She sits across from me each week, this woman who has held the hands of dying strangers, and
struggles to explain why she cannot hold her own life together anymore.
