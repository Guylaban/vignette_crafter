# Marcus

Marcus sits with his hands folded in his lap, occasionally running his thumb across the calluses on
his fingertips—marks that have defined him since childhood. At thirty-five, he's spent nearly three
decades as a classical guitarist, performing in concert halls across the country and teaching at a
conservatory. His wife, Elena, says he used to light up when talking about music. Now, he mostly
talks about the accident.

It happened eight months ago on a clear Tuesday evening. Marcus was driving home from a rehearsal,
mentally running through the Concierto de Aranjuez, when a delivery truck ran a red light. The
collision was violent and sudden. His car spun twice before hitting a telephone pole. He remembers
the sound of breaking glass, the smell of gasoline, and then—nothing until the paramedics were
cutting him out of the vehicle. A fractured collarbone, three broken ribs, a concussion. The
physical injuries healed. The other injuries persist.

Now, Marcus avoids driving. Elena handles the car entirely. When he must ride as a passenger, his
leg bounces constantly and his breathing becomes shallow. He's taken several Ubers to the
conservatory, but he always requests drivers with pristine safety records, checking their ratings
obsessively before confirming the ride. Last month, a driver made a sudden lane change and Marcus
felt his chest tighten so severely he thought he was having a heart attack. He asked to be let out
three blocks from his destination and walked the rest of the way in the rain.

The worst part, though, isn't the driving. It's the music. Or rather, the inability to access it.

Marcus tried returning to the conservatory six weeks after the accident. His collarbone had healed
enough to hold a guitar, and he was determined to reclaim some normalcy. He sat in his studio,
picked up his 1965 Ramirez—the instrument he's owned for fifteen years—and positioned it against his
body. His hands knew what to do. His muscle memory was intact. But the moment he began to play,
something fractured inside him. The music triggered an overwhelming sense of dread, as if the notes
themselves were dangerous. His heart raced. His hands trembled so badly he couldn't hold the strings
steady. He set the guitar down and didn't touch it for three weeks.

He's tried again since then, but the pattern repeats. Even listening to recordings of himself
performing causes anxiety so acute that he has to leave the room. Elena found him in the garage last
Tuesday, sitting in the dark, unable to explain why he'd gone there or how long he'd been sitting.

Sleep has become fragmented. Marcus wakes at 3 or 4 a.m., drenched in sweat, sometimes from
nightmares about the accident, sometimes from dreams where he's playing and his hands won't obey
him. He lies awake for hours, listening to Elena breathe beside him, feeling the distance between
them grow. She's tried to be patient, but he sees the worry in her eyes, the way she watches him.
He's become irritable with her over small things—a door closing too loudly, a car horn in the
distance—and then feels crushing guilt afterward.

His students at the conservatory have noticed too. He's taken a medical leave, telling them he's
still recovering. The truth is more complicated. He doesn't know if he can play again, and he's
terrified of what that means for his identity, his marriage, his life. The accident took more than
his sense of safety. It took his language, the one thing that has always made sense when nothing
else did.
