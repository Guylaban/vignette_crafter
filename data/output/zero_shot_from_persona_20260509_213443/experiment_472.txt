# A Matter of Control

Sarah sits in the principal's office at Lincoln Elementary, her hands steady as she reviews the
morning's incident reports. The fluorescent lights hum overhead—a sound that used to make her
flinch, though she's learned to manage it now. She's been principal here for three years, and on the
surface, she manages the role with the same precision she brought to everything else in her life.
But beneath that competence runs a current of vigilance that never quite switches off.

The trauma happened fourteen years ago during her deployment as a military intelligence officer. An
IED detonated near her convoy outside Kandahar. She wasn't severely injured physically—some shrapnel
in her left shoulder, a concussion—but the moment itself fractured something deeper. The sound was
the worst part. Not just loud, but total. It consumed everything.

She left the military after that deployment, telling herself she was ready to move on. For years,
she almost believed it. She finished her master's degree in educational administration, climbed the
career ladder, built a reputation as a no-nonsense administrator who kept her school running like a
well-oiled machine. Discipline, structure, accountability—these were the things that made sense to
her. These were things she could control.

But the dreams started again about two years ago. Not every night, but often enough that she began
dreading sleep. In them, she's back in the Humvee, and the explosion happens differently each
time—sometimes she sees it coming, sometimes she doesn't. She wakes gasping, her heart hammering
against her ribs, her sheets soaked through. She's learned not to scream. Her ex-husband left partly
because of this, though he never said it directly. He couldn't understand why she wouldn't talk
about it, why she needed to sleep with the bedroom door locked, why sudden noises sent her spiraling
into a place he couldn't reach.

During the school day, Sarah maintains an iron composure. But lately, she's noticed how her body
betrays her. Last month, a student dropped a stack of textbooks in the hallway outside her office.
The sound—that sharp, sudden crash—sent her into the bathroom where she sat in a stall for twenty
minutes, breathing deliberately, reminding herself where she was. A school. Safe. Present day.

She's hyperaware of exits in every room. She parks her car where she can see the entrance and exit.
She knows the quickest routes through the school building, the places with clear sightlines. Her
teachers probably think she's just thorough during her walkthroughs, but really, she's scanning.
Always scanning. It's exhausting in a way she can't quite articulate to anyone.

The noise sensitivity has gotten worse. The lunch room chaos that other administrators handle with
casual amusement makes Sarah want to escape her own skin. She's started eating lunch in her office
more often, telling herself it's efficiency. The truth is that the cacophony of children's voices,
the unpredictability of it, the way she can't control what might happen next—it all feels dangerous
in a way she knows, intellectually, doesn't make sense.

Sarah sets down her pen and looks out the window at the playground. Children run and shout, their
voices carrying on the wind. Part of her envies their freedom, their ability to be surprised by joy
rather than startled by threat. She wonders sometimes if she'll ever feel that way again, or if the
person she was before Kandahar is simply gone, replaced by this version of herself—capable,
functional, but always, always braced for the next explosion.
