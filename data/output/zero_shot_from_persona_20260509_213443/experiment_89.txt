# Marcus

Marcus is a fifty-one-year-old Samoan man who has worked as a welder for the past twenty-eight
years. He takes pride in his craft—the precision of it, the way metal bends to intention under heat
and skill. His hands are scarred and capable, marked by decades of work he respects. He and his
wife, Fiona, have been married for twenty-six years. They have three adult children and a
grandchild. By most measures, Marcus has built a solid life.

Eighteen months ago, everything fractured.

Marcus was driving home from a job site on a Friday evening, heading north on the highway toward his
family's home in the suburbs. The weather was clear. He wasn't speeding. A utility truck crossed the
median without warning, its driver apparently asleep at the wheel. There was no time to react. The
collision happened at sixty kilometers per hour. Marcus's sedan crumpled like paper. His left leg
shattered in three places. His ribs cracked. For several minutes, he was trapped in the vehicle,
unable to move, certain he was going to die.

The physical injuries healed—or mostly did. His leg still aches in cold weather, and he walks with a
slight limp now. But the healing that should have followed has stalled somewhere inside him.

Marcus no longer feels safe driving. He takes the passenger seat when Fiona drives, and even then,
his body remains tense, his jaw clenched. He finds himself tracking other vehicles obsessively,
imagining collision scenarios, mentally calculating escape routes. On highways, his breathing
becomes shallow. His palms sweat. He's convinced that another car will cross the median. He knows
logically that this is unlikely, but knowing and believing are different things.

He returned to welding six months after the accident, against his doctor's initial advice. Work was
supposed to help—a return to normalcy, to identity. Instead, the workshop has become another
minefield. The sound of metal striking metal makes him flinch violently. His concentration
fractures. He'll be in the middle of a weld, and suddenly he's back in the crushed car, the smell of
burning rubber and gasoline filling his nostrils, his leg screaming with pain. His hands shake. He's
made mistakes he's never made before, small ones so far, but they terrify him. A welder's mistake
can injure someone. The responsibility sits heavier now.

Sleep is fractured and unreliable. Most nights, Marcus lies awake beside Fiona, his mind replaying
the moment of impact in vivid, exhausting detail. When he does sleep, he jolts awake gasping,
convinced he's still trapped in the vehicle. Fiona has stopped trying to comfort him in these
moments; her touch startles him further. He can see the worry in her face, the exhaustion from
living with his fear.

He's withdrawn from the men he's worked alongside for decades. Their easy camaraderie feels
impossible now. He declines invitations to weekend barbecues and his nephew's wedding. There's a
flatness to his days, an absence of pleasure even in things he once enjoyed—watching rugby, working
in his garden, sitting with his grandchild.

Marcus knows something is wrong with him. He's not the man he was before the truck crossed the
median. That version of himself—capable, steady, present—feels like someone else's memory now. He
carries the collision inside him still, as if it's not finished happening. Every loud noise, every
unexpected movement, every moment behind the wheel threatens to pull him back into that fractured
metal and the certainty of dying.
