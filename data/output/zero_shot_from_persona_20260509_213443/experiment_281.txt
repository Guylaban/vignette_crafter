# Margaret's Story

Margaret sits in the waiting room with her hands folded in her lap, her posture impeccably
straight—a habit cultivated over fifty years of professional life. At seventy-four, she still
maintains the bearing of someone accustomed to moving through the world with purpose and competence.
Yet something in her eyes suggests a weariness that goes deeper than age.

She was born in Budapest in 1950, the daughter of a physician and a pianist. Her childhood was
ordinary until it wasn't. In 1956, when she was six years old, the Hungarian Revolution erupted.
Margaret remembers fragments—the sound of tanks rolling through streets, her mother's trembling
hands as she packed a single suitcase, the weight of her father lifting her onto his shoulders as
they ran toward the Austrian border at night. She remembers the cold. She remembers her mother
whispering that they must be absolutely quiet.

They made it to Vienna, then eventually to England, where Margaret's father found work in a hospital
and her mother gave piano lessons in their small flat. Margaret grew up bilingual, caught between
two worlds. She became a physical therapist—a profession that allowed her to help people rebuild
their bodies after injury, to restore function and mobility. There was something deeply satisfying
about that work, about witnessing people reclaim their physical selves.

She married David, a kind man who worked in accounting, and they built a quiet, orderly life
together. They traveled occasionally, entertained friends, maintained their home. Margaret worked
steadily for decades, retiring at sixty-eight. She has two adult children and four grandchildren. By
most measures, she had successfully left her childhood trauma behind.

But three years ago, at age seventy-one, Margaret began having nightmares. They started after
watching a news program about the Syrian refugee crisis. She saw images of families at borders,
children separated from parents, the same desperation she remembered feeling as a child. That night,
she dreamed she was six again, running through darkness, unable to find her parents. She woke
gasping, her heart racing, convinced for several moments that she was still in danger.

The nightmares have continued, though they come and go unpredictably. Some weeks pass quietly; other
weeks she wakes four or five times a night, drenched in sweat. David has learned not to touch her
suddenly when she wakes, as she startles violently. She's begun avoiding news programs entirely,
which frustrates her—she's always been an informed, engaged person—but the images trigger something
she can't control.

Margaret finds herself hypervigilant in ways she didn't used to be. When she's in crowded places,
she notices exits compulsively. She's become uncomfortable in situations where she feels she can't
leave easily. A visit to the theater with David last year ended with her leaving midway through,
overwhelmed by a sense of being trapped. She was embarrassed, couldn't quite explain it to him.

Her sleep remains fragmented even on nights without nightmares. She wakes at 3 or 4 a.m. and often
cannot return to sleep, her mind cycling through memories she thought she'd processed decades ago.
She finds herself thinking about her childhood more frequently now—small details she'd forgotten
suddenly returning with vivid clarity.

What troubles Margaret most is a sense of disconnection from the present. She's still capable, still
competent, but she feels as though part of her is always watching, waiting for danger. She feels
older than her years, tired in a way that sleep doesn't cure. She wonders if this is simply what
aging brings, or if the past she thought she'd resolved has finally caught up with her.
