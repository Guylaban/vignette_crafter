# A Familiar Hallway

Marcus has been a school counselor for thirty-eight years. He knows the rhythms of adolescence—the
social hierarchies, the academic pressures, the quiet desperation of teenagers trying to figure out
who they are. He has built his career on being the steady presence in the hallway, the man students
seek out when things feel unbearable. His office is filled with thank-you cards from former
students, many of whom write about how his calm demeanor and genuine listening saved them during
their darkest moments.

Three years ago, on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon in March, everything changed.

Marcus was walking through the parking lot after school, heading to his car around 4:30 p.m. A man
he didn't recognize approached him, asking for directions. Within seconds, the man's demeanor
shifted. There was a knife. Marcus was pulled behind a van, struck repeatedly in the face and torso,
and robbed of his wallet and watch. The assault lasted perhaps four minutes, though Marcus describes
it as both instantaneous and eternal. A teacher jogging past the lot heard his shout and the
attacker fled. Marcus ended up with a fractured cheekbone, several broken ribs, and lacerations that
required thirty-two stitches.

The physical injuries healed within weeks. The other injuries have not.

Marcus returned to work after six weeks of medical leave. His wife, Patricia, was relieved—she
thought routine would help him move forward. But within days, Marcus found himself unable to enter
the parking lot without his heart racing so violently he thought he might collapse. He began parking
three blocks away and walking through the neighborhood instead. Then he stopped parking nearby
altogether. Now he arrives at school an hour early and parks directly in front of the main entrance,
where people are always present.

The hallways that once felt like his domain have become a minefield. When students approach him
unexpectedly, he startles violently, his body tensing as though preparing for impact. He's aware
this is irrational—these are children he's known for years—but his nervous system doesn't seem to
care about logic. He's had to excuse himself from his office several times to compose himself in the
bathroom, his hands shaking, his breathing shallow.

At night, Marcus lies awake beside Patricia, replaying the assault in excruciating detail. He finds
himself stuck on small moments: the exact timbre of the man's voice, the specific smell of the
parking lot asphalt in spring. He wakes at 3 a.m. drenched in sweat, his body convinced the knife is
still coming. Patricia has learned not to touch him suddenly when he wakes; twice she's startled him
and he's reflexively pushed her away with surprising force.

He's become hypervigilant in ways that exhaust him. In the school cafeteria, he monitors exits.
During faculty meetings, he sits with his back to the wall. When students knock on his office door,
he finds himself assessing them for threat before opening it—a thought that horrifies and shames
him. These are vulnerable young people, and he's viewing them as potential dangers.

What troubles Marcus most is that he can no longer be the counselor his students need. A freshman
came in last week in genuine crisis, and Marcus found himself unable to focus on her words. His mind
was elsewhere, caught in the parking lot, unable to access the empathy and presence that has always
been his strength. He sat there nodding, saying the right things, but feeling utterly absent from
his own life.

Patricia keeps suggesting he see someone, and Marcus knows she's right. But part of him wonders if
the man who was assaulted that day—the one who feels constantly in danger, who flinches at
shadows—is now the only version of himself that's left.
