# A Firefighter's Reckoning

Marcus is a seventy-seven-year-old man who spent forty-three years as a firefighter before retiring
five years ago. He is Vietnamese-American, having immigrated to the United States in 1975 at age
twenty-eight. He lives with his wife of fifty-one years in a modest suburban home, and they have
three adult children and five grandchildren. On the surface, Marcus appears to be a man who has
built a stable, respectable life in his adopted country. Yet something fractured inside him decades
ago, and it has never quite healed.

Marcus was not a soldier during the Vietnam War. He was a civilian—a carpenter and construction
foreman in Saigon—when the city fell in April 1975. He describes the final days with a flatness in
his voice that suggests he has told this story many times, perhaps to himself more than to others.
The North Vietnamese army was closing in. The American embassy was evacuating. Chaos consumed the
streets. Marcus remembers the sound of helicopters, the smell of smoke, the sight of people climbing
over walls trying to escape. He managed to secure passage on one of the last flights out, leaving
behind his parents, his childhood home, and everything he had known.

For decades, Marcus kept this buried. He worked hard, raised his family, earned the respect of his
fellow firefighters. He was known as someone who showed up, who didn't complain, who could be
counted on. But in his sixties, after he retired, something shifted. The memories began surfacing
with an intensity he couldn't control or explain. He would be sitting in his living room, watching
television, and suddenly he was back in Saigon, feeling the ground shake from distant explosions. He
started avoiding crowds—something that troubled him because he had spent his career rushing toward
danger to help people. Now, even a busy grocery store could send his heart racing and his palms
sweating.

His sleep deteriorated. Most nights, Marcus lies awake until two or three in the morning, afraid of
the dreams that come when he finally does fall asleep. He often finds himself standing in the
kitchen at odd hours, or sitting on the back porch in the darkness, just waiting for morning. His
wife, Linh, has learned not to touch him suddenly; he startles badly, and once he knocked over a
lamp when she placed a hand on his shoulder in the dark.

The sounds of sirens—once the soundtrack of his professional life—now trigger waves of anxiety. Even
the distant wail of an ambulance can make him feel like he's suffocating. He has become
hypervigilant in ways that confuse and frustrate him. He finds himself scanning rooms for exits,
watching people's hands, anticipating threats that he knows, intellectually, aren't there.

Marcus feels ashamed of these reactions. He spent his entire career as a protector, a man of
composure and control. The fact that memories from fifty years ago can still unravel him feels like
a personal failure. He hasn't talked to his children much about what he experienced; they know he
came from Vietnam, but he has never really told them about those final days, about the terror and
the guilt of escape.

Linh has suggested he talk to someone. Marcus resisted for a long time—that was not how men of his
generation handled things. But recently, as his sleep has worsened and his world has continued to
shrink, he has begun to wonder if perhaps, after all these years, it might be time to speak about
what he left behind.
