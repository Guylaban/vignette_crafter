# Sarah's Story

Sarah sits in the therapy office with her hands folded neatly in her lap, her posture impeccable—the
result of years spent correcting others' alignment as a physical therapist. She's thirty-five,
composed, and speaks in measured tones about the eight years she spent with her ex-husband before
everything changed two years ago.

The relationship had always been complicated. Her ex-husband, a successful accountant, was charming
in public and increasingly controlling at home. Sarah, raised in a traditional Southeast Asian
family where she learned to prioritize harmony and accommodation, found herself gradually accepting
his criticisms about her appearance, her work, her friendships. She told herself this was what
marriage required—compromise, patience, understanding. When he began isolating her from her family,
she rationalized it as him wanting more time together. When his anger escalated, she wondered what
she was doing wrong.

The night everything erupted began ordinarily. Sarah had stayed late at the clinic to finish patient
notes. When she arrived home, her husband was waiting with accusations—he'd seen a text from a male
colleague asking about a treatment protocol. His rage built quickly. Sarah tried to explain, but he
didn't want explanations. He grabbed her arm, his fingers digging into her skin so hard she felt the
bruises forming in real time. When she pulled away, he shoved her against the kitchen counter. Her
ribs hit the edge. She remembers the specific quality of pain—sharp, breathtaking, different from
anything she'd experienced.

What followed was three minutes that fundamentally rewired her nervous system. She tried to protect
herself, to reason with him, to make herself small. Nothing worked. When it finally stopped, she was
on the floor, gasping, with a cut above her eye and the terrifying realization that the man she'd
built a life with had just beaten her.

Now, two years later, Sarah still works as a physical therapist, though everything feels different.
Her hands, once confident and precise, sometimes shake when she's treating patients. She finds
herself hyperaware of her body—noticing every ache, every tension, convinced something is wrong.
She's become careful about how she moves, as if her body might betray her again.

Sleep is fragmented. Most nights, she lies awake until 2 or 3 a.m., her mind replaying that evening
in excruciating detail. When she does sleep, she has nightmares where she's back in that kitchen,
except this time she can't move at all. She wakes gasping, her heart racing, and it takes her a long
time to remember she's safe—that he's gone, that she's alone in her apartment with the door locked.

Loud noises startle her profoundly. A patient dropping equipment, a door slamming, even her
husband's voice on a video call with her sister sends adrenaline flooding through her system. She

stopped going to the gym where she used to feel strong. Her marriage to a new partner—a gentle,
patient man—is strained because she freezes when he tries to be intimate, her body remembering
trauma even when her mind knows this person would never hurt her.

She describes feeling perpetually on edge, as if danger is always imminent. The woman who once moved
through the world with professional confidence now navigates it in a state of constant vigilance,
