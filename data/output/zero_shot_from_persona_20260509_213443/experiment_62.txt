# James's Story

James sits in the clinic with his hands folded tightly in his lap, his weathered fingers bearing the
calluses of forty years spent working the land. At forty-five, he carries himself with the careful
stillness of someone who has learned to take up as little space as possible. He speaks quietly about
his childhood in rural Zimbabwe, where he grew up watching his father's unpredictable rages
transform their small home into a place of terror.

He remembers being seven years old, crouched behind the kitchen door as his father's voice thundered
through the house. His mother had burned the sadza, a small mistake that somehow warranted his
father's fists connecting with her face, her body crashing into the wall. James can still see the
blood on her lip, still hear the sound of his younger sister crying from the bedroom. He learned
early to make himself invisible—to move quietly, to anticipate danger, to read the tension in a room
the way other children read picture books.

The violence was unpredictable. Sometimes weeks would pass in relative calm, and James would let
himself believe it was over. Then something trivial would ignite his father's rage again. His mother
would try to protect the children, positioning herself between them and his father's anger. James
internalized a terrible message: love meant witnessing suffering. It meant being helpless.

He left home at eighteen and eventually found work as an agricultural laborer, first in Zimbabwe and
later in South Africa. The physical work suited him—there was honesty in it, a clear relationship
between effort and outcome. No surprises. No sudden explosions. For years, he functioned reasonably
well, moving from farm to farm, keeping to himself.

At thirty-eight, he met Naledi. She was patient with his emotional distance, his difficulty with
intimacy, his tendency to shut down during conflict. They built a life together, and for a time,
James believed he had escaped his past. But over the last seven years, something has shifted. He
finds himself hypervigilant in ways that puzzle even him. When Naledi raises her voice—even about
something trivial like a bill—his body floods with adrenaline. His chest tightens. He feels the old
panic rising, the sense that violence is imminent.

He has begun withdrawing from her, spending longer hours in the fields even when work doesn't
require it. At night, he lies awake listening to the sounds of their house, unable to relax into
sleep. When Naledi touches him unexpectedly, he flinches. Sometimes he snaps at her harshly, his
words sharp in ways he doesn't intend, then feels overwhelming shame and guilt afterward. He knows
intellectually that she is not his mother, that he is not that frightened child anymore, but his
nervous system hasn't received that message.

Recently, he noticed he's been drinking more—a beer or two in the evening to quiet the restlessness
in his body. He's also become more isolated, declining invitations from coworkers, spending weekends
alone in the garden. The intrusive images have intensified: his mother's face, his father's rage,
the helplessness. Sometimes these memories arrive unbidden while he's working, and he loses focus,
nearly injuring himself with machinery.

James describes all of this matter-of-factly, as though recounting the weather. But his eyes betray
him—they hold the weariness of someone carrying a weight that never gets lighter. He came to the
clinic because Naledi insisted, because she told him she couldn't continue like this, and because
somewhere deep within him, James knows he's running out of places to hide.
