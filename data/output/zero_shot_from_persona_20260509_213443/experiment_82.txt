# The Sound That Won't Fade

Sarah sits in the therapy office with her cello case propped against the wall—a familiar companion
she brings to every session, though she hasn't opened it in three months. At thirty-seven, she has
the posture of someone accustomed to holding herself carefully, her shoulders perpetually tensed as
though bracing against an invisible impact.

She was performing at an outdoor summer festival in downtown Toronto when the van jumped the curb.
The memory arrives not as a coherent narrative but as fragments: the screech of tires cutting
through Dvořák's "Cello Concerto," the sudden scatter of the audience, bodies tumbling like dolls.
She remembers the cellist next to her—Marcus, whom she'd known for fifteen years—pulling her down.
She remembers the smell of burning rubber and something else, something acrid that her mind still
hasn't quite categorized. Six people died. Seventeen were injured. Sarah walked away with a
fractured wrist and what would become a fractured sense of safety.

The physical injury healed within weeks. The wrist is fine now. But something in her nervous system
seems to have been fundamentally rewired that afternoon.

For the first two months after the attack, Sarah couldn't enter the concert hall where she'd been
principal cellist for a decade. The parking garage triggered her. The sound of sirens—which seemed
to follow her everywhere—sent her into a state of breathless panic. Her partner, James, found her
one evening standing motionless in their kitchen, unable to explain why she'd frozen. She'd heard a
motorcycle backfire outside.

She's tried returning to music. The intention is there; the muscle memory is there. But when she
draws her bow across the strings, something fractures inside her chest. The vibrations that once
felt like home now feel like warning signals. She's taken a leave of absence from the orchestra. She
tells people it's temporary, but they both know she doesn't believe it.

Sleep has become a negotiation. She lies awake until exhaustion overwhelms her vigilance, then jolts
awake at 3 a.m., her heart hammering, convinced she's still there—still hearing that sound. James
sleeps in another room now. He tries to hide his frustration, but she sees it in the way his jaw
tightens when she asks him to check the locks for the third time before bed.

The hypervigilance has become her constant companion. In grocery stores, she positions herself so
she can see exits. At restaurants, she won't sit with her back to the door. She's aware of how this
looks, how it marks her as damaged, but she can't seem to override the part of her brain that
insists danger is imminent. Her therapist calls it "threat detection gone awry," but that clinical
phrase doesn't capture the suffocating exhaustion of always scanning, always preparing for the next
terrible thing.

What troubles Sarah most is the guilt. She survived when others didn't. Marcus, who pulled her down,
suffered a traumatic brain injury and will never play again. She wakes in the night thinking about
his wife, about the families of those who died, and the guilt sits on her chest like a stone.

Music was her language before words, her way of making sense of the world. Now, even listening to
recordings feels like a betrayal—a reminder of what she's lost and what she can't seem to reclaim.
She doesn't know how to be Sarah without being a musician. And she doesn't know how to be a musician
anymore.
