# Priya's Story

Priya sits in the therapy office with her hands folded in her lap, her fingers working against each
other in a familiar pattern—the same motion she uses when threading needles at her sewing machine.
She is twenty-eight but carries herself with the careful restraint of someone much older. When she
speaks about her life before, her voice becomes quieter, as if she is afraid of taking up too much
space.

She came to the United States five years ago on a student visa, full of plans to study fashion
design. Instead, she became involved with a political activist group at university, drawn in by
friends who were passionate about workers' rights in the garment industry. Three years ago, during a
visit home to Bangladesh to see her family, she attended a protest rally. The police arrived without
warning. She was separated from her friends in the chaos, detained at a station, and held for four
days.

What happened during those four days lives in her body in ways she struggles to articulate. She
describes it in fragments: the cold concrete floor, the sound of other people crying in nearby
cells, the interrogator's voice asking the same questions over and over. There were beatings. There
was sleep deprivation. There was a particular kind of humiliation that she cannot yet name in
English, though the Bangla words for it emerge sometimes when she wakes at three in the morning
gasping.

She was released after her family paid a bribe. She returned to America and tried to resume her
life, but the life she had known no longer fit. She dropped out of university. The activist circles
felt unsafe now—she was afraid of being tracked, of putting others at risk. Eventually, she found
work in a tailor's shop, and something about the repetitive, solitary nature of the work felt
manageable. Her hands knew what to do. She could control the fabric, the stitches, the finished
product.

Her husband, Ravi, whom she married two years ago, describes her as "distant." He is not wrong. She
experiences their intimacy with a kind of dread, even though she loves him. Her body does not feel
like her own in the way it once did. When he touches her unexpectedly, she flinches. She has stopped
sleeping in their bedroom most nights, instead falling asleep on the couch in front of the
television, where she can see the door. She tells herself it is because she does not want to disturb
him with her nightmares, but the truth is more complicated.

At the tailor shop, Priya is meticulous and reliable. Her boss trusts her with the most delicate
work—wedding saris, formal wear, pieces that require precision and care. But some days, the
fluorescent lights overhead trigger a sensation of being trapped. The sound of the door chime makes
her jump. She has started arriving earlier and earlier, working in the quiet hours before anyone
else arrives, when she can control her environment completely.

She experiences intrusive images without warning: a face, a voice, a smell that sends her back to
that detention cell. Sometimes these images arrive while she is measuring fabric or pinning a hem,
and her hands begin to shake. She has pricked her fingers so many times recently that Ravi has
noticed the small scars accumulating on her fingertips. She tells him she is clumsy, which is a lie.
Her body is trying to communicate something her mind cannot yet say.

Priya wants to feel normal again. She wants to sleep beside her husband without fear. She wants to
work without her nervous system hijacking her thoughts. But mostly, she wants to understand why she
cannot seem to move forward, why the past insists on living so loudly in her present.
