# Marcus

Marcus is a twenty-nine-year-old painter who works primarily on residential and commercial
properties throughout the city. He has a steady hand, an eye for detail, and a reputation for
showing up on time. His wife, Priya, works in accounting, and they've been married for three years.
They live in the neighborhood where Marcus grew up, where his parents still live two blocks away.

Eighteen months ago, Marcus was leaving a job site on a Friday evening around 6 p.m. He'd finished
painting the interior of a medical office building and was walking to his truck, carrying his
equipment bag and the day's earnings in cash—about eight hundred dollars. Three men approached him
from behind near the parking garage entrance. Before he could process what was happening, one of
them had him by the collar while another demanded his wallet and the bag. When Marcus hesitated for
just a moment, he was shoved hard against the concrete wall. His head struck the edge of a metal
railing. There was blood, shouting, the sound of his own breathing. Then they were gone, running
with his things.

The physical injuries healed within weeks—a concussion, some bruising, a few stitches. But something
else didn't heal.

For the first few months after the incident, Marcus told himself he was fine. He returned to work,
though he found himself choosing jobs in different neighborhoods, farther from where it happened. He
told Priya he was just diversifying his client base. But the truth was more complicated. His chest
would tighten when he saw three men together, even in completely ordinary circumstances. He started
leaving job sites earlier, before dusk, turning down lucrative evening projects. When he did have to
be out after dark, his shoulders stayed rigid, his jaw clenched. He'd replay the moment of impact
against the wall over and over, sometimes while painting, sometimes while lying in bed next to
Priya, who would wake to find him drenched in sweat.

The hypervigilance has become part of his daily rhythm now. When he's at a job site, he positions
himself so his back is never to a door or window. He notices exits compulsively. In grocery stores
or anywhere public, he scans faces, calculating distances, identifying potential threats. His mind
does this automatically now, without his permission. He's aware it's excessive—he knows the odds of
lightning strike and all that—but knowing doesn't stop the vigilance.

What troubles him most is how it's affecting his work and his marriage. He's turned down so many
jobs that his income has dropped significantly. Priya tries to be understanding, but he can see the
worry in how she watches him. Sometimes she'll reach for him in the dark and he'll flinch violently,
his body already prepared for violence. He feels ashamed of this, ashamed that he can't just move
past it like he moves past a difficult paint job.

He's also begun avoiding the neighborhood entirely. He takes a longer route home to avoid the
parking garage. He's made excuses not to visit his parents, which has hurt them and confused Priya.
The area that once felt like home now feels like a place where something fundamental about his
safety was shattered.

Marcus hasn't told anyone at work about what happened. He's a man who solves problems with his hands
and his skill, and he's not sure how to solve this one. He comes home tired in a way that sleep
doesn't fix, and he wonders sometimes if this is just who he is now.
