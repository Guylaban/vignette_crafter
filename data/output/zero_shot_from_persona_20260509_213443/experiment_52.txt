# A Life in Fragments

Priya tends to her fields each morning as she has for forty-three years, but the woman moving
through the rows of wheat is not entirely the same woman who walked these paths before. Her hands
still know the work—the soil, the seeds, the rhythm of seasons—yet something essential has
fractured, and the pieces no longer fit together as they once did.

The assault happened three years ago, in the late afternoon, when she had stayed behind to repair
the irrigation line near the eastern boundary of her land. She was sixty-eight then, a widow for
five years, and accustomed to managing the farm alone. A man from the neighboring village, someone
she had seen at market but did not know, emerged from the tree line. What followed lasted perhaps
fifteen minutes, though Priya experiences those minutes as if they are still happening, layered
beneath every moment of her present life.

She did not tell her family for six months. Her son noticed her withdrawn silence, the way she
flinched when he touched her shoulder, how she had stopped attending the weekly gatherings at her
sister's house. When her daughter-in-law finally asked directly, Priya's hands had trembled so
violently she could not hold the cup of tea she was offered. The confession came out in fragments,
the words catching in her throat like something broken.

Now, each morning before dawn, Priya wakes with her heart already racing. Her body knows the
time—4:47 a.m.—when the assault began. She lies beside her sleeping husband, a man she has been
married to for forty-two years, and feels entirely alone. When he reaches for her in the darkness,
even in tenderness, she becomes rigid, her breath shallow. He has learned not to touch her. This
knowledge sits between them like another person in the bed.

The fields remain her anchor and her torment. She cannot stop working them—the land is her identity,
her independence, her purpose—but each time she walks to the eastern boundary, her vision narrows
and her legs become unsteady. Twice, she has collapsed near the irrigation line. Her son now insists
on accompanying her, which brings its own shame, a grown man watching his mother as though she
cannot be trusted alone. She resents this, even as she needs it.

Loud sounds ambush her without warning. The sound of a motorcycle, a man's raised voice, the crack
of a branch—each one sends her nervous system into full alarm. Three weeks ago, a farmhand dropped a
metal tool, and Priya found herself on the ground, gasping, unable to remember how she fell. She has
stopped eating properly. Her clothes hang loose on her frame, and her skin has taken on a grayish
quality.

Sleep is a battlefield. When she finally manages to rest, the dreams return her to that moment with
a cruelty that feels personal. She wakes gasping, sometimes crying out, and her husband lies awake
beside her in the darkness, both of them pretending the other is asleep. She has stopped going to
the temple. She cannot bear the weight of other people's eyes, the questions that hover unspoken,
the whispered conversations that pause when she enters a room.

The worst part is not the fear or the nightmares or the way her body has become a stranger to her.
The worst part is that she knows this is her life now—not the life she had before, but the life she
will have until she dies. The fields continue. The seasons turn. And Priya moves through it all,
present but somehow always somewhere else, watching herself live from a great distance.
