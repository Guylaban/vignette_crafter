# Marcus

Marcus tends his olive groves in the early morning, when the light is soft and the world feels
manageable. He has done this for thirty-two years, since he was a young man, and the rhythm of the
seasons has always steadied him. Now, at fifty-five, he finds himself waking at 4 a.m. regardless of
when he falls asleep, his heart already racing before his eyes open. He lies beside his wife Fatima
in the darkness, waiting for the panic to subside enough that he can move without waking her.

Three years ago, on an ordinary Tuesday in late spring, Marcus was closing up the equipment shed
when three men approached him. He didn't recognize them. What began as a demand for money escalated
quickly into violence. They beat him with a metal pipe. He remembers the sound more than the pain—a
hollow *thunk* that seemed to come from outside his body, as though it were happening to someone
else. When one of them raised the pipe again, Marcus covered his head and thought he might die in
his own shed, surrounded by the tools he'd used to build his livelihood.

He survived. The police never found them.

Since then, Marcus has become a different version of himself, though he struggles to articulate
exactly how. He still goes to the groves—he has no choice; the land doesn't care about his fear—but
the work that once brought him quiet satisfaction now feels like a series of threats. Every sound
makes him turn sharply. Last month, his nephew came up behind him to ask about pruning schedules,
and Marcus swung around with such violence that he nearly struck the boy. The shame of that moment
sits in his chest like a stone.

Fatima watches him carefully now. She notices that he no longer takes the longer route through the
village to reach the groves; instead, he drives the longer way around, avoiding the main road where
the assault happened. She sees how his eyes move constantly when they're in public—scanning faces,
cataloging exits, assessing threat. When they go to the market together, he positions himself
between her and other people, his shoulders rigid. He tells her it's just habit, but they both know
better.

At night, he dreams of the shed. In the dreams, the pipe connects. He wakes gasping, sometimes
calling out, and Fatima holds his hand until his breathing steadies. He's started sleeping in the
chair by the window instead, telling himself it's because his back hurts. The truth is he needs to
see outside, needs to know what's coming.

The physical injuries healed months ago. But Marcus carries the assault differently now—not in his
body, but in how his body moves through the world. He's become hypervigilant, exhausted, irritable
with the hired workers over small mistakes. He snaps at Fatima over nothing, then withdraws into
silence, ashamed of his own anger. He's lost weight. The doctor says his blood pressure is
dangerously high.

He hasn't told most people what happened. It feels like weakness, like failure—a man should be able
to work his own land safely. But the groves no longer feel like his. They feel like a place where
danger lives, where he is always waiting for the next blow. Some mornings, standing among the olive
trees he planted as a younger man, Marcus feels like a ghost haunting his own life, present but not
quite alive, going through the motions of a man who still belongs to himself.
