# Marcus

Marcus is an eighteen-year-old project manager at a mid-sized construction firm, a position that
surprised even him when he landed it straight out of secondary school. He's articulate and
organized, the kind of person who keeps detailed spreadsheets and thinks three steps ahead. His
girlfriend, Anahera, says he's thoughtful and reliable. But there's a brittleness beneath the
competence that becomes apparent as he talks about his childhood.

For most of his early years, Marcus lived in a small house with his parents and younger sister. His
father worked in logistics; his mother was a nurse. On the surface, they looked like any other
family in their Auckland suburb. But starting around age seven, Marcus became hyperaware of his
father's moods. He learned to read the shift in his father's voice when he came home from work, the
particular way he'd pour his first drink. He learned which nights his mother would try to placate
him and which nights she'd push back. Those nights were the worst.

Marcus remembers the sounds more than anything else—his mother's voice rising, his father's roar,
the crash of something breaking. He remembers being eleven years old, standing in the hallway with
his sister, both of them frozen. His father had his mother pinned against the kitchen counter. The
violence wasn't constant, but it was unpredictable enough that Marcus developed an almost
preternatural ability to sense danger. He could walk into a room and know immediately whether it was
safe.

His parents separated when he was thirteen. His mother got custody, and life became quieter. Marcus
threw himself into school and then into work. He's good at projects because he can control
variables, anticipate problems, manage people. At eighteen, he's already been promoted once.

But the control is fragile.

Recently, during a meeting with his team, one of his senior contractors raised his voice about a
delayed shipment. It wasn't aggressive—just frustrated—but something in Marcus's body didn't
register that distinction. His heart rate spiked. His hands went cold. He found himself unable to
speak, unable to think clearly. He excused himself and sat in his car for twenty minutes, breathing
slowly, waiting for his body to believe it was safe. That night, he couldn't sleep. He kept
replaying the moment, analyzing what the contractor had said, wondering if he'd overreacted,
wondering if he was losing his mind.

The hypervigilance has intensified. At work, he monitors his team's emotional states obsessively. He
notices when voices get slightly louder, when someone's jaw tightens. He's started arriving early to
meetings to assess the room before anyone else arrives. He's begun avoiding situations where
conflict might emerge, which is increasingly difficult in project management. He's made two
decisions recently to back down from necessary confrontations because the thought of someone's anger
was unbearable.

His sleep is fragmented. He lies awake listening to the sounds of his apartment building—neighbors
moving around, traffic outside—and his mind races through worst-case scenarios. What if his
girlfriend gets angry with him? What if he's not managing his team well enough? What if something
bad happens and he doesn't see it coming?

With Anahera, he's become more withdrawn. She's noticed. She asked him last week if he was okay, and
he said yes automatically, the way he's learned to do. But he's not sure he knows how to be okay
anymore. The eighteen-year-old project manager who seems so capable is, in many moments, still that
eleven-year-old boy listening for danger in the dark.
