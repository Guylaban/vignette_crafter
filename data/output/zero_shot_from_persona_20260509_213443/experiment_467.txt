# Leila's Story

Leila sits in the tutoring center's quiet corner where she's worked for the past eight years,
organizing worksheets with the same meticulous care she brings to everything. At seventy-seven, she
moves with deliberate slowness, her reading glasses hanging from a beaded chain. But something about
the way she pauses when the fluorescent lights flicker—the way her hands still for just a
moment—suggests an internal world far more turbulent than her composed exterior reveals.

She came to this country forty-two years ago, leaving behind a city that no longer existed as she
had known it. In 1982, Leila was thirty-five years old, teaching literature at a secondary school in
Beirut. She had never wanted to leave. She loved her students, loved the particular way afternoon
light fell through her classroom windows, loved the rhythm of her life. Then the war arrived in a
way that made leaving the only option.

The day she still cannot speak about directly—though fragments escape in our conversations—involved
her apartment building. She had stepped out to buy bread. When she returned, the street had
transformed into something unrecognizable. The building where she had lived for twelve years was
partially collapsed. She stood there for hours, she tells me, unable to move. She never found most
of her belongings. She never found her neighbor Mrs. Haddad, who always brought her homemade jam.

Now, decades later, Leila teaches remedial reading to middle schoolers and adults seeking their GED.
Her students adore her—she is patient, encouraging, never condescending. But they do not know that
she sometimes cannot concentrate on their essays because of a sound. Any sound that resembles the
particular pitch of sirens sends her into a state she describes as "not quite panic, but like my
body is remembering something my mind refuses to think about."

She lives alone in a small apartment, keeps minimal possessions, and has never fully unpacked
certain boxes, even after four decades. She sleeps poorly, often waking at 3 a.m. with her heart
racing, unsure whether she was dreaming or remembering. When students ask about her accent or where
she's from, she offers vague answers and quickly redirects the conversation. The effort of
maintaining this boundary exhausts her.

What troubles her most is the guilt—a weight she carries without naming it directly. She survived
when others did not. She has a comfortable life now, which somehow feels like a betrayal of those
who do not. When she hears news about conflicts anywhere in the world, she becomes withdrawn for
days, barely speaking to colleagues, moving through her tutoring sessions mechanically. She has
turned down invitations to social gatherings, convinced that her presence would dampen others' joy.

Recently, a student mentioned a family member who had experienced war, and Leila found herself
crying in the supply closet for twenty minutes. She could not explain it to her supervisor. How
could she explain that hearing someone else's story of displacement had cracked open something she
has spent forty-two years carefully containing?

She does not think of herself as someone with a problem. She thinks of herself as someone who
survived, and survival, she believes, should not require explanation or intervention. Yet she
notices how the world feels simultaneously too loud and too fragile, how trust feels like a luxury
she cannot quite afford, and how the distance between her past and present sometimes collapses
without warning, leaving her stranded in a moment that refuses to stay buried.
