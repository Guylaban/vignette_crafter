# Marcus

Marcus sits in the waiting room with his hands folded tightly in his lap, a habit he's developed
over the past three years. At forty-one, he carries himself with the careful precision of someone
who designs buildings for a living—shoulders squared, posture deliberate—yet there's a brittleness
to his composure that suggests the structure might crack at any moment.

His marriage to David lasted eight years before everything fractured. The relationship had started
with the intensity that Marcus now recognizes as a warning sign, though he didn't see it that way
then. David was charming, attentive, and within months had become the center of Marcus's world.
Marcus, who had spent his twenties building his architectural practice and his thirties establishing
himself as a respected designer in his firm, suddenly found himself reorganizing his life around
another person.

The first time David hit him, it was over something small—Marcus had forgotten to pick up groceries.
The blow came from behind, a shove that sent him into the kitchen counter. Marcus remembers the
shock more than the pain, the disbelief that this had actually happened. He told himself it was an
anomaly, a moment of stress that wouldn't repeat. David apologized profusely, brought flowers,
promised it would never happen again.

But it did happen again. The incidents escalated over two years—sometimes triggered by perceived
slights, sometimes by nothing Marcus could identify. A late meeting at work. A conversation with a
colleague. The tone of Marcus's voice. The violence became more deliberate, more targeted. David
learned what hurt most, both physically and psychologically, and he wielded that knowledge with
precision. Marcus found himself walking on invisible eggshells, constantly monitoring his behavior,
his words, his very presence in the apartment they shared.

The final incident sent Marcus to the emergency room with a fractured rib and a concussion. He told
the medical staff he'd fallen down the stairs. It was only after David's arrest—a neighbor had
finally called police—that Marcus began to understand what had happened to him.

That was three years ago. He's been divorced for two, and he's been married to Priya for eighteen
months. Priya is patient, kind, and confused by the man she married.

Marcus still flinches when Priya raises her voice, even in jest. His sleep is fractured—he wakes at
2 or 3 a.m., his heart racing, sometimes unable to remember where he is. At the architectural firm,
he's become hypervigilant about his relationships with colleagues, interpreting neutral feedback as
criticism, convinced that people are watching him, judging him. He's turned down two promotion
opportunities because the increased visibility felt unbearable.

The intrusive images come without warning. He'll be reviewing blueprints and suddenly see David's
face, feel the phantom sensation of hands around his throat. Last week, during a client
presentation, he had what he now understands was a panic attack—his vision narrowed, his breathing
became shallow, and he had to excuse himself from the conference room.

Priya has gently suggested therapy. She doesn't understand why he seems to be moving backward rather
than forward, why the safety she's offered hasn't healed him. Marcus doesn't know how to explain
that his body hasn't received the message that he's safe now, that the threat has passed. His
nervous system remains convinced that danger is imminent, that he must remain vigilant, that trust
is a luxury he cannot afford.

He's here because Priya asked him to be, and because somewhere beneath the hypervigilance and the
nightmares, Marcus knows that the careful structures he's built—both professional and personal—are
beginning to collapse under the weight of what he cannot process alone.
