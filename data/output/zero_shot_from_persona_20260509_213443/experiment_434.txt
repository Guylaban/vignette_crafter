# A Paramedic's Vigilance

Sarah sits in the therapy office with her shoulders slightly hunched, her fingers tracing the edge
of her coffee cup though she hasn't taken a sip. At thirty-one, she carries herself with the
practiced efficiency of someone trained to manage crises, yet there's a brittleness underneath—a
readiness that never quite settles.

She's been a paramedic for eight years, and until eighteen months ago, she loved the work. She loved
the immediacy of it, the clear purpose of showing up for people in their worst moments. Her ex-
husband used to joke that she was married to her job, and looking back, she thinks he was probably
right. The ambulance felt safer than home.

The incident happened on a routine call to a domestic disturbance in a neighborhood she'd worked a
hundred times before. Sarah and her partner arrived to find a man in crisis—intoxicated, agitated,
threatening his girlfriend with a knife. Police were already there. What should have been
straightforward became chaotic when the man lunged toward the officers. Sarah was standing nearby,
assessing the girlfriend for injuries, when one officer opened fire. The man fell. The girlfriend
screamed. And then there was the moment Sarah will never quite shake: the officer who fired the
shots turned sharply, weapon still raised, and for what felt like an eternity but was probably two
seconds, she wasn't sure if he saw her as a paramedic or as a threat.

She wasn't hit. No one else was hurt beyond the man who'd been shot. By every objective measure, she
did her job—she stabilized him, got him to the hospital, filled out her paperwork. But something
fractured that day in a way that doesn't show up on any scan.

The first months were strange. Sarah found herself replaying the moment obsessively—the officer's
face, the angle of the gun, the split second of uncertainty. She'd be at home and suddenly her heart
would race, convinced she'd heard a gunshot. She stopped sleeping well. Her ex-husband noticed she
was drinking more, staying later at the station, finding excuses not to come home. He didn't
understand why she couldn't just move past it. Neither did she.

Now, she's still working, though everything about the job has changed. She approaches scenes
differently—hypervigilant, always scanning for threats that probably aren't there. Last month, a car
backfired near the ambulance bay and she dove behind the vehicle. Her partner pretended not to
notice. She's become the paramedic who always insists on waiting for police clearance, who positions
herself differently, who sometimes feels her hands shaking as she's trying to establish an IV. The
work that once felt purposeful now feels dangerous in a way she can't quite articulate to anyone who
wasn't there.

She's started having nightmares several times a week—not always about the shooting itself, but
variations where she's the one being aimed at, where she can't move, where she's responsible for
someone dying because she froze. She wakes gasping, drenched in sweat, and for a few disorienting
seconds she can't remember where she is.

Her marriage ended six months ago, though the shooting had already done most of the damage. Her ex
tried to be supportive, but she couldn't let him close. She couldn't explain why she flinched when
he moved suddenly, or why she needed to know where all the exits were in every room. She still
doesn't know how to tell people that she's afraid—not of the man with the knife, but of authority,
of situations she can't control, of the moment when someone's intentions become unclear and
everything becomes life or death.

She continues to show up for her shifts. She continues to save lives. But part of her is always
still standing in that driveway, waiting to see which way the gun will turn.
