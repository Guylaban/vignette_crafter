# Maria's Storm

Maria sits in the waiting room ten minutes early for her appointment, as she always does. At fifty-
seven, she carries herself with the careful posture of someone who has spent thirty years teaching
others how to move through the world without pain. Her hands are steady as she fills out the intake
form, though there's a slight tremor when she writes her name.

Three years ago, Hurricane Beatriz made landfall near Maria's hometown on the coast. She had ridden
out storms before—she was born and raised in a region where hurricanes were simply part of life—but
Beatriz was different. The category 4 system stalled over the area for eighteen hours. Maria's small
house, where she'd lived alone since her divorce five years prior, sat only two miles from the
shore.

She remembers the sound more than anything. Not the wind itself, but the sound of her roof beginning
to separate from the walls, a groaning and splintering that seemed to come from inside her own body.
She'd huddled in the interior bathroom with her dog, pressing her back against the wall, listening
to her home being unmade around her. When a section of the roof finally gave way, rain and debris
poured into the hallway. She ran to her car with her dog and drove inland, her hands shaking so
badly she nearly missed the exit twice.

The house was largely destroyed. Her possessions, accumulated over decades, were scattered across
her neighborhood or simply gone. But Maria survived. Her dog survived. She tells herself this was
fortunate, and she means it, yet something in her doesn't quite believe it.

In the months after the hurricane, Maria returned to work at the physical therapy clinic where she'd
been employed for twenty-two years. Her colleagues were sympathetic but eager to move forward. Maria
threw herself into her work with familiar discipline. She spent her days helping patients regain
mobility after surgery, teaching them breathing techniques, encouraging them through the difficult
work of rehabilitation. She was good at it—patient, knowledgeable, genuinely invested in their
progress.

But something had shifted. During sessions, particularly when working with older patients or those
recovering from trauma-related injuries, Maria would occasionally find herself unable to focus. A
patient would describe the moment they fell or were injured, and Maria's chest would tighten.
Sometimes her hands would go cold. She'd excuse herself to the bathroom and stand at the sink,
running water over her wrists, waiting for her breathing to return to normal.

Sleep became unreliable. Maria would fall asleep without difficulty but wake at three or four in the
morning, her heart racing, her sheets damp with sweat. She couldn't remember the dreams, only the
feeling of being trapped, of something essential being taken from her. She began sleeping with her
bedroom door open, though she couldn't explain why this helped.

On rainy days, Maria finds herself hyperaware of the weather forecast. She checks it obsessively on
her phone, tracking storm systems that are still thousands of miles away. When the sky darkens
unexpectedly, she feels a surge of panic that her rational mind recognizes as disproportionate. Last
month, during a routine afternoon thunderstorm, she left work early without explanation.

What troubles Maria most is a creeping sense of unreality about her own resilience. She rebuilt her
house. She returned to work. She feeds her dog and pays her bills. From the outside, she has
recovered. Yet internally, she feels like someone who is performing the role of a functional person
while living in a persistent state of dread—waiting for the next thing to be taken away, unable to
quite believe in safety anymore.
