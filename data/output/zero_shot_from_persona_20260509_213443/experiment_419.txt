# Maya

Maya sits with her legs folded beneath her in the therapy chair, her posture the careful arrangement
of someone trained since childhood to occupy space with precision. At nineteen, she has the lean
musculature and postural awareness of a professional dancer—she's been dancing since she was four,
and by sixteen she was performing with a regional ballet company. She speaks about her life in the
present tense, as if narrating something happening right now, though the events she describes
occurred fourteen months ago.

The nightclub fire happened on a Saturday in March. Maya wasn't performing that night; she was there
with friends, celebrating the end of their winter season. She remembers the smell first—acrid,
chemical—before she understands what it is. The strobe lights make the smoke look like it's moving
in rhythm with the music. She remembers thinking, absurdly, that it was part of the show. Then
someone screams, and the spell breaks.

The evacuation is chaos. Maya's dancer's body becomes a liability rather than an asset in the crush
of people moving toward exits. She's small, compact, and gets separated from her friends almost
immediately. She finds herself pressed against a wall near the back of the building, unable to move
forward, unable to breathe properly. The smoke is thick now, and her eyes burn. She remembers
thinking she was going to die in a nightclub, which seemed like a stupid way to die, and then she
doesn't remember anything else clearly until she's outside, coughing on the pavement, her dress
singed on one side.

Eleven people died that night. Maya didn't know any of them personally, though she recognizes some
faces from the local dance community. She spent three days in the hospital for smoke inhalation and
minor burns on her shoulder. Physically, she healed quickly.

The rest has been slower.

Maya hasn't danced since the fire. She tells me this matter-of-factly, as if it's a simple statement
of fact, but her hands clench slightly as she says it. She tried, once, about two months after the
fire. She put on her rehearsal clothes, went to the studio, and made it through exactly four counts
before her heart started racing so violently she thought she was having a heart attack. The
fluorescent lights overhead suddenly felt like they were closing in. The mirrors reflected her
movements back at her in a way that felt threatening rather than instructive. She left and hasn't
been back.

Her sleep is fractured. She wakes at 2 or 3 a.m., drenched in sweat, sometimes from nightmares she
can't quite remember, sometimes from no identifiable dream at all. During the day, she's
hypervigilant—acutely aware of exits, of fire alarms, of the smell of anything burning. Her mother
mentioned cooking something with smoke last week, and Maya had a panic attack in her bedroom for
forty minutes.

She's started college, which was supposed to happen anyway, but she chose a program in business
administration rather than the dance degree she'd always planned. She tells me she's being
realistic, practical. When I ask what she misses most about dancing, her eyes fill with tears, and
she doesn't answer for a long time. Finally, she says, "I don't know who I am without it."

Maya sits very still in the chair, waiting for me to respond, her body held in that perfect dancer's
posture—controlled, contained, as if holding herself together tightly enough might prevent anything
else from falling apart.
