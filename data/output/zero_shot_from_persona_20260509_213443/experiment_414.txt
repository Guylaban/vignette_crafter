# Sarah's Story

Sarah sits in the clinic waiting room with her hands folded tightly in her lap, her shoulders drawn
up toward her ears. At twenty-five, she carries herself with the careful precision of someone who
has learned that control is survival. She's a registered nurse at the county hospital—a job she
loves, or loved, before everything changed.

Three years ago, Sarah was working as a volunteer nurse in a clinic in her family's home country, a
region caught in ongoing conflict. She had gone for six months, wanting to contribute something
meaningful, to use her training to help people she felt connected to. The clinic served a rural
community, and Sarah found the work deeply fulfilling despite the limited resources. She was good at
her job, calm under pressure, resourceful with what little they had.

On an ordinary Tuesday morning, armed conflict erupted near the clinic. What began as distant sounds
escalated rapidly. Within minutes, the clinic was receiving casualties—civilians caught in the
crossfire. Sarah and her colleagues moved into crisis mode, triaging patients, stopping bleeding,
doing everything they could with their meager supplies. Then a shell struck the clinic's outer wall.
The impact threw Sarah across the room. She remembers the sound more than anything else—a noise so
loud it seemed to obliterate everything else in the world.

When she regained awareness, she was on the floor, ears ringing, surrounded by dust and debris.
Several patients and one colleague were dead. Sarah had a fractured arm and multiple lacerations,
but she was functional. She kept working, moving through the next hours in a state of dissociation
she didn't recognize at the time as dissociation. She just remembers feeling distant from her own
body, watching herself bandage wounds and comfort dying people as though she were observing someone
else perform these actions.

She was evacuated two days later and eventually returned home to the United States.

Now, back at the county hospital where she works, Sarah finds herself struggling in ways she doesn't
fully understand. Loud noises—a crash in the supply room, a patient's urgent call button—send her
heart racing and her mind spinning back to that morning. She's become hypervigilant on the unit,
always listening, always scanning for danger. Her colleagues have noticed she's jumpy, that she
sometimes seems distant even when present.

Her sleep is fragmented. She wakes multiple times each night, sometimes drenched in sweat from
nightmares she can't quite remember. During the day, she feels exhausted but wired simultaneously—as
though her body is perpetually braced for impact. She's lost weight. Her relationship with her
partner has become strained; she finds herself irritable and withdrawn, unable to explain why she
sometimes feels angry at him without reason.

The hardest part is the guilt. She survived when others didn't. She keeps replaying the moments
before the impact, wondering if there was something she could have done differently, some way she
could have saved her colleague. Logically, she knows this isn't rational. Professionally, she
understands trauma. But knowing and feeling are different things entirely.

She's returned to work because she needs to, because she doesn't know who she is if she isn't a
nurse. But she's begun avoiding certain tasks—anything involving emergency situations makes her
chest tight with dread. She's considering leaving the profession entirely, and this thought
terrifies her more than anything else.
