# Marcus

Marcus sits in the leather chair across from me with his hands folded in his lap, a habit he's
developed over the past few weeks of our sessions. He's a tall man with salt-and-pepper hair,
dressed in the kind of carefully considered casual wear that suggests someone accustomed to
presenting himself professionally. His wife mentioned during the intake call that he hasn't been
back to his architectural firm in three months.

Two years ago, Marcus was working on-site at a mixed-use development downtown, reviewing structural
details with the general contractor. It was a Tuesday morning in late September. He was standing
near the loading dock when a delivery truck's brakes failed. The driver, a man Marcus had spoken
with several times over the project's duration, couldn't stop. Marcus watched from maybe thirty feet
away as the truck struck a young laborer—couldn't have been more than twenty-five—pinning him
against a steel beam. The image is still vivid in Marcus's mind: the shock on the man's face, the
sounds, the terrible stillness afterward.

The paramedics arrived quickly. The laborer survived, though with severe injuries. But Marcus didn't
witness the resolution. He left the site that day in a state of shock and never returned to that
project.

For the first few months, Marcus told himself he was fine. He went back to his office, to his home
office, drew plans, attended meetings. His wife, Jennifer, says he seemed distracted but functional.
Then came October, and the anniversary of the incident approached. That's when the nightmares
began—vivid, visceral replays where he's unable to warn the young man, unable to move, unable to
stop what's coming. He wakes at three or four in the morning, his heart racing, drenched in sweat.

Now, whenever Marcus drives past construction sites, his chest tightens. He finds himself scanning
for hazards, for things that could go wrong. When he's working on architectural designs, he gets
stuck on safety details, reworking specifications obsessively. His colleagues have noticed. One
asked if he was okay; another suggested he seemed "in his head" during a project review. Marcus
knows they're right. He's become hypervigilant about structural safety in a way that's begun to
paralyze his creative process.

The worst part, he tells me, is that he can't enjoy his work anymore. Architecture was never just a
job for him—it was how he made meaning. He loved the problem-solving, the responsibility of creating
spaces where people would live and work safely. Now, when he looks at his own designs, he sees only
the ways they could fail, the catastrophes waiting to happen. He's turned down two promising
projects in the past year because he couldn't shake the conviction that something would go wrong,
that someone would get hurt because of a detail he'd missed.

His marriage has strained under the weight of it. Jennifer tries to be supportive, but Marcus has
withdrawn. He doesn't want to talk about what happened. He's irritable in ways that surprise them
both—snapping at her over small things, then feeling overwhelming guilt. Their intimacy has nearly
disappeared. He lies awake beside her, ruminating, while she sleeps.

Marcus looks at his hands as he talks. He's intelligent, articulate, clearly trying to make sense of
something that doesn't make sense. He keeps saying he should be fine, that he wasn't the one hurt,
that he needs to just move past it. But his body hasn't gotten the message. His nervous system
remains locked in that moment on the loading dock, scanning endlessly for the next disaster.
