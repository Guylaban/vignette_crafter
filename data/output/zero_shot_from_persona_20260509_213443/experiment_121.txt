# Clinical Vignette: Amara

Amara is a twenty-one-year-old surgical resident at a teaching hospital in Lagos, Nigeria. She is
poised, articulate, and carries herself with the quiet confidence of someone who has always excelled
academically. Her parents are both physicians, and medicine has never felt like a choice for her—it
has always simply been the path forward. She speaks about her work with genuine passion, describing
the precision required in the operating theatre, the weight of responsibility, the satisfaction of a
successful procedure.

Two years ago, during the rainy season, a magnitude 6.1 earthquake struck the region without
warning. Amara was in the surgical ward of the teaching hospital where she was completing her
clinical rotations as a fourth-year medical student. The building swayed violently. She remembers
the sound first—a deep, terrible groaning of the structure itself—before she understood what was
happening. Equipment crashed. A surgical light fell from the ceiling, missing her by inches.
Patients screamed. A senior surgeon grabbed her arm and pulled her toward the doorway, shouting
instructions she couldn't fully process.

The building held, but the ward descended into chaos. Amara found herself helping to stabilize
patients, moving on instinct rather than training. For hours afterward, she assisted with emergency
procedures, her hands steady even as the ground continued to tremble with aftershocks. She tells
this part of the story matter-of-factly, as though recounting a particularly demanding shift.

What she struggles to articulate is what came after.

Now, nearly two years into her surgical residency, Amara experiences moments when the operating
theatre feels unbearably unsafe. During certain procedures, particularly longer ones where she
cannot easily leave the sterile field, her heart rate accelerates without warning. She becomes
hyperaware of the building around her—the sounds of the HVAC system, the subtle vibrations from
equipment, the structural integrity of the ceiling. Her mind generates catastrophic scenarios. She
finds herself mentally calculating exit routes, assessing which pieces of equipment might become
projectiles.

She hasn't told her attending physicians about this. The competitive surgical environment doesn't
accommodate vulnerability, and Amara has internalized the message that good surgeons simply perform,
regardless of what they feel. Instead, she manages through control. She arrives at the hospital
earlier than necessary. She checks the operating theatre herself before cases begin. She has
difficulty sleeping the night before scheduled surgeries, her mind cycling through worst-case
scenarios.

Outside the hospital, Amara has become increasingly isolated. Social gatherings feel intolerable—the
sensation of being enclosed indoors triggers the same creeping dread she experiences in the OR. She
has declined invitations from colleagues, from friends, from her family's dinner table. Her
apartment is on the ground floor of a low-rise building, chosen deliberately. She keeps her windows
open, even when the air conditioning would be more comfortable.

The worst moments come unexpectedly. A colleague dropped a metal tray last week, and Amara's entire
body went rigid. She excused herself from the room and spent twenty minutes in the bathroom,
breathing deliberately, waiting for her hands to stop shaking. She is aware that her reactions are
disproportionate to the triggers. This awareness itself is distressing.

Amara continues to work. She performs well technically. Her supervisors have noted no decline in her
competence. But she exists in a state of constant vigilance, her nervous system unable to fully
accept that the immediate danger has passed. The gap between the surgeon she appears to be and the
terrified young woman she feels like internally grows wider each day.
