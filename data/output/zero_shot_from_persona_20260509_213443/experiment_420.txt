# Marcus

Marcus is a fifty-seven-year-old man who has spent the last thirty years working as a private tutor,
primarily with high school students preparing for standardized tests. He's soft-spoken, methodical,
and genuinely invested in his students' success. His apartment walls are lined with thank-you cards
and college acceptance letters from former pupils. He lives alone, has maintained a small but steady
client base, and until recently, felt his life had achieved a kind of quiet stability.

Three years ago, Marcus attended a professional development conference in another city. On the final
evening, he accepted a drink from a colleague he'd known casually for years. Within an hour, he felt
disoriented—his vision blurred, his thoughts fragmentary. The man guided him to a hotel room and
assaulted him. Marcus remembers only pieces: the sound of the door locking, the weight of a hand
over his mouth, the overwhelming sense of powerlessness. He woke the next morning with bruises he
couldn't quite explain and a sickening certainty about what had happened.

He drove home in silence, showered repeatedly, and told no one. For the first few months, he
functioned. He prepared lesson plans, met with students, graded practice tests. But something had
fractured inside him that he couldn't quite locate or name.

Now, three years later, Marcus finds himself caught in patterns he doesn't fully understand. When a
new student arrives for tutoring—particularly if they're a young man—his body tightens. His hands
sweat. He positions himself carefully, always leaving space between them, always keeping the door
open, always aware of exits. He's lost several students because parents noticed his discomfort, his
stiffness, the way he seemed unable to make eye contact. He tells himself it's nothing personal, but
the shame of it compounds.

Sleep has become nearly impossible. He lies awake cataloging threats: the sound of footsteps in his
apartment building, a neighbor's raised voice, even the unexpected arrival of a delivery. When he
does sleep, he jolts awake gasping, his heart hammering, certain someone is in his room. He's
installed three deadbolts on his apartment door. He sleeps with his phone charged and nearby.

Alcohol has become a nightly ritual—two glasses of whiskey to quiet his mind enough to attempt
sleep. He tells himself it's just to take the edge off, but he notices now that he's drinking more,
that the bottle empties faster, that mornings have become harder.

In social situations, Marcus feels utterly alone. A colleague's casual hand on his shoulder sends
electricity through his nervous system. He avoids restaurants, coffee shops, anywhere he might
unexpectedly encounter his assailant, though logically he knows the man lives hundreds of miles
away. He's withdrawn from the tutoring community, declining invitations to professional gatherings.
His world has contracted to the familiar geography of his home and his students' homes—places he can
control.

What troubles Marcus most is the feeling that he's becoming someone unrecognizable. He's a man who
built his identity around patience, clarity, the ability to guide young people through difficult
material. Now he's hypervigilant, irritable, sometimes unable to concentrate on the very work that
has defined him. He catches himself snapping at students over minor mistakes, then feels waves of
guilt and shame. He's considered retiring early, though the thought of losing his work—the last
thing that makes him feel competent and useful—terrifies him more than staying.

He hasn't told anyone what happened. The secret sits inside him like something alive.
