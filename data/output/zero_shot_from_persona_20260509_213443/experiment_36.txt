# Catherine

Catherine sits in the waiting room with her hands folded in her lap, her posture ramrod straight
despite her sixty-five years. She's dressed carefully—pressed slacks, a cardigan buttoned to the
collar—the kind of deliberate presentation that suggests she's still used to maintaining standards,
still used to being responsible for things. When she speaks, her voice is measured and controlled,
though there's a tremor underneath that she seems determined to suppress.

Thirty-two years ago, Catherine was a medic in the military. She loved that work with a fierceness
that shaped everything that came after. She loved the problem-solving, the ability to act decisively
when others froze, the knowledge that her steady hands and quick thinking could mean the difference
between someone going home or not going home. She had a gift for it. Everyone said so.

The accident happened on a routine transport mission. Catherine was driving the supply truck,
navigating a mountain road she'd traveled dozens of times. It was early morning, the kind of clear
weather that made you feel like nothing bad could happen. She was thinking about her husband,
Michael, waiting for her back at base. They'd been married three years. They were planning to start
a family.

A vehicle coming around the curve lost control on black ice. Catherine saw it happening—saw the
moment the driver realized he couldn't stop—and she had maybe two seconds to react. She swerved hard
left. The vehicles collided anyway, a sickening crunch of metal and glass. Catherine's truck
flipped. She remembers the sensation of spinning, the world turning upside down, and then the smell
of gasoline.

What she remembers most vividly is the moment she regained consciousness and realized she was
trapped. Her legs were pinned beneath the dashboard. There was blood—so much blood—and she couldn't
tell if it was hers or from the other driver. For the first time in her professional life, Catherine
couldn't help. She could only wait. The waiting lasted four hours.

The rescue was successful. Catherine survived with two broken legs, a fractured pelvis, and injuries
that would take months to heal. The other driver died at the scene. Catherine learned this while
lying in a hospital bed, and something in her fractured along with her bones.

She returned to duty after rehabilitation, but she was changed. The control she'd prided herself on
felt like an illusion now. Driving became difficult—her hands would shake on the steering wheel, her
vision would narrow, and sometimes she'd have to pull over because her heart was racing so fast she
thought she might pass out. She left the military after another three years, unable to continue in a
role that had defined her.

Now, in her retirement, Catherine lives alone in a quiet house. Michael passed away eight years
ago—a heart attack, sudden and final, the kind of loss that somehow felt easier to bear than the
loss of herself she'd experienced decades earlier. She's learned to manage most things. She doesn't
drive much anymore. She has groceries delivered. She's organized her life around avoidance so
seamlessly that most people wouldn't notice.

But at night, she still wakes gasping, still feels the sensation of being trapped, still hears the
sound of metal crumpling. And in the quiet moments—when she's alone with her thoughts—she sometimes
wonders if the medic she was died in that truck, and if the woman sitting here now is just the ghost
of her, going through the motions of living.
