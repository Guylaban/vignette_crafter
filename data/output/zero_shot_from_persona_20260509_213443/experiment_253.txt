# Marcus

Marcus sits in the leather chair across from the therapist, his leg bouncing with a restless energy
that seems to have become permanent. At twenty-four, he has the polished appearance of someone who
works in finance—pressed shirt, carefully groomed—but there's a brittleness beneath the surface, a
sense that the presentation is something he maintains through sheer force of will.

He was deployed to Afghanistan for fourteen months. He served as a combat engineer, clearing routes,
identifying threats. The work was methodical, technical, important. He was good at it. When he talks
about those days now, his voice becomes flat, clinical, as if he's reading from a report rather than
describing his own life. On March 15th of last year, his team hit an IED while clearing a route near
Kandahar. Two soldiers were killed instantly. Marcus was thrown fifteen feet, sustained a fractured
tibia and shrapnel wounds to his left side. He remembers the smell—burning metal and something else
he can't quite name. He remembers the sound stopping, which was somehow worse than the noise.

He returned to the States nine months ago. The physical wounds healed relatively quickly. The Army
cleared him for discharge, and he moved back to his parents' home in Texas before landing a job as a
financial analyst at a mid-sized firm in Austin. His parents were relieved. A civilian job, a return
to normalcy. His mother still talks about how proud she is that he's "putting his life back
together."

What nobody talks about is that Marcus hasn't slept through a single night since he got home. He
lies in bed at 2 a.m., his heart racing, unable to remember the dream that jolted him awake but
unable to shake the conviction that something terrible is about to happen. He's tried
everything—melatonin, white noise machines, exhausting himself at the gym. Nothing works. By the
time he needs to be at the office, he's functioning on four or five hours of fragmented sleep, his
mind moving through spreadsheets and financial models like he's operating through water.

At work, he's hypervigilant in ways that don't make sense to his colleagues. He sits with his back
to the wall. He notices exits. When a colleague drops a stack of papers, Marcus's body goes rigid,
his hand instinctively moving to his side where his rifle used to be. He's gotten better at covering
the reaction, at smoothing his face back into neutrality, but the effort exhausts him.

Loud noises are the worst. A car backfire sends him into a panic that takes hours to settle.
Fireworks season is coming, and he's already dreading it. He's been avoiding social situations—his
buddy from high school invited him to a concert, but Marcus couldn't face the crowd, the
unpredictability, the noise. His parents have stopped asking him to family gatherings because his
presence seems to dampen everyone's mood. He stands at the periphery, unable to engage, watching.

He's started having intrusive thoughts about the explosion, moments when he's sitting at his desk
analyzing quarterly earnings and suddenly he's back in the desert, the heat pressing down on him,
the smell overwhelming. He'll come back to himself in the conference room with his manager staring
at him, asking if he's okay. He always says yes. He's always okay.

But he's not okay. He knows this. And somewhere beneath the numbness and the hypervigilance, there's
a part of him that's starting to wonder if he'll ever be okay again.
