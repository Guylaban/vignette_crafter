# Kaia

Kaia sits in the therapy office with her hands folded tightly in her lap, her calloused
fingers—marked by years of mending nets and hauling lines—working against each other in small,
repetitive movements. At nineteen, she carries herself with the weathered composure of someone much
older, though her eyes betray a restlessness that hasn't settled since last summer.

She grew up on the water. Her father taught her to fish when she was barely tall enough to hold a
rod, and by her mid-teens, she was working alongside him on his commercial vessel, the same boat his
father had worked. The ocean was her inheritance, her livelihood, her identity. She was good at
it—strong, quick, unafraid of the work's physical demands or its unpredictability.

On a morning in July that she can still see with perfect clarity, Kaia and her father were checking
their nets about two kilometers from shore. The weather had been cooperative all week. The water was
calm. Her father was in a good mood, joking about the size of the catch they might bring in. Kaia
remembers this detail precisely because it was the last thing she heard him say before the larger
commercial vessel appeared, moving faster than it should have been moving, its trajectory wrong.
There was no time to radio a warning, no time to do anything but watch.

The impact happened in slow motion and all at once. The sound—she still hears it in quiet moments, a
grinding crunch that doesn't match any noise she'd ever encountered. Her father was thrown. She
watched him go into the water. She watched the larger vessel's hull slide across the space where
he'd been standing. She was screaming, though she doesn't remember deciding to scream. She was in
the water, then somehow back on the boat, then the coast guard was there, and her father was not.

That was fourteen months ago. They never recovered his body.

Kaia returned to fishing six weeks after the funeral. Her mother had begged her not to, but Kaia
felt pulled back to the boat as though by an undertow she couldn't resist. She tells herself it's
because her family needs the income, because fishing is what she knows, because abandoning it would
mean abandoning him. But the truth is more complicated than that.

Now, when she's out on the water, her body exists in a state of constant vigilance. Every sound
makes her scan the horizon for approaching vessels. She flinches at the radio chatter from other
boats. Last month, a fishing line got tangled around her ankle, and she panicked so severely that
her boyfriend—who'd come along that day—had to cut her free while she gasped for air on the deck,
convinced she was going to be pulled overboard. She knows, logically, that the probability of
another accident is statistically low. Her body doesn't believe this.

The nights are harder in some ways. She sleeps poorly, her mind replaying the morning in
variations—sometimes she saves him, sometimes she goes in the water instead, sometimes the impact is
different but equally final. She wakes with her heart racing, sheets soaked with sweat, and it takes
her several minutes to remember where she is. Her boyfriend tries to comfort her, but she often
can't stand to be touched when she first wakes. She feels guilty about this, about how her fear is
becoming his burden.

She still goes out on the boat three or four days a week. She still mends nets and reads weather
patterns and knows the value of a good catch. But there's a part of her that hasn't left the water
on that July morning, a part that's still watching, still waiting, still unable to believe that
something so ordinary turned into something so final.
