# Margaret's Careful World

Margaret sits in the consultation room with her hands folded precisely in her lap, the way she
arranges everything in her life now—with intention and control. At fifty-seven, she maintains the
professional bearing of someone who has spent three decades designing buildings, creating spaces
where others feel safe. The irony isn't lost on her.

Four years ago, her daughter Emma was hit by a car while crossing the street near her university
campus. Margaret wasn't there. She was in a meeting, reviewing blueprints for a community center,
when her phone began to ring. The police officer's voice on the other end remains crystalline in her
memory—every word, every pause, preserved with perfect architectural precision.

Emma survived. She walks with a slight limp now and experiences chronic pain, but she survived.
Margaret should feel grateful. She does feel grateful. But gratitude and trauma, she's learned, can
occupy the same space without canceling each other out.

The first months after the accident, Margaret functioned on adrenaline and necessity. Emma needed
her—physical therapy appointments, medication management, emotional support through the depression
that followed the injury. Margaret arranged her practice around her daughter's needs, delegating
projects, working from home. She was useful. She had a purpose. She didn't sleep more than three
hours at night, but sleep felt like an indulgence anyway.

Now, years later, Emma is rebuilding her life. She's back at university, living in her own
apartment, moving toward independence. This should be when Margaret relaxes. Instead, the
hypervigilance has metastasized into her everyday existence.

She finds herself researching accident statistics compulsively, reading about pedestrian safety,
memorizing crosswalk protocols. When Emma texts late, Margaret's heart rate spikes before she reads
the message. She imagines scenarios—a fall down stairs, a delayed reaction from old injuries,
infections that might have been missed. Her mind generates catastrophes with the same precision she
once used to generate architectural renderings.

At work, Margaret has become meticulous to the point of paralysis. She checks and rechecks
structural calculations that her junior architects have already verified. She requests additional
safety reports for projects that pose no unusual risk. A colleague gently suggested she might be
overcompensating. Margaret heard it as an accusation that she'd failed to protect Emma once; she
won't fail again.

Her ex-husband says she's become controlling with Emma, that she calls too often, asks too many
questions about Emma's daily movements. Margaret knows he's right. She can see it happening—the way
Emma's voice tightens when she calls, the increasingly brief responses to her texts—but she cannot
seem to stop. The need to know where Emma is, what she's doing, whether she's safe, feels as
essential as breathing.

At night, Margaret lies awake and replays the accident in her mind, but with variations. Sometimes
Emma doesn't see the car in time. Sometimes the injuries are worse. Sometimes Margaret is there,
watching it happen, unable to stop it. She wakes gasping, her chest tight, her hands shaking. She
gets up and checks that her apartment doors are locked, that her phone is charged, that nothing has
changed in the darkness.

She's become a woman who designs safe spaces for others while living in a fundamentally unsafe world
in her own mind. The contradiction exhausts her. Margaret knows intellectually that she cannot
control outcomes, cannot predict or prevent all harm. But knowing and believing are different things
entirely, and she exists in the widening gap between them.
