# Margaret

Margaret sits in the therapy office with her hands folded in her lap, her posture rigid despite the
comfortable chair. At seventy-nine, she carries herself with the careful precision of someone who
has spent a lifetime measuring twice and cutting once. Her silver hair is pinned back neatly, and
she wears a cardigan she has clearly mended herself—the stitching is invisible, perfect.

She begins to speak about the war almost immediately, as if she has been waiting decades for someone
to ask. In 1944, at twenty-three, Margaret was a nurse with the British Army. She landed in Normandy
three weeks after D-Day and worked in a field hospital near the front lines. The sounds come back to
her first when she describes it—the screaming, the endless screaming, the way the tent flaps would
shake from the artillery fire. She remembers the smell, which she says she can still detect
sometimes when she's working with certain fabrics, particularly wool. She remembers a young soldier
whose legs had been shattered, how she held his hand while the surgeon amputated, how he kept asking
for his mother.

Margaret's hands shake slightly as she talks about this boy. She never learned his name.

After the war, she came home to London and learned tailoring. She needed something precise,
something where measurements meant something, where a quarter-inch mattered and could be controlled.
She married Robert, a quiet man who worked in accounting, and they had two children. For sixty
years, she ran a small tailoring shop from her home, taking commissions from regular clients,
mending coats and trousers with the same meticulous care she once applied to bandaging wounds.
Robert died eight years ago.

Now she lives alone, and the war has come back to live with her.

It started gradually. A loud noise—a car backfiring, a door slamming—and suddenly she is not in her
sitting room but in that tent. Her heart races. Her breath becomes shallow. Last month, a client
came to pick up a jacket, and when she knocked on the door, Margaret found herself on the floor,
unable to remember how she got there. She had been measuring a hem. The knock had transported her
elsewhere entirely.

She has stopped taking new clients. Her hands shake now, not from age but from a constant low-level
vigilance that exhausts her. She sleeps poorly, waking at three or four in the morning with her
nightgown soaked through with sweat. In these small hours, she lies awake cataloging the names of
patients she does remember—trying to honor them somehow by not forgetting. But mostly she remembers
the one boy whose name she never knew.

The worst part, she says, is the guilt. She feels guilty for surviving when so many didn't. She
feels guilty that she cannot do her work anymore, that her hands betray her. She feels guilty that
Robert is gone and she is still here, still carrying this weight. Sometimes she catches herself
holding her breath, and she realizes she has been waiting for something terrible to happen, the way
she waited in that tent for the next wave of casualties.

She has a daughter who worries about her, who suggests medication and therapy. Margaret came because
her daughter insisted. She is not sure anyone can help with something that happened so long ago,
something that is simply part of who she is now. But she comes, week after week, and she talks about
the war, and her hands shake in her lap, and she tries to explain what it means to survive when
survival itself feels like a betrayal.
