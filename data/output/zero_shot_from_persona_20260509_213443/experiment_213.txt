# Maria's Vigilance

Maria sits in the chair with her shoulders drawn up toward her ears, a posture she's held so
consistently over the past three years that the muscles across her upper back have become
permanently knotted. At thirty-nine, she looks older—not from wrinkles, but from the particular
exhaustion that comes from never quite letting your body rest. She speaks about her work as a park
ranger in the national reserve with the same careful attention she once gave to managing supplies in
a field clinic during the conflict in South Sudan.

Seven years ago, Maria took a leave from her ranger position to work with an international relief
organization. She had wanted to do something meaningful, something that mattered. The reality of the
camp was nothing like her training had prepared her for. She spent eighteen months providing basic
medical care and coordinating resources in a setting where resources never seemed adequate and the
need was infinite. She remembers the heat, the dust that got into everything, the sound of gunfire
at night that made the canvas walls of the clinic seem impossibly thin. She remembers a girl, maybe
twelve, who came in with shrapnel wounds. Maria held her hand while a surgeon worked. The girl
survived, but Maria has never forgotten the grip of those small fingers.

When Maria returned home, everyone said she was brave. She smiled and agreed and tried to slip back
into her old life. For a while, it seemed to work. She returned to the ranger program, married her
long-time partner James, and tried to focus on the familiar rhythms of the forest and the seasons.
But something had shifted inside her, something she couldn't quite name.

Now, four years later, Maria describes a persistent state of alert that has become her baseline.
When she's out on the trails—work she once loved—she finds herself scanning the tree line with the
same vigilance she once used to monitor the horizon for approaching vehicles or armed groups. Her
heart rate spikes at unexpected sounds: a branch snapping, a hiker's raised voice, even the backfire
of a truck. These moments send her mind immediately to worst-case scenarios. She's learned that if
she doesn't consciously redirect her thoughts, she'll spend the next hour imagining disaster.

Sleep has become a negotiation. Maria goes to bed around nine, but her mind doesn't quiet until well
after midnight. She lies beside James, acutely aware of every sound in the house, every shift in the
darkness. When she does sleep, she often finds herself back in the clinic—not remembering the dreams
exactly, but waking with her heart racing and her sheets soaked with sweat. Several times a week,
she jolts awake at 3 a.m., convinced something is wrong, and has to consciously talk herself back
down.

She's withdrawn from the hiking groups she used to lead. She tells James it's because she wants to
focus on her solo patrols, but the truth is more complicated. Being around other people requires a
kind of social performance that exhausts her. She finds herself irritable with visitors, snapping at
James over small things, then feeling guilty and distant. She's become hyperaware of her own
reactions—how quickly she moves to anger, how hard it is to feel genuinely present in conversations.

What Maria struggles to articulate is the fundamental shift in how she experiences the world. The
forest that once felt like home now feels like a place where something could happen. Not might
happen—could happen. The distinction matters to her, though she can't quite explain why. She knows
logically that the national reserve is safe, that the likelihood of violence here is infinitesimal.
But her body doesn't seem to believe what her mind knows to be true.
