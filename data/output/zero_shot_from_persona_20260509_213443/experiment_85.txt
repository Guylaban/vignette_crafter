# Sarah's Story

Sarah sits in the therapy office with her laptop bag still slung over her shoulder, as though she
might need to leave at any moment. At forty, she maintains the composed exterior of someone
accustomed to presenting findings to rooms full of executives—her dark hair pulled back neatly, her
posture upright. But her hands betray her. They move constantly, fidgeting with the zipper of her
bag, smoothing the fabric of her slacks. She hasn't fully relaxed into the chair.

Three years ago, Sarah was working late at her company's downtown office, preparing a major
presentation on consumer analytics. She was alone on the floor around 9 PM, focused on her dual
monitors, when she heard raised voices from the hallway. Then came the sound that still wakes her
some nights: a gunshot, followed by screaming. She froze, unsure what was real, and pulled her door
shut. Through the small window, she watched a man run past, and then she saw her colleague Marcus
stumble into view, blood spreading across his white shirt. He collapsed just outside her office.
Sarah called 911 with shaking hands, and the paramedics arrived within minutes, but Marcus died
before reaching the hospital.

The shooter was apprehended. Marcus's death made local news for two weeks. Sarah attended the
funeral. Her company offered counseling services. And then, gradually, everyone moved forward.
Everyone except Sarah.

She returned to work after two weeks, determined to be fine. Her manager was understanding about her
productivity dip, attributing it to the trauma of witnessing something awful. But months passed, and
Sarah found herself unable to sit at her desk without the door open, unable to work late, unable to
walk down that hallway without her chest tightening. She began requesting remote work, which her
company accommodated. Now, three years later, she works from home almost entirely.

Her marriage has become strained in ways that surprise her. Her husband, David, is kind and patient,
but he doesn't understand why she still can't drive past the office building, why she jolts awake at
loud noises, why she sometimes checks the locks on their doors multiple times before bed. He
suggests she's dwelling on it, that moving on would help. She's tried. God knows she's tried.

The intrusive images come without warning. A car backfires, and she's back in that office, watching
Marcus fall. She'll be analyzing a dataset, completely absorbed in work, and suddenly her mind
replays those screams. Her heart races. Her hands shake. Sometimes it takes her twenty minutes to
remember where she is, that she's safe.

What troubles Sarah most is the feeling that the world has fundamentally changed. She used to see
her work as meaningful—data patterns that revealed human behavior, insights that could improve
products and lives. Now, sitting in her home office, surrounded by the trappings of her successful
career, she feels oddly disconnected from it all. She goes through the motions of her job
competently, perhaps even brilliantly by external measures, but something inside her has shifted.
She's hypervigilant in ways she doesn't fully understand, always scanning for danger, always braced
for something terrible to happen again.

David suggested therapy. Sarah resisted for a long time, believing she should simply be stronger,
more resilient. But the weight of carrying this alone has become too much. She's here now, in this
office, still wearing her professional armor, still holding herself together. Yet beneath the
surface, she's exhausted—tired of being afraid in a world that suddenly feels far less safe than she
once believed it to be.
