# A Prosecutor's Distance

Layla sits in the office where she's spent the last fifteen years building cases, but these days the
space feels smaller than it used to. At forty-four, she carries herself with the composed authority
her position demands—tailored suits, organized files, a calendar that runs like clockwork. To her
colleagues, she remains the same sharp-minded prosecutor who can dissect testimony and find
inconsistencies others miss. What they don't see is how much effort that composure requires.

She was visiting her sister in Beirut in 2019, a two-week trip she'd taken annually since
immigrating to the States as a young woman. She'd been away from the courthouse for only five days
when the protests escalated. What started as demonstrations against corruption became something else
entirely—fires in the streets, sounds that made her instinctively pull away from windows, the acrid
smell of smoke that seemed to seep into everything. She remembers the specific moment: standing in
her sister's kitchen, hearing what she later understood was a stun grenade, and feeling her body
make a decision her mind hadn't made yet. She dropped to the floor. Her sister screamed.

Layla got out two days later through contacts in the embassy. She was fortunate. She tells herself
this regularly. She wasn't injured. She wasn't trapped. But her body didn't seem to understand the
distinction between fortune and trauma.

The hypervigilance came first. Back at her apartment in the States, ordinary sounds became threats—a
car backfiring on the street below sent her heart racing for minutes afterward. She found herself
tracking exits in every room, sitting with her back to walls in restaurants. At the courthouse,
during a loud argument between two attorneys in an adjacent office, she felt her hands shake so
badly she had to excuse herself to the bathroom.

The nightmares started within weeks. She doesn't always remember them clearly, but she wakes with
her heart pounding, her sheets damp with sweat, often unsure for several disorienting seconds where
she is. Her husband, Rashid, learned to sleep lightly, to gently orient her back to reality without
turning on the lights. He's patient, but she can see the exhaustion in his face.

What troubles her most is the disconnection. When she's prosecuting a case—presenting evidence,
cross-examining witnesses—she performs the role brilliantly. But afterward, she feels oddly removed
from it all, as though she's watching herself work from behind glass. She struggles to feel
satisfaction in victories that once energized her. She'll win a conviction and feel only a hollow
numbness. Her colleagues congratulate her; she nods and thanks them and feels nothing.

The concentration lapses worry her professionally. She's always been meticulous, but now she finds
herself re-reading the same paragraph three times without absorbing it. She's made small
mistakes—nothing catastrophic yet, but enough to unsettle her. She's a woman who builds her identity
on precision, on being indispensable, and now she's terrified of slipping.

She's avoided going back to Beirut, though her sister has asked. She hasn't told many people what
happened. In her professional world, vulnerability reads as weakness. Even Rashid doesn't fully
understand why she sometimes flinches when he touches her shoulder unexpectedly, or why she insists
on keeping their bedroom curtains drawn. She loves her work, loves the pursuit of justice, but
lately she wonders if the woman who loved those things is still accessible to her, or if she's
become someone else entirely.
