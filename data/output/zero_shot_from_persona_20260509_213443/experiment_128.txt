# A Mother's Vigil

Layla sits in the waiting room with her hands folded in her lap, her posture perfectly upright
despite her eighty years. She wears a carefully pressed cardigan over a modest dress, and her silver
hair is neatly arranged. She appears composed, almost serene—the kind of woman who has spent a
lifetime holding herself together for others. Yet when she speaks about her son, her voice becomes
thin and distant, as if traveling across a great chasm.

Thirty-two years ago, when Layla was forty-eight and working as a social worker in a hospital
system, her youngest son Rami fell ill. He was seven years old, bright and energetic, the kind of
child who filled a room with noise and laughter. Within three days of developing a fever, he was
hospitalized with bacterial meningitis. Layla remembers the hospital staff—she knew many of them
from her own work—looking at her with pity. She remembers the lumbar puncture, the antibiotics, the
machines surrounding her son's small body. Most vividly, she remembers the moment a neurologist told
her that if Rami survived, he might have permanent hearing loss and cognitive impairment.

For two weeks, Layla barely left his bedside. She would sit in the vinyl chair beside his hospital
bed, watching his chest rise and fall, terrified that each breath might be his last. She prayed in
ways she hadn't since childhood. She made bargains with God. When Rami finally turned a corner and
recovered—remarkably, with minimal lasting effects—Layla felt such overwhelming relief that she wept
for hours.

But something in her did not recover as readily.

Now, more than three decades later, Layla still cannot tolerate fevers. When her grandchildren get
sick, she experiences a panic that feels utterly disproportionate to the situation. Her heart races.
Her breathing becomes shallow. She finds herself catastrophizing in ways her rational mind knows are
unreasonable. She has spent her entire career helping others manage crises, yet she cannot manage
this response in herself.

She has also become hypervigilant about health—her own and everyone else's. She reads medical
articles obsessively, sometimes late into the night, searching for reassurance that never quite
comes. When she worked, colleagues would sometimes find her researching rare pediatric illnesses
during lunch breaks. She retired at seventy-five partly because she could no longer concentrate on
her social work cases; her mind kept returning to worst-case scenarios.

Layla's sleep is fragmented. She wakes at three or four in the morning, her heart pounding, from
dreams she cannot quite remember but that leave her drenched in dread. She lies awake then, her mind
cycling through memories of the hospital: the smell of antiseptic, the sound of monitors, the
particular quality of fluorescent light at midnight.

Her widowhood, which came five years ago, intensified these symptoms. Her husband had been her
anchor during those hospital days, and without him, the memories feel more present, less contained.
She finds herself avoiding certain places—the hospital where Rami was treated, even the pediatric
section of her local clinic. She declined an invitation to volunteer with a children's health
organization, something she once would have loved.

What strikes Layla most acutely is the sense of helplessness. As a social worker, she spent her
career teaching people coping strategies, helping them find resources and resilience. Yet she
remains trapped in a response her body will not release, held hostage by a threat that passed three
decades ago but somehow never fully left.
