# Marcus

Marcus sits in the waiting room with his hands folded tightly in his lap, his wedding ring catching
the fluorescent light. At thirty-two, he carries himself with the careful posture of someone
perpetually braced for impact. He works as a housekeeper at a downtown hotel—a job he's held for
nearly seven years, one that he's always taken pride in. The work is honest, and it provides
stability for him and his wife, Priya, whom he married five years ago.

Three months ago, everything fractured.

Marcus was leaving his shift at 11 p.m., walking toward the parking garage as he always did. He'd
been thinking about the dinner Priya had texted him about—her mother's recipe for dal that she was
trying for the first time. Two men approached him near his car. He remembers the sudden acceleration
of his heart, the way one of them held something that caught the light. He gave them his wallet
without hesitation, but they pushed him against the concrete pillar anyway. The impact knocked the
wind from his lungs. They took his phone, his watch, his dignity.

The police found them two blocks away. There was no lasting physical injury, though Marcus has a
faint bruise that took weeks to fade. But the injury that remains is invisible.

Now, when Marcus walks through the parking garage, his hands shake. His breathing becomes shallow
and rapid, as though his body is reliving that moment against the concrete. He's started taking the
bus instead, adding forty minutes to his commute each way, but even that doesn't feel entirely safe.
On the bus, he sits near the driver and positions himself so he can see the doors. He counts the
people around him, noting who looks suspicious, who might be a threat. His coworkers have noticed
he's quieter now, less likely to chat during breaks.

The nights are worse. Marcus lies awake beside Priya, his mind cycling through the incident in
obsessive detail. What if he'd left five minutes earlier? What if he'd fought back? What if they'd
had a real weapon? He rehearses these scenarios until 3 a.m., when exhaustion finally pulls him
under for a few restless hours. Priya has stopped trying to comfort him; her touch sometimes
startles him so violently that he jerks away, his nervous system misinterpreting her presence as
threat.

He's become hypervigilant in ways that exhaust him. At the hotel, he finds himself checking corners
before entering rooms, even though the building is secure. He's stopped taking his usual route
through the lobby and now uses the service stairs. When guests raise their voices about housekeeping
issues, his entire body floods with adrenaline and a desperate urge to escape.

Priya suggested he talk to someone. She said it gently, without judgment, but Marcus heard the worry
underneath. She's worried about him. He's worried about himself. He loves his job, loves the routine
and the quiet satisfaction of making spaces clean and orderly, but lately he dreads going in. The
thought of the parking garage at the end of his shift creates a knot of dread that begins the moment
he wakes up.

He knows intellectually that the attack is over, that he survived it, that statistically it's
unlikely to happen again. But his body hasn't received that memo. His body is still pressed against
that concrete pillar, still bracing for the next blow.
