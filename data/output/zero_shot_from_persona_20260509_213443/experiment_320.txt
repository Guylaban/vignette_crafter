# Captain's Watch

Marcus has been commanding vessels for nearly twenty years, and the sea has always been his
sanctuary. At forty-two, he carries himself with the quiet authority of someone accustomed to making
decisions that affect dozens of lives. His weathered hands and sun-deepened skin speak to a career
spent largely on the water. He is married to Patricia, a high school teacher who has learned to
manage the long stretches of his absence with practiced grace. They have no children—a choice Marcus
made early on, wanting to keep his focus where it needed to be: on his crew and his ship.

Three years ago, everything shifted.

Marcus was commanding a mid-sized cargo vessel in the North Atlantic when a catastrophic engine
failure occurred during a severe storm. The malfunction cascaded into a series of system failures
that he could not have anticipated or prevented. Within hours, the ship began taking on water.
Marcus coordinated the evacuation of his crew with methodical precision, ensuring every person made
it safely to lifeboats. He was the last to leave the bridge, watching his vessel sink into the dark
water as the helicopter lifted him away. No lives were lost. By every objective measure, he had done
his job perfectly.

But Marcus cannot shake the feeling that he failed.

Since that night, his world has contracted. He remains hypervigilant, constantly scanning for
threats that might emerge without warning. Loud noises—the slam of a door, a car backfiring, even
Patricia's blender in the morning—send his heart racing. He finds himself holding his breath, his
body tensing as though bracing for impact. These moments are brief, but they leave him exhausted.

Sleep has become unreliable. Most nights, Marcus lies awake beside Patricia, his mind replaying the
sequence of events with painful clarity. When sleep finally comes, he is back on that bridge,
watching the instruments fail one by one, feeling the helplessness rise in his chest like the water
rising in the hull. He wakes gasping, sometimes finding himself on the floor before he realizes
where he is. Patricia has learned not to touch him during these episodes; it only startles him
further.

The shipping company offered him a promotion to a desk position. Marcus declined. The thought of
returning to sea terrifies him, but the thought of leaving it feels like a death of another kind.
Instead, he finds himself in a painful limbo—unable to captain a ship, unable to accept that he
cannot. He has taken leave from work, telling Patricia he needs time to think, though they both know
it is more complicated than that.

He avoids conversations about the incident, even with Patricia, though she has gently asked if he
would consider talking to someone. He nods and says maybe, but the words feel hollow. Part of him
believes that discussing it would somehow make it more real, would transform the memory into
something he cannot contain.

What troubles Marcus most is not the memories themselves, but the way they have colonized his
present. He watches Patricia move through their home and feels a profound distance between them, as
though he is observing her life from behind glass. He used to love the smell of salt water and
diesel fuel. Now, he cannot bear to walk near the harbor. The man who once felt most alive on the
open ocean now finds himself afraid of the very thing that defined him.
