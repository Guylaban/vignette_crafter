# The Weight of Responsibility

Marcus sits in the worn leather chair across from me, his weathered hands folded carefully in his
lap. At eighty, he carries himself with the disciplined posture of someone who spent fifty-three
years in the Coast Guard, though his shoulders seem to curve inward slightly now, as though bearing
an invisible load. He speaks in measured tones, each word deliberate, the accent of his childhood in
Sri Lanka still present beneath decades of American speech.

The incident occurred three years ago, though Marcus speaks of it as though it happened last week.
He had been retired for nearly two decades by then, volunteering as a safety instructor at the local
maritime center. One afternoon, he was demonstrating proper rope-handling techniques to a group of
teenagers. A knot he tied—one he had tied thousands of times before without incident—failed. A young
girl, perhaps sixteen, fell eight feet onto the concrete dock. She suffered a compound fracture of
her left leg and a severe head injury. Marcus was the one who called emergency services, the one who
stayed with her until the ambulance arrived.

The girl recovered, eventually. Her mother even wrote Marcus a letter saying she held no blame. But
Marcus cannot hold the same grace toward himself.

He tells me that sleep has become almost negotiable. He lies in bed around midnight, his mind
immediately returning to that afternoon—the sound of the rope unraveling, the girl's cry, the
terrible stillness of her body on the concrete. Sometimes he falls asleep for an hour or two, only
to wake at three in the morning with his heart racing, his sheets soaked with perspiration. He has
tried everything: warm milk, medication prescribed by his internist, audiobooks. Nothing works
reliably. Most nights, he simply gives up and moves to the living room, where he sits in darkness
and watches the hours pass.

During the day, he finds himself replaying the moment obsessively. While eating breakfast, he thinks
about the knot. While reading the newspaper, his mind drifts to alternative outcomes. He has
researched rope failure rates, consulted engineering papers, and reviewed the incident from every
possible angle. Logically, he understands that accidents happen, that no one could have predicted
the failure. But logic provides no comfort.

Marcus has become increasingly isolated. The maritime center invited him back to volunteer within
six months of the incident, but he declined. The thought of returning to that dock, of being near
water and ropes and young people, triggers an overwhelming sense of dread. He imagines scenarios
where something goes wrong again, where his presence alone somehow invites catastrophe. His daughter
has invited him to family dinners multiple times; he attends less frequently now, offering vague
excuses. The truth is that sitting with his grandchildren fills him with a low-grade anxiety he
cannot quite name.

What strikes me most is the way Marcus speaks about responsibility. Throughout his Coast Guard
career, he was trained to be the person others depended on—the one who knew what to do in
emergencies, who kept people safe. He internalized this identity so completely that a single
failure, despite being genuinely accidental and despite the girl's recovery, has shattered something
fundamental in his sense of self. He is no longer the reliable Marcus. He is the Marcus who made a
mistake that hurt someone.

He looks at me with tired eyes and asks a question he has likely asked himself thousands of times:
"How do you live with knowing you hurt someone, even if you didn't mean to?"
