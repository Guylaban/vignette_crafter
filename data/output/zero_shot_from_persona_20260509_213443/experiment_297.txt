# Maria's Story

Maria sits in the waiting room with her hands folded in her lap, her dark eyes tracking the movement
of other people walking past. She's a woman who seems perpetually alert, her shoulders held slightly
forward, as though bracing for something. At thirty-seven, she carries herself with the careful
deliberation of someone who has learned to take up less space.

She works as a refugee aid worker, a position that drew her naturally—or so she thought. Growing up,
her own family had fled political unrest in Southeast Asia when she was a child. That history shaped
her, gave her purpose. For the past eight years, she's helped newly arrived families navigate
resettlement, working with a nonprofit that provides housing assistance, employment counseling, and
community integration services. Her colleagues describe her as tireless, compassionate, someone who
genuinely listens to the stories people carry across borders.

Three months ago, everything fractured.

Maria had been working late at the office, finishing paperwork for a family's housing application.
She left around nine o'clock on a Thursday night, walking to her car in the parking garage—something
she'd done hundreds of times before. Two police officers approached her, asking what she was doing
in the building. She explained she worked there, offered her ID. But something about the interaction
felt wrong. One officer's hand moved to his belt. When she turned to walk away, confused and
unsettled, one of them grabbed her arm roughly, twisting it behind her back.

The next moments blur in her memory—a chaos of shouting, her own voice saying "I work here, I work
here," the officer's knee driving into her lower back, the concrete floor against her cheek. It
lasted perhaps two minutes, though time seemed to splinter. When a colleague emerged from the
building and identified her, the officers released her. There were apologies, confusion, a
misunderstanding about a break-in reported in the area. She was bruised but not seriously injured.
The police department later called to apologize.

But Maria's body didn't receive that apology.

Now, three months later, she startles at sudden movements. When a colleague raises their voice in a
meeting, her heart races uncontrollably. She's stopped staying late at work, which means she's
falling behind on cases—families she cares about are waiting longer for help. Her husband notices
she flinches when he touches her unexpectedly. She's started taking different routes to avoid the
parking garage, adding forty minutes to her commute.

The nightmares began within days. She dreams of that moment repeatedly: the hand on her arm, the
weight of the officer pressing down, the taste of concrete. She wakes gasping, sometimes at three or
four in the morning, her shirt soaked with sweat. Her husband lies beside her, awake now too, and
she feels a deep shame about what her trauma is doing to him.

What troubles her most is the cognitive dissonance. She spends her days listening to refugees
describe police violence in their countries of origin—the very violence they fled. She helps them
process their fear and mistrust of authority. She validates their trauma, holds space for their
terror. And now she understands it differently, intimately, in a way that has shattered something in
her sense of safety.

She's begun to question whether she can continue this work. The irony isn't lost on her: she came to
help others heal from exactly what she now carries herself. She's caught between the identity she's
built and a body that no longer feels safe in the world she's tried to help create.
