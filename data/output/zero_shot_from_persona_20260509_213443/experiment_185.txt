# Maria's Vigil

Maria sits at her teller window at First Community Bank, her fingers moving through the familiar
motions of counting cash—twenties, fifties, hundreds—each stack aligned with precision. She's been
doing this for twenty-three years, and the repetition usually steadies her. Today, a customer's
raised voice over a disputed transaction sends her shoulders rigid. Her hands freeze mid-count. She
tells herself it's nothing, just a frustrated customer, but her heart is racing as though she's
eighteen again, standing in the Salvadoran mountains with gunfire echoing off the rocks.

Maria was drafted into the military during her country's civil war. She doesn't talk much about
those three years, but the weight of them lives in her body. She left El Salvador in 1989, married
an American, built what looked like a normal life in a quiet suburb. But normal and she have never
quite fit together.

The divorce was finalized eight years ago. Her ex-husband, a good man, eventually grew tired of
sleeping beside someone who jolted awake at 3 a.m., drenched in sweat, convinced she heard mortars.
He grew tired of the way she'd disappear into herself for days, the way she couldn't attend his
company parties, the way loud noises—fireworks, backfiring cars, even the garbage disposal—could
send her into a panic she couldn't explain to him. She couldn't explain it to herself either. She'd
survived, hadn't she? She'd built a career, a home. Why couldn't she just move forward?

Now, at fifty-five, Maria lives alone in a small apartment. She's good at her job because banks are
orderly. Money follows rules. Transactions have clear beginnings and endings. She knows what to
expect. She arrives thirty minutes early each morning, checks the security cameras, and positions
herself so she can see the entire lobby. Her manager thinks she's conscientious. She doesn't tell
him she's scanning for exits and potential threats.

The nights are harder. She lies awake most of them, her mind replaying moments she's spent decades
trying to forget—the smell of smoke, the faces of people she knew who didn't make it home, the
weight of the rifle in her hands. When she does sleep, she dreams in Spanish, and she wakes gasping.
Sometimes she finds herself standing in her kitchen at 2 a.m., unsure how she got there, her hands
shaking so badly she can't hold a glass of water.

She's withdrawn from her small community. Her sister invites her to family gatherings, but crowds
make her anxious. She worries she'll have an episode, that she'll embarrass herself or make others
uncomfortable. It's easier to stay home, to keep the apartment quiet and controlled. She watches
television with the volume low, reads books she doesn't quite absorb, and counts the days.

Three months ago, she noticed her hands trembling more frequently, even during routine transactions.
She's become hypervigilant about her surroundings, convinced something terrible is about to happen.
The feeling is constant, like living under a storm cloud that never passes. She's gained weight from
inactivity and lost interest in the few hobbies she once enjoyed. Her sister says she seems like
she's fading, and Maria wonders if that's true, if she's slowly disappearing into the space between
her survival and her living.

She still shows up to work. She still counts the cash. But increasingly, she feels like she's
watching herself from a distance, a ghost moving through routines that once meant something.
