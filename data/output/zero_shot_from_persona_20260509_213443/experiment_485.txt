# Maria's Mornings

Maria sits at her workstation at 7:45 a.m., fifteen minutes before her shift begins at the call
center. She has been arriving early for the past three years, ever since she returned to work after
the attack. The early arrival gives her time to settle, to check that her desk is exactly as she
left it, to breathe in the familiar smell of the industrial coffee maker in the break room. She is
sixty-two years old in this moment—not seventy-eight—though her hands shake slightly as she logs
into her computer.

It was a Tuesday morning in 2019 when the bomb went off at the metro station near her apartment in
Mexico City. Maria had just descended the stairs, her work badge already clipped to her blouse, her
mind already rehearsing the greeting she would use with callers that day. The explosion came without
warning. The sound was not loud so much as total—it consumed everything, erased the boundary between
her body and the world. She remembers the pressure, the way her ears felt like they might burst, the
taste of concrete dust. She remembers a woman's shoe, separated from its owner, landing near her
face.

She was one of the lucky ones. Thirty-one people died that day. Maria survived with a fractured
collarbone and cuts across her left side from flying glass. The physical wounds healed within
months. The other wounds have not.

Now, at seventy-eight, Maria still works the morning shift at the call center in Mexico City. Her
supervisor has suggested many times that she retire—she has certainly earned it—but Maria refuses.
When she is at her desk, speaking to customers about their billing questions or service issues, she
is not thinking about the metro station. The work requires a specific kind of presence: her voice
must be steady, her attention on the caller's problem, her tone patient and professional. There is
no room in this structure for the memories to surface. There is no room for the trembling.

But the trembling comes anyway. It arrives most mornings around 9:15 a.m., about ninety minutes into
her shift. Her hands begin to shake. Her heart accelerates. Sometimes she can feel her chest
tightening, as if the pressure from the explosion is being applied again, slowly, deliberately. She
excuses herself to the bathroom, locks the stall, and sits on the toilet seat with her eyes closed,
waiting for it to pass. Her coworkers have learned not to ask questions. They know about the attack.
They know Maria is one of the survivors.

At night, in her small apartment—the same apartment she has lived in since her husband died fifteen
years ago—Maria lies awake. Sleep comes in fragments, interrupted by dreams in which she is still in
the metro station, still covered in dust, still waiting for the next explosion. She has tried
sleeping pills. They make her feel foggy the next day at work, and she cannot afford to be foggy.
The calls must be answered. The customers must be served.

She has not returned to the metro station. She takes a bus to work now, a longer route that adds
forty minutes to her commute. The bus is crowded and loud, and sometimes the sounds of the traffic
trigger the same panic response, but at least it is not the metro. At least she is not underground,
trapped in that narrow space where the air still seems to vibrate with the memory of violence.

Her daughter calls from Guadalajara once a week, asking the same gentle question: "Mamá, are you
sure you don't want to stop working?" Maria always answers the same way: "Not yet, mija. Not yet."
