# Clinical Vignette: Amara

Amara is a thirty-five-year-old tutor who works with secondary school students in mathematics and
English, often holding sessions in her small home office or at clients' homes. She presents as
articulate and thoughtful, with a careful way of choosing her words. She has been in a committed
relationship for four years, though she describes recent tension that she attributes to her own
"emotional distance."

Seven years ago, Amara was assaulted by someone she knew—a colleague from a previous job who offered
her a ride home after an evening event. What began as what she thought was a friendly gesture
escalated quickly in his car. She remembers the shock of it more than anything else; the way her
mind seemed to separate from her body, the way she kept thinking this couldn't actually be
happening. She didn't report it at the time. She told no one for nearly two years.

The assault has cast a long shadow across her life in ways that have only become clearer to her
recently. For years, she managed by simply not thinking about it—a strategy that worked until it
didn't. About eighteen months ago, something shifted. A student's father came to pick up his
daughter from a tutoring session, and something about his tone, his proximity in her small office,
triggered something in Amara that she couldn't control. She found herself unable to breathe
properly, her hands shaking as she ushered them out. Since then, the world has felt increasingly
unsafe.

Now, she experiences intrusive images at unpredictable moments—a flash of the car interior, the
feeling of being trapped. These images come most often in the late afternoon, around the time the
assault occurred, and they leave her disoriented for hours afterward. She's started canceling
evening tutoring sessions, which has affected her income and her professional reputation, though her
clients don't know the real reason. She tells them she's restructuring her schedule.

At night, sleep is fragmented. She lies awake for hours, her mind cycling through scenarios—what she
could have done differently, whether she somehow invited what happened, whether she should have
reported it. When she does sleep, she has vivid nightmares that jolt her awake in a state of panic.
Her partner has noticed her flinching at unexpected touch, even his touch, which creates a painful
distance between them. She's tried to explain, but the words feel inadequate. How do you tell
someone that sometimes their hand on your shoulder in the dark feels like a threat?

Hypervigilance has become her constant companion. In public spaces, she scans exits, positions
herself strategically, notices men who remind her of her assailant. She's become acutely aware of

certain neighborhoods, changes her route home if she feels uncertain about it. What started as
reasonable caution has expanded into a pervasive sense that danger is lurking, that she cannot fully
trust her own judgment about safety.

The tutoring work, which she once found deeply satisfying, now carries an undercurrent of anxiety.
She's hyperaware of boundaries in ways she wasn't before, sometimes to the point of seeming cold or
distant to her students. She worries constantly about being alone with male students, about how her
behavior might be perceived, about whether she's overreacting. The cognitive load of managing these
fears while trying to teach has become exhausting.

What troubles Amara most is the sense that the assault has taken up residence inside her, that it's
no longer something that happened to her but something that she is. She describes feeling
fundamentally changed—less trusting, more afraid, more alone even when surrounded by people who care
about her.
