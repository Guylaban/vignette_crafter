# María Elena

María Elena sits in the afternoon light filtering through the library's tall windows, her weathered
hands arranging index cards with the same precision she has maintained for forty-three years. At
seventy-eight, she moves more slowly than she once did, but her organizational system remains
impeccable. Her colleagues have long since retired, yet she comes in three days a week, unable to
fully let go of the work that has anchored her life.

She does not speak often about 1981, the year everything changed. Her husband, Roberto, knows the
outline of what happened—most of it, anyway. What she has never fully told him are the specific
textures of that memory: the smell of smoke in the streets of San Salvador, the sound of her
daughter Rosa's crying as they hid in the cellar of the church, the weight of her mother's hand in
hers as soldiers moved through the neighborhood. María Elena was forty-five then, a young librarian
with a quiet life and a future she could see clearly mapped before her.

The violence came suddenly, though everyone knew it was coming. Her husband's brother had been
involved with a cooperative that the government deemed subversive. That association was enough. One
afternoon, soldiers arrived at their home. Roberto was at work; the children were at school. María
Elena remembers the sound of boots on tile, the way her voice sounded strange and distant when she
told them she did not know where her brother-in-law was. She was not beaten, but the threat hung in
the air like humidity. That night, they decided to leave.

The journey north took three weeks. She thinks of it now only in fragments, which is perhaps a
mercy. There was a truck bed with thirty other people. There was a river crossing at night. There
was a camp in Guatemala where she held Rosa while the child burned with fever. When they finally
reached the United States, they had nothing but documents that barely proved they existed and the
clothes they wore.

For decades, María Elena told herself the story was over. She and Roberto had built a life in Los
Angeles. She had found work in a public library, eventually becoming head of the Spanish-language
collection. Their children had grown, gone to college, made lives of their own. She had learned to
smile at patrons, to help researchers find obscure texts, to train younger librarians in the careful
work of cataloging. She had become, in her own mind, a person for whom the past was truly past.

But in the last year, something has shifted. She lies awake at night, her mind replaying
conversations from decades ago, analyzing them for signs of danger she might have missed. When she
hears sirens, her body goes rigid. She has begun checking the locks on her home multiple times
before bed, sometimes waking at three in the morning to verify them again. Roberto finds her
standing in the kitchen at odd hours, staring at nothing.

The library, which has always been her refuge, has become more complicated. Certain sounds—a door
slamming in the stacks, raised voices—send her heart racing. Last month, a young man came in asking
questions about immigration history, and María Elena had to excuse herself, finding she could not
catch her breath. She sat in the back office for twenty minutes, telling herself she was being
foolish, that this was not 1981, that she was safe.

She does not know why the memories have become so vivid now, at an age when she thought she had
earned the right to peace. Roberto suggests it might be the news, the political climate, the things
she sees on television. Perhaps he is right. What she knows is that the past, which she had
carefully filed away like books in a library, has begun to slip from its proper place, scattering
across the present in ways she cannot organize or control.
