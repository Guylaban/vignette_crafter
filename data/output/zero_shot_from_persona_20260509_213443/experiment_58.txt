# Marcus

Marcus is a 44-year-old nurse who has worked in the emergency department of a busy urban hospital
for the past eighteen years. He's built a reputation as someone reliable and steady—the kind of
colleague who stays late to help overwhelmed residents and remembers his patients' names even after
they've been discharged. He's been married to David for twelve years, and they live in a modest
house in the neighborhood where Marcus grew up.

Three months ago, Marcus was leaving the hospital after a double shift at 11 p.m. He'd been working
trauma cases all evening and was exhausted, thinking only about getting home. As he walked to his
car in the parking garage, two men approached him from behind. They demanded his wallet and phone.
When Marcus hesitated—more from shock than resistance—one of them shoved him hard against the
concrete wall. His head struck the edge of a parked car. They took his belongings and ran. A
security guard found him sitting on the ground ten minutes later, dazed and bleeding from a cut
above his eye.

The physical injuries healed quickly. The cut required four stitches. The bruising on his shoulder
and ribs faded within weeks. But something else didn't fade.

Now, Marcus finds himself unable to return to the parking garage. He takes a longer route through
the main hospital corridors, even though it adds twenty minutes to his commute. His colleagues have
started to notice. They ask if he's okay, and he says yes, he's fine, just trying a different route.
But the truth is more complicated. The thought of that dark garage—the echoing footsteps, the way
sound travels off concrete—makes his chest tight and his hands shake.

At home, Marcus has become hypervigilant in ways he doesn't fully understand. When David comes
through the door after work, Marcus startles violently, his body tensing as if bracing for impact.
He's started sleeping with his phone on the nightstand, fully charged, though he's not entirely sure
why. Some nights he doesn't sleep at all, lying awake listening to the sounds of the house settling,
cataloging each noise. He's begun checking the locks on doors and windows multiple times before bed,
something he never did before.

At work, Marcus finds his patience wearing thin in ways that feel foreign to him. A patient's family
member raised their voice during a shift last week, and Marcus felt an overwhelming surge of rage
and fear that left him shaking in the break room for an hour afterward. He's always prided himself
on remaining calm under pressure—it's essential in emergency nursing—but now small confrontations
feel dangerous, unpredictable.

The mugging itself plays on repeat in his mind, though not always as a clear memory. Instead, it
comes in fragments: the specific pressure of hands on his back, the taste of blood, the feeling of
being utterly powerless. These moments ambush him at unexpected times—while charting patient notes,
while driving, while David is talking to him about his day. Marcus finds himself nodding along while
internally he's back in that garage, his body flooded with adrenaline and dread.

He's begun to withdraw from the life he built. He declined an invitation to his sister's birthday
dinner. He's stopped going to the gym, a routine he maintained for years. Even his work, which has
always been his anchor, now feels precarious. He wonders if he's still capable of being the nurse
his patients need him to be, or if he's fundamentally changed into someone more fragile, more
afraid.

David has gently suggested that talking to someone might help, and Marcus knows he's right. But
asking for help feels like admitting that what happened has won.
