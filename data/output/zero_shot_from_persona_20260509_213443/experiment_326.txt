# Marcus

Marcus sits in the chair with his shoulders drawn inward, a posture that seems almost permanent now.
At forty-six, he carries himself like someone much older—the kind of weariness that comes not from a
single bad night but from years of accumulated dread. He was a firefighter for eighteen years before
taking a leave of absence, a career that had always suited him. He was good at rushing toward
danger, at staying calm when others panicked. But that was before.

Four years ago, Marcus took a sabbatical to work with an international relief organization in a
region torn apart by conflict. His ex-wife thought it was noble; his colleagues thought he was
crazy. Marcus himself isn't entirely sure what drove him there. He remembers wanting to feel useful
in a way that fighting fires—however important—no longer satisfied. He was restless, searching for
something he couldn't name.

The first month was difficult but manageable. Marcus worked in a makeshift clinic, helping to
distribute medical supplies and assist with basic triage. He saw things that disturbed
him—malnourished children, untreated wounds, the blank stares of people who had lost everything. But
he could compartmentalize it, the way he'd learned to do with fire scenes. Then came the day that
changed everything.

The clinic was hit during a sudden escalation of violence. Marcus was in the back room when the
explosion occurred. He doesn't remember much about the immediate aftermath—a kind of merciful blank
space. What he does remember is crawling out from under rubble, his ears ringing, and finding one of
the local nurses, a woman named Amara who had become his friend, pinned under a collapsed beam. He
remembers the weight of her, the warmth of her blood on his hands, the way her eyes found his as she
struggled to breathe. He remembers that she died while he was trying to free her.

He was evacuated the next day. The organization called it a medical evacuation, though physically he
was largely unharmed.

Since returning home, Marcus exists in a strange fog. He lives alone in the same house where he and
his ex-wife raised their two adult children, though they barely speak now. He told them he was fine,
that he just needed time, and they've respected that distance—or perhaps they've simply accepted his
withdrawal as permanent.

The hypervigilance is constant. Every unexpected sound makes his body tense; he finds himself
cataloging exits wherever he goes. He's stopped going to the grocery store at busy times. The smell
of smoke, which once meant something manageable and professional, now sends him into a spiral of
panic. He'll sit in his car in the driveway for twenty minutes before he can bring himself to go
inside, convinced something terrible is about to happen.

Sleep is fragmented and unreliable. When he does sleep, he's back in the clinic—sometimes he's still
trying to save Amara, sometimes he's buried under the rubble himself. He wakes gasping, his heart
hammering against his ribs.

He hasn't returned to the fire department. He can't imagine suiting up, climbing into a truck,
trusting his body to respond the way it once did. His captain has stopped calling. Marcus tells
himself this is fine, that maybe this chapter of his life is simply over. But mostly he feels
untethered, like a man who volunteered to be useful and instead discovered something broken inside
himself that usefulness can no longer reach.
