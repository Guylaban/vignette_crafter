# Marcus

Marcus is a sixty-year-old warehouse supervisor who has worked in logistics for the past twenty-two
years. He carries himself with the deliberate posture of someone accustomed to physical labor,
though his frame has softened slightly with age. He speaks in measured tones, choosing words
carefully, and there is a watchfulness to him—the way his eyes track movement in the room, the way
he positions himself near exits.

He served in the military for eight years, leaving in 1991, shortly after the Gulf War ended. He was
there for the ground campaign, part of a mechanized infantry unit. He rarely speaks about those
months directly, but the weight of them settles into the room when he does.

What happened on February 27th, 1991, has never fully left him. His unit was advancing through
Kuwait when they came under fire from Iraqi positions. The vehicle ahead of his took a direct hit.
Marcus watched it happen—the sudden bloom of flame, the way the metal twisted. He was driving the
second vehicle. He remembers the order to dismount, the chaos of returning fire, the smell of
burning fuel and something else he doesn't name. He remembers the bodies. He remembers that some of
the men in that first vehicle had been his friends.

For decades, Marcus compartmentalized this. He built a life: a stable job, a relationship with
Patricia that lasted fifteen years before ending, now a new partner named Susan for the past four
years. He drank more than he should have during his forties and fifties, and he's aware of this,
though he doesn't elaborate. He kept working, kept showing up, kept himself occupied.

But something shifted about two years ago. A younger coworker was injured in a forklift accident at
the warehouse—nothing fatal, but sudden and violent. The sound of the collision, the young man's
screams, the way everyone froze. Marcus found himself unable to return to that section of the
warehouse for weeks. When he finally did, his hands shook.

Since then, the intrusions have intensified. He wakes at three or four in the morning, his heart
racing, sometimes unsure of where he is. The dreams are vivid and specific: the smell of the desert,
the heat, the particular timbre of the explosion. Some nights he's back in the vehicle. Other nights
he's searching through wreckage.

During the day, he manages better, but there's a brittleness to it. Loud noises—a truck backfiring,
a door slamming—send a jolt through his nervous system. He finds himself avoiding the warehouse
floor during busy hours when there's more activity, more unpredictable sounds. He's taken on more
administrative work, which his supervisors have allowed, though he senses they're puzzled by the
change.

His relationship with Susan has become strained. She wants him present in ways he struggles to be.
He's withdrawn, spending evenings in the garage or sitting alone, ostensibly watching television but
really somewhere else. Susan has asked him repeatedly if something is wrong, if it's about her, and
he tells her it isn't, but he can't quite explain what it is. How do you tell someone that thirty-
three years hasn't been enough distance, that a sound or a smell can collapse time, that part of you
never actually left that desert?

He came to therapy because Susan suggested it, and because he's tired. Not the kind of tired that
sleep fixes, but the kind that comes from holding yourself together for so long that the seams are
beginning to show.
