# Maria's Story

Maria is a 38-year-old fisherman who has spent the last twenty years on the water off the coast of
West Africa. She is a skilled navigator and a respected member of her fishing community, known for
her early mornings and reliable catches. She is married to Kofi, a man she has known since
childhood, and together they have three children. Until two years ago, her life followed the rhythms
she had always known—the tides, the seasons, the predictable cycles of work and family.

Her younger brother, Ama, worked alongside her on the boat. He was twenty-eight, quick-witted, and
had recently become engaged. Maria had watched him grow from a boy into a man, and they shared a
bond deepened by years of working together in close quarters, reading the water, anticipating
storms. On a morning that Maria can still describe in exact detail—the color of the sky, the
temperature of the air, the particular sound the engine made—their boat capsized in rough seas.
Maria was thrown into the water. Ama never surfaced.

The rescue came within an hour. Maria was pulled from the ocean and revived on the dock. Ama's body
was recovered three days later.

Since that morning, Maria has not returned to the water. She tells people she is still grieving,
which is true, but the word "grieving" does not capture what has happened to her. She sits in her
home during the hours when she would normally be preparing the boat, and her mind fills with images
she cannot control. She sees Ama's face underwater, though she knows she did not actually see this.
She hears him calling her name, though he could not have. At night, she wakes gasping, her body
convinced it is drowning, her lungs burning with phantom seawater.

When Kofi touches her unexpectedly, she flinches violently. When her children laugh loudly, she
feels a surge of anger that frightens her. The world has become a place of hidden dangers. She finds
herself checking the locks on the doors repeatedly, unable to trust that she has already checked
them. She avoids the market where Ama's fiancée sometimes shops, unable to bear seeing the woman's
grief reflected back at her. She has stopped attending community gatherings, preferring the
isolation of her home, where at least the threats she imagines feel manageable.

The financial pressure is mounting. Kofi works when he can, but fishing is their livelihood, and
Maria's absence from the boat has created a gap in their income. Her children ask her why she no
longer works, and she has no answer that makes sense to them. She feels the weight of their
confusion, the burden of her own incapacity, the guilt that she survived when Ama did not.

Maria knows her family needs her to return to work. She knows that Ama would not have wanted this
for her. But when she stands at the edge of the dock and looks at the water, her body refuses to
move. Her heart races. Her breathing becomes shallow. The water that has been her home, her
livelihood, her identity, has become the place where her brother died, and she cannot reconcile
these two truths. She exists now in the space between the life she had and the life she cannot seem
to live, waiting for something to shift, though she has no clear sense of what that something might
be.
