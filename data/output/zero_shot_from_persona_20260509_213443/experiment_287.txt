# Carlos

Carlos sits with his hands folded on the table, thumbs rotating slowly against each other—a habit
he's had for as long as he can remember. He's sixty-four now, with silver threading through his dark
hair and reading glasses perpetually hanging from a cord around his neck. He speaks carefully,
choosing words as if selecting stones for a piece he's designing.

He grew up in a small city in Mexico, the middle child of five. His father worked in construction;
his mother kept the household. Carlos was eight years old the first time he heard his mother
screaming. He remembers the specific quality of that sound—not anger, but terror. He was in the
bedroom he shared with his two younger brothers when he heard the crash of something breaking in the
kitchen. He didn't go out. None of the boys did. Instead, they lay in the dark listening to their
father's voice rising and falling, their mother's pleas, the wet sound of impact. It lasted maybe
ten minutes. It felt like hours.

This happened again. Many times. Carlos learned not to listen, but his body never quite accepted
that instruction. His shoulders would tense at sudden noises. His chest would tighten. He became the
kind of child who made himself very small and very quiet.

When Carlos was fifteen, his mother finally left. She took the three youngest children and went to
stay with her sister. Carlos was old enough to stay behind with his father, or so everyone said. He
learned jewelry-making from an uncle during those years—the meticulous work of it, the way you could
hold something precious and fragile in your hands and shape it into something beautiful and
enduring. He moved to the United States at twenty-two with a suitcase and a set of jeweler's tools.

For decades, the work sustained him. He opened his own shop, developed a reputation for restoration
and custom pieces. He married a woman named Rosa, had no children—a choice they made together,
though he never told her why. The marriage lasted twenty-three years before dissolving into the kind
of quiet unhappiness that requires no dramatic catalyst. He's been divorced for eleven years now.

Lately, though, something has shifted. He's sixty-four and finds himself unable to do the work that
once absorbed him completely. His hands shake sometimes when he's doing detail work—not from age, he
insists, but from something else. He'll be at his bench, magnifying glass in place, ready to work on
a delicate repair, and suddenly he'll hear something—a car backfiring outside, someone raising their
voice in the street, even just the sound of his neighbor's television turned up too loud—and his
hands become useless. His heart races. He has to stop and sit in the back room until the feeling
passes.

He's sleeping poorly. When he does sleep, he dreams of his childhood home, though the geography is
wrong in the way dreams always are. He wakes at three or four in the morning, his shirt damp, and he
sits in his living room watching the hours pass until dawn. He tells himself he's just getting old,
that insomnia is natural, but he knows better.

He's become isolated. He used to enjoy his shop, the interaction with clients, the satisfaction of
returning someone's cherished item restored to beauty. Now the unpredictability of his own responses
frightens him. He's never told anyone—not his ex-wife, not his few close friends—what he witnessed
as a child, or how it continues to live in his body all these years later.
