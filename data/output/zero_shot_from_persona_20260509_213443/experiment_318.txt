# Margaret

Margaret sits with her hands folded in her lap, her posture still carrying the grace of someone who
spent fifty years moving through space with intention. At sixty-six, she maintains the kind of
understated elegance that comes from a lifetime in front of cameras, though these days her face
bears the weight of something the camera never captured.

She was doing a charity fashion show eighteen months ago—a benefit for a children's hospital, the
kind of event she'd done dozens of times before. She was backstage at the venue, a converted
warehouse space with narrow corridors and tight quarters. Margaret was walking quickly between the
dressing area and the runway, mentally running through her final appearance, when she didn't see the
young production assistant crouched down adjusting a lighting cable. Margaret's heel caught the edge
of the cable, and she stumbled forward directly into the woman, driving her backward into a metal
support beam with tremendous force.

The assistant suffered a severe head injury and a fractured shoulder. She spent three weeks in the
hospital. Margaret later learned she'd been a single mother of two young children.

Margaret was cleared of any legal responsibility. Witnesses confirmed it was an accident, a tragic
confluence of circumstances. But Margaret cannot seem to accept this verdict of her own innocence.

She stopped modeling immediately after the incident. Not because anyone asked her to—the industry,
as it does, moved on—but because the thought of standing in front of an audience, of being watched
and admired, became unbearable. She finds herself replaying those seconds endlessly: the moment
before she stepped into the corridor, the fraction of a second when she could have looked down,
moved differently, been anywhere but where she was.

At night, Margaret lies awake and imagines the assistant's children asking where their mother is,
imagines the surgeries, the rehabilitation. These are not fleeting thoughts. They arrive with vivid
sensory detail—the sound of the impact, the assistant's cry, the particular shade of the hospital
room she visited once, weeks later, when the woman was finally willing to see her. Margaret has
replayed these moments thousands of times.

During the day, she moves through her apartment in Manhattan with careful, almost ritualistic
precision. She avoids crowds, avoids the warehouse district where the show took place. When she does
venture out, she finds herself hyperaware of everyone around her, calculating trajectories and
risks, certain that she is a danger to others. A minor bump in the grocery store sends her into a
state of acute anxiety that can last for hours.

She's developed an insomnia that no amount of sleep medication seems to touch. When exhaustion
finally forces her under, she dreams of the moment of impact—sometimes she's the one hitting the
beam, sometimes she's the assistant, sometimes she's the children she's never met.

Margaret has lost twelve pounds. She sees her reflection in the mirror and barely recognizes the
woman looking back—not because of age or physical change, but because she no longer knows who that
person is. The woman who was careful, who was graceful, who moved through the world without causing
harm—that woman no longer exists.

She tells her therapist that she understands, intellectually, that accidents happen. She knows the
assistant has recovered, that she's returned to work. Margaret even sent a substantial donation to
the family's medical fund. But knowing these things and believing them are entirely different. The
knowing sits in her mind like a passenger who refuses to disembark.
