# The Weight of Uncertainty

Maria stands at her kitchen counter at 5 AM, as she has for the past twenty years, preparing mise en
place for the day's service. Her hands move through the familiar motions—julienning carrots,
brunoise of onion, the precise measurement of stocks and reductions. But lately, these movements
feel disconnected from her body, as though she's watching someone else execute the choreography
she's performed a thousand times. She's been awake since three, unable to sleep past that hour
anymore.

Two years ago, at forty-one, Maria collapsed during a dinner service. The pain in her abdomen was
unlike anything she'd experienced—sharp, radiating, all-consuming. Within hours, she was in
emergency surgery. The diagnosis was ovarian cancer. The surgeon had caught it, she was told, but
the word "cancer" seemed to rewire something fundamental in her understanding of her own body. She
remembers the surgeon's face as he described the tumor, the way his words sounded like they were
coming through water.

The chemotherapy lasted eight months. Maria tried to maintain her position at the restaurant, but
after the second round, she couldn't. She took medical leave, something she'd never done in her
career. She spent weeks in her apartment, watching her hair fall out in clumps, unable to taste
food—the cruelest irony for a chef. The nausea was relentless. She'd lie in bed and imagine the
poison moving through her veins, killing cancer cells but also killing something of herself.

Now, eighteen months into remission, Maria is back at work. Her oncologist says she's cancer-free.
The scans are clear. She should feel relieved. Instead, she exists in a state of perpetual dread.

Every minor physical sensation sends her into a spiral. Last week, a twinge in her side sent her to
the emergency room at midnight, convinced the cancer had returned. The CT scan was negative, but the
relief lasted only hours before the fear crept back. She finds herself Googling survival statistics
at night, reading horror stories about recurrence, though she knows this feeds the anxiety rather
than calms it.

At the restaurant, Maria has become hypervigilant about her body in ways that interfere with her
work. She'll be plating a dish, focused on the precision of a sauce drizzle, when suddenly she's
aware of her heartbeat, and her mind spirals into catastrophic thinking. Is that arrhythmia? Is
something growing? She excuses herself to the bathroom and sits on the toilet, breathing
deliberately, waiting for the panic to pass. Her sous chef has noticed her absences, her
distraction. She can see the concern in his eyes, but she can't explain it.

Her relationship has strained under the weight of her fear. Her partner, David, tries to be
supportive, but Maria senses his exhaustion. When he suggests she's "catastrophizing," she hears
judgment rather than care. She's withdrawn from intimacy, unable to be present in her body,
hyperaware of its fragility. Sometimes she lies awake beside him, listening to him sleep,
calculating probabilities and odds like a mathematician possessed.

Maria knows intellectually that she's survived, that the treatment worked. But her nervous system
hasn't received that message. Her body remains in a state of emergency, scanning constantly for
threat. The kitchen, once her sanctuary, has become a place where she must perform normalcy while
internally bracing for the next crisis. She moves through her days on autopilot, executing perfect
plating while feeling fundamentally unsafe in her own skin.
