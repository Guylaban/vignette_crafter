# Robert

Robert sits in the leather chair across from the therapist with the careful posture of someone who
has spent forty years in courtrooms—shoulders squared, hands folded on his lap, eyes tracking
movement. At sixty-five, he has just taken early retirement from his position as a senior
prosecutor, though he doesn't quite say that directly. He says instead that he "needed a change,"
that his wife thought it would be good for him to step back.

Three months ago, Robert was leaving a late dinner with colleagues downtown when he witnessed a
traffic stop two blocks away. A young Black man was being removed from a vehicle. Robert, who has
built his career on the careful application of law, found himself walking toward the commotion. He
was trying to be helpful—he knew the officers, had worked with them for years. But when he
approached to say hello, the situation escalated in seconds. The man resisted. An officer, thinking
Robert was part of the situation, shoved him hard against a nearby wall. Robert's head struck the
concrete. There was blood. There were moments he cannot quite account for.

He was not seriously injured. No fractures, no lasting neurological damage according to the
emergency room physician. But something fractured nonetheless.

For the first month, Robert told himself it was fine—just an unfortunate misunderstanding, a moment
of chaos in an otherwise orderly profession. He returned to work. But sleep became difficult. When
he closed his eyes, he felt the impact again, the shock of violence from someone he had considered a
colleague. He began waking at three in the morning, his heart racing, his hands clenched. His wife,
Margaret, found him one night standing at the kitchen window at 4 a.m., staring into darkness.

At work, his concentration fractured. He would be reading case files and suddenly find twenty
minutes had passed without him absorbing a word. His colleagues noticed he was irritable in ways
unfamiliar to him. During a meeting about a police misconduct case, he felt his chest tighten so
severely he had to excuse himself. The irony was not lost on him—he had spent decades prosecuting
others, and now he could not even trust the system he had devoted his life to.

Crowds became intolerable. On the street, he found himself scanning for uniforms, for potential
threats. When a car door slammed near him at the grocery store, his entire body tensed. Margaret
began going to social events alone. Robert made excuses. He told people he was tired.

What troubles him most, though he struggles to articulate this, is the betrayal. Not of the
officer—he can rationalize that as a mistake, an excess of caution. But the betrayal of his own
worldview. For four decades, he believed in order, in the system, in the essential goodness of those
who enforced the law. He believed that violence had rules, that it happened to other people in other
circumstances. Now he knows differently. His body knows it in a way his mind cannot argue with.

Margaret insisted he see someone. She used the word "trauma," which Robert resisted until he
couldn't anymore. He retired early because the thought of returning to the courthouse, of seeing
those officers in the hallways, of prosecuting cases he no longer felt certain about, had become
impossible. He sits now in this office with a man who is not a judge or a lawyer, trying to
understand why he cannot seem to move past what was, objectively, a minor incident. Trying to
understand why his hands are shaking.
