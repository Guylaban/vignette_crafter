# Michael's Story

Michael has spent the last forty-seven years painting. It started as a way to pay rent after
secondary school, but somewhere between his twenties and now, it became who he is. He's painted
houses across the city—thousands of them—developing a reputation for meticulous work and
reliability. His hands know the rhythm of preparation, primer, and finish coat the way other men's
hands know their children's faces.

Three years ago, at sixty-eight, Michael was working on a commercial building in the city center
when a vehicle plowed through the crowded street below. He was on the second-floor scaffolding,
painting window frames, when he heard the impact—a sound he still struggles to describe to anyone
who wasn't there. He watched from above as people scattered and fell, as emergency vehicles arrived,
as the chaos unfolded beneath him like something from another world. He wasn't physically injured.
He came down the ladder, gave a statement to police, and went home.

But something fractured that day that Michael couldn't see in a mirror.

Within weeks, he found himself unable to work on any building near a street. The thought of being
elevated, exposed, with crowds below made his chest tight and his hands shake. He started taking
jobs in quieter areas—residential painting, interior work—anything that kept him on solid ground and
away from windows overlooking thoroughfares. His clients began noticing the difference. Some jobs
he'd accept and then cancel. Others he'd arrive at and sit in his van for an hour before finding the
strength to begin. The precision that had defined his career started slipping. He'd paint a wall and
not remember doing it. He'd find himself staring at his brush, unable to remember what color came
next.

Sleep became a negotiation with his own mind. Michael lies awake most nights, his body rigid beside
the window he now keeps curtained. When he does sleep, he's back on that scaffolding, but this time
the vehicle comes for him. He wakes gasping, his heart hammering against his ribs, and he doesn't go
back to bed. Instead, he sits in his kitchen with the lights on, sometimes until dawn.

Loud noises—a car backfiring, a siren, even a door slamming—send electricity through his nervous
system. His body reacts before his mind catches up, tensing as though bracing for impact. He's
become hypervigilant in ways that exhaust him. When he's out, he positions himself carefully, always
aware of vehicles, always calculating escape routes. He avoids crowds now. Even the grocery store
feels dangerous.

Michael has withdrawn from the few friendships he maintained. His neighbors don't see him much
anymore. He tells people he's semi-retired, which is partly true—he works sporadically, taking only
the jobs he feels he can manage. But the truth is more complicated. He's trapped between who he was
and who he's become, unable to reconcile the man who took pride in his work with someone whose hands
now tremble at the thought of climbing a ladder.

He came to therapy because his doctor insisted, though Michael isn't entirely sure what talking will
accomplish. He's seventy-one years old. He's lived most of his life without needing help. But three
years after that day, he remains stuck—unable to return to the work that defined him, unable to
sleep through a single night, unable to shake the feeling that danger is always waiting just around
the corner.
