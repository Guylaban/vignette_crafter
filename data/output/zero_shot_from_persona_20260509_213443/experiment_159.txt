# A Safe Distance

Fatima sits in the waiting room of the community center where she works, her hands folded precisely
in her lap. She has arrived forty minutes early for her appointment, as she does most days—early
enough to survey the space, to know where the exits are, to feel the solid wall against her back. At
fifty-one, she has spent most of her adult life in service to others, helping vulnerable families
navigate systems that often feel designed to exclude them. But the work that sustains her is also
the work that fractures her.

The trauma began when Fatima was seven years old, in her grandmother's house in Beirut. Her parents
had left her in what they believed was safe hands while they worked. Her grandmother's husband—a man
Fatima had been taught to respect and obey—would wait until the household grew quiet. He would pull
her into the back room, his grip firm on her small arm, and hurt her in ways that made her body feel
like it belonged to someone else. The pain was sharp and confusing. What confused her more was the
silence afterward, the way adults looked through her at family gatherings, the way her body learned
to make itself smaller.

She never told anyone. Not when her parents finally retrieved her. Not when she was twelve, or
twenty, or thirty. She buried it so deeply that for decades she could almost convince herself it
hadn't happened.

Now, at her desk in the Social Ministry office, Fatifa works with abused children and their
families. She is exceptionally good at her job. She has an instinct for recognizing the subtle
signs—the way a child's eyes flatten when a parent enters the room, the careful distance they
maintain, the stories they tell that don't quite match their injuries. Colleagues describe her as
tireless, compassionate, impossibly patient. What they don't see is the cost.

Three years ago, while conducting a home visit to assess a seven-year-old girl, Fatima entered a
bedroom that smelled exactly like her grandmother's house. The same staleness, the same particular
quality of shadow in the corner. Her body remembered before her mind caught up. She felt the small
girl's hand in hers, heard the child's careful, practiced explanations, and something fractured open
inside her. That night, she couldn't sleep. For weeks afterward, she couldn't sleep.

Now she startles at unexpected sounds—a door closing, a man's raised voice in the hallway, the
particular timber of authority in someone's speech. Her heart accelerates and her mind floods with a
single imperative: escape. She has become hypervigilant in her own home, checking locks multiple
times, sleeping with her bedroom door locked and a phone in her hand. At work, she finds herself
unable to enter certain rooms. She has developed elaborate routes through the office building that
allow her to avoid passing her supervisor's desk.

The worst part is the intrusive thoughts. Unbidden images arrive during routine moments—while she is
eating lunch, while she is listening to a client, while she is driving home. They come with a
physical sensation, a tightness in her chest that makes breathing difficult. She has started to
dread the work she loves, because the work requires her to be present with children's suffering, and
proximity to their pain has become proximity to her own.

She sits in the waiting room, early as always, and watches the door. Her hands remain folded. She
waits to be called.
