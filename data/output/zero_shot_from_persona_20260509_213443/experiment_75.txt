# Sarah's Vigil

Sarah sits in the office with her hands folded precisely in her lap, a habit born from years of pre-
flight checklists and the discipline required to pilot military aircraft. She is forty-two now,
though she looks older—there's a tightness around her eyes that hasn't loosened in the three years
since she left active duty. Her husband Mark sits beside her, close but not quite touching, the way
they've learned to sit since she came home.

The story she tells unfolds methodically, the way she describes everything. In 2021, after twelve
years flying combat missions, Sarah requested a sabbatical. She wanted to do something meaningful
outside the military structure that had defined her entire adult life. She volunteered with an
international relief organization and was deployed to a region torn apart by civil conflict. For
four months, she flew humanitarian missions—delivering medical supplies, evacuating wounded
civilians, transporting aid workers between remote clinics.

On her seventy-third mission, everything changed.

She was coordinating with ground personnel at a makeshift clinic when the building across the street
took a direct hit. Sarah saw the explosion from the window—the bloom of dust, the secondary
collapse. Without thinking, she ran outside. The clinic staff tried to stop her, but she was already
moving. She found them in the rubble: two nurses and a logistics coordinator she'd flown in just
days before. One was already gone. The others were trapped beneath concrete and twisted metal.

Sarah pulled at the debris with her bare hands while waiting for emergency services. She could feel
the pulse of the younger nurse—a woman named Elena, maybe thirty—flickering beneath her fingers. The
nurse died before the ambulance arrived. Sarah held her hand through it.

She finished her contract, though she was barely present. Back home, she told Mark it was fine, that
she was processing it normally. But the normal never came.

Now, three years later, Sarah exists in a state of constant vigilance that exhausts everyone around
her. She cannot sleep past 3 a.m., the time the explosion occurred. Most nights, she doesn't try.
Instead, she sits on the back porch, scanning the darkness, her body rigid with an alertness that
has no target. When she does sleep, she dreams she's back in the rubble, her hands passing through
Elena's body like she's made of water.

Loud noises send her into a state of acute panic. Last month, Mark dropped a pot in the kitchen, and
Sarah found herself on the floor behind the couch before she understood what had happened. She spent
two hours there, breathing carefully, waiting for her heart to slow. She's stopped going to the
grocery store. The fluorescent lights and crowded aisles trigger an overwhelming sense of danger.

She's withdrawn from her flying community, from friends, from most of life. She tells Mark she's
"fine" constantly, but he watches her stare at nothing for hours. She's developed a grim habit of
researching conflict zones obsessively—reading casualty reports, tracking news from the region where
Elena died, as though staying informed might somehow change what happened.

Sarah knows intellectually that she cannot have saved Elena. She knows the building was hit by
forces beyond her control. But her nervous system has not accepted this knowledge. It remains
convinced that if she stays vigilant enough, if she never lets her guard down, if she keeps bearing
witness through endless news cycles and sleepless nights, she might somehow undo what cannot be
undone.
