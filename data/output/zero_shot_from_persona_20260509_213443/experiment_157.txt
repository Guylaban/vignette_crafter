# Detective Marcus

Marcus is a twenty-six-year-old detective with the city police department, a position he worked
toward deliberately throughout his twenties. He has a sharp mind for detail and an ability to remain
composed under pressure—qualities that made him excellent at his job. He's been married to David for
three years, and until eighteen months ago, he believed he had built a stable, happy life.

The abuse began subtly. David's criticism of Marcus's work hours escalated into accusations of
infidelity. When Marcus tried to discuss boundaries, David would become explosive, throwing objects
across their apartment. Marcus found himself apologizing for things he hadn't done, rearranging his
schedule to appease David, anything to prevent the next eruption. As a detective trained to read
people and situations, Marcus recognizes the pattern now—the cycle of tension, incident,
reconciliation—but in the moment, he was caught in a kind of cognitive dissonance. This was his
husband. This wasn't supposed to happen to him.

One evening, after Marcus mentioned grabbing drinks with a colleague, David attacked him. The
confrontation escalated from shoving to David pinning Marcus against the kitchen counter, landing
repeated blows to his ribs and face. Marcus, trained in restraint and de-escalation, did not fight
back. He froze. Afterward, David wept and apologized profusely, and Marcus made the decision to
leave the next morning.

That was eighteen months ago. Marcus filed for divorce and moved into a small apartment. Externally,
he returned to work, to the routines and structures that had always grounded him. Internally,
everything fractured.

Now, Marcus sits at his desk at the precinct and finds his hands trembling when he reviews case
files involving intimate partner violence. He's hyperaware of every movement his colleagues make
around him—a sudden gesture, a raised voice during interrogation, and his body floods with
adrenaline. He excuses himself to the bathroom to breathe through the panic. His sergeant has
noticed him requesting desk assignments rather than field work, something uncharacteristic for
someone who loved the investigative process.

Sleep is nearly impossible. Marcus lies awake replaying the evening, wondering what he could have
done differently, whether he somehow provoked it. When he does sleep, he experiences vivid
nightmares where he's back in that kitchen, unable to move, unable to call for help. He wakes
gasping, drenched in sweat, and often doesn't return to bed.

His wife—he still thinks of David that way sometimes, despite the divorce papers—appears in
unexpected places. Marcus will be at the grocery store and see someone with David's build, and his
entire nervous system goes into overdrive. He's begun avoiding certain routes, certain
establishments, narrowing his world to feel safer. He's stopped attending the gym where he used to
find solace, stopped meeting friends for dinner. His apartment has become smaller and smaller.

What troubles Marcus most is the shame. He's a detective. He's trained to recognize danger, to
protect himself and others. Yet he was unable to protect himself from someone who claimed to love
him. This contradiction plays on repeat in his mind. When colleagues mention their relationships
casually, Marcus feels a deep isolation—they wouldn't understand. They might question his
competence, wonder if the same man who missed the warning signs in his own home can be trusted to
investigate others'.

He hasn't told anyone at work the full truth. He's constructed a narrative about irreconcilable
differences, about growing apart. The lie feels necessary for survival, yet it compounds his sense
of disconnection from the people around him.
