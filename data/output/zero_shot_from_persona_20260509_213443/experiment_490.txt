# Dr. Thomas Hartley

Thomas sits in the leather chair across from me with his hands folded in his lap, a posture he
maintains throughout most of our sessions. At sixty-eight, he carries himself with the careful
deliberation of someone who has spent four decades teaching others about the human mind. His silver
hair is precisely groomed, his shirt pressed, his tie perfectly knotted. Everything about his
presentation speaks to control—which makes sense, given how much of it he's lost.

Thirty-two years ago, Thomas was in a long-term relationship with Michael, a man he believed he
would spend his life with. They had built a life together in a quiet suburb, had established
routines, had integrated into each other's worlds. Thomas was already established in his psychology
practice by then, respected in academic circles, someone colleagues came to for advice. He had
learned early in his career to compartmentalize, to maintain professional boundaries, to observe
rather than feel.

Michael's drinking had escalated gradually—so gradually that Thomas, trained as he was to recognize
patterns, somehow missed the significance of it. Or perhaps he didn't miss it. Perhaps he simply
believed, as he tells me now with a bitter laugh, that he could manage it, understand it, apply his
professional knowledge to fix it at home.

The night that changed everything started like many others. Michael came home intoxicated, angry
about something trivial—a comment Thomas had made weeks earlier that Michael had been turning over
in his mind. The argument escalated quickly. Thomas remembers trying to use his voice, his words,
his training. He remembers thinking he could de-escalate the situation the way he would with a
crisis client. He remembers, very clearly, the moment Michael's fist connected with his face, and
then the moment after that when Thomas stopped trying to talk.

The beating lasted perhaps five minutes. It felt much longer. Thomas has told me he kept waiting for
himself to fight back, to do something, but his body seemed to belong to someone else. He was
observing it happen—the psychologist observing the patient. When it was over, Michael cried and
apologized. Thomas cleaned up the blood and went to bed.

He didn't leave. He told himself it was an anomaly, a crisis moment that wouldn't repeat. It
repeated. Over the course of three years, it repeated many times, each incident followed by
apologies and promises, each incident preceded by Thomas's increasing sense of unreality, as though
he were watching his own life from outside it.

When he finally left, Thomas was fifty-one years old. He moved into an apartment alone and
eventually, five years later, met his current wife, Margaret, whom he married. Margaret is kind and
patient and knows nothing about what happened, though Thomas suspects she senses something in the
way he flinches sometimes when she moves too quickly.

Now, at sixty-eight, Thomas still teaches. His students still respect him. But he lies awake most
nights, his heart racing at unexpected sounds. He has stopped going to department functions because
crowds feel dangerous. When Margaret reaches for him in bed, he sometimes goes rigid, his mind
suddenly somewhere else entirely. He has recently retired from his private practice, telling
colleagues it was time, though the truth is that sitting with patients has become increasingly
difficult. He finds himself unable to focus, unable to be present. He is too busy monitoring the
room for threats, too busy managing the constant low-level dread that lives in his chest.

He has never told anyone what happened. Not his wife. Not his colleagues. Not his therapist, until
now. Thomas is learning, slowly and with great resistance, that understanding trauma intellectually
is not the same as surviving it.
