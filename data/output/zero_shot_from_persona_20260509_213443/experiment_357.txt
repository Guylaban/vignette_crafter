# Dr. Linh's Careful Distance

Dr. Linh has been practicing emergency medicine for twenty-three years. Her colleagues describe her
as unflappable—the kind of physician who moves through chaos with purpose, who remembers patient
names and asks about their children. She arrives early, stays late, and has trained three
generations of residents. But for the past eight months, something has shifted.

It started on a Tuesday evening in the emergency department. A man came in agitated, bleeding from a
laceration on his forearm. Dr. Linh was suturing the wound when he suddenly became aggressive,
accusing her of poisoning him. She tried to de-escalate, speaking quietly in her practiced, calm
voice. Security was called, but before they arrived, the man lunged at her, striking her face and
neck repeatedly. Other staff pulled him away. She has a small scar above her left eyebrow that
catches the light when she turns her head.

The physical injuries healed within weeks. The other injuries have not.

Dr. Linh returns to work two weeks later, as she had always imagined she would. She is a doctor; she
understands trauma. She has treated hundreds of assault victims. She knows the statistics, the
recovery trajectories, the importance of returning to routine. But in the department now, her body
operates on a different frequency than her mind intends.

She finds herself positioning her body differently during patient interactions—angled toward the
door, never fully turning her back. When patients raise their voices, even in pain or frustration,
her heart rate spikes. She has become hypervigilant in a space she once felt in control. The
fluorescent lights seem harsher. The sounds of the monitors and alarms, which once formed the
comfortable backdrop of her professional life, now feel threatening. She startles easily.

At home, her husband notices she no longer relaxes on the couch beside him in the evenings. She sits
on the edge of the cushion, her body tense. She has stopped going to the community center where she
used to swim. The thought of being in a space where she cannot see all the exits makes her stomach
clench. She lies awake most nights, and when she does sleep, she dreams of hands reaching for her
face, of being unable to move. She wakes with her heart pounding, gasping as though she has been
underwater.

The intrusive moments come without warning. A patient with similar features to her attacker will
walk in, and suddenly she is back in that moment—the shock of being vulnerable, of her expertise and
authority meaning nothing. Her hands shake slightly as she continues the examination, hoping no one
notices.

Dr. Linh finds herself making excuses to avoid certain shifts. She volunteers for administrative
tasks, something she never did before. She has begun to wonder if she can continue in emergency
medicine at all. This thought terrifies her more than the assault itself. Her identity has been
built around this work, around being the steady presence in crisis. Now, she feels like a fraud—a
physician who cannot manage her own nervous system.

She tells no one at work. She has confided only in her husband, and even then, she minimizes. She is
fifty-one years old and has spent her entire adult life in control. The idea of being labeled as
traumatized, of needing help, of admitting that an eight-minute encounter has fundamentally altered
how she moves through the world—this feels impossible.

Yet she comes in each shift, watching herself from a distance, wondering when the person she was
before will return.
