# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus sits with his hands folded tightly in his lap, his jaw working slightly as though he's
grinding his teeth. He's a lean man with dark eyes that seem to look past rather than at the person
across from him. When he speaks, his voice is measured and controlled, as though each word has been
carefully selected before leaving his mouth.

Three years ago, Marcus was assaulted by a colleague during a late night at the clinic where he was
working as a physical therapist. The assault happened in the staff break room after hours—a space
where Marcus had felt safe countless times before. His attacker, a senior therapist Marcus
respected, had cornered him and forced sexual contact. The assault lasted perhaps ten minutes,
though Marcus describes time as fragmented during the event. What he remembers most clearly is the
feeling of being unable to move, unable to speak, unable to do anything but endure.

Marcus didn't report the assault immediately. For weeks afterward, he told no one—not his wife
Layla, not his supervisor, not his family. Instead, he threw himself into work with an intensity
that bordered on obsessive. He began arriving earlier and staying later, always ensuring he was
never alone, always keeping his clinic door open. His colleagues noticed he seemed tense, but
attributed it to the usual pressures of the profession. Layla noticed something was wrong but Marcus
deflected her concerns, telling her he was simply stressed about a difficult patient case.

The secrecy compounded everything. Marcus began experiencing intrusive images of the assault at
unpredictable moments—sometimes while treating a patient's shoulder, sometimes while driving home.
His body would suddenly flood with adrenaline. His hands would shake. Once, while performing manual
therapy on a patient's back, the physical proximity triggered such intense panic that he had to
excuse himself to the bathroom where he sat hyperventilating for fifteen minutes.

Sleep became fragmented and unreliable. Marcus would lie awake beside Layla for hours, his mind
replaying the assault in excruciating detail. When he did sleep, nightmares woke him gasping. He
began sleeping in the guest room, telling Layla his back was bothering him. In reality, he couldn't
bear the vulnerability of sleeping next to another person.

Eight months after the assault, Marcus finally told Layla. The conversation was agonizing. He felt
shame—a deep, pervasive sense that somehow he should have prevented what happened, that his
inability to fight back was a personal failure. Layla's initial shock gave way to support, but
Marcus found he couldn't fully accept her comfort. When she tried to hold him, he would tense and
pull away. He wanted her closeness but his body wouldn't allow it.

Now, Marcus continues working as a physical therapist, though the work that once brought him genuine
satisfaction has become something he endures. He's hypervigilant around colleagues, particularly
those he perceives as physically stronger or more dominant. He finds himself monitoring exits in
rooms, maintaining distance from people, avoiding the break room entirely. He's taken a leave from
the clinic's evening hours, claiming scheduling changes, but the real reason is that darkness now
feels dangerous in ways it never did before.

The assault has fractured his sense of safety in his own body and in spaces he once inhabited
without fear. Marcus describes feeling like he's living behind glass—present in his life but
separated from it, watching himself go through the motions of being a husband, a therapist, a
functioning adult, while internally remaining trapped in that break room where time stopped.
