# A Life Interrupted

Meera has worked the register at the same neighborhood grocery store for twenty-three years. She
knows the regulars by name, remembers which customers prefer paper bags, and takes pride in the
small kindnesses of her job—chatting with elderly customers, helping someone find an item, wishing
people well as they leave. This consistency has been her anchor, especially since her husband died
eight years ago. The store became her routine, her purpose, her reason to wake each morning.

Three months ago, everything fractured.

It was a Tuesday evening in October. Meera had stayed late to help with inventory, something she'd
done countless times before. The store was nearly empty, just her and one manager restocking shelves
in the back. A man she'd never seen came through the rear entrance—a door that should have been
locked. What happened next exists in her mind as fragments: his voice, rough and demanding; her own
voice saying no, repeating it; the shock of physical force; the smell of him; the terrible awareness
that no one could hear her.

Afterward, she called the police. There was an investigation. A detective visited her home, asking
questions in a careful voice. The man was eventually arrested. But the legal process, meant to bring
closure, instead stretched the trauma across months. Each court date meant reliving it. Each time
she had to describe what happened, something inside her broke a little more.

Now, three months later, Meera hasn't returned to work. She tells herself she will, but each
morning, the thought of entering that store—walking past the back entrance, standing at the register
where she spent so many years—creates a physical sensation of dread that makes her stomach clench.
She's called in sick repeatedly. The store manager, kind at first, has begun asking when she might
return. She doesn't know.

At night, Meera lies awake, her mind replaying the assault in excruciating detail. She's tried
sleeping pills, but they leave her groggy and vulnerable, and she wakes gasping anyway. Sometimes
she jolts awake at 2 a.m., certain she hears someone at her door. She checks the locks—all three of
them—sometimes twice in one night. She's installed a new deadbolt and considered a security system,
though she's not sure it would help.

During the day, she moves through her small apartment in a fog. She's lost weight; cooking feels
pointless when she has no appetite. Her daughter calls from California, worried, but Meera finds it
hard to explain. How do you tell your child that you don't feel safe in your own home? That you
can't imagine going back to a place where you felt so utterly powerless?

The grocery store calls again today. The manager is sympathetic but firm—she needs to know when
Meera will return, or they'll need to discuss other options. Meera sits on her couch, the phone in
her hand, unable to dial back. She looks out the window at the street below, at people moving
through the world with what seems like careless freedom. She envies them. She envies her former
self, the version of Meera who could work a shift without checking over her shoulder, who could lock
a door and feel safe.

She wonders if that version of herself still exists, or if she's been permanently replaced by this
frightened woman who can barely leave her apartment.
