# Maya's Story

Maya is a thirty-year-old woman with dark eyes that seem to hold something guarded, even when she
smiles. She works the evening shift at a cocktail bar in the city, a job she's held for the past
five years. She's quick-witted with regulars, knows everyone's usual order, and can read a room with
impressive accuracy. But there's a brittleness to her ease, a performance quality that becomes
apparent once you sit with her long enough.

She grew up in a household where her father's moods determined the temperature of everything. Her
mother moved through their home like she was trying to take up as little space as possible. Maya
remembers being seven years old, hiding in the hallway closet while her father's voice thundered
through the apartment, while her mother's protests grew quieter and quieter. She remembers the sound
of breaking glass—she's still not entirely sure if it was a dish or a window—and the terrible
silence that followed. Her father left that night. He came back three weeks later with flowers and
apologies. Her mother let him stay.

This pattern repeated throughout Maya's childhood. The tension building like weather before a storm.
The explosion. The aftermath where everything felt fragile and temporary. She learned to read her
father's jaw, the particular way he held his shoulders when anger was gathering. She learned to make
herself small. She learned that love and violence could wear the same face.

At eighteen, Maya moved out and tried to build something different. She married at twenty-four—a man
who seemed gentle, who didn't raise his voice. But three years in, she began to notice how he'd
position himself between her and the door during arguments. How his "jokes" about her intelligence
became more frequent. How she found herself apologizing for things that weren't her fault, just to
keep the peace. She left him two years ago, though the leaving itself was its own kind of
violence—the things he said, the threats about custody of their dog, the way he showed up at the bar
one night and watched her from the corner.

Now, at thirty, Maya finds herself caught in a body that won't quite settle. Loud noises make her
flinch—a glass breaking at the bar sends her heart into her throat for hours afterward. She's
hyperaware of exits, of where men are standing relative to her, of the particular cadence of raised
voices. She's had three relationships since the divorce, and each one has ended because she couldn't
quite trust, couldn't quite stop waiting for the other shoe to drop. She sabotages things. She
pushes people away before they can hurt her.

At the bar, she's learned that keeping busy helps. The noise of the crowd, the focus required to
make drinks, the social interaction—these things quiet the static in her mind. But on slow nights,
when there's time to think, the memories surface. She finds herself replaying moments: her mother's
face, her own small voice calling out. She wonders what her life might have looked like if she'd
grown up differently, if she'd learned that conflict could be resolved without someone getting hurt.

She doesn't sleep well. She wakes at 3 a.m. with her heart racing, sometimes unable to remember what
she was dreaming about, only knowing that something felt wrong. She's considered therapy before but
hasn't followed through—there's a part of her that believes this is just how she is, that some
people are simply born to be vigilant, to never quite rest.
