# Marcus

Marcus sits in the leather chair with his flight jacket draped over his knee, the leather worn soft
at the creases. At thirty-two, he carries himself with the controlled precision of someone trained
to manage split-second decisions at forty thousand feet. But his hands betray him—they move
restlessly, fingers drumming against the jacket in irregular patterns he doesn't seem to notice.

The assault happened three years ago during a training deployment in the Middle East. Marcus had
been celebrating a successful mission with fellow pilots at a base bar. He remembers the
camaraderie, the relief of a dangerous day ending well. He remembers accepting a drink from someone
he thought was a friend. After that, the memories fracture into sensations he wishes he could
unspool and examine, but instead they come in violent flashes he can't control.

He woke in a supply room. A superior officer was there. Marcus still won't say exactly what
happened, only that it was violent, that he felt utterly powerless in a way that contradicted
everything his training had prepared him for. He was a man trained to command multi-million-dollar
aircraft, to make life-or-death decisions in milliseconds, reduced to paralysis in the dark.

For months afterward, Marcus told no one. He returned to active duty and continued flying. In the
cockpit, he could still function—the procedures were automatic, the focus absolute. But outside the
cockpit, something had fractured. He began having nightmares where he couldn't eject from his
aircraft, where the controls wouldn't respond. He'd wake gasping, his sheets soaked through, his
heart hammering as though he'd actually crashed.

He started drinking heavily. Not during flight operations—his record remained pristine—but in the
evenings, alone in his quarters. The alcohol bought him a few hours of numbness, a reprieve from the
constant hypervigilance that had become his baseline. He'd find himself scanning rooms when he
entered them, noting exits and potential threats. In the officers' club, he'd position himself with
his back to the wall. Around men he'd once trusted, he felt a visceral tension in his chest.

The real crisis came when Marcus couldn't fly. Not physically—his medical certifications remained
valid. But during a routine training mission, something in his nervous system simply refused. As he
sat in the cockpit, going through preflight checks, his hands began shaking. His breathing became
rapid and shallow. The familiar space that had always been his refuge suddenly felt like a trap. He
aborted the mission and reported sick.

That's when the military medical system finally pulled him out of operations and into evaluation.
The diagnosis came quickly, but the word itself—PTSD—felt like admitting defeat in a way that
terrified him more than the symptoms themselves.

Now, in civilian life and in therapy, Marcus is trying to rebuild. He's on medical leave from the
Air Force, living in a small apartment where he keeps the blinds drawn. He's stopped drinking,
though the urge is constant. Some days are manageable. He can sit in a coffee shop for twenty
minutes. He's started going to the gym, where the controlled environment and solitary focus feel
safe.

But many nights, he still can't sleep. He lies in bed waiting for his mind to betray him again, for
the fragmentary memories to crystallize into something he has to relive. He's lost the identity that
defined him for over a decade—the pilot, the warrior, the man in control. He's learning, slowly and
with considerable resistance, that there might be something else he can become.
