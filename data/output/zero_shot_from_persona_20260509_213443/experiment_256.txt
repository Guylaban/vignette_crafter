# Marcus

Marcus sits in the worn leather chair across from me, his fingers fidgeting with the zipper of his
jacket. At thirty-five, he carries himself with the careful posture of someone who has learned to
make himself smaller, less noticeable. He teaches special education at a middle school in the city—a
job he's held for eight years and once loved deeply. His students with learning disabilities and
behavioral challenges responded to his patience and creativity. He designed lessons that made them
feel capable. Now, he's not sure he can go back.

Three months ago, Marcus was walking home from the grocery store on a Tuesday evening. He'd taken
the same route dozens of times, cut through the park near his apartment as a shortcut. A man
approached from behind, demanded his wallet. Marcus remembers the voice more than the face—rough,
urgent, terrifying in its absoluteness. When he hesitated, fumbling with his back pocket, the man
shoved him hard against a tree. Marcus hit his shoulder, felt the wallet torn from his hands, heard
footsteps running away. The whole thing lasted maybe ninety seconds.

What happened in those ninety seconds has stretched across three months like a stain that won't wash
out.

Marcus tells me he's jumpy now—constantly. When his colleague knocked on his classroom door last
week, he flinched so violently that papers scattered from his desk. His heart races without warning.
He's started taking the longer route home, avoiding the park entirely, even though it adds thirty
minutes to his commute. Some nights he doesn't sleep at all, lying in bed replaying the moment of
impact, imagining different outcomes, worse outcomes. Other nights he wakes at 3 a.m. gasping,
convinced someone is in his apartment.

The worst part, he says quietly, is what's happened at school. He loves his students—he really
does—but lately he finds himself struggling to focus during lessons. His mind drifts to the mugging,
to vulnerability, to the randomness of it all. He's become hypervigilant in the hallways, scanning
faces, noticing exits. A student named Devon, who has oppositional defiant disorder and sometimes
gets aggressive during frustration, triggered something in Marcus two weeks ago. Devon raised his
voice during a math lesson, and Marcus felt that same surge of panic and helplessness from the park.
He snapped at Devon in a way he never has before—harsh, unkind. He apologized afterward, but the
shame has stayed with him.

Marcus has stopped going out socially. His brother invited him to a barbecue; he declined. A friend
suggested coffee; he made an excuse. He doesn't want to explain what happened, doesn't want to see
the pity in people's eyes. Instead, he goes home, locks his door, and scrolls through his phone
until exhaustion finally wins.

He's also started drinking more—a few beers most nights, sometimes whiskey. He tells himself it
helps him sleep, and it does, in a way. But he wakes feeling hollowed out and ashamed, adding
another layer to the weight he's already carrying.

What troubles Marcus most is the sense that the world has fundamentally changed. He used to feel at
home in his city, at home in his own life. Now it all feels dangerous and unpredictable. He wonders
if he should quit teaching, if he's becoming someone his students shouldn't be around. He wonders if
he'll ever feel safe again, or if this is just who he is now—afraid, isolated, and slowly
disappearing.
