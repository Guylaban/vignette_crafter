# Margaret's Story

Margaret sits in the office with her hands folded neatly in her lap, a habit born from decades of
precision work. At sixty-five, she still maintains the meticulous appearance of someone accustomed
to control—her silver hair pinned back, her posture upright. But there's a brittleness to her
composure, like glass that's been tempered but remains fragile.

Two years ago, Margaret was driving home from her cybersecurity firm on a Tuesday evening. She'd
spent the day analyzing network vulnerabilities, work that required absolute focus and attention to
detail. The drive was familiar—the same route she'd taken for fifteen years, ever since her husband
Robert passed. The routine grounded her. On that particular evening, a delivery truck ran a red
light at an intersection near her home. The collision was violent and sudden. Margaret's sedan was
T-boned, the driver's side crushed inward. She remembers the sound more than the impact—a terrible
grinding of metal, the screech of brakes, then silence. She was trapped for forty minutes before
emergency responders extracted her. She suffered a fractured pelvis, several broken ribs, and a
concussion. Physically, she has healed.

The accident has rewritten her internal landscape in ways her body's recovery could not address.

Margaret no longer drives. She takes a car service to work, and even that requires preparation.
Before each ride, her heart begins to race. She finds herself checking the driver's credentials
obsessively, examining the vehicle's condition as though she might spot a mechanical failure that
would prevent departure. Once in motion, she grips the door handle and monitors every intersection
with hypervigilance. She knows the statistics—that she's statistically safer in a vehicle than
walking—but knowledge doesn't quiet the alarm bells in her nervous system.

Her work, which once provided deep satisfaction, has become complicated. The concentration that
cybersecurity demands now feels impossible to sustain. She'll be analyzing a security breach, fully
absorbed in the logic of it, when a car horn sounds outside her office building and her entire body
jolts. Her heart pounds. Her hands shake. She loses thirty minutes trying to refocus. This has
happened dozens of times. Her colleagues have noticed her performance declining, though no one has
mentioned it directly.

Sleep is fragmented. Margaret lies awake at night replaying the moment of impact, her mind cycling
through a loop of what-ifs. Sometimes she dreams she's still trapped in the car, unable to move,
unable to call for help. She wakes gasping, her nightgown soaked with sweat. She's tried sleep aids,
but they leave her groggy and unreliable—qualities she cannot tolerate in herself.

Margaret has withdrawn from her community. The book club she attended for twenty years? She stopped
going. The weekly dinners with her sister? Canceled repeatedly. She tells herself she's simply
tired, but the truth is more complex. Crowds feel dangerous. Unpredictability feels dangerous. The
world has become a place where terrible things happen without warning, where a moment of inattention
in someone else's vehicle can obliterate your sense of safety.

She still goes to work. She still manages her apartment. She maintains the external structure of her
life with the same precision she once applied to network security. But inside, Margaret exists in a
state of constant vigilance, waiting for the next impact, knowing intellectually that it may not
come while feeling absolutely certain that it will.
