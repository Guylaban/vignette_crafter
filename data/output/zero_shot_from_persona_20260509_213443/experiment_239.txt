# Dr. Roberto Martínez

Roberto sits in the waiting room fifteen minutes early for his appointment, a habit he's developed
over the past two years. At seventy, he maintains the posture of someone who once commanded a
room—shoulders back, chin level—though his hands betray him. They rest on his thighs, fingers
occasionally flexing as if working through muscle memory. He wears a pressed shirt despite the early
hour, a small assertion of control in a life that increasingly feels uncontrollable.

Twenty-three months ago, Roberto was attending a dental conference in Madrid. He'd been looking
forward to it for months—a chance to reconnect with colleagues from his training days, to see the
city where he'd spent a formative year of his residency. His wife, María, had wanted to join him,
but her arthritis had flared that week, so he'd gone alone. He remembers the morning of the attack
with crystalline clarity: the hotel lobby, the smell of fresh coffee, the way sunlight streamed
through the glass doors. He was reading an email on his phone when the first explosion occurred
outside the building. The sound didn't register as dangerous at first—something distant, almost
abstract. Then came the second blast, much closer, and the world became chaos.

He'd hidden in a storage room with twelve other people for four hours. The darkness was absolute.
Someone's phone eventually died. He remembers the weight of a woman's head on his shoulder as she
fell asleep from shock, remembers the man who kept whispering prayers in Portuguese, remembers his
own complete inability to move or speak. When police finally found them, Roberto had to be helped to
his feet—his legs had forgotten how to work.

The physical injuries healed within weeks. Three cracked ribs, a laceration on his forehead, minor
burns on his left arm. The invisible injuries have proven far more persistent.

For the first six months after returning home, Roberto tried to maintain his dental practice. He'd
built his reputation over forty-five years—he knew his patients by name, knew which ones were
anxious, which ones needed extra time. But his hands began to shake during procedures. Not tremors,
exactly, but a sudden loss of fine control that terrified him. He'd be scaling plaque from a molar
when his hand would jerk involuntarily, and he'd have to stop, excuse himself, steady himself
against the sink. He worried constantly about harming someone. Eventually, he took early retirement.

Now he fills his days with careful routines. He wakes at five, reads the newspaper from front to
back, takes a walk through his neighborhood at exactly seven o'clock. He avoids crowds, avoids
sudden noises, avoids the news. Fireworks on Independence Day sent him into his bedroom with the
door locked. A car backfired last month while he was at the market, and he found himself on the
floor between the produce aisles, unable to explain to the concerned manager why a seventy-year-old
man was lying on the ground.

The nights are worst. He sleeps poorly, often waking at two or three in the morning with his heart
racing, convinced he hears sirens. Sometimes he dreams he's still in that storage room, but the door
is welded shut. He wakes gasping, reaching for María before remembering she's been gone for three
years—a loss that somehow feels less real than the attack, less present in his daily consciousness.

His daughter worries about him. She visits every Sunday and tries to convince him that he's safe,
that Madrid was an isolated incident, that he should consider leaving the house more often. He loves
her, but he doesn't know how to explain that safety is no longer something he can feel, only
something he can construct through meticulous control and avoidance.
