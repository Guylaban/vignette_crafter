# Margaret

Margaret sits in the office with her hands folded in her lap, her posture upright but somehow
collapsed inward. At sixty-one, she carries herself with the careful precision of someone who has
spent decades managing details for government agencies, but there's a brittleness to her composure
that wasn't there before.

Seven months ago, Margaret was working late at the environmental policy office, reviewing air
quality standards for an upcoming legislative session. Around 9 p.m., a storage container in the
basement began leaking—a hazardous industrial solvent used in equipment maintenance. She noticed the
smell first, acrid and chemical, drifting up through the ventilation system. By the time she reached
the stairwell, her eyes were burning and her throat felt raw. She made it outside, but not before
breathing in significant amounts of the fumes.

The physical effects were immediate: nausea, difficulty breathing, chemical burns in her nasal
passages. She spent three nights in the hospital undergoing observation. The doctors assured her
that her acute symptoms would resolve, and medically, they largely did. But something shifted that
night that Margaret couldn't quite explain to anyone—not to her husband, not to her doctor, not even
to herself at first.

Now, she finds herself hypervigilant in ways that feel both foreign and inescapable. When she
returns to the office—which she's tried to do several times—she becomes acutely aware of every
smell. The copy machine triggers it. Cleaning supplies trigger it. Even the faint odor of new carpet
makes her chest tighten and her breathing shallow. She's convinced something else is leaking, even
when facilities assures her the building is safe. Her colleagues have noticed her sniffing the air,
moving toward windows, asking if anyone else smells anything. She feels foolish but cannot stop
herself.

Sleep has become a negotiation with her own mind. She drifts off around midnight but jolts awake at
2 or 3 a.m., her heart racing, her body drenched in sweat. In these moments, she's back in that
stairwell—the burning in her lungs, the panic that she might not be able to breathe. She lies beside
her sleeping husband, afraid to move, afraid to wake him, trapped in the dark with her racing
thoughts. Some nights she doesn't return to bed at all, instead sitting in the kitchen with herbal
tea, waiting for dawn.

Her concentration at work has fractured. She's always been someone who could hold complex policy
frameworks in her mind, who took pride in meticulous analysis. Now she reads the same paragraph
three times and retains nothing. She's made small errors in reports—nothing catastrophic yet, but
enough to shake her confidence. She's taken to working from home more often, telling herself it's
more efficient, but really it's because the office feels unsafe in a way she knows intellectually is
irrational.

What troubles her most is the sense of vulnerability that has settled into her bones. She was
exposed to something dangerous, something she couldn't see or control, something that invaded her
body without her consent. Even as her lungs have healed, she can't shake the feeling that her body
might betray her again. She finds herself researching toxic exposures late at night, reading worst-
case scenarios, then feeling foolish for catastrophizing.

Her husband has been patient, but she can see the concern in his eyes, the way he watches her. She
wants to be the person she was before—capable, steady, unshaken. But that person feels very far
away.
