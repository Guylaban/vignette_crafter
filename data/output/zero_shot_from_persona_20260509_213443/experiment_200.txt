# Sarah's Story

Sarah sits in the waiting room of her apartment, laptop open but untouched, scrolling through her
phone with the kind of mechanical efficiency that suggests she's doing it without really seeing. At
thirty-one, she has the polished appearance of someone accustomed to curating images for a
living—her dark hair is perfectly styled, her makeup carefully applied—but there's a brittleness to
her composure, a tension in her shoulders that no amount of self-care can mask.

Three years ago, Sarah was driving home from a client dinner in downtown Portland. She'd been a
social media manager for a mid-sized tech company, the kind of role that demands constant vigilance,
constant awareness of what's trending, what's being said, what image needs to be projected. That
night, she was mentally composing a post about the company's new product launch. She glanced down at
her phone for perhaps two seconds—just long enough to jot down a phrase that had come to her.

When she looked back up, a man was in the crosswalk. She doesn't remember hitting him, though she
remembers the sound, and she will never forget the sound. What she remembers with crystalline
clarity is the moment her car stopped, the way her hands were shaking so violently she couldn't
unbuckle her seatbelt, and then the sight of him on the pavement, his leg bent at an angle that made
her stomach turn inside out.

He survived. That's what everyone told her, as if survival alone should be enough to ease the
weight. He survived, but he lost most of the function in his left leg. He spent four months in
physical therapy. He sued, eventually, and the settlement was substantial—Sarah paid it without
contest, without negotiation.

But survival, she's learned, is a complicated thing.

For the first year, Sarah threw herself into work with an intensity that bordered on obsessive.
She'd arrive at the office early and leave late, crafting content, managing crises, building
engagement metrics. The work felt safe because it was quantifiable, because she could see the
results in real time. But somewhere around month fourteen, she noticed she couldn't drive anymore.
Not really. She could operate the vehicle—she could turn the wheel and press the pedals—but her
hands would sweat through her gloves, her vision would narrow, and she'd find herself holding her
breath for entire stretches of road.

Now she works from home, which her employer permits because she's good at what she does. But home
has become its own kind of prison. She finds herself checking and rechecking her apartment locks,
sometimes three or four times after she's already secured them. She's hyperaware of every sound—a
car horn, a siren, the screech of brakes—and her body responds before her mind can catch up,
adrenaline flooding through her like she's still in that moment of impact.

Her ex-husband left two years ago. He said he couldn't live with her anxiety, that it was
suffocating him. She didn't argue. She understood, in a way, that she had become someone
different—someone fragmented, someone whose mind kept returning to that crosswalk, to that man's
face, to the knowledge that a moment of inattention had irrevocably altered two lives.

She still manages social media accounts, still crafts narratives for brands, still helps companies
tell their stories. But she does it from behind a screen, from a space she can control, where the
only damage she can cause is digital and therefore somehow less real. She doesn't drive past the
intersection where it happened. She doesn't drive past it because she rarely drives at all.
