# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus is a 37-year-old clinical psychologist who specializes in trauma work at a community mental
health center in a mid-sized city. He carries himself with a composed professionalism that has
served him well in his career—colleagues describe him as thoughtful, insightful, and genuinely
invested in his clients' wellbeing. Yet over the past eighteen months, the careful architecture of
his life has begun to crack in ways he struggles to articulate to anyone, including himself.

Three years ago, Marcus attended a conference in another state. On the final evening, he accepted a
ride from a colleague he'd known professionally for years. What began as a conversation in the car
escalated into a situation where Marcus found himself physically overpowered and sexually assaulted.
The assault lasted perhaps twenty minutes, though Marcus describes time as having become unreliable
in his memory—sometimes it feels like hours, sometimes like seconds. What he remembers with
crystalline clarity is the feeling of powerlessness, the shock that this was happening to him, the
shame that bloomed even as it was occurring.

He reported the assault to police, but the case never progressed. The colleague denied everything,
and without corroborating witnesses, the investigation stalled. Marcus returned to his city and
tried to move forward. For months, he functioned adequately. He saw clients, attended supervision,
maintained his apartment. But somewhere around the eighteen-month mark, the compartmentalization
that had carried him began to fail.

Now, Marcus finds himself caught in a strange professional irony. He sits with clients who describe
their own trauma, and he can feel their pain with genuine empathy—yet increasingly, he cannot sit
with his own. During sessions, he sometimes becomes hyperaware of where he sits relative to his
client, whether the door is locked, whether he could leave quickly if needed. He's started
scheduling only female clients, though he hasn't consciously admitted this to himself. When male
clients do appear on his schedule, his chest tightens days in advance.

Sleep has become a minefield. Marcus lies awake for hours, his mind cycling through scenarios—what
he should have done differently, what he could have said, whether he somehow invited the assault
through his own naivety. When he does sleep, he wakes gasping, his body convinced that he is still
in that car, still trapped, still unable to move. He's taken to sleeping with his bedroom door
locked and a knife on his nightstand, a precaution that he knows is irrational but cannot abandon.

Socially, Marcus has withdrawn. He declines invitations from colleagues, avoids the conference
circuit that once energized him, and has let friendships drift into dormancy. Dating feels
impossible—the thought of physical intimacy triggers a visceral dread that he cannot reason away,
even as he intellectually understands its source. He finds himself drinking more than he used to,
usually alone in the evening, a habit he recognizes with the clarity that comes from professional
training but feels powerless to change.

What troubles Marcus most is the internal contradiction of his existence. He spends his days helping
others process trauma, offering them frameworks and hope, yet he cannot extend that same compassion
to himself. There is a particular ache in knowing the therapeutic language, understanding the
neurobiology of what is happening to him, and still being unable to stop it from happening. His
expertise has become both his greatest resource and, in some moments, his deepest source of shame.
