# Sarah

Sarah is a compact woman with calloused hands and an efficient manner of moving through space. She's
been a plumber for eight years, one of the few women in her trade in her region, and she carries
herself with the quiet confidence of someone who knows how to solve problems. She's been married to
James for four years, and they're talking about having children, which is partly what brought her to
therapy. "I want to be different than my mum," she says simply in our first session.

Sarah grew up in a small town with her mother, who worked long hours as a nurse. Money was always
tight, and her mother was often exhausted. When Sarah was young—somewhere between five and eight,
the exact timeline blurs in her memory—her mother began hitting her. Not sporadic, frustrated swats,
but deliberate strikes with a belt, with hands, sometimes with whatever was nearby. The beatings
often came without warning, sometimes triggered by small infractions, sometimes seemingly at random.
Sarah learned to read the signs in her mother's face, the particular tension in her shoulders, and
she'd try to disappear into her room or make herself very small and very quiet. It didn't always
work.

By adolescence, the physical abuse had stopped, though Sarah can't quite remember when it ended or
why. What remained was a different kind of vigilance, a permanent scanning of her environment for
threat.

Now, at thirty, Sarah finds herself startled easily. When James comes up behind her in the
kitchen—which he does frequently, affectionately—her whole body flinches. Her heart races. Sometimes
she swings around defensively before she even recognizes him. He's learned not to take it
personally, but she can see the hurt in his eyes. She apologizes every time, feels terrible every
time, and it happens again the next week.

Her work as a plumber actually suits her in some ways. She likes the solitude of working in crawl
spaces and under sinks, the problem-solving, the tangible satisfaction of fixing something broken.
But she's noticed she's become hypervigilant with clients, especially older women who remind her of
her mother. She finds herself tensing, preparing for criticism or anger that never comes. Last
month, a client raised her voice about a billing error, and Sarah had to excuse herself to the
bathroom where she sat shaking for ten minutes.

Sleep is fragmented. Sarah wakes most nights in the early hours, her mind immediately alert, her
body tense. Sometimes she has nightmares she can't quite remember upon waking, just the residual
feeling of dread and the sensation of being trapped. Other nights, she simply lies awake, her mind
cataloguing potential dangers.

She drinks more than she probably should—a glass of wine most evenings, sometimes two. It helps her
sleep, she tells herself, though it doesn't really. She's also started avoiding situations where she
might feel trapped or powerless: crowded places, situations with authority figures, even some social
gatherings where she feels exposed.

What troubles Sarah most is how her past is shadowing her future. She wants to be a mother, but the
thought terrifies her. What if she loses her temper? What if she becomes her mother? She also
worries about James. He's patient and kind, but how long can he tolerate her constant startle
responses, her occasional emotional distance, her need to maintain control? She loves him deeply and
is terrified of losing him because she can't seem to regulate her own nervous system.

"I know it wasn't my fault," she says. "But I still feel like something's wrong with me."
