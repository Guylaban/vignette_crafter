# Sarah's Story

Sarah sits in the worn office chair across from her desk, reviewing case files for her afternoon
intake appointments. The fluorescent lights hum overhead—a sound that usually fades into background
noise—but today it feels intrusive, almost threatening. She adjusts the small lamp beside her
computer, dimming the overhead brightness. Her colleagues have stopped commenting on this habit.

At fifty, Sarah carries herself with the composed professionalism of someone who has spent twenty-
five years in social work. She knows how to listen without flinching, how to validate suffering
without absorbing it. But there is a brittleness beneath her competence that wasn't always there.

She was born in Damascus to a family of modest means. Her father was a teacher; her mother kept
their home with meticulous care. Sarah was studying nursing when the war began in 2011. She
remembers the specific day—March 18th, a Friday—when the security forces came to their neighborhood.
She was twenty-seven years old.

What began as protests became something darker. Her family decided to leave in stages. Sarah's
younger brother went first, then her parents. She stayed to help her aunt whose husband was ill.
That decision has haunted her for thirteen years.

The night of her escape comes back to her in fragments that feel more real than her current life.
The sound of boots on concrete. The way her aunt's hands shook as they packed a single bag. The
checkpoint where a young soldier—he couldn't have been more than nineteen—looked at their papers and
waved them through. Sarah has never understood why. She has spent thousands of hours wondering if he
was tired, if he was kind, if it was simply luck. The randomness of survival has never settled in
her mind as acceptable.

She arrived in the United States on a humanitarian visa, alone. Her family was already in
California. The first year was a blur of paperwork, language classes, and bureaucratic navigation
that felt almost as disorienting as the war itself. She threw herself into social work—helping other
displaced families, processing their trauma, connecting them to resources. It felt like purpose. It
felt like redemption for having survived when others hadn't.

But survival has its costs.

Sarah wakes most mornings at 3:47 a.m., her heart racing as though she's been running. She doesn't
remember the dreams, only the terror they leave behind. She has learned not to fight the insomnia.
Instead, she makes tea and reviews her cases until dawn breaks, which somehow feels safer than lying
in the dark.

Loud noises still startle her—car backfires, slamming doors, her colleague's enthusiastic greeting
around a corner. She feels the adrenaline spike through her body before her rational mind can catch
up and remind her that she is safe. She is in Portland. She is in an office building. She is not in
Damascus.

Her divorce five years ago was partly her fault. Her ex-husband, a kind man, eventually grew tired
of her hypervigilance, her emotional distance, the way she would sometimes disappear into herself
without warning. He wanted a wife who could be present. She couldn't offer that.

Now she lives alone in a quiet apartment on the third floor—high enough to see the street, low
enough to access the stairs quickly. She has never articulated why this matters. She volunteers with
refugee families three evenings a week, finding meaning in their stories, their resilience.
Sometimes she sees her younger self in their eyes—the girl who left everything behind and somehow
kept breathing.
