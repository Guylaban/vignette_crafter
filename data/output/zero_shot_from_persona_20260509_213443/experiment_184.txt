# Mr. Phan's Story

Mr. Phan is a sixty-nine-year-old plumber who has spent the last forty-three years building a quiet
life with his wife in a modest house with a well-maintained garden. He moves deliberately through
our sessions, his weathered hands—stained permanently with traces of copper and solder—resting on
his knees as he speaks. There is a careful politeness to him, the kind that comes from a lifetime of
listening more than talking.

The trauma that brings him to therapy happened fifty-two years ago, when he was seventeen. In 1972,
during the final collapse of Saigon, Mr. Phan was caught in a sweep by soldiers and taken to a
detention camp on the outskirts of the city. For three months, he was held in a concrete structure
with thirty other men, in conditions he still struggles to describe. He was interrogated repeatedly,
though he had done nothing to warrant arrest. It was arbitrary—he was simply in the wrong place.
After ninety-two days, he was released without explanation.

He left Vietnam within a year, arriving in the United States at eighteen with nothing but the
clothes he wore. He learned plumbing through apprenticeship, worked methodically, and married a
woman he met at a community center in 1981. They had two children, both now adults with families of
their own. By all measures, Mr. Phan built a successful life.

But the past five years have been different. He retired from his plumbing business at sixty-four,
expecting to enjoy his wife's company and tend to his garden. Instead, something shifted. The
nightmares started first—he would wake gasping, convinced he was back in the camp, his heart racing
so violently that his wife would have to hold his wrist to reassure him he was alive. The dreams are
vivid and sensory: the smell of unwashed bodies and concrete dust, the sound of boots on stone, the
feeling of his hands being bound.

During the day, he finds himself hypervigilant in ways he hasn't been since he was young. When his
wife closes the bedroom door too quickly, he startles violently. Loud noises—a car backfiring,
construction sounds—send his nervous system into alarm. He has stopped going to the community center
where he once played chess. He avoids crowds. He tells his wife it's because he's getting old, but
the truth is more complicated.

What troubles him most is the intrusive quality of his thoughts. Random moments—sitting on the
porch, turning a wrench to fix a neighbor's sink—he is suddenly flooded with memories so vivid they
feel like they are happening again. His mind feels unreliable, as if the past and present exist in
the same space. He has difficulty concentrating, which frustrates him. He was always a man who could
focus, who took pride in doing careful, precise work.

He has become withdrawn with his grandchildren, something that causes him quiet shame. He loves them
deeply, but he finds himself unable to stay present with them. His wife has noticed his drinking has
increased—not dramatically, but enough that she has mentioned it gently. He drinks to quiet his mind
at night.

Mr. Phan does not think of himself as traumatized. He survived. He built a life. He worked hard and
provided for his family. But he is here now because his wife finally insisted, and because something
in him knows that he cannot carry this alone anymore.
