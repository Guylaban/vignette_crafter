# Dr. Ravi's Hands

Dr. Ravi sits in the consultation room with his hands folded in his lap, a habit he's developed over
the past eighteen months. At seventy-seven, he's been a surgeon for nearly fifty years—a vascular
surgeon whose steady hands have guided countless patients through delicate procedures. Now those
same hands tremble slightly when he thinks about the morning of the accident.

It was a Tuesday in March when his grandson Arun, then twenty-three, came to visit from the city.
They were driving to the hospital together—Ravi wanted to show Arun the new surgical wing—when a
motorcycle ran a red light at an intersection. The impact came from the side, sudden and violent.
The motorcycle driver, a young woman, was thrown across the street. Ravi watched her body land on
the pavement, watched her limbs settle at unnatural angles, watched the blood pool beneath her head.
He was out of the car before it fully stopped, his surgical instincts taking over, but there was
nothing to do. Nothing his fifty years of training could fix. She died before the ambulance arrived.

Arun was fine. Physically unharmed. But Ravi cannot stop seeing the young woman's face in those
final seconds.

For months afterward, Ravi tried to continue his work. He performed surgeries, attended rounds,
mentored residents. But something had fractured inside him. When he stood at the operating table,
his hands—those reliable instruments he'd trusted his entire career—would sometimes shake. He'd find
himself staring at the patient's exposed vessels, suddenly paralyzed by the fragility of human life,
by how quickly everything could stop. Three months after the accident, he took early retirement.

Now he wakes most mornings between three and four in the morning, his heart racing. In those dark
hours, he replays the accident in precise, surgical detail. He sees the motorcycle's trajectory,
calculates the angle of impact, reconstructs the woman's injuries with the same analytical precision
he once used to plan surgical approaches. His wife, Meera, lies beside him pretending to sleep, but
he feels her tension, her helplessness.

When he does manage to drift back to sleep, the nightmares come. He's operating on the young woman,
but his hands won't work. The instruments are too heavy, or his fingers won't bend properly. Blood
fills the surgical field faster than he can suction it away. He wakes gasping, often finding himself
standing in the kitchen at five in the morning, unable to remember how he got there.

Loud noises—car horns, motorcycle engines, even the sound of the garbage truck on collection
day—send him into a state of acute vigilance. His body floods with adrenaline. He grips whatever is
nearest. Meera has learned not to touch him suddenly. They rarely leave the house anymore. The
grocery shopping, the temple visits, the evening walks they used to take together—all of it feels
impossible now. The world feels dangerous in a way it never did before, even during his years of
medical training in a country ravaged by civil conflict.

What troubles him most is that he cannot fix this. He cannot approach his own mind the way he
approached a blocked artery or a weakened vessel wall. There is no surgical plane here, no clear
anatomy to navigate. The young woman's face appears unbidden—in the shower, in the garden, in the
middle of a conversation with Meera about something entirely ordinary. He's a man who spent his life
solving problems with his hands, and now his hands are useless. The tremor has become permanent.

He sits with this contradiction daily: a healer who cannot heal himself.
