# Margaret's Story

Margaret stands behind the counter of her small fabric shop in the same building where she's worked
for thirty-seven years. The late afternoon light filters through bolts of cotton and silk, casting
warm patterns across the worn wooden counter. She's arranging spools of thread by color—a task that
usually calms her—when a car backfires outside. Her hands freeze. Her breath catches. For a moment,
she's somewhere else entirely.

It was three years ago, on a Tuesday morning in autumn. Margaret had closed her shop early to attend
a community meeting about local development. Walking back to her car afterward, she'd witnessed two
police officers stopping a young man on the corner near her storefront. She'd seen this young man
around the neighborhood—he lived a few blocks away. Concerned that something seemed off about the
interaction, Margaret had approached the officers to ask if everything was all right. She'd meant
only to be a good neighbor, to check in.

What happened next unfolds in her memory in fragments, never in a straight line. One officer had
turned toward her aggressively, telling her to get back. When she'd tried to explain that she owned
the nearby shop and was just concerned, he'd grabbed her arm. She remembers the shock of it—the
force, the sudden loss of control. She'd pulled back instinctively, and he'd thrown her against the
brick wall of a building. Her shoulder had struck hard. She'd fallen to the pavement. The officer
had kicked her leg, then called for backup. By the time other officers arrived, she was on the
ground, terrified and confused, unsure what she'd done wrong.

The charges were eventually dropped. But Margaret never returned to herself.

Now, at sixty-six, she moves through her shop like a ghost inhabiting her own life. The fabric
business that once brought her joy—selecting new patterns, advising customers on color combinations,
creating displays that drew people in—has become an endurance test. She opens at nine and closes at
five, but the hours feel stretched, distorted. Her shoulders remain perpetually tense, braced as if
for impact.

She doesn't sleep well. Most nights she lies awake until two or three in the morning, her mind
replaying the encounter. She's developed a hypervigilance that exhausts her—when a customer raises
their voice, even slightly, her entire body goes rigid. Last month, a police car parked outside her
shop for fifteen minutes while an officer got coffee from the café next door. Margaret had locked
herself in her back room, unable to breathe properly until the car left.

Her ex-husband had suggested she see someone, but she'd resisted for a long time. She's a practical
woman who's always managed her own problems. Yet the weight of carrying this alone has become
unbearable. She's lost interest in the quilting circle she'd attended for twenty years. She's
stopped visiting her sister in the next suburb because the drive requires passing the police
station. Some days, the effort of simply opening the shop feels insurmountable.

What troubles her most is the loss of her sense of safety in her own neighborhood, in her own body.
The woman who'd approached those officers with good intentions—that version of Margaret feels like
someone from another lifetime. Now she questions every interaction, second-guesses every decision.
She worries constantly about what might happen if she speaks up again, if she's in the wrong place
at the wrong time, if she's simply perceived as a threat.

She still arranges her thread by color each afternoon. But her hands shake slightly as she does it,
and she never quite feels at home anymore.
