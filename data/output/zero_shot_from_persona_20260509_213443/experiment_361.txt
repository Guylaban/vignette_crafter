# Dr. Hector M.

Hector sits in the leather chair across from me with his hands folded precisely in his lap, a habit
that speaks to decades of professional composure. At sixty-two, he carries himself with the measured
dignity of someone who has spent his life listening to others' pain. He is a psychiatrist—has been
for nearly forty years—and the irony is not lost on him that he finds himself now in the patient's
seat.

He begins by telling me about 1982, though the date comes out slowly, as if the numbers themselves
are difficult to pronounce. He was twenty-four then, a medical student in his home country,
politically active in ways that seemed important at the time. He attended a student gathering that
was raided by security forces. He was arrested along with fifteen others.

What happened over the next seventy-two hours, Hector describes in a clinical tone that occasionally
fractures. They used electrical current. They used stress positions. There were other methods he
names with the detachment of someone reading a medical textbook, though his jaw tightens as he
speaks. He was released eventually—he never fully understood why—and he left the country within
months. He finished his psychiatric training in the United States and built a quiet, respected
career. He never married. He has no children. He says this matter-of-factly, but there is something
in the way he says it that suggests these absences are connected to what happened in that room.

For decades, Hector managed. He saw patients. He published papers on trauma and resilience. He was,
by all accounts, an excellent clinician—perhaps especially because he understood suffering in a way
that transcended theory. His colleagues describe him as thoughtful, never rushed, never dismissive.
But about eighteen months ago, something shifted.

A patient came to him—a woman who had survived torture in a conflict zone. As she described her
experience, Hector felt his chest tighten. He excused himself, told her they would need to
reschedule, and sat alone in his office for two hours. That night, he could not sleep. The
electrical sensations returned to his body, phantom pains in his wrists and along his ribs. He began
having intrusive images—not just memories, but vivid, present-tense experiences of being back in
that room.

Since then, his life has contracted. He still sees patients, but he is more selective now. He avoids
cases involving torture or severe violence. He has taken a reduced schedule, telling his colleagues
he is thinking about retirement, though he is not sure if this is true. He lives alone in an
apartment he has occupied for thirty years, and he rarely leaves it except for work. He does not
socialize. He stopped attending his psychiatry association meetings. He cannot watch news programs
or films that contain scenes of violence. His sleep is fragmented—he wakes at 3 a.m. most nights,
his heart racing, uncertain what has startled him awake.

What troubles him most, he tells me, is the shame. He has spent his career helping people process
trauma, teaching them that what happened was not their fault, that survival is not weakness. Yet he
cannot seem to apply this wisdom to himself. He feels that his body has betrayed him by remembering,
that his mind should be stronger than this. He is a psychiatrist. He should know how to fix himself.

He looks at me directly when he says this last part, and I see in his expression both the wounded
young man from 1982 and the exhausted clinician he has become—two versions of himself that have
finally collided.
