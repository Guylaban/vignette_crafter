# Maria's Story

Maria sits in the office with her hands folded tightly in her lap, her posture rigid despite the
comfortable chair. At fifty-nine, she carries herself with the practiced composure of someone
accustomed to managing complex projects and difficult people. Her dark hair, streaked with gray, is
pulled back neatly. But her eyes tell a different story—they move constantly, tracking the door, the
window, the space behind the therapist's shoulder.

Twelve years ago, Maria was on a business trip in Central America, managing the rollout of a supply
chain operation for her company. She was walking back to her hotel after a late dinner with local
contractors when two men forced her into a van. She was held in a small, windowless room for four
days. The details come out slowly, in fragments, as if her mind still resists assembling them into a
coherent narrative.

During those four days, she was kept in darkness most of the time. She doesn't know what the men
wanted—money, information about the company, or something else entirely. They never made their
intentions clear. The uncertainty was perhaps the worst part. She was released when the company paid
a ransom, though Maria only learned this afterward. For years, she told almost no one what had
happened.

She returned to work within two weeks. Her boss called her "resilient." Her colleagues seemed
relieved when she didn't make a big deal of it. Maria threw herself back into her projects with
characteristic determination. She was promoted twice in the years that followed. Outwardly, she
appeared to have moved past the experience entirely.

But the captivity never really left her.

Now, Maria finds herself unable to enter enclosed spaces without her heart racing uncontrollably.
The conference rooms where she conducts her meetings feel suffocating. She's learned to hold video
calls instead, claiming it's more efficient. Her team assumes she's simply adapted to modern work
practices. In reality, she times her breathing and counts ceiling tiles to manage the panic.

She's stopped taking the elevator at work, climbing the stairs to her fourth-floor office even when
running late for meetings. The feeling of the elevator doors closing triggers something primal in
her body—a conviction, despite her rational mind knowing better, that she won't be let out. She's
nearly fainted twice in stairwells when she's been alone and heard footsteps behind her.

Sleep has become a negotiation. She lies awake most nights, her mind cycling through scenarios of
being trapped again. When she does sleep, she dreams of small spaces, of searching for exits that
don't exist. She wakes in a cold sweat, sometimes gasping. Her ex-husband left partly because of
this—the nightmares, the way she would jolt awake and not recognize him in the darkness, the
increasing distance she maintained even in their bed.

Maria has thrown herself into work because it's the one place where she still feels capable and in
control. She's meticulous about her schedules, her plans, her ability to anticipate problems before
they arise. As long as she's managing projects, she can manage the feeling that her life is
fundamentally unmanageable.

But recently, something has shifted. A younger colleague was promoted to a position Maria had been
working toward. The disappointment cracked something open. She found herself unable to focus in
meetings. Last week, she called in sick for three days and simply sat in her apartment, unable to
explain to herself why she couldn't get out of bed. She knows she needs help, though asking for it
feels like admitting that the captivity has won.
