# A Careful Eye for Detail

Margaret sits in the consultation room with her hands folded precisely in her lap, her posture
impeccable despite the tremor she tries to conceal. At fifty-seven, she carries herself with the
practiced elegance of someone accustomed to presenting a composed exterior to clients. Her tailored
blazer is neutral gray, her jewelry minimal—she has always believed that a designer should let the
space speak, not the person. Today, however, her carefully maintained composure feels like a costume
she can no longer quite fit into.

Eighteen months ago, Margaret was in Bangkok attending an international design conference. She had
been excited about the trip—a chance to present her work on sustainable hospitality design, to
network with colleagues from across Southeast Asia, to reconnect with her roots. She was staying at
a hotel near the conference center, a place she had chosen specifically for its architectural
integrity and thoughtful design. On the second evening, she was in the lobby reviewing notes for her
presentation the next morning when the explosion occurred.

She doesn't remember the moment itself, only the before and the after. The before: she was sitting
in a cream-colored armchair, fountain pen in hand, sketching a detail. The after: chaos, smoke, the
sound of screaming that didn't seem to belong to any human voice she had ever heard. She was
physically unharmed, though a woman beside her was not. Margaret spent three days in a Bangkok
hospital being observed, released with a clean bill of health and a prescription for anxiety
medication she never filled.

The months that followed have been a slow unraveling disguised as normalcy. Margaret returned to her
design firm in Seattle, resumed her projects, attended client meetings. On the surface, she performs
her role with the same precision she always has. But inside, something fundamental has shifted.
Colors feel too bright. Spaces feel too open. She finds herself calculating exits in every room,
positioning herself with her back to walls, unable to relax in the very environments she has spent
her career creating.

Her work has become both a refuge and a torment. She is currently designing a luxury hotel lobby—a
commission she would have relished a year ago. Instead, she lies awake at night obsessing over
safety features, imagining worst-case scenarios, unable to envision the space without simultaneously
envisioning disaster. She has redesigned the entrance layout four times, each iteration reflecting a
new anxiety she has discovered. Her client is growing impatient. Her business partner has noticed
the perfectionism has become paralyzing.

At home, Margaret's husband finds her increasingly distant. She startles at sudden sounds—the
microwave beeping, a door closing—her body flooding with adrenaline before her mind catches up. She
has stopped attending dinner parties, making excuses about work deadlines. The thought of being in
crowded spaces, even restaurants she has frequented for years, triggers a suffocating sense of dread
that she cannot fully articulate. She knows it is irrational. She has always prided herself on
rationality.

Sleep comes in fragments. She wakes at three in the morning with her heart racing, sometimes unsure
whether she is in her Seattle bedroom or back in that Bangkok hotel. The nightmares are vivid and
specific—not always about the explosion itself, but about variations of it, scenarios where she
fails to escape, where people she loves are caught in the blast.

Margaret recognizes that something is wrong. She recognizes that the woman who once moved through
the world with confident vision is now moving through it carefully, fearfully, as if the ground
might give way at any moment.
