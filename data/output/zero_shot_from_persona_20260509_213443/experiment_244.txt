# A Nurse's Reckoning

Priya sits in the clinic waiting room with her hands folded in her lap, her shoulders drawn slightly
inward. At thirty-two, she carries herself with the practiced composure of someone accustomed to
managing crises—but today, even that armor feels thin. She's been a registered nurse for nine years,
most of that time working in the emergency department at Metropolitan Hospital. She loved that work
once. The controlled chaos, the purpose of it, the way she could hold someone's hand while a doctor
delivered impossible news.

Eighteen months ago, a gas leak in the hospital's basement chemical storage area sent a plume of
toxic fumes through the building's ventilation system. It happened on a Tuesday afternoon during
shift change. Priya was in the break room when the alarms started—not the fire alarms, but something
worse, something that made the overhead pages sound frantic in a way she'd never heard before.
Within minutes, the ED descended into something between a disaster scene and a war zone. Patients
were wheeled out on gurneys. Staff members were coughing, eyes streaming. One of her colleagues,
Marcus, collapsed near the medication dispensary. Priya helped carry him to the ambulance bay. She
remembers the weight of his arm across her shoulders, remembers thinking he might die right there in
her arms.

He didn't. Most people recovered within days. But something in Priya fractured in a way that didn't
heal as quickly.

For the first few months, she told herself she was fine. She returned to work after the mandatory
two-week closure. But the ED felt different now—not safer, just smaller, more fragile. She found
herself checking the ventilation system obsessively during her shifts, listening for unusual sounds.
When a patient came in with respiratory distress, her chest would tighten with a panic that felt
disproportionate to the clinical situation. She'd excuse herself to the bathroom and breathe into
paper towels.

The hypervigilance bled into her home life. She began sleeping with her bedroom window cracked open,
even in winter, terrified of being trapped in recirculated air. She checks her apartment's smoke
detectors three or four times a week. She's started taking the stairs instead of the elevator at
work, and once, she sat in her car in the parking garage for forty minutes before she could bring
herself to drive home because she was convinced the air quality readings on her phone were warning
her of something.

Her family has noticed. Her mother calls from Mumbai with concern threading through her voice,
asking if Priya is eating properly, if she's dating anyone, if she's happy. Priya doesn't know how
to explain that happiness feels like a luxury she can't afford right now, that her body exists in a
state of perpetual readiness for the next disaster, even though her mind knows intellectually that
lightning rarely strikes twice.

The worst part is the guilt. She thinks about Marcus sometimes and wonders if she should have held
on tighter, as if her grip could have prevented what happened. She wonders why she survived the
experience intact while others seem to have moved forward. She's considered leaving nursing
entirely, but the thought of abandoning it feels like another failure, another way the disaster
wins.

She sits in the waiting room and watches other people walk past without that same weight. She envies
them their ordinariness, their ability to simply exist in a room without cataloging every exit,
every potential threat. She's trying to understand why her body still feels like it's living through
that Tuesday afternoon, even though the calendar insists it was so long ago.
