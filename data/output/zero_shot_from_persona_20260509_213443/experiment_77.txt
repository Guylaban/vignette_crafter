# Marcus

Marcus is a seventy-six-year-old man who spent the last forty-three years managing a mid-size hotel
in the industrial district. He takes pride in his work—the kind of pride that comes from knowing
every corner of a building, every staff member's name, every system that keeps guests comfortable.
He has always been the type to arrive early and stay late, to notice what others miss.

Twenty months ago, a contractor's crew was performing routine maintenance in the basement storage
area. Marcus was reviewing inventory when he smelled something acrid, chemical. He found the workers
had accidentally punctured a corroded pipe containing industrial-grade cleaning solvent. The
substance had pooled and begun to vaporize. Marcus, trying to be helpful, tried to contain the spill
himself before calling for emergency services. He spent perhaps fifteen minutes in the space,
breathing the fumes, before the fire department arrived and evacuated the building.

He was hospitalized for three days with chemical pneumonitis. The doctors said he was fortunate—his
exposure could have been far worse. He recovered physically within weeks. But something else did not
recover.

Marcus cannot be in the basement anymore. He has tried. He puts on a mask, descends the stairs, and
within moments his chest tightens as though the air itself has become toxic. His heart races. He
becomes convinced he smells the chemical again, though the space was thoroughly decontaminated
months ago. He leaves quickly, sometimes stumbling in his haste to reach the ground floor. His
assistant manager now handles all basement matters, but Marcus feels the weight of this delegation
as a failure.

The hotel itself has become smaller. Marcus finds himself avoiding certain hallways where the
basement entrance sits. He takes the longer route to his office. When guests ask about amenities in
the lower level—the storage of extra linens, the backup generator room—Marcus's jaw tightens. He
answers their questions but feels a strange sense of dread, as though the building itself has become
unreliable, dangerous in ways he cannot control.

At night, Marcus lies awake. He replays those fifteen minutes in the basement, but his mind alters
them. In these versions, he collapses. The fumes are thicker. He does not make it to the stairs. He
lies on the concrete floor while the chemical burns his lungs. Sometimes he wakes gasping, certain
he smells that acrid scent, though his apartment is clean and sealed. He checks the windows. He
opens doors to air out rooms that need no airing.

His ex-wife calls occasionally. They divorced fifteen years ago, but she still worries about him.
She has noticed he sounds tired. She asks if he is sleeping. Marcus tells her he is fine. This is
not entirely untrue and not entirely false.

He has reduced his hours at the hotel, telling ownership he is thinking about retirement. The truth
is more complicated. He still wants to work, still wants to feel useful, but the building where he
spent four decades now holds something he cannot name—not quite fear, not quite distrust, but
something in between. A sense that safety is temporary, that invisible threats exist in ordinary
places, that his competence and decades of experience cannot protect him from what he cannot see.

Marcus continues to come to work. He manages from his office more than the floor. He wonders
sometimes if this is what aging feels like, or if it is something else entirely.
