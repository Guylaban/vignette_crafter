# Rosa's Careful Distance

Rosa sits in the waiting room of her office building, laptop open but untouched. She's been an
editor for forty-two years, and she still loves the precision of language—finding the perfect word,
the necessary comma, the rhythm of a well-constructed sentence. But lately, the words on her screen
blur together. She catches herself reading the same paragraph five times without absorbing anything.

She was twenty-four when the war came to her neighborhood in El Salvador. Rosa had just finished her
journalism degree and was working at a small newspaper when the violence escalated. She remembers
the morning vividly—not because it was her worst day, but because it was the day everything changed
from "before" to "after." She was walking to the market with her mother when gunfire erupted from a
building across the street. The sounds were impossibly loud, sharper than anything she'd imagined.
Her mother pulled her into a doorway, and they crouched there for what felt like hours but was
probably twenty minutes. A young man lay bleeding on the pavement in front of them. Rosa couldn't
look away from his face, from the confusion in his eyes, from the way his hand kept opening and
closing.

They made it home. Her mother cried. Rosa didn't. She felt something inside her go very still.

Over the next three years, Rosa witnessed more violence—some directed, some random, all of it
terrifying. She learned to move through the city like a ghost, taking routes that avoided certain
areas, timing her errands carefully, never drawing attention. She stopped going out at night
entirely. Her marriage, which had been fragile before the war, fractured completely under the weight
of shared fear and her husband's increasing drinking. When he finally left, she felt relief more
than grief.

Now, decades later and safely settled in the United States for thirty years, Rosa lives a life of
careful control. She's built a reputation as meticulous, unflappable, the editor people trust with
their most important work. What they don't know is that she's constructed her entire life to
minimize unpredictability. She works from home most days. She's never married again. She shops at
the same grocery store at the same time each week. She knows every exit in every building she
enters.

The problem is that her body hasn't caught up with the fact that the war ended decades ago. When a
car backfires outside her apartment, her heart races so violently she has to sit down. When a
colleague raises their voice in a meeting, she freezes, her mind briefly elsewhere—back on that
street, in that doorway. She's started avoiding the office entirely, claiming she's more productive
at home. In truth, she's avoiding the unpredictability of other people, the sudden noises, the
crowded elevator.

Sleep has become her enemy. She lies awake most nights, her mind cycling through scenarios—what if
this, what if that. When she does sleep, she dreams of that young man's face, of her mother's grip
on her arm, of sounds that don't exist anymore but feel entirely real at three in the morning.

Rosa is still an excellent editor. She still finds those perfect words, that necessary comma. But
she's doing it from an increasingly small space, and she knows it. She's become expert at managing
her life around the fear, at maintaining the appearance of normalcy. But the distance she's
maintained from the world—once protective—is beginning to feel like a prison.
