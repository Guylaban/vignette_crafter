# Marcus

Marcus sits with his hands folded in his lap, his weathered fingers still bearing the calluses of
thirty years spent maintaining trails and managing wildlife in the state park system. At forty-
seven, he carries himself with the careful posture of someone used to long days outdoors, but
there's a brittleness to him now, a tension that doesn't ease even when he's off the clock.

His daughter, Lily, was eight years old when she developed a persistent fever that wouldn't break.
Marcus remembers the morning vividly—he'd been planning a controlled burn in the northern sector,
something he'd done dozens of times before. His wife called around noon. The pediatrician wanted
them at the hospital. By evening, Lily was in the ICU with bacterial meningitis, her small body
wracked with seizures, her skin mottled with the telltale rash that made Marcus's stomach drop.

For three weeks, Marcus stopped existing in any meaningful way. He sat in the hospital chair beside
her bed, watching monitors, counting her breaths. He'd always prided himself on being the capable
one—the man who could navigate by stars, who knew every plant and animal in his territory, who could
manage any crisis that arose in the wilderness. But in that fluorescent room, he was helpless.
Lily's fever spiked to 104 degrees. The doctors spoke in careful language about possible
complications, permanent hearing loss, neurological damage. Marcus found himself bargaining with a
God he'd stopped believing in years ago.

Lily recovered. After two weeks, the fever broke. After three, she came home. The doctors said she'd
been lucky—no hearing loss, no cognitive impairment. She was, by all measures, fine.

Marcus was not.

That was eighteen months ago, and he still can't sleep more than four hours at a time. He wakes at 2
or 3 a.m., his heart hammering, convinced something is wrong with Lily. He gets out of bed and
stands outside her room, listening to her breathe, sometimes for an hour. His wife, Sarah, has
stopped asking him to come back to bed. The worry has become background radiation in their home.

At work, Marcus finds himself unable to focus on routine tasks. He'll be marking a trail and
suddenly his mind is back in that hospital room, and his hands start shaking so badly he has to sit
down. He's made excuses to avoid leading the junior rangers program—something he loved for years.
The thought of being responsible for other people's children, of something happening on his watch,
makes his chest tight with panic.

He's hypervigilant in ways that exhaust him. When Lily complains of the smallest ache, Marcus is
convinced it's the beginning of something catastrophic. He's taken her to the pediatrician seventeen
times in the past year for minor complaints. Sarah has gently pointed this out. He knows it's
excessive. He can't stop.

The intrusive images come without warning. He'll be eating lunch and suddenly see Lily's face on
that hospital pillow, her body small and fragile beneath the blankets. He'll be driving and remember
the sound of the ventilator, the beeping of the monitors. These moments steal his breath.

Marcus has always defined himself by his competence, by his ability to read and manage his
environment. The park is a place where he understands the rules, where he can predict outcomes and
prevent disasters. But he couldn't protect his daughter from something invisible and random. That
knowledge sits with him constantly, a weight he carries even on the trails he loves.
