# Margaret's Collision

Margaret sits in the leather chair across from the therapist, her posture upright despite her eighty
years. She wears a cream-colored blouse and reading glasses on a beaded chain—the uniform of someone
accustomed to professional spaces. But her hands betray her; they rest in her lap, fingers
interlaced tightly enough that the knuckles have gone pale.

Three months ago, Margaret was driving home from the office on a Tuesday afternoon. She had spent
the day reviewing policy briefs on healthcare access, work that had consumed her career for nearly
four decades. The commute was familiar, the route one she had traveled thousands of times. She was
thinking about dinner, about whether she had ingredients for the groundnut soup her late husband
Samuel used to love.

A delivery truck ran a red light at the intersection near the shopping district. Margaret didn't see
it coming. The impact came from her passenger side, violent and sudden. The car spun. Glass
shattered. Metal screamed against metal. When the world stopped moving, Margaret found herself
gasping, unable to draw a full breath, her left leg pinned beneath the crushed dashboard. It took
the fire department forty minutes to extract her.

She sustained a fractured femur and several broken ribs. The physical injuries healed with
reasonable speed—Margaret's doctor marveled at her bone density, her overall health. But something
else fractured in that moment, something the X-rays couldn't capture.

Now, three months later, Margaret hasn't returned to her office. She tells people it's the physical
therapy, the lingering pain. This is partly true. But the real obstacle is something she struggles
to articulate. Last week, she attempted to drive to her daughter's house across town. She made it
three blocks before her vision tunneled, her heart began to race uncontrollably, and her hands shook
so badly she could barely grip the wheel. She pulled into a parking lot and sat there for two hours,
convinced she was dying.

Margaret's nights are worse. She lies awake, her mind replaying the moment of impact in excruciating
detail. Sometimes she hears the sound of breaking glass and jolts upright, her heart hammering, only
to realize she's in her bedroom, safe. Samuel's side of the bed remains empty, has remained empty
for five years now, but on the worst nights she reaches for him anyway, forgetting momentarily that
he's gone.

The policy work that once energized her now feels impossibly distant. She tried to review some
briefs her colleague sent, but the words wouldn't cohere. Her concentration, once sharp as a blade,
has become fragmented. She finds herself reading the same paragraph five times without absorbing its
meaning. The part of her mind that loved the intellectual architecture of policy analysis feels
inaccessible, as though it belongs to someone else.

What troubles her most is the shame. Margaret has lived through civil unrest, raised four children
largely alone while building a career in a field dominated by men, buried a husband. She is not
someone who breaks. Yet here she is, afraid to sit in a car, startled by loud noises, unable to
sleep more than an hour or two before her nervous system jolts her awake in a state of alarm. Her
children worry. Her colleagues have stopped calling. She has become someone she doesn't
recognize—smaller somehow, more fragile, as though the collision didn't just damage the car but
fundamentally altered her.

She sits in the therapist's office and tries to explain this, and finds herself crying, which she
hasn't done since the hospital.
