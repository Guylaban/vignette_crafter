# Amara's Vigil

Amara has worked as a park ranger for nearly a decade, and she loves the solitude of the vast
wilderness that surrounds her. She moves through the forest with practiced ease, her boots knowing
every trail, her eyes alert to the subtle shifts in animal behavior and seasonal change. Before
everything changed, she would spend her days maintaining trails, conducting educational programs for
school groups, and monitoring the health of the ecosystem she's sworn to protect. Her husband,
Daniel, would joke that she was married to the forest first and to him second. She'd laugh and kiss
him, knowing it wasn't quite true—but the work did consume her in a way that felt sacred.

Three years ago, Daniel drove to meet her at the ranger station on a Friday evening. They'd planned
a quiet dinner at home, nothing special. He was running late from his job as an accountant, texting
her updates as he navigated the winding roads that cut through the park. Amara was finishing up
paperwork when the call came. A car had veered off the mountain road. Daniel had been alone. He was
pronounced dead at the scene.

For months afterward, Amara couldn't drive those roads. She couldn't look at the stretch of forest
where it happened without her chest tightening, her vision narrowing. She returned to work because
staying home felt worse—the silence of their house became unbearable. But the forest, her refuge,
had transformed into something else entirely. Every sound made her jump. The rustling of branches
sounded like tires skidding. The sight of a vehicle in the distance triggered a visceral panic that
would leave her shaking for hours.

Now, three years later, Amara still works, but something fundamental has shifted. She avoids the
western trails entirely, the ones that pass closest to where Daniel died. She tells her supervisor
it's because of trail maintenance, but everyone knows. On days when she does venture into those
areas, she moves through them mechanically, her mind fractured between the present moment and an
endless loop of imagining his final seconds. Did he suffer? Was he afraid? These thoughts arrive
unbidden, violent in their intensity.

Her marriage to Daniel feels suspended in amber—she still wears her ring, still speaks about him in
the present tense to strangers. At home, she's become a different wife to his memory. She's
reorganized their bedroom twice, unable to settle, unable to sleep for more than a few hours before
jolting awake in a cold sweat. Daniel appears in her dreams frequently, but not as he was. In these
dreams, he's asking her why she wasn't there, why she didn't somehow prevent what happened.

Her colleagues have noticed she's become withdrawn. She no longer stays after shifts to chat, no
longer leads the interpretive programs she once loved. A young ranger recently asked her to co-lead
a group hike, and Amara felt a surge of irritation so acute it frightened her. How could anyone
expect her to be present, to be teaching, when the world had proven itself so fundamentally unsafe?

She sits in the ranger station now, filling out incident reports with mechanical precision, her body
present but her mind scattered across multiple timelines—the life she had, the moment everything
fractured, and this strange, diminished present where she moves through the forest like a ghost
haunting her own existence.
