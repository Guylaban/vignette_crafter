# Marcus: The Design Studio

Marcus sits in the waiting room with his laptop open, though he isn't really working. At thirty-
three, he has the lean, somewhat exhausted appearance of someone who hasn't slept well in months.
His fingers hover over the keyboard without purpose. He's a graphic designer—good at his job, or at
least he was—but lately the work feels like pushing through water.

Three years ago, Marcus was stopped by police during a routine traffic check on his way home from a
late project deadline. He'd been speeding slightly, distracted, thinking about a client
presentation. The officer's approach to his window was aggressive from the start. When Marcus
reached for his glove compartment to retrieve his registration, the officer misread the movement.
What followed was a violent takedown—Marcus was thrown against his car, handcuffed, and held at
gunpoint while the officer screamed at him. It lasted perhaps five minutes, though Marcus describes
it as feeling like hours. When it became clear there was no threat, the officer released him without
explanation or apology. Marcus was never charged with anything.

He didn't talk about it for weeks. He told himself it was over, that he was fine. But sleep became
impossible. He'd lie awake replaying those moments—the shock of being slammed against the hood, the
officer's face inches from his own, the absolute certainty that something terrible was about to
happen. When he did sleep, he'd wake gasping, his heart racing, convinced he was still there.

At work, his concentration fractured. His boss noticed first—missed deadlines, designs that lacked
his usual precision and creativity. Marcus found himself unable to focus on the detailed work that
once came naturally. A project involving police department branding made him physically ill; he had
to excuse himself from the meeting. His colleagues didn't understand why he suddenly seemed anxious
and withdrawn.

Driving became unbearable. Every police car on the road sent his nervous system into overdrive. He'd
see flashing lights in his rearview mirror and his hands would shake uncontrollably. He started
taking circuitous routes home, avoiding highways where he might encounter patrol cars. Eventually,
he stopped going out after dark altogether. His social life, already strained by his divorce two
years prior, collapsed entirely.

The hypervigilance consumed him. In public spaces—coffee shops, grocery stores—he finds himself
scanning for threats, noting exits, watching people's hands. His body stays in a state of constant
alert, muscles tense, jaw clenched. He's exhausted but wired simultaneously, a paradox that leaves
him feeling hollowed out.

At night, intrusive images arrive unbidden. He's designing a website when suddenly he's back against
that car hood. He's in a meeting when the officer's voice—that particular tone of aggression—echoes
in his mind. The boundary between then and now has become porous.

His ex-wife tried to be supportive initially, but his emotional numbness, his irritability, the way
he'd sometimes snap at her without warning—it contributed to the distance between them. He hasn't
been on a date since the separation. The thought of being vulnerable with someone, of not being in
control, feels impossible.

He continues working from his apartment now, which his employer tolerates because he's still
technically productive, though at half his former capacity. But even at home, in what should be his
safe space, Marcus finds little peace. He checks his windows repeatedly. He starts at unexpected
sounds. He exists in a state of perpetual bracing, waiting for the next moment when his world might
shatter again.
