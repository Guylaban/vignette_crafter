# Marcus

Marcus sits with his shoulders slightly hunched, fingers interlaced in his lap. At forty-six, he
carries himself with the careful deliberation of someone who has learned to take up less space. He
is a curriculum designer by profession—someone accustomed to thinking about how people learn, how
information unfolds, how to build understanding step by step. His own story, however, resists such
neat scaffolding.

Seven months ago, Marcus was pulled over during his evening commute home from work. He'd been
driving through a neighborhood where he'd lived for twelve years, a route he knew by heart. The
traffic stop was ostensibly for a broken taillight, though he recalls the officer's hand resting on
his weapon from the moment he approached the window. Marcus is a careful man by nature, someone who
reads situations methodically. He kept his hands visible, spoke in measured tones, complied with
every instruction. None of it mattered. What began as a routine stop escalated rapidly—the officer
called for backup, Marcus was removed from his vehicle, forced to the ground, his face pressed
against asphalt still warm from the day's heat. There was shouting, the weight of multiple bodies,
the metallic taste of fear. The entire encounter lasted perhaps four minutes. He was released
without citation, without apology, with only a warning about the taillight.

The physical injuries healed within weeks. The rest has not.

Now Marcus finds himself unable to drive that route. He takes a longer way home, adding thirty
minutes to his commute, which he frames to his wife as a preference for the scenic road. The truth
is more complicated. Traffic stops trigger an immediate surge of adrenaline—his heart accelerates,
his vision narrows, his hands grip the steering wheel so tightly his knuckles blanch. He has begun
leaving work earlier to avoid rush hour traffic altogether, a decision that is slowly eroding his
professional presence. He misses team meetings. His supervisor has noticed.

Sleep has become fragmented. Marcus lies awake for hours, his mind replaying the encounter in
obsessive detail, cataloging everything he might have done differently, everything he could have
said. When sleep finally comes, he dreams of variations on that afternoon—versions where the outcome
is worse, where backup never arrives, where no one ever finds him. He wakes gasping, drenched in
sweat, careful not to disturb his wife, though she wakes anyway. She reaches for him in the
darkness, and he flinches before he can stop himself.

At work, his concentration has become unreliable. He finds himself staring at curriculum frameworks
without actually reading them, his mind caught in loops of rumination. His colleagues have begun to
seem vaguely threatening—the way they move through hallways, the tone of their voices in meetings.
He cannot quite explain this to himself. These are people he has worked alongside for years. Yet his
body responds to them with a vigilance that exhausts him.

Marcus has started avoiding social situations. His wife wanted to attend a community gathering last
month; he invented an excuse. The thought of being in a crowd, of being unable to see all exits, of
being trapped among strangers—it felt impossible. She looked disappointed in a way that suggested
she was beginning to understand that something fundamental had shifted in him, something that might
not shift back.

He knows, intellectually, that what happened to him was wrong. He knows he did nothing to provoke
it. But knowledge and feeling have become two separate territories in his mind, and he is struggling
to find the bridge between them.
