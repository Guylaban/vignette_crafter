# Maya's Story

Maya is nineteen years old, working the evening shift at a distribution warehouse in the suburbs
where she's been employed for just over a year. She speaks about her job matter-of-factly—the
scanning, the sorting, the repetitive rhythm of it—but there's a flatness in her voice that doesn't
quite match the words. She's good at the work, her supervisor says. Reliable. Quiet. She doesn't
mention that the warehouse's fluorescent lights and the beeping of scanners remind her of hospitals,
or that she requests shifts when fewer people are around.

When Maya was sixteen, her younger brother Karim was diagnosed with acute leukemia. The diagnosis
came suddenly—a fever that wouldn't break, bruising that appeared overnight, a routine blood test
that changed everything. For the next eighteen months, Maya's world compressed into hospital
corridors, waiting rooms, and the sound of her mother's prayers whispered in Arabic at three in the
morning.

She remembers the specific smell of the pediatric oncology ward. She remembers the day the doctors
said his white blood cell count had spiked dangerously, how her mother's hand went cold in hers. She
remembers sitting beside his hospital bed for hours while he slept fitfully, afraid that if she
looked away something would happen. She was sixteen—old enough to understand what leukemia meant,
young enough to believe that her vigilance might somehow matter.

Karim survived. He's fourteen now, in remission for over a year. This is the fact that should matter
most, and Maya knows this intellectually. But her body hasn't caught up with the good news.

Now, when she's at work, she finds herself catastrophizing without warning. A coworker mentions
their sister's headache, and Maya's mind spirals into tumor scenarios. She'll be scanning boxes and
suddenly her chest tightens, her breathing becomes shallow, and she's back in that hospital room.
The feeling passes in minutes, but it leaves her exhausted. She's started taking longer breaks,
sitting in her car in the parking lot, trying to breathe the way she read about online.

Sleep has become complicated. She lies awake most nights, her mind cycling through what-ifs. What if
the doctors had missed something during Karim's last checkup? What if the cancer returns? What if
she had noticed his symptoms earlier? The logic of the questions doesn't matter—they feel urgent and
real at 2 a.m. When she does sleep, she has fragmented dreams about hospital codes and alarms.

Her relationship with her boyfriend has suffered. He tries to be patient, but he doesn't understand
why she flinches when he touches her unexpectedly, or why she becomes distant when he talks about
making future plans. She wants to explain that planning feels dangerous, that imagining a future
where something bad doesn't happen feels like tempting fate. Instead, she withdraws. It's easier.

Maya has told no one at work about any of this. She performs normalcy—she shows up, does her job,
goes home. But there's a part of her that feels permanently altered, as though she's aging faster
than her peers. Her friends from high school seem to inhabit a different world, one where tragedy is
theoretical. She's seen how fragile life is, how quickly everything can unravel. That knowledge sits
with her constantly, a weight she carries through the warehouse aisles.

She agreed to come to therapy because her mother asked her to. She's not sure what talking will
change, but she's here, and she's trying.
