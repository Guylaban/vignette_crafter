# Margaret's Story

Margaret has managed the Riverside Hotel for thirty-two years. She knows every corner of the
building, every staff member's name, and every regular guest by heart. At seventy, she still arrives
before dawn to review the day's reservations and walk the corridors, a ritual that has grounded her
through decades of seasonal rushes and staffing crises. But something has changed in the past
eighteen months, and Margaret struggles to recognize herself in the person she has become.

It started on an ordinary Tuesday evening last spring. Margaret was closing the front desk around
eleven when a guest in the lobby became agitated about his room assignment. He was shouting,
gesturing wildly, and other guests were watching with alarm. Margaret, drawing on three decades of
experience, approached him calmly and offered to move him to a suite at no charge. She had de-
escalated dozens of situations this way. But this time, the man suddenly lunged at her, grabbing her
arm. In seconds, police arrived—someone had called from a guest room. What happened next remains
fragmented in Margaret's mind: the officers shouting, her own voice crying out, the man being forced
to the ground, and then, inexplicably, one of the officers grabbing *her* arm roughly, pushing her
against the wall. "Step back," he'd commanded, his voice sharp with aggression. She had complied
immediately, her hands shaking, but she remembers his grip felt unnecessarily forceful, his tone
suggesting she was somehow complicit in the disturbance.

The guest was arrested. Margaret was unharmed physically. The incident report was filed. Life
resumed.

Except it didn't.

Now, eighteen months later, Margaret finds herself unable to be in the lobby during busy evening
hours. She's delegated most of her evening duties to her assistant manager, a decision that
embarrasses her because she's always prided herself on being present. The truth is, when the lobby
fills with guests and voices rise—even in normal conversation—her heart begins to race. She feels a
tightness in her chest, a sensation of being trapped. Twice, she's had to excuse herself to her
office, where she sits with the door locked, breathing slowly until the feeling passes.

She's stopped attending the hotel association meetings she once enjoyed. Large rooms with unfamiliar
people feel dangerous now, though she cannot articulate why. She keeps replaying the moment the
officer touched her, analyzing what she might have done differently, wondering if she appeared
threatening somehow. At night, she lies awake for hours, her mind cycling through the incident. On
the rare nights she does sleep, she dreams of being pinned against walls, unable to move or speak.

Margaret has become hypervigilant about authority figures. When she sees police cars passing the
hotel, her breathing becomes shallow. She notices herself watching officers' hands, their postures,
assessing threat levels in ways that feel exhausting and irrational. She knows logically that most
police officers are trying to help, yet her body doesn't believe this anymore.

Her daughter has suggested therapy, but Margaret worries about being judged—about seeming weak or
overly sensitive for being affected by something so brief. She was pushed against a wall for perhaps
five seconds. Other people have experienced far worse. Yet she cannot seem to move forward. The
hotel that has been her sanctuary, her identity, her purpose for thirty-two years now feels like a
place where danger lurks in ordinary moments. Margaret continues to show up each morning, still
arriving before dawn, still knowing the building by heart. But now she moves through it like a ghost
haunting her own life.
