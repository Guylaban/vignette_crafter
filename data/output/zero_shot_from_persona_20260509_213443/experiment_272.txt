# The Judge's Reckoning

Margaret sits in the leather chair across from the therapist's desk, her posture impeccable despite
the tremor in her hands. At fifty-four, she carries herself with the bearing of someone accustomed
to commanding respect—her navy suit tailored, her gray-streaked hair pulled back neatly. She has
been a judge for eighteen years, known in her district for her fairness and composure. No one in her
courtroom would guess that she often wakes at three in the morning, drenched in sweat, unable to
remember where she is.

Twenty-three years ago, Margaret was a human rights lawyer in her home country. She was working on a
case documenting state violence when she was taken from her office by men in unmarked uniforms. What
followed was forty-three days she rarely speaks about in concrete terms. The physical torture—the
beatings, the stress positions, the electrical shocks—left scars both visible and invisible. But it
is the psychological dimension that has proven most stubborn: the helplessness, the dehumanization,
the knowledge that her screams meant nothing to the people inflicting the pain.

She escaped when the government fell, made her way to the United States, and eventually rebuilt
herself through law school. The work grounded her. Being a judge felt redemptive—she was using her
voice to protect others, to ensure that law meant something. For years, she told herself the
nightmares were behind her. She married a kind man who never pressed her about her past. They have a
daughter in college. Margaret's life, from the outside, is one of hard-won success.

But something shifted last year. A case came before her bench involving allegations of police
brutality. The victim's testimony about being restrained, about begging for mercy, about the
officers' indifference—it cracked something open. Margaret found herself unable to focus during the
trial. In her chambers, she experienced what she now understands was a flashback: she was back in
that room, the walls closing in, unable to move. She had to excuse herself. The bailiff found her in
the bathroom, hyperventilating.

Since then, the symptoms have intensified. Margaret has difficulty sleeping, not just from
nightmares but from a constant state of vigilance—her body convinced that danger is imminent. She
startles at sudden noises in her home. She avoids watching news coverage of violence, but her mind
supplies the images anyway. Most troubling is her emotional numbness, a flatness that has seeped
into her marriage and her work. Her husband describes her as distant. She feels it too—a
disconnection from the people around her, as though she is observing her own life from behind glass.

At work, she has become hyperaware of her own authority. During sentencing, she sometimes feels a
surge of something dark—not mercy, but its opposite. She recognizes in herself an impulse to punish
harshly, and she has to consciously pull back, aware that her judgment is being colored by her own
trauma. This frightens her. She entered the law to protect people, not to become someone whose
trauma distorts her ability to dispense justice fairly.

Margaret came to therapy reluctantly, pushed by her husband's gentle insistence. She is not someone
who easily admits vulnerability. But she is beginning to recognize that the past she thought she had
contained is still very much alive, moving through her body and her decisions, shaping how she moves
through the world. She describes feeling caught between two identities: the survivor who escaped,
and the woman who cannot escape.
