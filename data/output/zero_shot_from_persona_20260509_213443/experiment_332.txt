# Sarah's Story

Sarah sits at her desk in the IT support center, her fingers poised over the keyboard as she waits
for the next call to come through. She has worked here for eight years, and her colleagues consider
her reliable—always available for the difficult tickets, rarely taking time off. What they don't see
is how her shoulders tense when the phone rings, how her heart rate spikes before she even speaks,
or how she rehearses her responses in her mind to avoid saying anything that might be misinterpreted
as incompetent or disrespectful.

The trauma that shapes Sarah's present began in her childhood home in Lagos, where her grandmother
raised her after her mother's death. Her grandmother was strict—a woman who believed in discipline
delivered swiftly and without question. Sarah remembers the leather belt, the sudden explosions of
anger over minor infractions: a plate washed incorrectly, a question asked at the wrong moment, a
grade that wasn't high enough. The unpredictability was perhaps the worst part. Sarah learned to
read her grandmother's moods obsessively, to anticipate danger, to make herself small and compliant.
She became hypervigilant before she had words for it.

When Sarah was sixteen, her father brought her to London. She thought the violence would end there,
and in a physical sense, it did. But the patterns her body had learned didn't simply disappear.

Now, at forty-three, Sarah is married to Michael, a kind man who has learned not to move too quickly
around her, not to raise his voice, not to touch her without warning. He has learned this through
trial and error, through nights when Sarah woke gasping, through mornings when she couldn't explain
why she'd flinched at his hand on her shoulder. Michael tries his best, but Sarah knows he doesn't
fully understand why she sometimes becomes rigid in his arms, why certain tones of voice send her
spiraling, why she apologizes constantly for things that require no apology.

At work, Sarah has become a master of emotional regulation, channeling her anxiety into
perfectionism. She arrives early, stays late, anticipates problems before they occur. When she makes
even minor mistakes—and everyone makes mistakes—she is consumed with shame and fear. She replays
conversations obsessively, searching for evidence that she has disappointed someone, that she has
failed to be good enough. Her sleep is fragmented; she often wakes at three in the morning, her mind
racing through scenarios of criticism and rejection.

Socially, Sarah has become increasingly isolated. She struggles to trust others' intentions,
interpreting casual comments as veiled criticism. When colleagues invite her for drinks after work,
she declines, convinced that they are merely being polite, that they don't genuinely want her there.
The thought of being in a group setting where she cannot control the environment fills her with
dread. She finds herself avoiding situations where she might be evaluated or judged, which has
inadvertently limited her career advancement despite her technical competence.

The physical symptoms are constant: tension headaches, a tightness in her chest that never fully
releases, a persistent startle response. Loud noises make her jump. Arguments on television can
trigger hours of anxiety. She finds herself holding her breath without realizing it, only noticing
when Michael gently reminds her to breathe.

What Sarah carries with her is not simply a memory of childhood violence, but a nervous system that
has never learned to feel safe—a body that remains convinced, decades later, that danger is imminent
and that her survival depends on her ability to anticipate and prevent harm.
