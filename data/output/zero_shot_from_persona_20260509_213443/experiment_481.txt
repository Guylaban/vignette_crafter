# Dr. Marcus Chen

Marcus sits in the leather chair across from his therapist, his hands folded precisely in his lap.
At sixty-three, he maintains the composed demeanor that has served him well during four decades as
an emergency medicine physician. Yet there's a brittleness beneath the surface, a tension in his jaw
that suggests the effort required to hold himself together.

Eighteen months ago, Marcus was attending a medical conference in Barcelona. He'd looked forward to
the trip—a rare break from the demanding rhythm of the emergency department, a chance to reconnect
with colleagues he hadn't seen in years. On the morning of the second day, he was walking through
the lobby of his hotel when the sound came. Not immediately recognizable as what it was. His brain,
trained to process information quickly, took several seconds to register: explosion.

The lobby descended into chaos. Marcus found himself moving on instinct, falling back on the muscle
memory of countless trauma activations. He moved toward the sound rather than away, identifying the
injured, applying pressure to wounds, directing people toward exits. He was covered in blood—some
from the victims, some his own from a gash on his forehead—when Spanish police finally secured the
area. A terrorist attack. Seven dead. Twenty-three injured.

Marcus returned home after being interviewed by authorities. His wife, Sarah, picked him up from the
airport. He remembers feeling oddly disconnected from her embrace, as though observing the scene
from outside his body. He told her he was fine. He told himself the same thing.

But fine fractured quickly. Within days, Marcus found himself unable to tolerate the sounds of the
emergency department—the monitors, the overhead pages, the controlled urgency that had once felt
purposeful now triggered an overwhelming sense of dread. He'd find himself hypervigilant during
shifts, scanning rooms for exits, noticing every person who entered, every potential threat. His
colleagues commented that he seemed distracted. He called in sick more often than he had in his
entire career.

Sleep became impossible. Marcus would lie awake for hours, his mind replaying the moment of the
blast in vivid detail. When he did sleep, he'd wake in a cold sweat, his heart racing, unable to
remember the nightmare but certain it had been terrible. Sarah tried to comfort him, but he found
her touch unbearable, her presence somehow suffocating rather than soothing.

He began to withdraw. He stopped attending the weekly dinner with his medical school friends. He
declined Sarah's suggestion to visit their daughter in Seattle. He spent increasing amounts of time
alone in his study, telling Sarah he was catching up on journals, though he rarely opened them. The
world outside felt dangerous in a way he couldn't quite articulate. Crowds triggered panic. The
sound of sirens—once a call to action—now sent him into a state of acute anxiety.

What troubled Marcus most was the guilt. He'd survived while others hadn't. He'd been trained for
emergencies, had the knowledge and skills to help, yet he couldn't shake the feeling that he should
have done more, been more, prevented something. As a physician, he'd always been the one in control,
the one who knew what to do. That identity felt shattered now, replaced by someone he didn't
recognize—someone afraid, withdrawn, unable to return to work.

Sarah has gently suggested he see someone. Marcus resisted at first. Therapists were for patients,
not for him. But the weight of carrying this alone has become unbearable, and he's finally reached a
point where maintaining his composure requires more energy than he has left.
