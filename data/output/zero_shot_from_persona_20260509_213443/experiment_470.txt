# Clinical Vignette: Priya

Priya sits across from me with her hands folded in her lap, her posture rigid despite the
comfortable chair. At thirty-two, she carries herself with a deliberate composure that suggests
years of practice holding herself together. She works as a hospital chaplain, a role that drew her
initially because of her deep faith and desire to comfort others in their darkest moments. Now, that
same faith feels fractured in ways she struggles to articulate.

Six years ago, Priya was working as a human rights advocate in South Asia, documenting cases of
political detention and abuse. During a research trip to investigate allegations of torture in a
remote detention facility, she was arrested at a checkpoint. What followed was seventy-two hours
that she describes in fragments—disconnected pieces she struggles to assemble into a coherent
narrative. She was interrogated, beaten, and subjected to isolation and sensory deprivation. Her
captors used her faith against her, mocking her beliefs while inflicting pain. The experience was
designed to break her, and in many ways, it did.

She was eventually released after international pressure, but the trauma didn't end with her
physical freedom. The first years back home were consumed by nightmares so vivid she would wake
gasping, convinced she was still in that cell. Her ex-husband tried to support her, but her
hypervigilance made intimacy impossible. She would startle at sudden sounds, her body flooding with
adrenaline. The smell of diesel fuel—a scent from the detention facility—would send her into a
spiral for hours. Eventually, the marriage couldn't withstand the weight of what had happened to
her. The divorce felt like another loss, another failure, though intellectually she knows that's not
fair.

Now, Priya works as a chaplain in a large hospital, and the irony isn't lost on her. She moves
through corridors helping patients face their suffering while battling her own daily. She's good at
her job—perhaps too good. Patients find her presence calming, her words meaningful. But the work
extracts a cost. When she sits with a trauma survivor, she often finds herself back in that cell,
her nervous system unable to distinguish between their pain and hers. She's started avoiding certain
units, particularly the psychiatric ward where trauma patients are treated. She tells herself it's
because she needs to maintain boundaries, but the truth is more complicated: she's afraid of what
proximity to others' trauma might trigger in her.

Her sleep remains fragmented. She'll lie awake for hours, her mind cycling through the same
intrusive memories, or she'll fall asleep only to be jolted awake by nightmares she can't quite
remember but whose emotional residue lingers through the morning. She's gained weight from stress
eating and lost the ability to exercise—the thought of being enclosed or confined, even in a gym
locker room, sends panic through her chest.

Priya describes a persistent sense of disconnection from her own life. She goes through the motions
of her days with competence, but there's a numbness underneath, a sense that she's watching herself
from outside her body. She struggles to feel joy, even in moments that should bring it. Her faith,
which once anchored her, now feels complicated. She questions why her God allowed this to happen,
why she survived when others didn't, whether her suffering has any meaning at all.

She came to therapy because her supervisor gently suggested it, expressing concern about her
increasing emotional distance. Priya agreed, though she carries doubt about whether talking will
actually help. She's learned that some things can't be unseen, unfelt, or unremembered.
