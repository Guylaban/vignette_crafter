# The Weight of Being Watched

Amira has worked at the McDonald's on Maple Street for nearly twelve years. She knows the rhythm of
the place—the morning rush of construction workers ordering coffee, the lunch crowd of office
employees, the teenagers after school. She's good at her job, efficient and kind to customers, and
her manager has always relied on her to train new staff. But for the past eighteen months, she
hasn't felt safe there, or anywhere else.

It started in the spring of 2022 when a man began coming in during her shifts. He would order the
same thing—a large coffee, black—and sit in the corner booth facing the counter where Amira works
the register. At first, she thought nothing of it. Regular customers are common. But then she
noticed he wasn't reading or using his phone. He was watching her. When she'd glance his way, he'd
smile. When she turned away, she could feel his eyes on her back.

Within weeks, he knew her schedule. He'd appear on her days off at the grocery store where she
shopped. He showed up at the mosque she attended on Friday evenings, though she'd never seen him
there before. One afternoon, she found him standing across the street from her house. When she came
home from work, he was there in a parked car, just sitting. Her hands had shaken so badly she could
barely unlock the door.

Her husband, Hassan, had wanted to call the police immediately, but Amira hesitated. She was afraid
of making things worse, of seeming dramatic. She grew up in a place where you didn't involve
authorities lightly. Instead, she changed her work schedule, asked her manager for different shifts.
The man adapted. He appeared at the new times. She stopped going to Friday prayers. She asked Hassan
to pick her up from work instead of taking the bus. Still, she'd catch glimpses of him—in a car
behind theirs, at the edge of a parking lot, always just far enough away that she couldn't quite
prove he was there.

By fall, Amira had become someone she didn't recognize. She wakes at three in the morning, her heart
racing, convinced she's heard a car pulling up outside. She checks the locks on the doors and
windows three, sometimes four times before bed. At work, she positions herself so her back is never
to a window. She flinches when the door chimes, her body tensing until she confirms it's not him.
Her coworkers have noticed she's become withdrawn, jumpy. The easy conversation she once had with
regulars has dried up. She's afraid to engage, afraid that friendliness might be misinterpreted.

The police eventually filed a report, but without a direct threat or physical contact, there wasn't
much they could do. The man—they identified him as someone with a history of obsessive behavior—was
told to stay away. He did, mostly. But Amira can't shake the feeling that he's still out there,
still watching. She doesn't know when she'll see him again, or if he's simply waiting.

Now, sitting in her living room in the evening, she finds herself unable to relax. Hassan will
suggest they go out to dinner, and her stomach tightens with dread. The thought of being in public,
of being visible, exhausts her. She's sixty-nine years old and feels trapped in her own life—a life
she built carefully over decades, now fractured by the relentless attention of a stranger. The job
she loved has become a place she dreads. The world outside her front door feels dangerous in a way
that's hard to explain to people who haven't lived it.
