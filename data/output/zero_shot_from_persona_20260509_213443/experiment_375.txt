# Maria

Maria sits in the waiting room with her hands folded in her lap, her navy conductor's uniform still
crisp from the morning shift. At nineteen, she carries herself with the careful precision of someone
who has learned to take up as little space as possible. She begins to speak only after being asked
directly, her voice steady but quiet.

She grew up in a small apartment with her parents and younger brother. Her father worked
construction; her mother cleaned houses. Maria remembers the sounds more than anything else—the way
her father's voice would shift from normal to something sharp and dangerous, usually after he'd been
drinking. She would lie in bed listening to her mother's protests, the crash of objects, the wet
sound of impact. Her brother would crawl into bed with her on those nights, and she would hold him
and count the seconds until silence returned. This happened maybe twice a month, sometimes more.
Maria learned to predict it by watching her father's jaw tighten at dinner, by the way her mother's
movements became smaller and more careful.

By the time Maria was twelve, she had stopped being afraid in the way children are afraid. Instead,
she felt a kind of numb responsibility—for her brother, for her mother, for keeping everything
contained. She became excellent at school, at making herself useful, at noticing what people needed
before they asked. When she turned sixteen, she got a job at a local restaurant. Work felt like
escape. At eighteen, she applied to the regional transit authority's conductor training program and
was accepted. Her parents were proud in their own ways. Her mother cried when Maria graduated the
program.

The job suited her. Maria liked the structure of routes and schedules, the clarity of her
responsibilities. She liked being trusted with something important. She liked that the train moved
forward in a straight line, that there were rules everyone followed. For several months, everything
felt manageable.

Then, during a late evening shift in February, a couple boarded the train during a stop. Maria
noticed them immediately—the way the man gripped the woman's arm, the woman's face turned away, the
quality of tension between them. The woman was trying to move toward a different car; the man was
pulling her back. Maria's stomach tightened in a way that felt both distant and immediate. She heard
her own voice—professional, trained—asking if everything was okay. The man swore at her, told her to
mind her business. The woman wouldn't make eye contact.

Maria continued the route, but something had fractured. That night, she couldn't sleep. She kept
replaying the scene, wondering if she should have done more, if she had somehow failed. Within days,
the intrusive images began—not just of that couple, but of her parents, of moments she thought she'd
moved past. Sounds that resembled raised voices made her heart race. She found herself unable to
focus on her routes, her mind splitting between present and past.

Now, three months later, Maria still works her shifts, but they've become an exercise in
compartmentalization. She drives the train while feeling like she's underwater. She goes home to her
small apartment and sits in the dark because light feels too exposing. She hasn't told anyone at
work. She hasn't told her mother, who would blame herself. She's started having moments where she's
not entirely sure which decade she's in, where the boundary between watching and participating has
blurred. She came to therapy because a coworker noticed her hands shaking, and Maria realized she
couldn't hide anymore.
