# Margaret

Margaret sits in the consultation chair with her hands folded tightly in her lap, her fingers
bearing the familiar calluses of someone who has spent thirty years working with precision
instruments. At fifty, she carries herself with the composed posture of a craftsperson—shoulders
back, spine straight—a habit formed over decades of leaning over her jeweler's bench. But there is
something brittle about this composure now, something that fractures at unexpected moments.

Three years ago, Margaret was closing her shop on a Tuesday evening in late October. She had owned
*Luminous*, her jewelry store, for fifteen years. It was her life's work—a small, elegant space in a
downtown neighborhood where she designed and repaired pieces, where regular customers knew her by
name and trusted her with their most precious items. She was forty-seven then, content in her
solitude, her work her primary companion.

A man came in as she was counting the register, just before seven o'clock. She didn't recognize him.
He asked about a repair, and she turned to check the work orders, and then she felt the knife. Not
the pain first—the shock. The absolute impossibility of it. He took the cash and some pieces from
the display case, and when she moved toward the phone on the counter, he cut her again. The second
time, she felt the pain. She remembers the warmth of her own blood and the sound of the bell above
the door as he left.

The physical wounds healed within weeks. The knife had been shallow; no vital structures were
damaged. The police made an arrest six months later. Margaret testified at trial. The man was
convicted. By every external measure, the situation was resolved.

But Margaret's body did not receive this memo.

Now, when she hears a bell chime—at a shop, at a door—her heart rate spikes and her vision narrows.
She has stopped going to restaurants where servers approach from behind. She drives the long way to
her apartment rather than pass the block where her store still sits, its windows dark. She has not
reopened *Luminous*. She tells people she is taking a sabbatical, but it has been three years, and
the lease is up for renewal next month.

At night, she lies awake, or she dreams of the knife—sometimes in the shop, sometimes in her
apartment, sometimes in places that don't exist. She wakes gasping, her sheets soaked with sweat,
and she cannot fall back asleep. She is exhausted in a way that sleep no longer touches.

Her hands, once so steady they could set a diamond in the dark, now tremble when she tries to work.
She has attempted returning to her craft—small projects at home, nothing demanding—but her
concentration shatters. She starts a repair and finds herself staring at the piece unseeing, her
mind replaying the moment she turned away from the register. Sometimes she imagines different
endings, as if enough mental rehearsal might change what actually happened.

She has withdrawn from her small circle of friends. The thought of being in public spaces, even safe
ones, generates a dread that seems disproportionate to the actual risk. She knows intellectually
that lightning rarely strikes twice, that the man is incarcerated, that she is statistically safer
now than she was before. This knowledge does not help. Her body remains convinced that danger is
imminent, that she cannot trust her own judgment, that the world has revealed itself to be
fundamentally unsafe.

She sits in the chair with her hands folded, a woman who built something beautiful with her own
hands now unable to believe in the possibility of beauty or safety or the simple act of turning her
back on the world.
