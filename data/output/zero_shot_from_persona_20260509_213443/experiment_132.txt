# Marcus

Marcus sits with his camera resting on his lap, fingers tracing the worn leather strap absently. At
eighteen, he has the technical skill of someone twice his age—his portfolio landed him work with a
local news agency before his senior year of high school—but something in his posture suggests he's
aged beyond his years in other ways.

Two years ago, Marcus was working as a freelance photographer in a conflict zone in Southeast Asia,
documenting humanitarian efforts for an international NGO. His parents, both immigrants, had
encouraged his passion for photojournalism. They were proud when he got the assignment. Marcus
remembers feeling invincible then, armed with his camera and a sense of purpose.

The attack came without warning. He was photographing a medical clinic when the mortar struck
nearby. He remembers the sound first—a noise so loud it seemed to erase all other sensations for a
moment. Then came the chaos: screaming, dust, the acrid smell of smoke. A woman next to him fell. He
kept photographing. His hands moved automatically, muscle memory overriding shock, until someone
pulled him away.

He got out safely. Others didn't.

Back home now, Marcus exists in a state of constant vigilance. Loud noises send him into an
immediate panic—a car backfiring on the street outside his apartment makes his heart race
uncontrollably, and he finds himself on the floor, hands shaking, unable to catch his breath. He's
learned to avoid fireworks, construction sites, and busy streets. The unpredictability terrifies him
more than anything.

Sleep has become a battleground. Most nights, he lies awake until three or four in the morning, his
mind replaying fragments of that day in obsessive detail. When he does sleep, nightmares jolt him
awake drenched in sweat. He dreams of the woman falling, of his camera capturing moments that should
never have been documented. Sometimes he wakes unsure of where he is, convinced he's still there.

His relationship with photography has fractured. He hasn't touched his camera for professional work
in months. Looking through a lens now feels like looking at a wound. The few times he's tried to
edit photos from assignments before the trauma, he's found himself unable to focus, his mind
flooding with intrusive images that have nothing to do with the work in front of him. He wonders if
he'll ever be able to do what he loves again, or if that part of himself died in that clinic.

Marcus has withdrawn from his friends, declining invitations and canceling plans. He feels
disconnected from people who haven't experienced what he has, as though there's an invisible barrier
between him and the normal world he used to inhabit. His family worries constantly. His mother asks
repeatedly if he's eating. His father suggests he "just move forward," which only deepens Marcus's
sense of isolation.

He carries a persistent sense of shame—not rational, exactly, but present nonetheless. He survived
when others didn't. He keeps photographing in his mind, replaying those moments, and hates himself
for it. There's a heaviness to his existence now, a weight that follows him from room to room.

At eighteen, Marcus exists in two timelines simultaneously: the present where he's supposed to be
finishing applications and planning his future, and the past where he's still in that clinic, still
hearing that sound, still watching people fall.
