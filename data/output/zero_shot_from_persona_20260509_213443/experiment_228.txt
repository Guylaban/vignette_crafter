# Marcus's Vigilance

Marcus sits in the office with his hands folded precisely on his lap, posture rigid despite the
comfortable chair. At seventy-five, he maintains the bearing of someone accustomed to control—a
career spent identifying threats in digital systems has trained him to notice everything, to miss
nothing. He still works part-time as a cybersecurity analyst, remote from his apartment, monitoring
networks for intrusions. The work suits him; threats have patterns, and patterns can be managed.

Thirty-two years ago, his daughter Sophia was eight years old when she developed a persistent fever
that wouldn't break. Within days, the diagnosis came: bacterial meningitis. Marcus remembers the
hospital room with the precision of a man cataloging evidence—the beeping monitors, the IV lines,
the way the pediatrician's expression shifted when complications emerged. His wife, Elena, kept
vigil at Sophia's bedside while Marcus moved between the hospital and home, unable to remain still,
unable to sit with the uncertainty. He needed to *do* something, to fix something, though there was
nothing to fix but the uncontrollable biology of his daughter's body.

Sophia survived, though the infection damaged her hearing in one ear and left her with occasional
seizures. She recovered more fully than the doctors initially predicted, but those three weeks in
the pediatric ICU fractured something in Marcus that never quite healed.

For the first years, he managed. He worked, he raised Sophia, he supported Elena through the
lingering anxiety. But after Elena died twelve years ago, something shifted. The grief of losing his
wife collapsed into the unresolved terror of nearly losing his daughter. Now, when he closes his
eyes, he's back in that hospital room—the fluorescent lights, the smell of antiseptic, the
helplessness.

He wakes most nights, often around 3 a.m., his heart racing as though he's received an alert of a
system breach. Sometimes he's disoriented for several minutes, uncertain whether he's in his
apartment or the hospital. He checks his phone compulsively, though Sophia—now a successful
architect in another state—is well. He texts her frequently, asking if she's eaten, if she's
sleeping well, if she's taking her seizure medication. She responds patiently, but he can sense her
exhaustion with his worry.

At work, his hypervigilance serves him well. He spots suspicious network activity others miss,
anticipates threats before they materialize. His colleagues consider him invaluable. But the same
alertness that makes him excellent at his job has colonized his daily life. He scans faces in crowds
for signs of illness. He researches medical conditions obsessively late at night, falling down
internet rabbit holes about rare infections. A cough from a neighbor sends him into a spiral of
catastrophic thinking.

He avoids crowds and has stopped attending the community center where he once played chess. Loud
noises—sirens, alarms—trigger an immediate surge of adrenaline and a flood of hospital memories.
He's become isolated, his social world contracting to Sophia's phone calls and the predictable
landscape of his computer screen.

What haunts Marcus most is not the acute trauma of those weeks, but the knowledge it revealed: that
he cannot protect the people he loves. That love offers no immunity against suffering. That
vigilance, no matter how perfect, cannot prevent catastrophe. At seventy-five, still monitoring
networks for threats, he remains suspended in the terror of a father who learned that some dangers
cannot be controlled, only endured.
