# Marcus and the Flood

Marcus sits in his chambers overlooking the city, his fingers tracing the edge of a case file he
cannot quite focus on. At thirty-one, he carries himself with the measured composure expected of a
district judge—tailored suits, deliberate speech, an air of careful consideration. But lately, the
weight of that composure has become almost unbearable.

Two years ago, Marcus and his wife Sarah were visiting his parents in their hometown when the
monsoon came. Not the usual seasonal rain, but something catastrophic. The river that had run
peacefully through the neighborhood for generations swelled into something unrecognizable, a
churning wall of brown water that swept through the district in the predawn hours. Marcus remembers
waking to Sarah shaking him, the sound of water already downstairs. He remembers the decision to
climb to the roof, carrying his elderly mother while his father held Sarah's hand. He remembers
watching neighbors he'd known his whole life swept past in the current. He remembers the sound—a
roaring that seemed to come from everywhere at once.

They were rescued by helicopter eight hours later. No one in his immediate family died. By any
measure, Marcus was fortunate. He returned to his position after six weeks, believing that structure
and routine would restore his equilibrium.

But the river followed him home.

Now, sitting in his chambers, Marcus finds himself hypervigilant in ways that make no sense. Every
sound of water—a running faucet, rain against the windows, even the fountain in the courthouse
lobby—sends a spike of adrenaline through his chest. His hands clench. His breath becomes shallow.
Last month, during a routine hearing about water rights in an agricultural dispute, Marcus found
himself unable to continue. He'd excused himself, claimed a migraine, and spent twenty minutes in
the courthouse bathroom with his forehead against the cool tile, trying to remember how to breathe
normally.

Sarah notices everything. She's learned to warn him before turning on the shower. She's stopped
suggesting they visit the coast. She watches him at night when he wakes drenched in sweat, always in
the same moment—the moment the water reached the second floor. He cannot articulate to her why he
now checks weather reports obsessively, why he's researched the elevation of their home three times,
why he wakes at 4 a.m. and cannot return to sleep.

The professional consequences are mounting in ways Marcus hasn't fully admitted. He's postponed
trials. He's recused himself from cases involving natural disasters or water law with frequency
that's begun to draw quiet concern from his colleagues. The chief judge asked him, gently, if
everything was all right. Marcus assured her it was. This is what judges do—they compartmentalize,
they maintain the fiction of impartiality and control.

But Marcus is beginning to understand that the flood didn't end when the water receded. It's still
rising inside him, still threatening to breach the careful walls he's constructed. In quiet moments,
when he's alone in his chambers, he imagines the water returning. He imagines it reaching higher
this time, finding him wherever he goes. He imagines standing in his black robe as it rises, unable
to move, unable to stop it, unable to control anything the way he's spent his entire adult life
learning to do.

The case file sits unopened on his desk. Outside, clouds are gathering. Marcus watches them,
waiting.
