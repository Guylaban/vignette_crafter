# Marcus

Marcus sits in the office with his shoulders slightly hunched, fingers fidgeting with the edge of
his sleeve. At fifty-four, he carries himself with the careful deliberation of someone who has
learned to take up as little space as possible. He works as a senior software engineer at a mid-
sized tech company, a position he's held for nearly eight years—a job that requires intense focus
and problem-solving, skills that once came naturally to him.

Three years ago, Marcus attended a professional conference in Las Vegas. He was there to present on
database architecture, work he was genuinely proud of. On the second evening, after drinks with
colleagues in the hotel bar, he accepted a ride back to his room from someone he'd just met—another
attendee who seemed friendly and professional. In the elevator, the situation shifted. What began as
unwanted touching escalated into assault. Marcus froze. He remembers the ceiling tiles, the smell of
cologne, the feeling of being unable to move or speak. It lasted perhaps five minutes, though he
says it felt suspended in time. When it ended, the man simply left. Marcus was left standing in the
hallway, shaking.

He never reported it. He told himself it was because he was embarrassed, because he was a man and
men don't talk about these things, because coming forward would mean reliving it in detail. The
conference ended the next day, and he drove home to his apartment in Portland, where he has lived
alone since his divorce fifteen years ago.

For the first year, Marcus tried to continue as though nothing had happened. He went to work,
attended meetings, wrote code. But something fundamental had shifted. Now, when he enters an
elevator, his breathing becomes shallow and rapid. He takes the stairs instead, even in his office
building, even when it means arriving late to meetings. He's stopped attending conferences entirely,
disappointing his manager and limiting his career advancement. He's made excuses—budget constraints,
family obligations—but the truth is that the thought of being in a hotel, in an unfamiliar place,
makes his chest tight.

At work, he struggles to concentrate. Code that once flowed from his fingers now requires him to
read the same line six, seven times before it registers. His colleagues have noticed. His
performance reviews have declined. He's been passed over for promotions twice. He tells himself he's
aging out of tech anyway, that it doesn't matter, but he knows it's the hypervigilance that's
stealing his focus—the constant scanning of his environment, the way his body tenses when someone
stands too close in the break room.

Sleep has become unreliable. He lies awake for hours, his mind replaying the assault with vivid
clarity, sometimes adding details that weren't there, as if his mind is determined to torture him
with variations. When he does sleep, he often wakes in a panic, his heart racing, unsure for a
moment where he is. He's taken to sleeping with his bedroom door locked, though he lives alone on
the third floor of a secure building.

Marcus has become isolated. He declines invitations from the few friends who still reach out. He
doesn't date. He avoids crowds, preferring to order groceries online and work from home when
possible. His apartment has become both sanctuary and prison. He spends his evenings watching
television or scrolling through his phone, anything to keep his mind occupied. He drinks more than
he used to—a glass of wine most nights, sometimes two or three. It helps him sleep, or at least
helps him not care that he's not sleeping.

He presents now because his primary care doctor finally asked the right question, and Marcus found
himself telling the truth.
