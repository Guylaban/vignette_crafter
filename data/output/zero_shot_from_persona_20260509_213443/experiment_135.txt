# James's Story

James has worked the evening shift at the grocery store for nearly thirty years. He knows the layout
of those aisles like he knows the lines on his own palms—produce in the front, dairy along the back
wall, the stockroom where he spends most nights organizing merchandise. His coworkers depend on him.
The younger ones especially look to him for guidance, and he takes that responsibility seriously. At
sixty-five, he was planning to work another two or three years before retirement. He and his wife,
Amara, had started talking about visiting her family back in Ghana, about finally taking that trip
they'd postponed for decades.

That was before the hurricane.

The storm warnings came on a Tuesday in September, but James didn't leave. He'd weathered storms
before—nothing too severe in his forty years living in the city. When the manager called asking for
volunteers to stay and protect the store, James volunteered. Someone needed to be there. The store
was important to the community. He stayed through the night with two younger employees, boarding up
windows and moving merchandise to higher shelves.

By morning, the storm had turned catastrophic. The flooding came faster than anyone predicted. Water
poured through the loading dock, rising with terrifying speed. James was in the stockroom when it
happened—when the ceiling began to buckle, when the sound became something he'd never heard before.
He grabbed onto a metal shelf, his feet slipping on the wet concrete. The younger men were
screaming. One of them—Marcus, who'd worked there for only six months—went under. James reached for
him, but the water was too strong, too cold, too absolute.

They found Marcus's body three days later.

That was seven weeks ago. James returned to work three weeks after the store reopened. He thought it
would help, that routine would anchor him. Instead, he finds himself standing in the stockroom
unable to breathe, staring at the water stain on the wall that won't wash away. His hands shake when
he hears heavy rain on the roof. He's stopped taking the evening shift, unable to face that
stockroom in the dark, but the morning shifts aren't better. He watches the weather
obsessively—compulsively checking his phone, the news, anything that might tell him another storm is
coming.

At home, Amara watches him with worried eyes. He barely sleeps. When he does, he's back in that
water, Marcus's hand slipping from his grasp. He wakes gasping, drenched in sweat. Amara reaches for
him, but he pulls away. He can't explain why her touch feels suffocating. Some nights he sits in the
living room until dawn, unable to return to bed, unable to close his eyes.

He's snapped at coworkers over small things—a misplaced box, a question about inventory. He doesn't
recognize himself in these reactions. The man who mentored younger employees, who prided himself on
patience and steadiness, feels like a stranger wearing his skin. He's called in sick more times in
the past month than he did in the previous five years combined.

Amara finally insisted he talk to someone. He's agreed, though he's not sure what talking will
change. Marcus is still gone. The water is still rising in his nightmares. And somewhere in his
chest, beneath his ribs, there's a weight that never lifts—the terrible certainty that he should
have done more, should have been stronger, should have saved him.
