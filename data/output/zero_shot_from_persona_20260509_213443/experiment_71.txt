# Marco's Vigilance

Marco sits in the waiting room with his hands folded carefully in his lap, his weathered fingers
bearing the permanent scars of forty-five years spent welding—small burns, calluses, the pale marks
where metal has seared skin. He is seventy years old now, though his posture suggests someone older,
someone who carries weight that has nothing to do with his body. He arrives fifteen minutes early
for every appointment, a habit born from necessity rather than preference.

He came to this country in 1978, leaving behind a life he no longer speaks about. For decades, he
built a quiet existence—a small house, a steady job at a fabrication shop, a wife he loved deeply.
Rosa died three years ago. Since then, the silence has grown louder.

The torture happened in 1976, though Marco measures time differently now: before and after. He was
twenty-six, a union organizer in a country where such work carried consequences. He was taken from
the street one evening and held for seventeen days. He does not offer details, and his therapist
does not press. What matters is that he remembers every moment with crystalline, unwanted clarity.

For nearly fifty years, Marco managed. He worked long shifts, the rhythmic work of welding—the heat,
the focus, the creation of something solid and real—seemed to keep the memories at a manageable
distance. His hands stayed busy. His mind stayed occupied. He came home to Rosa, who knew without
being told that some conversations were not meant to happen. They built a life around what could be
spoken and what could not.

After Rosa died, the structure collapsed. The apartment became too quiet. The work, which had always
grounded him, began to feel like a cage. Three years later, Marco still goes to the shop most
days—he is a man who understands his obligations—but he finds himself unable to concentrate. He will
stand before a welding project and suddenly lose track of time, his hands trembling, his breathing
shallow. His supervisor, a younger man who respects Marco's skill, has begun to notice the mistakes,
the hesitations.

At night, Marco does not sleep. He lies in bed listening to the sounds of the building—the pipes,
the neighbors' footsteps, the traffic outside—and his body interprets each noise as a threat. His
heart races. He gets up, checks the locks on his doors and windows, sometimes multiple times in a
single hour. He knows this is not rational. He is a man who has always prided himself on
rationality, on control. The knowledge that he cannot control his own nervous system fills him with
a particular kind of despair.

He has begun to avoid places. The grocery store at rush hour feels dangerous—too many bodies, too
little control. The doctor's office, with its fluorescent lights and confined rooms, sends him into
a state of panic he cannot name. He stopped attending his church's community dinners, stopped
accepting invitations from Rosa's sister. He tells himself he is simply tired, that he is an old man
entitled to solitude.

But the solitude is suffocating him. His hands, which once knew exactly how to bend metal into
useful shapes, now shake when he tries to hold a cup of coffee. He is forgetting things—not
important things, but the small details that used to be automatic. He is losing himself in
increments, and he has finally come to someone because he does not know how to stop it.
