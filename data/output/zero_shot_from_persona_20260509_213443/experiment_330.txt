# Case Vignette: Alex

Alex sits across from me with their hands folded tightly in their lap, shoulders drawn up toward
their ears. They're a software engineer at a mid-sized tech company, someone accustomed to problem-
solving and logic, yet they describe their current life as feeling fundamentally broken in ways code
cannot fix.

Three years ago, Alex was driving home from work around 9 PM when they were pulled over for a broken
taillight. They remember the officer approaching the window, remember the sudden brightness of the
flashlight, remember their hands trembling as they reached for their license. What they struggle to
articulate—what makes them pause and look away—is what happened next. The officer claimed Alex made
a sudden movement. There was shouting. Alex was pulled from the car, thrown against the hood,
handcuffed. The officer's knee pressed into their back. Alex kept saying they hadn't done anything
wrong, kept trying to explain, but the officer's voice only got louder. When backup arrived, Alex
was arrested on charges that were later dropped. The entire interaction lasted perhaps eight
minutes, though Alex describes it as both instantaneous and endless.

Since that night, Alex's world has contracted. They still go to work—the routine helps, the logic of
programming provides temporary refuge—but the drive there involves careful route planning to avoid
that intersection. Some days, when a notification on their phone makes a sharp sound, or when a
colleague speaks loudly during a meeting, Alex's body floods with adrenaline. Their heart races.
Their breathing becomes shallow. Once, during a code review, a manager approached from behind, and
Alex startled so violently they nearly fell from their chair. The embarrassment lingered longer than
the fear.

At night, sleep has become unreliable. Alex lies awake, replaying the encounter, cataloging
everything they might have done differently—moved slower, spoken differently, been a different
person. On the nights sleep does come, there are dreams: being trapped, unable to move, unable to
make anyone understand. They wake gasping, their spouse beside them, worried and helpless.

Alex's marriage has strained under the weight of this. Their spouse tries to be supportive, but
there's a distance now. Intimacy has become difficult. When their spouse touches them unexpectedly,
Alex flinches. They've noticed their spouse has started announcing their presence before entering a
room, learned to move slowly and deliberately around them. The guilt of this—of making their partner
tiptoe through their own home—compounds everything else.

What troubles Alex most is the loss of autonomy they once took for granted. As a South Asian non-
binary person, they'd already navigated complex questions of identity and belonging. They'd built a
careful, deliberate life. Now they feel constantly monitored by their own nervous system, unable to
trust their body's responses. A routine traffic stop—something millions of people experience without
incident—has become a threshold they cannot cross without profound fear.

At work, Alex's performance has declined slightly, though they've managed to hide the worst of it.
They've turned down a promotion because it would require more client-facing work, more
unpredictability. They avoid going out, avoid driving when possible, avoid situations where they
might encounter police. The world has become a place of careful calculations and hidden exits.

What Alex wants most is to feel safe again—not just physically, but in their own mind, in their own
body. They want to stop scanning every environment for threat. They want to sleep without dread.
They want their spouse to stop worrying. They want to reclaim the person they were before nine
minutes changed everything.
