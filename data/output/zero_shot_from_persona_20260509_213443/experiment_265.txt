# Marco's Mornings

Marco sits at his kitchen table with a cup of café con leche that has gone cold. It's 6:47 AM, and
he has been awake since 4:15, when the sound of wind rattling the bedroom window jolted him from
sleep. His wife, Rosa, is still sleeping. He doesn't want to wake her with his restlessness, so he
comes downstairs in the dark, moving carefully past the photographs on the hallway wall—their
children, their grandchildren, the house they built together forty years ago.

That house no longer exists.

Two years ago, Hurricane María swept through Puerto Rico with a fury that Marco still cannot quite
articulate. He and Rosa had ridden out storms before—they had lived on the island their entire
lives—but this was different. The wind didn't sound like wind; it sounded like something alive and
malevolent, tearing at the structure around them with deliberate violence. The roof came off first.
Then the walls seemed to collapse inward. Marco remembers holding Rosa's hand in the darkness, both
of them pressed against the interior bathroom wall, listening to the sound of their life being
unmade.

They survived. Many of their neighbors did not.

Now, living in Florida with their daughter's family, Marco works remotely as a QA engineer, testing
software systems for a medical device company. The work requires precision, attention to detail, the
ability to identify failures before they reach patients. It is work he has done competently for
twenty-three years. But since the hurricane, something has shifted. He finds himself unable to
concentrate on the testing protocols that once felt natural to him. His mind drifts to structural
integrity—not of code, but of walls, of roofs, of the things meant to protect us. When a colleague
mentions that a software update failed to account for edge cases, Marco's chest tightens with an
anxiety that feels disproportionate to the situation.

The hypervigilance is worst in the mornings. Any sound—the garbage truck on the street, the air
conditioning cycling on, Rosa's footsteps in the kitchen—sends a jolt of adrenaline through his
body. He lies in bed waiting for the next noise, the next threat. Sleep, when it comes, is thin and
interrupted. He dreams of wind, of the particular sound of drywall cracking, of Rosa's voice calling
his name from a place he cannot reach.

There are days when Marco avoids the weather section of the news entirely, though he finds himself
checking the hurricane forecast obsessively during storm season. He has installed storm shutters on
their daughter's house—expensive, unnecessary for their neighborhood—and keeps an emergency kit
packed in the garage. Rosa has gently suggested that they are safe here, that he needs to stop
preparing for a disaster that won't come. But Marco cannot quite believe in safety anymore. The
world has shown him what it is capable of.

His daughter notices that he has become quieter, that he declines invitations to family gatherings.
He tells her he is simply tired, that retirement is different than he expected. What he doesn't tell
her is that crowds make him feel exposed, that he cannot bear to be in spaces without multiple
exits, that the smell of rain—which he once loved—now makes his stomach clench with dread.

Marco finishes his cold coffee and watches the sky lighten from black to gray. In an hour, he will
open his laptop and begin testing. He will find the errors, document them, move methodically through
the checklist. This is what he knows how to do. Control the variables. Identify the failures.
Prepare for what might break. It is the only way he knows to make sense of a world that has already
broken him once.
