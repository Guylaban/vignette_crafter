# Marcus

Marcus sits in the waiting room of the clinic with his hands folded carefully in his lap. At fifty-
three, he has the measured composure of someone who has spent three decades learning to listen to
others' pain without flinching. His wedding ring catches the fluorescent light as he shifts in his
seat.

Eighteen months ago, Marcus was assaulted by a client—a man he'd been working with for six months
through the community mental health agency where Marcus coordinates services for vulnerable
populations. The assault happened in Marcus's office after hours, on a Tuesday in March. Marcus had
stayed late to finish paperwork, something he did regularly. The client had come back claiming he'd
forgotten his jacket. What happened next is something Marcus still struggles to articulate clearly,
even to his wife.

Marcus describes the assault itself with clinical precision, as if reporting on someone else's case
file. But his body betrays him. His jaw tightens. He stops breathing normally. What emerges more
clearly is what came after—the way he called 911 from his car in the parking lot, the way he sat in
the emergency room for hours feeling simultaneously hyperaware of every sound and completely numb,
the way he had to tell his supervisor, his wife, the police.

The weeks following were strange. Marcus returned to work after two weeks, partly because he needed
to feel like himself, partly because he feared that staying home would mean admitting something had
fundamentally broken. His colleagues were careful around him, which made everything worse. He found
himself minimizing what happened in conversations, watching people's faces shift when he mentioned
it, then quickly changing the subject. It felt easier to absorb their discomfort than to sit in his
own.

But the body keeps score in ways the mind tries to deny. Marcus began having trouble sleeping around
month three. He'd lie awake next to his wife, Sarah, listening to her breathe, afraid of what he
might dream about. When sleep finally came, he'd wake gasping, sometimes unsure of where he was or
who was touching him. Sarah would reach for him in the dark, and he'd flinch—not because of her, but
because his nervous system had learned to interpret touch as threat.

His work as a social worker, which had always felt purposeful and grounding, became increasingly
difficult. He'd be in a session with a client and suddenly feel his chest tighten. He'd excuse
himself to the bathroom and sit in a stall, grounding himself with the tile floor beneath his feet,
reminding himself that he was safe. He started finding reasons not to work late. He stopped
attending team meetings. The irony wasn't lost on him—he spent his career helping people process
trauma, and now he couldn't manage his own.

The hypervigilance crept in slowly. Marcus began noticing exits in every room, calculating
distances, assessing threats in ordinary interactions. At the grocery store, he'd feel his pulse
quicken if someone stood too close. He found himself avoiding the office where it happened,
requesting to meet clients elsewhere. His supervisor suggested a transfer to administrative work,
and Marcus felt the shame of that suggestion like a physical blow.

Sarah noticed the distance between them growing. He'd stopped initiating intimacy months ago. When
she tried to talk about it, Marcus would say he was fine, just tired, just adjusting. But fine felt
like a lie. Fine felt like pretending that some part of him hadn't fundamentally shifted on that
Tuesday in March.

Now, eighteen months later, Marcus sits in this waiting room because Sarah finally said something he
couldn't minimize away: "I need you to get help. Not for me. For you."
