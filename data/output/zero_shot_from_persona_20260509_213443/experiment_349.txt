# Robert's Burden

Robert has worked at the same bank branch for thirty-seven years. He knows the regulars by name,
remembers their children's birthdays, and takes pride in the accuracy of his drawer at day's end.
His colleagues describe him as steady, reliable—the kind of person you want handling your money. His
wife, Margaret, says he's always been someone who carries responsibility heavily, who lies awake
worrying about things most people would forget by morning.

Three years ago, Robert's life fractured in the span of a few seconds.

A regular customer, Mr. Chen, came in on a Tuesday afternoon to deposit a check. Robert was helping
another customer but noticed Mr. Chen waiting. He gestured for him to use the adjacent teller
window, where a newer employee, Jessica, was working. Robert turned back to his transaction. Minutes
later, he heard a crash and then a scream. Mr. Chen had collapsed. Later, they learned he'd suffered
a massive stroke. Jessica, in her nervousness at Robert's signal, had moved too quickly and
accidentally knocked over a metal display stand that struck Mr. Chen as he turned. The stand itself
hadn't caused the stroke, but the shock and fall made everything worse. Mr. Chen survived but with
significant left-side paralysis and aphasia. He never returned to the bank.

Robert replays those seconds constantly. He sees his own hand gesture—was it unclear? Too abrupt? He
remembers the metallic crash, the quality of that scream. He wonders if he should have helped Mr.
Chen himself instead of redirecting him. He knows, logically, that he didn't cause the stroke. But
logic and the feeling in his chest are two different things.

The first months were the worst. Robert couldn't concentrate at work. He'd count the same drawer
three times, certain he'd made an error. He started arriving early to check that everything was
exactly as he'd left it. He began researching strokes obsessively at night, reading about warning
signs he might have missed, complications that might still develop. Margaret found him at 2 a.m.
watching videos of stroke rehabilitation, his face illuminated and anguished.

When Robert returned to his teller window, he felt watched—by customers, by colleagues, by some
invisible judge. He became hypervigilant about safety protocols, pointing out minor hazards to
management that had existed for years. He found himself unable to delegate tasks, convinced that if
he asked someone else to do something, it would end badly. The responsibility felt unbearable and
entirely his.

Now, three years later, Robert still works the same position, but he's not the same person. He
startles easily when customers approach his window. Loud noises in the bank—a dropped pen, a
slamming door—send his heart racing. He avoids looking at customers directly, afraid of missing
something crucial, afraid of being responsible for another catastrophe. His sleep remains
fragmented. He wakes at 3 a.m. with his heart pounding, convinced something terrible is about to
happen.

Margaret says he's withdrawn from their life. They used to travel; now Robert can't imagine being
away from home, away from control. He's stopped seeing friends. The guilt sits in his stomach like
something solid and permanent. He knows Mr. Chen is alive, that the stroke wasn't his fault, but
knowing and feeling are different languages, and Robert is fluent only in the language of blame.

He continues to show up at the bank, to count his drawer, to help customers. But something essential
has been taken from him—the simple peace of doing his job well.
