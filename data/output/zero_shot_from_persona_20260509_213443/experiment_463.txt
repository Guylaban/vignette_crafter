# Clinical Vignette: Leila

Leila is a seventy-five-year-old retired judge of Syrian descent who sits across from me with her
hands folded tightly in her lap. She carries herself with the composed bearing of someone accustomed
to authority and careful deliberation, yet there is a brittleness beneath this composure that
becomes apparent as she speaks. Her husband of fifty-one years sits beside her, his presence
steadying but not quite able to ease the tension in her shoulders.

Three years ago, Leila's youngest son, Karim, died suddenly of an undiagnosed heart condition. He
was forty-eight. She found him on the kitchen floor of his home while visiting to help with his
children during a school holiday. The shock of that moment—the way his body felt when she knelt
beside him, the way time seemed to fracture between the person he was and the person he had
become—remains vivid in ways that other memories have begun to fade with age.

For forty years, Leila presided over family law cases, making decisions that affected the lives of
children and families. She was known for her careful consideration, her ability to weigh competing
truths and arrive at reasoned judgments. She retired at seventy-two, imagining a life of travel and
time with her grandchildren. She had earned that peace, she believed. Instead, she found herself
unable to leave her home for weeks after Karim's death.

The nightmares began almost immediately. In them, she is always too late—she arrives at his house to
find him already cold, or she is somehow in the kitchen but cannot move, watching helplessly. She
wakes at three or four in the morning, her heart racing, and lies awake until dawn, afraid to return
to sleep. Her husband has learned not to speak during these hours. She simply sits on the edge of
the bed, sometimes for hours, until the sky lightens.

During the day, she feels numb in a way that confuses her. Grief, she understands. This is something
different—a hollowing out that makes ordinary activities feel surreal. When she tries to visit
Karim's children, her grandchildren, she feels distant from them, as though she is watching their
lives through glass. She wants to be present for them; instead, she finds herself irritable and
withdrawn. Twice, she has left family gatherings abruptly, overwhelmed by a sudden panic that feels
like drowning.

Leila avoids the kitchen where she found him, though she and her husband have lived in this home for
thirty years. She takes the long way around it, or asks her husband to retrieve what she needs. She
has not returned to the courthouse to visit former colleagues, despite their invitations. The
building where she spent four decades administering justice now feels dangerous somehow, though she
cannot articulate why.

What troubles her most is the intrusive quality of her thoughts. Memories arrive unbidden—Karim as a
boy, Karim at his law school graduation, Karim's laugh—and with them comes a crushing sense of
injustice that she cannot seem to reason away. She spent her entire career believing in order, in
the possibility of fair outcomes, in the human capacity to make sense of loss. Now she finds herself
unable to accept that a man so vital, so necessary to his children, could simply cease to exist
without warning or explanation.

She tells me that she does not recognize herself anymore. The woman who commanded courtrooms, who
made decisions with confidence, feels like a stranger. She is waiting, she says, to feel like
herself again. But she is also afraid that she never will.
