# Marcus

Marcus sits in the leather chair with his shoulders pulled back, spine straight—a posture he's
maintained for thirty-two years in bomb disposal. At fifty-five, he still moves like a man who has
learned to keep his body controlled, deliberate. His wife, Amara, insisted he come to therapy. She'd
stopped asking him to come to bed at night, she'd said, because she was tired of him leaving at
three in the morning when the nightmares came.

Three years ago, Marcus was in Port-au-Prince on a routine assignment when the earthquake struck.
He'd felt the ground liquify beneath his feet—a sensation he'd trained his mind to process as data,
as a problem to solve. But this wasn't an IED or a suspicious package. This was the earth itself
turning to water. He remembers the sound most vividly: a low, grinding roar that seemed to come from
everywhere at once, as though the world itself was screaming.

He was near the National Palace when it happened. The building folded like paper. Marcus found
himself running, which he rarely does—running is reactive, uncontrolled—but his legs moved without
permission. He pulled people from rubble for hours. A woman's arm. A child's torso. He doesn't speak
about the details, but his hands move when he talks about it, as though they're remembering
something his mind refuses to fully articulate.

The immediate aftermath was manageable. Marcus is a man who functions in crisis. He worked with
rescue teams, helped coordinate debris clearance, did what needed doing. It was after—when he
returned home to Montreal and tried to resume his life—that everything fractured.

The tremors started within weeks. Not tremors in his hands, which would have been straightforward,
but tremors in his sense of safety. His house, which had been a sanctuary, became a space of
vigilant surveillance. He checks the foundation. He studies weather reports obsessively. He's had
the structural integrity of the building assessed three times. Amara finds him in the basement at
night, running his hands along the concrete, looking for cracks that aren't there.

His work, which once grounded him, now destabilizes him. Two months ago, while disarming a device in
a commercial building, Marcus became suddenly certain the floor would collapse. He knew it
wouldn't—the building was sound, his training was solid—but his body had decided otherwise. His
hands shook. For the first time in his career, he had to step back and let a younger technician
finish the job. The shame of that moment still sits heavy in his chest.

Sleep has become an adversary. When Marcus closes his eyes, he's back in Port-au-Prince. The ground
moves. His body remembers the helplessness, the fundamental knowledge that nothing solid can be
trusted. He lies awake instead, monitoring the house, listening to the building settle and shift.
Amara sleeps in the guest room now. He tells himself this is temporary, that he's simply being
considerate, but they both know it's something else.

He has stopped going to the gym, where he used to find order in repetition. He avoids restaurants
with high ceilings. He's turned down two promotions because they would require travel to unstable
regions. The world has become a place of hidden threats, and Marcus—who has spent his life
identifying and neutralizing danger—cannot seem to identify or neutralize this one.

He sits now, waiting for the therapist to tell him what to do, his hands folded carefully in his
lap, as though they might betray him if he's not watching them closely enough.
