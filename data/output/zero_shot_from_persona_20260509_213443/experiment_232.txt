# Marcus

Marcus sits with his shoulders drawn inward, fingers interlaced so tightly his knuckles pale. At
twenty-eight, he carries himself with the practiced stillness of someone accustomed to reading rooms
for danger—a habit born from seven years working corrections at the county facility. But today, in
the quiet of the office, that vigilance seems to have curdled into something else entirely.

His wife, Elena, drove him here. She sits in the waiting room. Marcus mentions this unprompted, as
though needing to account for her presence, to explain why he couldn't manage the drive alone.

Three months ago, his father died. Not from illness or old age, though his father was sixty-one. A
sudden cardiac event while working in his small auto repair shop. A coworker found him slumped
against a toolbox. By the time the ambulance arrived, it was already over.

Marcus was at the prison when his mother called. He remembers the exact moment—he was conducting a
routine cell inspection on C-block, clipboard in hand, moving through the familiar rhythms of the
day. The call came through to the main office. His supervisor found him. The words themselves feel
unreal to Marcus even now, as though they were spoken in a language he doesn't quite understand.

What he remembers with absolute clarity is the drive to the hospital. The fluorescent lights. The
moment they told him there was nothing to be done.

Since then, Marcus has not returned to work. He sits at home most days, ostensibly recovering,
though Elena gently points out that he doesn't seem to be recovering so much as disappearing. He
wakes at three or four in the morning, his heart pounding, sometimes without any memory of a dream.
The bedroom feels too small. The house feels too small. He finds himself standing in the driveway at
dawn, staring at nothing.

When he does sleep, he dreams of finding his father. But in the dreams, his father is still alive,
and Marcus realizes—over and over—that he's too late. He arrives at the shop and his father is
there, conscious and aware, and Marcus understands in that terrible dream logic that if he had only
left work five minutes earlier, if he had only called that morning, if he had only gone to visit
like Elena had suggested the night before. The dreams are relentless in their variations on this
theme.

He's become hypervigilant in a different way than his job trained him to be. He monitors Elena
obsessively—checking that she's eaten, asking repeatedly if she's feeling well, panicking when she's
even slightly late coming home. He's convinced something will happen to her. He cannot articulate
why, exactly, except that it can happen without warning, without mercy, without any opportunity to
say goodbye or do anything differently.

At work, Marcus has become unable to focus. He stands in the yard or the corridors and feels
disconnected from his body, as though he's watching himself from outside. He's had two panic attacks
on the job—his chest tightening, his breathing becoming shallow, convinced he's having a heart
attack himself, convinced he's going to die the same way his father did, suddenly and senselessly.

Elena has suggested therapy. She's suggested medication. She's suggested he talk to his mother, who
is struggling in her own way, but Marcus cannot bear to speak with her. Their shared grief feels
like it might swallow him whole.

He sits now in this office, waiting to begin, carrying the weight of a loss that continues to ambush
him at unexpected moments—a song on the radio, the smell of motor oil, the simple fact of waking up
alive another day.
