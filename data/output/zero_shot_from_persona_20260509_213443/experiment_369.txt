# Michael

Michael owns a small hardware store in the downtown corridor of a mid-sized city, a business he
built over twenty-three years with his wife Sarah handling the books. He's the kind of man who knows
most of his regular customers by name, who remembers which contractor needs what brand of fasteners,
who takes pride in keeping the shelves organized and the aisles clear. The store is his identity—a
place where he feels competent and valued.

Three years ago, Sarah was hit by a delivery truck while crossing the street two blocks from the
store. Michael wasn't there. He was in the back office when the police called. She survived, but
barely. A crushed pelvis, internal bleeding, weeks in the ICU. Michael remembers the hospital
hallway smelling like antiseptic and his own sweat. He remembers Sarah's face swollen beyond
recognition, the machines, the sound of the ventilator that kept her breathing.

She recovered physically, though not completely. But Michael didn't.

Now, when Michael thinks about that day—and he thinks about it constantly—his chest tightens and his
mind floods with images he wasn't even present for. He's constructed them from the police report,
from Sarah's fragmented memories, from his own relentless imagination. He pictures the truck's
grille. He pictures her body. These images arrive without warning: while he's restocking shelves,
while he's eating lunch, while he's lying in bed at three in the morning.

The store, once his refuge, has become a source of constant dread. Michael worries obsessively about
Sarah being alone. He calls her repeatedly throughout the day, needing to hear her voice, needing to
know she's safe. His employees have noticed. They've learned to work around his anxiety, to handle
the morning inventory without him, to manage customers while he steps outside to call home again.
When Sarah is late picking up groceries, Michael's mind races through catastrophic scenarios. He
can't focus on anything else until she texts that she's home.

He's stopped leaving the store for lunch. The thought of being away from the phone, of being
unavailable if something happens, feels unbearable. He's also stopped going to the hardware
suppliers' association meetings he used to attend monthly. The drive alone triggers a cascade of
intrusive thoughts about accidents, about the randomness of tragedy, about how quickly everything
can be taken away.

At night, Michael lies awake, his body rigid with tension. When he does sleep, he has nightmares—not
about the accident itself, which he didn't witness, but about variations of it. Sometimes Sarah is
hit again. Sometimes it's his daughter. Sometimes he's the one in the truck, unable to stop.

Sarah has gently suggested he talk to someone. She says he's different now, that he's become
hypervigilant and withdrawn. She's right, though Michael struggles to articulate how fundamentally
the world has shifted. Before the accident, he felt like things made sense. Now he understands that
safety is an illusion, that disaster arrives without warning, that the people you love can be taken
in an instant.

He continues to open the store each morning, to greet customers, to maintain the facade of normalcy.
But inside, he's still in that hospital hallway, still waiting for the next terrible thing to
happen, still unable to accept that he cannot control or prevent the uncontrollable.
