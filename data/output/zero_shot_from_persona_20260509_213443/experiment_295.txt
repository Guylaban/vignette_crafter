# Marcus

Marcus is a twenty-three-year-old urban planner from Lagos who now lives in a mid-sized city on the
coast. He works for a municipal planning department, a job he'd been excited about when he started
eighteen months ago. His role involves analyzing neighborhood development proposals and attending
community meetings—work that requires him to be present, articulate, and engaged with the public.

Three years ago, while still in university, Marcus was assaulted outside a nightclub late one
evening. He'd gone out with friends to celebrate finishing his exams. When he stepped outside alone
to take a phone call, two men approached him quickly. The encounter lasted only minutes, but it was
violent. Marcus was beaten and robbed. He remembers the shock of the first blow, the taste of blood,
the feeling of being utterly helpless as multiple fists connected with his body. He was found by
security staff and taken to hospital, where he received treatment for a fractured rib and multiple
contusions.

In the weeks following, Marcus tried to move forward. He finished university, started his job, and
told himself that he was fine. But the assault had left something fractured inside him that his body
wouldn't let him forget.

Now, when Marcus sits in community planning meetings—rooms full of people discussing zoning changes
and infrastructure—his mind often isn't there. His attention fractures. He finds himself scanning
the room compulsively, noting exits, tracking who is standing near him, assessing threat. During
presentations, his chest tightens. His hands shake as he holds documents. Colleagues have begun
asking if he's alright, noting that he seems distracted or tense.

He has stopped going out in the evenings. The thought of being outside after dark generates an
immediate, visceral panic that he struggles to contain. His friends have largely stopped inviting
him. He tells them he's busy with work, but the truth is more complicated—the world outside feels
dangerous in a way it didn't before, and the idea of being in a public space where something
unexpected could happen makes his heart race uncontrollably.

Sleep has become difficult. Marcus lies awake replaying the assault, his mind cycling through what
he could have done differently, how he might have fought back more effectively, whether he was
somehow responsible for putting himself in that position. When he does sleep, he has
nightmares—sometimes he's back outside that nightclub, sometimes the violence is happening in his
apartment or his office. He wakes gasping, disoriented, his body drenched in sweat.

During the day, certain things trigger an overwhelming flood of fear and memory. A car backfiring on
his commute to work sends adrenaline surging through his system. A man who resembles one of his
attackers passing him on the street causes him to freeze, his body unable to move for several
seconds. Loud voices in the office make him flinch visibly.

Marcus has become increasingly withdrawn at work. He's begun requesting to work from home, citing
productivity reasons. His supervisor has expressed concern about his reduced participation in
meetings. Marcus himself recognizes that his career trajectory is being affected, but he feels
unable to control his responses. The physical sensations—the racing heart, the difficulty breathing,
the tension in his muscles—feel involuntary, as though his body has decided the world is a dangerous
place and won't accept his rational arguments to the contrary.

He carries a persistent sense of dread, even during quiet moments. There's a heaviness to his days,
a exhaustion that sleep doesn't relieve. He feels disconnected from the person he was before,
uncertain whether that version of himself still exists.
