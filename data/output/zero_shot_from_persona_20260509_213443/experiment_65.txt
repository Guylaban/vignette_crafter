# Margaret

Margaret sits in the office with her hands folded in her lap, her posture careful and contained. At
sixty-seven, she carries herself with the deliberate precision of someone who has learned to take up
as little space as possible. She speaks softly, choosing her words with the same methodical
attention she once brought to her database work before she retired last year.

She met Robert at a community center in her early sixties, five years after her husband passed. She
had been lonely—a quiet loneliness that settled into the routines of her days like dust. Robert was
attentive at first, interested in her stories about growing up in Samoa, curious about her work
managing complex data systems. He made her feel seen. Within eighteen months, they were living
together.

The first time he hit her, she was tidying the kitchen. She had reorganized his tools in the garage
without asking. His hand came so fast she didn't have time to process it—just a sharp pain across
her cheek and the shock of it. He apologized immediately, held her, said it would never happen
again. Margaret, who had spent her career solving problems through logic and careful analysis, tried
to understand what she had done wrong. She had been careless. She would be more thoughtful.

But the hitting continued, and it spread. A shove into the counter when she questioned where he'd
been. A slap when she served dinner he didn't like. A fist to her ribs one night when she tried to
leave the bedroom. Each time, there were reasons—things she had said or done, ways she had failed to
anticipate his needs. Margaret found herself moving through the house like she was navigating a
minefield, hyperaware of his moods, his tone, the way he held his shoulders.

After fourteen months, when he broke her wrist during an argument about money, something shifted.
She called her daughter. She went to the hospital. She filed a report, though her hands shook so
badly she could barely sign the paperwork.

That was three years ago. The restraining order is still in place. But Margaret doesn't feel safer.

She still flinches when someone moves too quickly near her. In the grocery store, if a man stands
too close behind her in line, her heart begins to race and her breathing becomes shallow. She has to
leave. Sometimes she sits in her car for twenty minutes before she can drive. She has stopped going
to her book club because the community center where it meets is too close to where she met Robert,
and the thought of running into him—despite the order, despite knowing it's unlikely—sends her into
a panic that lasts for days.

At night, she lies awake or jolts awake, her body convinced that Robert is in the house. She checks
the locks repeatedly. She keeps her phone on the nightstand. She has installed motion-sensor lights
outside, though she lives in a secure building. Sleep, when it comes, is thin and fragmented.

Her daughter worries that Margaret has withdrawn too much, that she's let this define her. But
Margaret knows the difference between caution and fear, and she lives in the space between them now.
She moves through her days with the same careful attention she once gave to databases—cataloging
threats, organizing her life around safety—but the logic that once made sense to her no longer
applies. The danger felt random and absolute, and no amount of analysis has made it feel distant.
