# The Flood

Marcus sits in the armchair across from me, his weathered hands folded in his lap. At seventy-four,
he carries himself with the careful precision of someone accustomed to control—a tutor's habit,
perhaps, of arranging thoughts in orderly sequence. But there is a brittleness to him now, a tension
in his shoulders that suggests the order has fractured.

Three years ago, Marcus lived in a cottage in the valley with his partner of twenty-two years,
David. The cottage was modest but beloved, filled with books and photographs spanning decades.
Marcus had spent forty-five years tutoring mathematics to high school students, finding deep
satisfaction in the moment when a struggling teenager suddenly grasped a concept. He had built a
quiet, meaningful life.

Then came the storm. Three days of relentless rain in July transformed the creek that ran behind the
cottage into a torrent. Marcus and David woke at two in the morning to water already seeping under
the doors. The evacuation order came minutes later. In the chaos of gathering essentials, Marcus
made a choice he replays constantly: he grabbed his laptop and a few photo albums but left behind
decades of student notes, letters, the physical record of his professional identity. They escaped to
higher ground with minutes to spare. The cottage was destroyed—swept downstream in the floodwaters
along with everything else they couldn't carry.

Since then, Marcus exists in a state of hypervigilance disguised as normalcy. He checks weather
forecasts obsessively, sometimes five or six times a day, his eyes scanning for storm warnings with
the intensity he once reserved for parsing mathematical proofs. When clouds darken, his chest
tightens. He has moved with David to a house on a hill, far from any waterway, but the geography
offers no real comfort.

The intrusive memories arrive without warning. He'll be preparing a lesson plan for one of his three
current tutoring students—he returned to work after a year, unable to bear the inactivity—and
suddenly he's back in the cottage, water rising around his ankles, David's voice calling from
another room. The memory is vivid and involuntary, pulling him away from the present moment.
Sometimes it takes him several minutes to orient himself again to the dry room, the desk, the
mathematics textbook open before him.

His sleep is fragmented. He lies awake listening to rain on the roof, cataloging each sound,
assessing threat. When he does sleep, he dreams of water—always rising, always inevitable. He wakes
gasping, sometimes waking David, who has learned to simply hold his hand in the darkness without
speaking.

David has been patient, but Marcus senses the strain. He has withdrawn from the social life they
once enjoyed. Dinner invitations are declined. The hiking group he belonged to no longer sees him.
There seems little point in planning future trips or discussing retirement renovations. He cannot
imagine a future with any certainty, cannot shake the bone-deep knowledge that everything can be
taken in hours.

What troubles Marcus most is the erosion of something less tangible than the cottage. He has always
been a man who solved problems, who helped others understand complexity. Now he finds himself unable
to organize his own thoughts coherently. The confidence that defined his professional identity—the
quiet certainty with which he once approached a difficult student or an elegant proof—has been
replaced by a persistent doubt in his own capacity to understand anything at all.

He shows up to his tutoring sessions because commitment matters to him. But he moves through them
now like someone reading from a script, present in body but fractured in mind, waiting always for
the next storm.
