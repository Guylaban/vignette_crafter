# Sarah

Sarah sits in the leather chair across from the office, her posture impeccable, her hands folded
neatly in her lap. At first glance, she appears composed—the kind of woman accustomed to public
speaking and boardroom negotiations. She is a city council member, after all, respected in her
community for her work on housing policy and community safety initiatives. But as she begins to talk
about why she's here, something shifts. Her jaw tightens slightly, and she pauses longer than
necessary between sentences.

She was eight years old when her mother left her father. Sarah remembers this not as a discrete
event but as a series of sensory snapshots: the sound of her father's voice escalating in the
kitchen, her mother's muffled crying from behind their bedroom door, the particular way the hallway
looked when she pressed herself against the wall, trying to become invisible. One night, when Sarah
was nine, her father threw a coffee mug. It shattered against the wall near where her mother stood.
Sarah watched her mother's face—the shock, the resignation—and something crystallized in her then.
She needed to be in control. She needed to be safe.

For decades, Sarah managed this through achievement and order. She built a career in public service,
married a steady, quiet man who rarely raised his voice, and cultivated a life of careful stability.
She became someone who solved problems, who made decisions, who was relied upon. The childhood
terrors lived somewhere below the surface, manageable, almost forgotten.

Then, six months ago, a constituent came to her office in crisis. A woman fleeing domestic violence,
desperate for emergency housing. As Sarah listened to her story—the escalating tension, the walking
on eggshells, the sudden explosion of rage—her hands began to shake. She got the woman help, but
something had cracked open inside her. That night, she couldn't sleep. The next night either.

Now, Sarah finds herself hypervigilant in ways she can't quite control. When her husband raises his
voice about something trivial—a late bill, a miscommunication—her body floods with adrenaline. She
feels her chest tighten, her breath becoming shallow. Logically, she knows he is not her father. But
her nervous system doesn't believe her. She lies awake beside him, listening to him sleep, her mind
cycling through catastrophic scenarios. What if he changes? What if she's made a terrible mistake?

The work has become harder too. In council meetings, when voices overlap or someone becomes
passionate in debate, she finds herself dissociating slightly, watching the scene from outside
herself. She's missed two committee meetings because the anxiety beforehand became unbearable. She's
never missed anything before. This failure disturbs her more than the symptoms themselves.

Sarah has started having intrusive images—not just of her parents' fights, but of situations she
wasn't even present for, scenarios her childhood mind must have constructed from overheard
arguments. These images arrive without warning: during a budget presentation, while making dinner,
in the middle of a conversation with a friend. She finds herself avoiding certain parts of town
where she witnessed domestic violence incidents, even though her job sometimes requires her to visit
those neighborhoods.

She tells the therapist that she feels like she's failing at the one thing she's always been good
at: maintaining control. The irony is not lost on her. She has built a career on helping others feel
safe in their communities, yet she cannot seem to feel safe in her own home, in her own body.
