# Marcus

Marcus is a high school history teacher who has spent the last fifteen years cultivating a
reputation as the kind of educator students actually want to learn from. He decorates his classroom
with maps and artifacts, tells stories that bring the past alive, and genuinely enjoys the
intellectual sparring that happens when teenagers start asking hard questions. His colleagues
respect him. His students write him notes at the end of the year. His wife, Elena, says he's the
most patient person she knows.

Three months ago, all of that felt very far away.

He was walking home from school on a Tuesday evening in early autumn, taking the route he's walked
hundreds of times through the neighborhood near campus. He remembers the light was starting to fade.
He remembers thinking about the stack of essays waiting on his kitchen table. Then a man appeared
beside him—or maybe behind him, he's still not entirely sure of the sequence—and there was a knife.
Marcus describes it now with a strange detachment, as though he's recounting something that happened
to someone else: the blade was small but the intention was clear. They wanted his wallet, his phone,
his watch. Marcus gave them everything. The man ran. Marcus stood there on the sidewalk for what
felt like a very long time, though it was probably only minutes, before he called the police.

The physical injuries were minimal. A few bruises. Nothing that required stitches. But something
fundamental shifted in those few seconds that Marcus is still trying to understand.

He returned to teaching two weeks later, and it was immediately clear that the classroom no longer
felt like his space. He finds himself hyperaware of the exits, of where students are positioned, of
any sudden movements. Last month, a student dropped a textbook and Marcus's heart began racing so
intensely he had to excuse himself. He told the class he wasn't feeling well. In the staff room
later, his hands were still shaking.

Elena notices that he no longer walks anywhere. He drives to school now, parks close to the
entrance, and leaves immediately after his last class. On weekends, he doesn't want to go out. When
they do venture to the grocery store together, he positions himself so he can see the entrance and
exits. He's stopped going to the faculty happy hours he used to enjoy. He tells Elena it's just
easier to stay home, though they both know that's not quite true.

The nights are worse. Marcus lies awake for hours, his mind cycling through scenarios—what if he
hadn't given them his wallet, what if he'd fought back, what if it had been worse. When he does
sleep, he wakes in a panic, his shirt soaked with sweat. Elena has learned not to touch him suddenly
when he wakes. He's apologized to her more times than she can count for being distant, for snapping
at her over small things, for the way he flinches when she comes up behind him.

He's considered taking leave from teaching. The thought of standing in front of a classroom full of
teenagers, of being vulnerable in that space, feels impossible some days. But he also knows that his
identity is wrapped up in being a teacher, in being competent and present and engaged. The man who
loved his job seems to have vanished in those few seconds on the sidewalk, replaced by someone
Marcus doesn't quite recognize—someone afraid, hypervigilant, trapped in a loop of what-ifs that
won't quiet down.

Elena keeps suggesting he talk to someone. Marcus knows she's right. He's just not sure where to
begin.
