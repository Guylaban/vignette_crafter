# The Weight of Witness

Marcus sits in the worn leather chair across from the office, his weathered hands folded carefully
in his lap. At seventy-two, his frame is still lean and composed, but there is a brittleness to his
posture—as though he might shatter if someone moved too quickly nearby. He speaks in measured tones,
his Southeast Asian accent still present after fifty years in the country, each word deliberate and
precise.

Twenty-three months ago, Marcus was at the aid distribution center in the capital where he had
worked for the past eight years. As a refugee aid worker, he had spent decades helping people
rebuild after displacement and loss. He knew trauma intimately—had listened to countless stories of
violence, separation, and survival. But knowing about trauma and experiencing it directly proved to
be entirely different things.

It was mid-morning when the explosion occurred. Marcus was in the back office reviewing supply
manifests when the blast wave threw him against the filing cabinets. He remembers the sound
first—not the explosion itself, but the awful silence that followed it. Then came the screaming. He
pulled himself up, his ears ringing, and moved toward the main distribution hall where families had
been waiting to collect food packages. The scene he encountered is one he has not been able to fully
articulate to anyone, including his wife of forty-three years.

Seventeen people died that day. Thirty-two were injured. Marcus was not physically wounded, but the
psychological fracture was complete.

In the months that followed, Marcus attempted to return to work. He told himself that his experience
made him more valuable to the organization, that his understanding of trauma could deepen his
compassion for the refugees he served. But something fundamental had shifted. He would stand in the
distribution center and feel his chest tighten. His vision would narrow. His hands would tremble as
he helped families select their rations. After three months, his supervisor—gently,
respectfully—suggested he take medical leave.

Now, nearly two years later, Marcus spends most of his time at home with his wife, Sophia. She has
learned to recognize the signs: the way his jaw clenches when a car backfires outside their window,
how he insists on sitting with his back to the wall in restaurants, the small gasps that wake him at
three in the morning. He no longer sleeps through the night. Some evenings, he lies awake for hours,
his mind replaying the moments before the blast—wondering if he could have done something different,
if his presence in that back office was somehow a failure to protect those families.

What troubles him most is the hypervigilance that has become his constant companion. Walking through
the grocery store, he finds himself scanning for exits, assessing which shoppers might pose a
threat. When his grandchildren visit, he feels an urgent need to account for their locations at all
times. He recognizes this as irrational, but recognition has not diminished the compulsion. The
intrusive images come without warning—sometimes triggered by something as simple as a news report,
sometimes arriving unbidden during quiet moments.

Marcus feels profoundly disconnected from the work that gave his life meaning for so long. He reads
about refugee crises in distant countries and feels simultaneously called to help and paralyzed by
the thought of returning to that environment. His identity as an aid worker, as someone who helps
others through their darkest moments, has fractured alongside his sense of safety. He wonders if he
will ever feel capable of returning, or if the person he was before that morning is simply gone.

Sophia sits beside him now, her hand resting on his arm. He is grateful for her presence, even as he
feels the weight of his own suffering pressing against their shared life.
