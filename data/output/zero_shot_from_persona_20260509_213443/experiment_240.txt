# Clinical Vignette: Amara

Amara sits across from me with her shoulders drawn inward, her fingers picking at a loose thread on
her jeans. She's a slight young woman with dark eyes that seem to look through rather than at me. At
twenty-three, she works as an environmental scientist for a nonprofit focused on water quality
monitoring—work she once loved but now describes as something she simply shows up for.

Six months ago, her younger brother Kwame, nineteen, died unexpectedly from an undiagnosed heart
condition. He collapsed during a routine shift at his summer job without warning. Amara was not
there. She was at a conference three hours away, presenting preliminary findings on microplastic
contamination. By the time she received the call from her mother, Kwame was already gone.

She describes the moment her phone rang with a kind of detached precision, as if narrating someone
else's story. The way her supervisor's face changed when she told him what happened. The drive home,
where she remembers nothing except arriving at the hospital and seeing her mother's face. She does
not cry when she tells me this. She rarely cries anymore, though she tells me she should.

The first weeks after the funeral exist in her memory as a blur of people, casseroles, and her own
body moving through space without her direction. But around the eight-week mark, the numbness
fractured. Now, six months later, Amara exists in a state of hypervigilance that exhausts her. She
checks her mother's health obsessively—texts her throughout the day asking how she's feeling,
whether her chest hurts, if she's eaten. Her mother has begun not responding, which terrifies Amara
more than the checking soothes her.

Sleep has become a negotiation with her own mind. She goes to bed around midnight but lies awake for
hours, her thoughts cycling through alternative scenarios. If she hadn't gone to the conference. If
Kwame had gone to a doctor that year. If she had called him that morning instead of texting. The
"ifs" feel concrete, like they're actually happening in parallel universes she can almost touch.
When she does sleep, she has recurring nightmares where she arrives at the hospital and Kwame is
still alive, but she can't reach him—there's always a glass wall or a locked door between them.

At work, Amara moves through her days with mechanical competence. She collects samples, runs
analyses, writes reports. But she feels fundamentally disconnected from the work's purpose. The
environmental problems that once felt urgent now seem almost absurd in their insignificance. She
finds herself irritable with colleagues who complain about minor setbacks, and she's withdrawn from
the lab's social life entirely. Her supervisor has noticed, though he hasn't said anything directly.

Loud noises startle her intensely—a car backfiring two weeks ago sent her into a state of panic that
lasted hours. She avoids the hospital where Kwame died, which means she's avoiding an entire section
of the city. She's stopped going to the gym because the cardiac monitoring equipment there triggers
a cascade of intrusive thoughts about heart attacks.

What troubles her most is a creeping sense that she should be "over this" by now. Six months feels
like it should be enough time. She tells me she's angry at herself for not being stronger, for not
being able to simply accept that Kwame is gone. But acceptance feels like betrayal—like if she stops
checking on her mother, stops replaying that day, she's abandoning him somehow.

She looks at me and says, "I know he's not coming back. I know that. But I can't seem to believe
it."
