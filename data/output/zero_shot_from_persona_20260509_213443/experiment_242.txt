# Marcus

Marcus sits in the office with his shoulders drawn inward, fingers fidgeting with the fabric of his
jeans. He's a lean man with dark circles under his eyes that suggest many nights of broken sleep.
His wife, Priya, sits beside him—she's the one who insisted he come in. Marcus works as a senior
editor at a mid-sized publishing house, a job he'd felt genuinely proud of until about eight months
ago.

The incident happened on a Tuesday evening. Marcus had been leaving work late, around 9 PM, walking
toward the parking garage. Two police officers approached him near his car, claiming they were
investigating break-ins in the area. Marcus, trying to be cooperative, answered their questions. But
something shifted quickly. One officer became aggressive, demanding to know what he was doing there,
suggesting he matched a description. Marcus felt his chest tighten as the officer's tone escalated.
Within minutes, he was against his car, being patted down, while the other officer called in his
information. Marcus kept thinking about his mother's warnings, stories his father had told him about
being treated differently. The search lasted maybe five minutes, but it felt endless. Eventually,
they apologized—said it was a mistake, the description didn't match after all—and let him go.

Now, eight months later, Marcus can't drive to work anymore. He takes the subway, though even that
triggers him. Whenever he sees a police officer on the platform, his heart races, his palms sweat,
and he finds himself counting exits. At work, his concentration has deteriorated significantly. He's
an editor—his job requires careful attention to detail, the ability to lose himself in text for
hours—but he can't sustain focus anymore. He reads the same paragraph four, five, six times. His
boss has noticed. They've been patient, but Marcus knows the comments are coming.

The hypervigilance has become exhausting. He scans every space he enters, noting exits, potential
threats. When Priya suggests they go out to dinner, Marcus feels a spike of anxiety. What if they
get pulled over? What if something happens? He's started avoiding situations where he might
encounter law enforcement. He didn't attend his cousin's wedding two months ago because it was in a
neighborhood he didn't know, and the thought of driving there, of being vulnerable and unfamiliar
with the area, felt unbearable.

At night, Marcus lies awake replaying the moment the officer's hand touched his shoulder. He can
feel the pressure of the car against his back. He remembers the shame, the powerlessness, the sudden
awareness that his identity—his brown skin, his appearance—had made him suspicious in a way that
felt deeply personal and utterly beyond his control. Some nights he wakes in a cold sweat, his shirt
soaked through, his heart pounding as though the encounter is happening in real time.

Priya has been supportive, but Marcus can see the toll it's taking on her. She wants her husband
back—the one who was excited about editing, who made plans, who wasn't afraid all the time. Marcus
wants that version of himself back too, but the world feels fundamentally different now. He's more
aware of how visible he is, how his presence can be read as threat rather than simply as a person
going about his day. That awareness hasn't faded. If anything, it's deepened, spreading like a stain
through his daily life.
