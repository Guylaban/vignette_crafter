# Margaret's Careful World

Margaret sits in the leather chair across from me with her hands folded precisely in her lap, her
posture the kind that comes from forty years of sitting across from anxious teenagers in a school
counselor's office. At seventy-seven, she still carries herself with the composed attentiveness that
made her good at her work—until she wasn't anymore. She retired three years ago, though she says it
wasn't really a choice.

In April 2013, Margaret was in Boston for her granddaughter's college visit. She'd taken the train
up from Connecticut that morning, arriving at the finish line area of the Marathon around noon. She
wasn't a runner herself, but she loved the energy of the event, the way the whole city seemed to
come together. She positioned herself near Boylston Street, a few blocks from the finish, thinking
she'd catch the elite runners and then head to lunch. She remembers the weather was perfect. She
remembers the smell of hot pretzels from a nearby vendor.

The first explosion came at 2:49 p.m. Margaret describes it as a sound that didn't match anything in
her experience—not a car backfire, not fireworks, something her brain couldn't quite categorize even
as it was happening. There was a moment of confusion, then screaming, then the second explosion. She
was knocked backward by the concussion, her glasses falling off. She remembers the smell
changing—sulfur, smoke, something acrid. She remembers the blood on the pavement beside her, though
she couldn't remember how it got there. Her own left hand was bleeding from shrapnel, though she
didn't feel it at first.

The chaos that followed exists in her memory as fragments: a police officer's face very close to
hers asking if she could stand, the sound of sirens, the hospital, her daughter arriving. She had a
deep laceration on her hand that required surgery. Physically, she healed. The hand works fine now.

But something shifted that day that never quite shifted back.

For months afterward, Margaret couldn't be in crowds. The grocery store would send her heart racing.
She'd have to leave mid-shop, her cart abandoned. Loud noises—a car backfiring, a door
slamming—would send her into a state of acute panic that could last for hours. She'd find herself
holding her breath without realizing it, her shoulders up around her ears. At night, she'd wake at
2:49 a.m., sometimes for weeks at a time, her heart pounding.

She tried to go back to work that fall. She'd been a school counselor for decades; she knew how to
manage her own anxiety, or so she thought. But she found herself unable to focus on her students'
problems. She'd be sitting with a teenager talking about college stress or friendship drama, and
suddenly she'd be back on Boylston Street, her mind just... leaving. Her hands would shake. One day,
a student dropped a textbook and Margaret gasped so loudly that the girl asked if she was okay. She
wasn't. She knew she wasn't.

Margaret retired that December. Her colleagues threw her a party, and she smiled through it, but she
felt like she was watching herself from outside her body.

Three years later, she still doesn't go into the city. She orders groceries online. She's arranged
her life into smaller and smaller circles—her home, her daughter's house, the park near her
apartment on quiet mornings. She's learned to manage the hypervigilance, the way her body still
scans for threats, the way she still wakes at 2:49 a.m. some nights, her hand throbbing as if it's
still bleeding on that spring afternoon.

She tells me she's adjusted. But her voice is careful when she says it, and her hands never quite
unclench.
