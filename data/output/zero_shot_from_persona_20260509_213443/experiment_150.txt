# Marcus

Marcus sits with his hands clasped tightly in his lap, his leg bouncing in a steady rhythm against
the floor. He's twenty-one, with the kind of youthful face that seems at odds with the exhaustion in
his eyes. His wife, Sophie, whom he married just eight months ago, sits beside him—though there's a
careful distance between them now, as if they're both afraid of what might happen if they get too
close.

The accident happened fourteen months ago. Marcus was working as a junior electrician then,
apprenticing with a local contractor. He'd been building a life with Sophie, planning their wedding,
imagining the future. On a Tuesday afternoon in March, he received a call from the hospital. His
younger brother, Daniel, had been hit by a car while cycling home from university. Daniel was
seventeen.

Marcus drove to the hospital in a blur. He remembers the waiting room, the smell of antiseptic, the
way time seemed to move in strange increments—minutes feeling like hours, then hours collapsing into
nothing. Daniel survived the initial impact, but the injuries were catastrophic. A traumatic brain
injury, multiple fractures, internal bleeding. For six days, Marcus sat beside his brother's bed,
watching machines breathe for him, watching his parents' faces crumble. On the seventh day, Daniel
died.

The funeral was three weeks before Marcus and Sophie's wedding. They considered postponing, but
Sophie insisted they go forward. Marcus isn't sure why he agreed. He wore his suit and said his vows
while feeling like he was watching himself from outside his body. Sophie cried during the ceremony.
Marcus felt nothing.

After Daniel's death, Marcus couldn't return to work. His boss was understanding at first, then
impatient. By month four, Marcus had stopped answering calls. He's been unemployed for ten months
now. Sophie works full-time as a marketing coordinator, and Marcus stays home. He tells himself he's
looking for work, but mostly he sits in their small flat, scrolling through his phone, unable to
concentrate on anything. Sometimes he finds himself staring at Daniel's Instagram profile for hours,
reading old comments, looking at photos from a life that no longer exists.

The nightmares started almost immediately after Daniel's death and haven't stopped. Marcus dreams
about the accident with vivid, brutal clarity—though he wasn't there, he's somehow constructed the
scene from hospital descriptions and his own tortured imagination. He sees Daniel's body flying
through the air. He wakes gasping, his heart hammering, soaked in sweat. Sophie has taken to
sleeping in the spare room. She says it's because she needs rest for work, but Marcus knows it's
because his thrashing wakes her, because sometimes he shouts in his sleep, because he's become
someone she doesn't recognize.

During the day, Marcus is hypervigilant. He flinches at sudden noises. When Sophie is late coming
home from work, he spirals into catastrophic thinking—convinced something has happened to her, that
he's about to lose someone else. He's tracked her location on her phone, though he hasn't told her.
He avoids driving past the intersection where Daniel was hit, even though it means taking a much
longer route if he goes anywhere at all. Mostly, he doesn't go anywhere.

He carries guilt that feels physical, a weight pressing against his chest. He was safe at work that
day. He was alive, doing ordinary things, while Daniel's life was ending. He replays conversations,
wishing he'd insisted on picking Daniel up, wishing he'd been more present in those final months.
Some days, the guilt and the grief are so intertwined he can't tell them apart.

Sophie loves him, he knows this. But he can see the strain in her eyes, the way she's learned to
move quietly around him, the way she's stopped asking him about his day because his days are all the
same. Marcus is trapped in the moment of that phone call, suspended in the seconds before everything
changed, unable to move forward into the life that's supposed to come next.
