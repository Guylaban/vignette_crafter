# Clinical Vignette: Sarah

Sarah sits in the therapy office with her hands folded tightly in her lap, her shoulders hunched
slightly forward. At nineteen, she has the physical appearance of someone older—there's a
guardedness in her posture, a wariness in how she positions herself in the chair, angled slightly
toward the door. She's been married for just over a year to Marcus, whom she met in high school.
They live in a small apartment across town from where the assault happened, though she says it
doesn't feel far enough.

Seven months ago, Sarah was assaulted by an acquaintance at a party. She had known him casually
through friends, and when he offered to walk her to her car, she accepted without hesitation. In the
parking garage, he cornered her against her vehicle, and what followed was a violent sexual assault
that lasted perhaps ten minutes but has stretched across every day since. She remembers specific
details with crystalline clarity—the smell of his cologne mixed with alcohol, the cold concrete
against her back, the way her voice sounded when she tried to say no. She also remembers gaps,
moments where her mind simply went blank, and she's never sure if that's a mercy or another kind of
loss.

In the months following, Sarah withdrew from the community college courses she'd been taking. She
tells herself it was just practical—she couldn't concentrate anyway, and the campus felt unsafe—but
there's a deeper current of shame beneath the decision. She hasn't worked since, and the financial
strain has created tension in her marriage, though Marcus insists he doesn't mind supporting them
both. Still, she feels the weight of her uselessness, the way her days blur into each other without
structure or purpose.

Sleep has become a battleground. Most nights, Sarah lies awake until three or four in the morning,
her mind cycling through the assault in fragmented pieces. When she does sleep, she wakes gasping
from nightmares where she's trapped or paralyzed. Marcus has learned not to touch her suddenly in
the dark; she's struck out at him twice, her body responding to threat before her conscious mind
recognizes her own husband. The guilt from those moments sits heavy in her chest.

During the day, certain things trigger her without warning. A particular cologne in the grocery
store sends her heart racing and her palms sweating. The sound of a car alarm makes her flinch
violently. She's started avoiding public spaces altogether, ordering groceries online, asking Marcus
to handle most errands. When she does venture out, she's hypervigilant—scanning faces in crowds,
tracking exits, feeling her nervous system in a state of constant alert that exhausts her.

What troubles Sarah most is the way she's begun to blame herself, despite knowing intellectually
that she wasn't responsible. She replays her decisions that night obsessively: Why did she go to the
party? Why did she trust him? Why didn't she fight harder? These questions loop endlessly, and she
finds herself believing, in her darkest moments, that she deserved what happened, that there was
something about her that invited it. Marcus tries to reassure her, but his reassurance sometimes
feels hollow against the weight of her own conviction.

She describes feeling numb most of the time, punctuated by sudden waves of anger or despair. She's
stopped texting friends who reach out, convinced they're judging her or that she's somehow tainted.
The future feels impossible to imagine—she can't picture herself returning to school, can't envision
a version of herself that feels whole again. She exists in the present moment, which is both a
prison and the only place she can manage to survive.
