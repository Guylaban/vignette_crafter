# Marcus's Route

Sarah sits in the driver's seat of the number 7 bus, hands gripping the wheel at ten and two,
waiting for the light to turn green. She's been driving this route for three years—downtown to the
hospital, hospital to the transit center, back again. She knows every pothole, every stop, every
regular passenger by name. But her jaw is clenched so tight her dentist has warned her about
grinding her teeth at night.

Five years ago, she was twenty. She'd joined the military because her father had, and her
grandfather before him. It felt like the natural progression of her life, the way some people go to
college. She was stationed in Kandahar for fourteen months, working as a convoy driver—essentially
the same job she does now, except the roads were unpredictable and the stakes felt impossibly
higher.

The light changes. Sarah accelerates smoothly, her body moving through the familiar motions while
her mind splits in two directions at once. Part of her is present: noting the elderly woman boarding
at Fifth Street, adjusting her mirror, checking her blind spot. The other part is scanning. Always
scanning. The sidewalk ahead. The parked cars. The overpass coming up. Her leg bounces against the
floor mat even as she maintains perfect control of the vehicle.

It happened on a Tuesday in March, though she doesn't like to count the exact days anymore. Her
convoy was returning from a supply run when an IED detonated beneath the lead vehicle. She wasn't
driving that truck—she was third in line—but she was close enough to feel the percussion in her
chest, close enough to smell the burning, close enough to see things she still can't fully
articulate to anyone, even now.

The worst part, she thinks sometimes, is that nothing visibly changed about her. She came home with
all her limbs, all her faculties. Her mother cried with relief at the airport. But something
fundamental had reorganized itself in her nervous system, rewired her brain to believe that the
world was fundamentally unsafe, that disaster lived in the spaces between one moment and the next.

She passes under the overpass and her shoulders rise toward her ears. She forces them back down. A
motorcycle weaves between lanes two cars ahead, and her heart rate spikes so sharply she can taste
metal. She grips the wheel tighter, reminds herself that she's on a city street in North America,
that the motorcycle is just someone in a hurry, that nothing is happening.

But her body doesn't believe her.

After discharge, she'd tried college for a semester. The lecture halls felt too enclosed, too many
exits she couldn't control. She'd tried retail, but the fluorescent lights and constant noise made
her feel hunted. Driving the bus seemed logical—she was already trained, already comfortable behind
the wheel. Control. Predictability. A defined route.

Except the scanning never stops. The hypervigilance that kept her alive in Kandahar now keeps her
exhausted in Seattle. She sleeps maybe four hours a night, waking at 3 a.m. with her heart racing,
certain something is wrong. She's stopped going out with friends because crowds feel dangerous. Loud
noises—fireworks, backfiring cars, slamming doors—send her into a state of panic she can barely hide
from her passengers.

She pulls into the hospital stop and opens the doors. An elderly man boards slowly, and Sarah
manages a tight smile. She's good at the smile. She's good at pretending.

As the doors close and she pulls back into traffic, she wonders if this is what the rest of her life
looks like: perfectly executed routes, perfect control, and an internal landscape that never stops
burning.
