# Marcus

Marcus sits in the waiting room each Thursday at 3 PM, usually fifteen minutes early. He's a lean
man with careful posture and eyes that scan the room methodically—the exits, the windows, the other
people. At twenty-five, he has the kind of premature weariness that comes from carrying weight he
never expected to bear.

Two years ago, Marcus was backpacking through Eastern Europe. He'd saved for months, working
overtime at his sales job, imagining the trip as a reward for hitting his quarterly targets. He was
in a small city when the conflict escalated. One moment he was buying coffee at a café; the next,
the world fractured into sound and chaos. An explosion nearby. Screaming. His mind went white. He
remembers running, though he's never quite certain where he ran to, only that he ended up in a
basement with strangers, listening to the building settle and creak above them, wondering if it
would collapse.

He was evacuated three days later. The physical injuries were minor—a cut on his shoulder, bruised
ribs. But something deeper had fractured.

When Marcus returned home, his girlfriend Sarah noticed immediately. He'd always been the kind of
person who talked about sales metrics and weekend plans. Now he was silent for hours, staring at
nothing. The apartment felt too quiet, so he started keeping the television on constantly—news
channels, mostly, though he'd turn them off abruptly if footage appeared from conflict zones. His
body had become a stranger to him. At night, he'd wake gasping, convinced the ceiling was
collapsing, his heart hammering so violently he'd have to sit on the bathroom floor for an hour
before his breathing steadied.

Work became a performance he could barely sustain. Marcus returned to his sales position because he
didn't know what else to do, but the job that once energized him now felt like wearing a costume.
He'd be in the middle of a pitch, suddenly aware that he was talking about software solutions while
remembering the smell of dust and fear. His numbers dropped. His manager asked if everything was
okay. Marcus said yes, because what else could he say?

The hypervigilance is constant now. In meetings, he positions himself so his back isn't to the door.
Loud noises—a car backfiring, a door slamming—send electricity through his nervous system. He's
become unreliable in ways that confuse people. He'll commit to going out with Sarah and her friends,
then cancel at the last moment because the thought of being in a crowded restaurant makes his chest
tight and his mind race with escape routes.

Sarah tries to understand. She holds his hand during the nightmares, brings him water when he wakes
in panic. But Marcus sees the exhaustion in her face, the careful way she moves around him, as
though he's become fragile. He hates that he's become this version of himself—reactive, withdrawn,
unable to enjoy things that used to feel simple and good.

He still goes to work. He still shows up. But there's a part of Marcus that remains in that
basement, waiting for the next collapse, never quite believing he's actually safe. The world has
become a place where terrible things happen suddenly, without warning, and his body has learned to
expect the worst.
