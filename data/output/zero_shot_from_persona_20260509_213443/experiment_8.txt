# Marcus

Marcus sits in the office with his shoulders drawn inward, fingers drumming against his thigh in a
rhythm that seems involuntary. At thirty-five, he has the lean frame of someone who has forgotten
how to eat regularly, though he mentions his wife, Priya, tries to cook his favorite dishes most
evenings. He works as a UX designer for a tech company, a job he once loved—the problem-solving, the
creative flow states, the sense of building something meaningful. Now he describes it as something
he endures.

Three years ago, Marcus experienced what he calls "the collapse." He was thirty-two, at the height
of his career, leading a major redesign project. One afternoon at work, he felt a sudden, crushing
chest pain. The paramedics came. The diagnosis was myocarditis—inflammation of the heart muscle—a
life-threatening condition that could have killed him in that moment. He spent two weeks in the
cardiac unit, undergoing tests, lying in a hospital bed listening to the beeping of machines
monitoring his heartbeat, wondering if each irregular rhythm meant he was dying.

He recovered physically. The doctors cleared him to return to work after six weeks. But something
fractured in those hospital rooms that hasn't fully mended.

Now, whenever Marcus sits at his desk designing interfaces, his body betrays him. His heart will
suddenly accelerate, his breath becomes shallow, and he becomes convinced something is wrong. He'll
leave meetings to check his pulse, sometimes multiple times per hour. The irony isn't lost on him—he
designs for wellness apps, helping other people track their health, while he obsessively monitors
his own body for signs of failure. He's had three EKGs in the past year, all normal. The
cardiologist assures him his heart is strong. But the reassurance lasts only hours before doubt
creeps back in.

His sleep is fragmented. He lies awake next to Priya, hyperaware of his own heartbeat, sometimes
convinced he can feel irregularities that probably aren't there. When he does sleep, he has
nightmares—he's back in the hospital, or his heart stops, or he's trying to call for help but no
sound comes out. He wakes gasping, and Priya reaches for him in the dark, her touch both comforting
and triggering, because it reminds him that someone else is depending on him staying alive.

Marcus has withdrawn from friends. Social gatherings feel dangerous—too many people, too much
stimulation, nowhere to escape if panic rises. He's missed two family gatherings, telling himself
he's protecting others from his unpredictability. His concentration at work is fractured; he finds
himself staring at design mockups, unable to engage with the creative problem-solving that once
sustained him. His manager has noticed. There have conversations about "getting back to full
capacity."

He avoids anything that elevates his heart rate. He stopped running, stopped the occasional
basketball games with colleagues. He researches his symptoms constantly, falling down medical
websites late at night, finding new diseases to worry about. He's been to the emergency room twice
in the past year—both times convinced he was having another cardiac event. Both times, nothing was
wrong.

What haunts Marcus most is the randomness of it. The heart attack came without warning, without
reason. He did everything right—he was young, active, healthy. The unpredictability feels
unbearable. His body has become a threat, and he doesn't know how to trust it anymore. He doesn't
know how to trust that tomorrow will come.
