# Marcus

Marcus sits in the leather chair with his laptop bag clutched against his chest, even though the
session hasn't started yet. At nineteen, he has the polished aesthetic of someone who spends his
days thinking about user interfaces—clean lines, muted colors, minimal friction. But his eyes move
constantly around the room, cataloging exits and windows, the way someone might map an unfamiliar
app.

He works as a junior UX designer at a mid-sized tech firm downtown, a position he landed after
teaching himself design through online courses. His portfolio is meticulous, each project refined
through dozens of iterations. His colleagues describe him as talented but withdrawn, someone who
communicates primarily through Slack and prefers asynchronous feedback to meetings. What they don't
know is that Marcus chose UX design partly because it allowed him to create experiences for people
he'd never have to meet in person.

The stalking began eight months ago, though Marcus didn't recognize it as stalking at first. A girl
from his secondary school—someone he'd barely spoken to—began appearing in his Instagram comments,
then his DMs. Positive comments at first. Compliments about his design work. Then the comments
became more personal, more frequent. She found his Twitter, his GitHub, his LinkedIn. She showed up
at his workplace one afternoon, having figured out where he worked from a photo he'd posted. She was
waiting outside the building when he left for lunch.

Marcus had tried to be polite. He'd asked her to stop contacting him. She didn't. Instead, she
escalated. She created fake accounts to message him when he blocked her. She sent him photos of his
apartment building, taken from the street. She showed up at the coffee shop near his flat. She
contacted his mother on Facebook, introducing herself as his girlfriend. Each incident was small
enough that Marcus wondered if he was overreacting, but the accumulation created a weight he carried
everywhere.

He reported it to the police. They took a statement but told him there wasn't much they could do
without a direct threat. She was just expressing her feelings, they suggested. Marcus felt unmoored
by this response, as if his legitimate fear had been dismissed as a misunderstanding.

Now, eight months later, Marcus lives in a state of hypervigilance that exhausts him. He checks
every window before sitting down. He varies his routes obsessively, creating mental maps of
alternative paths. At work, he requests a desk facing the door. He's stopped going to the coffee
shop, the gym, the park—anywhere that wasn't part of a predictable routine she might have learned.
His sleep is fragmented; he wakes at 2 a.m. convinced he's heard something outside his window. Most
nights, he doesn't try to go back to sleep.

The stalking has stopped, technically. She moved away three weeks ago, and her last message was a
goodbye. But Marcus can't believe it's over. He finds himself scanning crowds for her face, checking
his phone compulsively for messages, reading old screenshots of her messages looking for clues about
what she might do next. His concentration at work has suffered. He's missed deadlines. A project he
was excited about feels hollow now—he designed a social feature and then realized he couldn't use
it, couldn't imagine wanting to connect with strangers online.

His mother suggested therapy. Marcus agreed because he wants to feel normal again, wants to sit in a
coffee shop without his chest tightening, wants to trust that his apartment is actually safe. But
he's skeptical. How do you convince your nervous system that a threat has passed when your body
still insists on scanning for danger?
