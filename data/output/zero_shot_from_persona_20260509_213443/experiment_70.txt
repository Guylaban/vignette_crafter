# Clinical Vignette: Captain Mai

Mai sits in the office with her shoulders drawn inward, her posture suggesting she's trying to take
up less space despite her accomplishments. At twenty-three, she commands a cargo vessel—a position
most people spend decades working toward—yet she carries herself like someone perpetually bracing
for impact.

She grew up in a port city, watching her father move between ships, watching her mother manage the
household alone. Her father was home irregularly, and when he was, the apartment became a different
place. Mai's earliest clear memories are of her mother's careful movements around him, of learning
to read the tightness in his jaw. By age six, she understood that certain sounds—a raised voice, a
door slamming—meant she should find a corner and become very small. By eight, she was protecting her
younger brother, positioning herself between him and their father's unpredictable anger. The hitting
came without warning. Sometimes it was his hand, sometimes his belt. The worst part, she tells me,
wasn't always the pain—it was the randomness. She could never predict what would set him off.

When she was fourteen, her father left for a ship and didn't return for six months. By then, the
damage was done. Mai had learned that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that people who claimed to
love you could hurt you, and that the only reliable person was herself.

She threw herself into maritime studies with fierce determination. The ocean represented escape,
control, and order—everything her childhood lacked. By twenty-one, she'd earned her captain's
license, the youngest woman in her company's fleet. Her colleagues describe her as meticulous,
demanding, uncompromising on safety protocols. She runs her ship with military precision.

But the hypervigilance that made her an excellent captain has become her constant companion on land.
Mai startles violently at unexpected sounds. When someone raises their voice in conversation—even in
disagreement, never directed at her—her body floods with adrenaline. She finds herself scanning
people's faces obsessively, reading micro-expressions, trying to predict danger before it arrives.
In the crew's quarters, she sleeps poorly, often waking gasping from nightmares she can't quite
remember but that leave her drenched in sweat and racing heartbeat.

She avoids relationships entirely. The thought of being physically vulnerable with someone, of
trusting someone that intimately, triggers such intense anxiety that she hasn't dated since high
school. She's had two brief romantic connections that she terminated abruptly when they tried to
touch her unexpectedly—even gently. The panic was overwhelming, irrational, but undeniable.

What troubles her most is a pattern she's noticed in herself: when crew members make mistakes, she
responds with disproportionate anger. She intellectually knows her reaction is excessive, but in
those moments, something primal takes over. It's as if she's not responding to a junior officer's
error but to something much older and deeper. Afterward, she feels ashamed and confused.

Mai came to therapy because she's begun questioning whether she can sustain this life. The ocean
still feels like home, but her mind has become a less welcoming place. She experiences intrusive
memories—not always of specific incidents, but of feelings: powerlessness, fear, the conviction that
safety is an illusion. She's started having panic attacks before her shifts, something that's never
happened before. She's wondering if the strength that got her here is actually sustainable, or if
she's been running on fumes all along.
