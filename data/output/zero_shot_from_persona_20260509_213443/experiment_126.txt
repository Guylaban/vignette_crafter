# A Calling Tested

Priya sits in the worn leather chair across from the therapist's desk, her fingers tracing the edge
of her dupatta—a habit from childhood that resurfaces when she feels unmoored. At forty-seven, she
carries herself with the composed dignity expected of a pastor, but her eyes betray something
fractured underneath.

She grew up in a small city in Gujarat, the daughter of a schoolteacher and a nurse. Religion was
woven into the fabric of her family, though it was her grandmother's quiet faith that shaped Priya
most profoundly. After university, she felt called to ordained ministry—a path that surprised no one
who knew her gentle certainty. She eventually moved to the United States, established herself at a
progressive interfaith congregation, and built a life that felt purposeful. Her marriage lasted
fifteen years before dissolving quietly; there were no dramatic ruptures, just the slow recognition
that they had become strangers.

Fifteen years ago, Priya took a sabbatical to do humanitarian work in a region of South Asia
experiencing political unrest. She went with the blessing of her congregation, driven by a
conviction that faith demanded more than words spoken from a pulpit. She worked with an NGO
documenting human rights abuses, providing pastoral care to displaced families. The work was
exhausting and sacred.

She was detained one afternoon at a checkpoint—a case of mistaken identity that should have been
resolved in hours. Instead, she spent four days in a facility where the line between interrogation
and torture blurred. She was beaten. She was subjected to stress positions. The interrogators
demanded information she didn't have, and when she couldn't provide it, they continued anyway. She
remembers the sound of her own voice breaking when she recited prayers—not out of faith in that
moment, but out of desperate need to remember who she was.

She was released without explanation. An international NGO intervened; she was never told exactly
how. She returned to the United States, told her congregation a sanitized version of events, and
tried to resume her life.

But something fundamental had fractured.

Now, when she prepares sermons about suffering and redemption, she finds herself paralyzed by the
gap between the words and the truth of her body. Her hands sometimes shake when she grips the
pulpit. She's developed an elaborate system of checking locks—her car, her apartment, her office—a
ritual she performs multiple times before sleep, though she rarely sleeps more than three or four
hours. Her dreams are vivid and terrible; she wakes gasping, her body drenched in sweat, convinced
she's still there.

She's hypervigilant in ways she can't entirely control. Loud noises make her flinch. She avoids
crowds, which complicates her work as a pastor. She's had to delegate more public-facing duties to
younger clergy, a decision that feels like failure. Her congregation attributes her increasing
retreat to burnout or age. They don't know.

The divorce, she acknowledges quietly, was partly about this. Her ex-husband tried to understand,
but there was a distance she couldn't bridge—an intimacy she couldn't quite reclaim. She felt safer
alone.

She tells the therapist that she still believes in God, still feels called to ministry. But she also
feels betrayed by a faith that didn't protect her, by a body that won't let her forget, by a mind
that has learned to see threat everywhere. She's begun to wonder if she can continue in her role, if
her presence—fractured as it is—serves her congregation at all.

She sits with these contradictions, waiting for answers that don't seem to come.
