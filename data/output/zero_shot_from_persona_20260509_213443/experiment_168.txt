# A Chef's Hands

Maria has been running her restaurant kitchen for twenty-three years. She knows every station, every
supplier, every regular customer who comes through the door on Friday nights. She is precise,
demanding of herself and her staff, the kind of chef who tastes every sauce and adjusts the
seasoning before it goes out. Her hands move with practiced certainty through her kitchen—they are
her tools, her identity.

Eight months ago, everything changed. It was a Tuesday evening, near closing. Maria was in the back
office doing inventory when a man forced his way in through the delivery door. He was looking for
money from the register. When Maria told him there wasn't much cash, he became enraged. He threw her
against the wall, grabbed her by the throat, and demanded she open the safe. She couldn't breathe.
She thought he was going to kill her. After what felt like an eternity but was probably only
minutes, he grabbed a handful of bills from the register and fled. The police found him three days
later.

Now, eight months later, Maria still comes to work. She has to—the restaurant is her life, her
family's income, her legacy. But it is not the same. She arrives before dawn, as always, but her
hands shake as she preps vegetables. Sometimes she has to stop and grip the counter to steady
herself. Her sous chef has noticed. Everyone has noticed, though they don't say anything.

The worst part is when someone comes through the back door unexpectedly. Last week a delivery driver
knocked loudly and Maria felt her entire body seize. Her heart raced so hard she thought she might
faint. She had to excuse herself to the walk-in freezer—not to grab anything, but because the cold,
the enclosed space, somehow feels safer than the open kitchen. Her staff exchanged worried glances.
She pretended not to see.

At night, Maria lies awake beside her husband, replaying the assault over and over. She can feel the
man's hands on her throat. She can smell his sweat. Sometimes she wakes up gasping, her own hands
clutching at her neck. Her husband holds her, but there's a distance between them now. She doesn't
want to be touched, not even by him, though she can't quite explain why. During the day she pushes
through—there are orders to fill, a team to lead, a reputation to maintain. But the pushing comes at
a cost.

She has stopped taking breaks. She barely eats, though she is surrounded by food all day. The
kitchen that once brought her joy now feels like a place where something terrible could happen
again. She finds herself checking the locks on the back door obsessively, sometimes five or six
times during a shift. She's installed new security cameras, motion-sensor lights. She has started
keeping her phone in her pocket at all times.

Maria still tastes every sauce, still maintains her standards. But lately she notices she tastes
nothing. Everything is ash in her mouth. She goes through the motions with the same precision, but
the passion has dimmed. Her husband says she seems distant, distracted. She snaps at him over small
things. She snaps at her staff too, though they don't deserve it.

She tells herself that she is fine, that she is managing. She is a chef—she is strong, she is
capable. But in quiet moments, when she's alone in the office, she wonders how much longer she can
keep doing this.
