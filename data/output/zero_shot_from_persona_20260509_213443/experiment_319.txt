# Maya's Careful Distance

Maya sits in the office with her shoulders drawn inward, her dark eyes tracking the movement of the
ceiling fan with an intensity that seems disproportionate to the task. She's a parole officer now,
has been for three years, and she's good at the work—her supervisors say so. But getting here took a
winding path that still catches her off guard some mornings.

Seven years ago, she was different. She was younger, more certain, and she believed that her
presence mattered in ways that could be measured and verified. She'd signed on with an international
relief organization to work in a displacement camp in South Sudan. The work was supposed to be
temporary—eighteen months of helping distribute medical supplies and coordinating with local health
workers. It felt important in a way that her previous jobs never had.

The camp was larger than she'd imagined. Forty thousand people in a sprawl of tents and temporary
structures, dust perpetually in the air, the sound of children crying a constant background hum.
Maya worked twelve-hour days, tracking inventory, training volunteers, trying to maintain some
semblance of order in chaos. She remembers the weight of the clipboard in her hands, the way her pen
would skip when she was tired, the particular smell of the medical tent—antiseptic and something
else she couldn't name.

On her seventeenth month there, armed men came. She doesn't talk about the details much, but she
knows them with the kind of clarity that doesn't fade. The attack lasted hours, though her mind has
fragmented it into disjointed moments: running, the sound of gunfire that seemed to come from
everywhere at once, hiding in a storage container with two other aid workers, the blood on her hands
that wasn't hers. When it was over, twelve people from the camp were dead. The organization
evacuated all foreign staff within forty-eight hours.

She returned home to her apartment in Auckland and fell apart in a way that surprised her. She'd
expected to process it, to integrate it, to move forward. Instead, she found herself unable to sleep
without the lights on. Loud noises—a car backfiring, a door slamming—sent her heart into a panicked
sprint that took hours to settle. She'd be sitting at dinner with her then-husband, and suddenly
she'd be convinced that the restaurant was unsafe, that they needed to leave immediately. He tried
to understand. She tried to explain. Eventually, they both gave up.

Now, at thirty, she works in the parole office downtown, and there's a certain logic to it that she
doesn't fully articulate but deeply understands. She monitors people who have committed violence.
She assesses risk. She maintains careful distance. Her colleagues sometimes comment on how
unflappable she is, how she never seems rattled by the men and women she interviews, some of whom
are hostile or manipulative or both. What they don't see is the careful architecture of control
she's built.

She still sleeps with the lights on. She still startles at sudden noises, though she's become expert
at hiding it—a slight tightening around her eyes, nothing more. She avoids crowded spaces when she
can. She's hyperaware of exits in every room she enters, a habit so ingrained now that she does it
automatically. She watches people's hands. She notices who seems dangerous.

The relief work feels like something that happened to someone else, some other version of Maya who
believed that presence could stop violence. This Maya knows better. This Maya understands that bad
things happen regardless of intention or effort, and the only reasonable response is to stay alert,
stay separate, stay safe.
