# The Passenger Seat

Marcus has been the principal of Riverside Elementary for twenty-three years, a role that has
defined much of his identity. He prides himself on knowing every child's name by October, on walking
the hallways with purpose, on maintaining the kind of orderly, safe environment where children can
learn. His wife, Catherine, says he brings that same precision home—their life together has been
predictable and comfortable, the way Marcus prefers it.

Four months ago, everything changed in the space of five seconds.

Marcus was driving home from a district meeting on a Thursday evening, taking the same route he's
driven a thousand times. He remembers the traffic light turning green. He remembers the sound
first—a terrible screech of tires and the roar of an engine—before the impact hit his driver's side
door. A pickup truck had run the red light at forty miles per hour. The collision crumpled the front
quarter of his sedan, shattered the window beside him, sent his head snapping sideways. He hung
suspended in that moment of impact, certain he was going to die.

He didn't. The paramedics extracted him carefully. He spent three days in the hospital with a
fractured collarbone, deep lacerations, and severe bruising. Physically, he's healed. The doctors
cleared him to return to work after six weeks.

But Marcus hasn't returned to work. He's on medical leave, though he hasn't told many people why.

The problem is that Marcus can't drive anymore. Not really. Every time he sits in the passenger seat
of Catherine's car, his heart begins to race before she's even started the engine. His palms sweat.
He finds himself gripping the door handle or the dashboard, his eyes fixed on the road ahead,
watching for the cars that might come from nowhere. When another vehicle approaches an intersection,
Marcus's entire body tenses. He holds his breath. Sometimes he closes his eyes, which is worse
because then his mind fills in the images—the truck barreling toward him, the window shattering, the
sensation of being helpless inside a metal box.

The school district has asked when he'll return. Marcus has told them he's still recovering, but the
truth is more complicated. He can't imagine sitting in his office, managing the day-to-day
operations of the school, when the thought of driving there—of being vulnerable on the road
again—makes him feel like he can't breathe. He's tried. He sat in the driver's seat of his own car
last month while Catherine watched from the porch. He made it to the end of the driveway before he
had to stop, his hands shaking so badly he couldn't grip the wheel.

At night, Marcus lies awake beside Catherine, replaying the moment over and over. He wonders if the
other driver saw him. He wonders if he could have done something differently. Around three in the
morning, when sleep won't come, he sometimes gets up and sits in the kitchen, drinking tea and
watching the street through the window, as if vigilance might prevent the unpredictable from
happening again.

Catherine tries to be patient, but Marcus can feel the strain. She's become the driver for
everything now—groceries, doctor's appointments, the occasional dinner out. She handles his silence
with the same gentle firmness she's always used, but he sees the worry in her eyes. Marcus has
always been the capable one, the one who manages. Now he feels like he's trapped inside his own
fear, watching his life contract around him, unable to find the way back to who he was before that
green light turned.
