# A Steady Hand

Marcus sits in the chair with the posture of someone accustomed to rigid discipline. At seventy-
four, his frame is still lean, shoulders back, though his hands betray something his bearing does
not—they rest flat on his thighs, fingers occasionally curling inward as if gripping invisible
controls. He was a fighter pilot for thirty-two years, flew F-16s, logged over two thousand hours.
He speaks about those years with a particular kind of pride, the way a man talks about something he
understood completely.

It was after his retirement that everything changed. He met Catherine at a community center in his
town, six years ago. She was vibrant, interested in his stories, attentive in ways he hadn't
experienced since his wife died fifteen years prior. They moved in together after eight months. He
was lonely, he admits this without shame. The military had given him discipline and purpose;
civilian life had given him a house that felt too quiet.

The abuse began subtly. Catherine had a temper that could shift like weather. She'd throw things—a
coffee mug once, which shattered against the kitchen wall. She'd corner him in the bedroom, her
voice sharp and cutting, telling him he was useless, that no wonder his first wife had grown distant
before she died. When he tried to leave one evening, she blocked the doorway and struck him across
the face. He was shocked more than hurt. A man trained to handle extreme stress, to make split-
second decisions under fire, found himself paralyzed by the intimacy of her violence.

It escalated over eighteen months. There were nights she'd wait until he was asleep and hit him, her
fists landing on his ribs, his shoulders, his back. He'd wake disoriented, his pilot's instinct to
assess threats kicking in, but it was her face above him in the dark. The worst night, she came at
him with a kitchen knife. He managed to disarm her, but the moment fractured something in him—the
realization that he couldn't predict her, couldn't control the situation, couldn't simply eject and
survive.

He left. Went to a shelter, then to his daughter's house. The legal process took months. Catherine
was arrested, eventually pleaded guilty to assault.

Now, in his apartment alone again, Marcus struggles with the aftermath in ways that confuse him.
He's survived combat, mechanical failures at thirty thousand feet, the death of his first wife. But
something about being hurt by someone he loved, in his own home, in the dark—it's rewired him. He
startles at unexpected sounds, his body moving before his mind catches up. He's installed new locks,
multiple deadbolts. He checks them compulsively, sometimes five or six times before bed.

Sleep is fragmented. He'll wake at 2 a.m., heart racing, convinced someone is in the apartment, and
he'll move through the rooms with his phone's flashlight, checking closets, behind doors. The
hypervigilance that once kept him alive in combat now keeps him trapped in his own home.

He's stopped attending his veterans' group. Stopped having his daughter over for dinner. The shame
is complicated—he was never supposed to be vulnerable like this, never supposed to let someone get
close enough to hurt him. He was supposed to be the one in control.

Marcus speaks about all of this in measured tones, as though narrating a flight log. But his hands
tell the real story—they shake slightly when he talks about the night with the knife, and they never
fully relax.
