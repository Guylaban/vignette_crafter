# Priya

Priya sits at her desk in the open-plan office, her fingers poised over the keyboard, but her mind
is elsewhere. A colleague's voice rises slightly during a meeting two desks away—nothing aggressive,
just animated discussion about a code deployment—and her chest tightens. She finds herself holding
her breath, waiting for something she cannot name. By the time she exhales, she has lost the thread
of her own work entirely.

She is thirty now, a senior software engineer at a mid-sized tech company, respected for her
meticulous code reviews and her ability to solve complex architectural problems. But the person
sitting here is not the one her colleagues see in pull request comments. That version of Priya is a
performance, carefully constructed and exhaustingly maintained.

Growing up in her parents' home in the suburbs, Priya learned early that her body was not entirely
her own. Her father's discipline was swift and physical—a hand across her face for a wrong answer in
school, a belt across her legs for talking back, his fist closing around her upper arm when she
cried. Her mother watched from the kitchen doorway, her expression unreadable, offering neither
intervention nor comfort. By adolescence, Priya had learned to move through the house like a ghost,
to anticipate his moods, to make herself smaller. She became an excellent student, channeling her
vigilance into achievement. School was escape; work was salvation.

Now, at thirty, married to Arjun for three years, Priya finds that safety remains elusive. When her
husband reaches for her quickly—even to catch her arm playfully as she passes—she flinches. Her body
remembers before her mind can catch up. Arjun has never raised his voice in anger, has never touched
her in anything but gentleness, and yet she sometimes lies awake beside him at night, her nervous
system convinced that danger is imminent. She monitors his facial expressions the way she once
monitored her father's, searching for the subtle shift that precedes violence.

At work, she has developed an almost supernatural ability to detect tension in others. She reads her
manager's emails for hidden criticism, interprets a colleague's brief response as personal
rejection, and finds herself apologizing reflexively even when she has done nothing wrong. In
meetings, when someone disagrees with her technical approach, her hands shake slightly. She
experiences it as criticism of her entire self, a judgment rendered on her worthiness.

The nightmares have intensified in recent months. She wakes gasping, sometimes unsure whether she is
in her childhood bedroom or her marital bed. In these moments, the years collapse. She is both the
frightened girl and the accomplished woman, trapped in a body that refuses to believe it is safe.
She has started avoiding certain textures—anything that reminds her of a belt, a hand—and finds
herself changing clothes multiple times a day, never quite comfortable in her own skin.

Her sleep is fragmented. She lies awake for hours, her mind running through worst-case scenarios,
her body rigid beside Arjun's sleeping form. She has taken to leaving the bedroom light on low,
telling herself it is for practical reasons, but she knows it is for vigilance. Darkness feels
dangerous.

Priya excels at her job precisely because of her hypervigilance, her need for control, her ability
to anticipate problems before they emerge. But the same nervous system that makes her an exceptional
engineer also keeps her imprisoned—scanning constantly for threat, unable to rest, unable to fully
inhabit the safe life she has built.
