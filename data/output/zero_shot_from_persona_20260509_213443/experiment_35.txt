# Margaret's Story

Margaret sits in the leather chair across from the therapist, her posture careful and deliberate. At
eighty years old, she carries herself with the practiced composure of someone who has spent a
lifetime listening to others' pain. Her silver hair is neatly arranged, her cardigan buttoned
precisely. Yet something in the way she holds her hands—fingers interlaced tightly in her
lap—suggests an internal tension that her outward presentation cannot quite contain.

She begins by explaining that she retired from social work five years ago, after forty-three years
of helping vulnerable populations navigate crises and trauma. She had always proured herself on her
ability to remain steady, to be the calm presence others needed. But lately, she says, she finds
herself unable to maintain that steadiness in her own life.

The trauma occurred when Margaret was in her mid-sixties. She had met Robert at a community center
where she volunteered. He was charming, attentive in ways her previous relationships had not been.
After two years of dating, they moved in together. Margaret believed she had finally found
companionship in her later years.

The first incident happened on a Tuesday evening. Robert had been drinking. Margaret mentioned,
casually, that she'd spent the afternoon with her sister. His response was immediate and violent. He
grabbed her arm, twisted it, called her names. She fell against the kitchen counter. When she tried
to leave the room, he blocked the doorway. The physical assault lasted perhaps ten minutes, though
Margaret says time became strange that night. What strikes her most now is not the pain, but her own
shock—that this could happen to her, someone who had spent decades recognizing patterns of abuse in
others, someone who had counseled countless women in similar situations.

There were other incidents over the following eighteen months. Each time, Margaret told herself it
was an anomaly, a moment of stress he was managing poorly. Each time, she drew on her professional
knowledge to rationalize, to excuse, to hope that understanding his childhood trauma might help him
change. She was, she realizes now, the worst kind of client—one who uses her expertise as a shield
against her own reality. When she finally left, it was only because her sister found her with a
black eye and refused to leave her alone until she agreed to go to a shelter.

Now, fifteen years later, Margaret experiences her body as unreliable. Certain sounds—a man's raised
voice in a grocery store, the crack of a door slamming—send her heart racing. She finds herself
hypervigilant in ways that exhaust her. She's stopped going to the community center where she once
volunteered. She's withdrawn from most of her friendships, telling people she's simply tired, which
is partially true. The fatigue is profound.

Sleep eludes her most nights. When she does sleep, she dreams of being trapped, of her limbs not
responding when she tries to escape. She wakes gasping, her sheets damp with sweat, disoriented
about what year it is.

What troubles her most is the shame. She spent her career teaching other women to recognize red
flags, to value themselves, to leave dangerous situations. Yet she stayed. She minimized. She made
excuses. She tells the therapist that she understands, intellectually, that abuse doesn't
discriminate based on education or professional expertise. But understanding and believing are
different things. Some part of her still whispers that she should have known better.

Margaret is learning, slowly, that the person she was before—the confident social worker who had all
the answers—cannot simply be reassembled. Something fundamental shifted when Robert's hands became
weapons. She is trying, now, to discover who she might become in this aftermath.
