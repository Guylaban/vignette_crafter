# Marcus Chen: A Life Interrupted

Marcus sits in the consultation room with his hands folded carefully in his lap, a habit he's
developed over the past eighteen months. He's a lean man with salt-and-pepper hair and the kind of
posture that suggests someone accustomed to sitting at a desk for long hours. He works as a senior
software engineer at a mid-sized tech company, the kind of job that once brought him genuine
satisfaction—solving complex problems, mentoring junior developers, the quiet pleasure of elegant
code. His wife, Diane, mentions during a joint session that he was always the calm one, the problem-
solver, the person everyone relied on.

That was before Afghanistan.

Marcus had served in the military during his twenties but left in 2005, built a civilian career,
married Diane, bought a house in the suburbs. He'd made peace with that chapter of his life, or so
he believed. When his reserve unit was activated in 2022, he felt a duty to go. He was older now,
more experienced, and the work seemed straightforward enough—advising on security protocols at a
forward operating base. He was supposed to be home in fourteen months.

The convoy was hit on his forty-fifth day in country. An IED detonated near the lead vehicle. Marcus
was in the third truck. He remembers the sound—not the explosion itself, but the terrible silence
that followed it, the way his ears rang with a high-pitched whine. He remembers the smell of burning
rubber and something else, something organic that he's never quite been able to name. Two soldiers
died in the lead vehicle. Marcus walked away with a fractured collarbone and a concussion. The
physical injuries healed within weeks.

The other injuries have proven far more stubborn.

He returned home in the spring of 2023, and for the first two months, Diane says he seemed almost
normal. Then the nightmares began. Most nights, he's back in that truck. Sometimes he's the one who
doesn't make it out. He wakes at three or four in the morning, his heart hammering, his shirt soaked
through with sweat. Diane learned not to touch him when he wakes—his body responds with a violent
startle, his fists clenching reflexively. She moved to the guest bedroom six months ago.

At work, Marcus struggles in ways that frustrate him deeply. He's always been someone who could
compartmentalize, who could focus intensely on a problem for hours. Now, in meetings, he finds
himself scanning exits. When a colleague raises their voice in frustration over a coding issue,
Marcus's entire nervous system floods with adrenaline. Last month, he left a meeting abruptly when
someone's chair scraped loudly against the floor. He told his manager he had a migraine.

He's stopped going to the gym, stopped the occasional beers with friends. Loud noises—fireworks, car
backfires, even the garbage disposal—send him into a state of hypervigilance that can last hours. He
avoids driving on highways where he can't see far enough ahead. He's begun researching remote work
options, wondering if he could engineer his job to eliminate the commute, the office, the
unpredictability of other people.

Diane tries to be patient, but there's a distance between them now that neither seems to know how to
bridge. She makes dinner; he eats mechanically, tasting nothing. She suggests a vacation; he
declines, the thought of being in an unfamiliar place, far from home, triggering a quiet panic he
can't fully articulate.

Marcus knows something is wrong. He knows it's affecting his marriage, his career, his ability to
simply exist in the world the way he used to. He's here because Diane finally said, with tears in
her eyes, "I need my husband back." He's not sure if that's still possible.
