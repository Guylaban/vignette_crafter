# Margaret

Margaret is a 73-year-old painter who still maintains a studio in her home, though she hasn't picked
up a brush in nearly two years. She sits in her kitchen on a Tuesday morning, hands wrapped around a
cooling cup of coffee, and talks about the collapse—the Champlain Towers South collapse in June of
2021. She wasn't there when it happened. She was home in her condo three miles away, but her
daughter Kara lived on the ninth floor with her two children.

Margaret's voice remains steady as she describes that night. She'd called Kara around 1:15 a.m.
because she couldn't sleep, something she'd been struggling with for weeks even before the disaster.
Kara had answered, groggy, and they'd talked for maybe three minutes. Margaret remembers saying, "I
love you, honey. Get some rest." Kara had laughed—that particular laugh Margaret can still hear
perfectly—and said, "You too, Mom." At 1:24 a.m., the building came down. Kara, her son-in-law, and
Margaret's two grandchildren were killed instantly.

In the months that followed, Margaret existed in a fog. She attended the funerals, made
arrangements, spoke to lawyers and insurance companies. She moved through these tasks as though
watching herself from above. Her other daughter, Jennifer, tried to get her to eat, to leave the
house, to talk to someone. Margaret couldn't explain what was happening inside her—the way her body
seemed to move independently of her mind, the hours she'd lose sitting in her studio staring at
blank canvases.

Now, two years later, Margaret describes the particular shape of her suffering. She wakes most
mornings at 3 a.m., her heart racing, sometimes gasping as if she's been drowning. She lies in the
dark and waits for dawn, unable to return to sleep. The apartment feels unsafe, despite the
structural engineers' reports and the city inspections. She's convinced something is wrong with her
own building, though she cannot articulate what. She has the superintendent check the foundation
twice a month.

The paintings are the most telling part. Margaret explains that she simply cannot paint anymore. For
fifty-two years, painting was her language—her way of understanding the world. She'd painted
abstracts mostly, large canvases that required her to move through space, to think in color and
form. But now when she enters her studio, her hands shake. She feels dizzy, as though the room
itself might collapse. She's tried several times, standing before a blank canvas with a brush, but
the paralysis is complete. Once, she managed to apply three brushstrokes before vomiting.

Margaret avoids news reports about building safety, structural failures, or anything related to
condominium living. She doesn't drive past tall buildings. She's stopped visiting Jennifer's house
because it's a mid-rise apartment, and the elevator ride makes her feel as though she's suffocating.
She's had to decline invitations to art shows and gallery openings—places that used to bring her
joy. Instead, she spends her days reading, watching television, and sitting in her kitchen.

She describes a persistent sense of unreality, as though she's living in someone else's life. She
startles easily—a door slamming, a truck rumbling past, her phone ringing unexpectedly. When these
moments happen, her body floods with the conviction that something catastrophic is about to happen.
She finds herself thinking about Kara's last words, replaying that final phone call, wondering if
there was something she should have said differently, some way she could have prevented what came
next, though logically she knows this is impossible.

Margaret is here because Jennifer insisted, and because she's exhausted by the weight of simply
existing.
