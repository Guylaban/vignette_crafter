# Marcus

Marcus sits in the worn leather chair across from the therapist's desk, his weathered hands folded
carefully in his lap. At sixty-five, he has the bearing of someone accustomed to maintaining
composure in difficult situations—a habit formed long before he became a special education teacher.

He was nineteen when he enlisted in the Army, driven by a combination of patriotism and the need to
escape the limited opportunities available to him in his small New Mexico town. The military felt
like a pathway forward, a way to prove himself. He served honorably for eight years, and for most of
that time, he felt capable and valued. Then came the patrol in Central America in 1982.

Marcus doesn't volunteer details about that day unprompted. When he does speak of it, his voice
becomes measured and distant, as though he's narrating something that happened to someone else. The
ambush was sudden. His unit was caught in crossfire. He watched two men he'd trained alongside die
before the medevac arrived. He himself was wounded—shrapnel in his left leg—but the physical wound
healed cleanly. The other injuries proved more persistent.

For decades, Marcus functioned. He left the military, attended community college, and eventually
found his way into special education. He discovered he had a gift for working with children who
struggled to fit into conventional classrooms. There was something about their vulnerability, their
need for patience and structure, that resonated with him. He built a career, married, had two
daughters, and to most observers, seemed to have moved forward successfully.

But the past decade has been different. His ex-wife cited his emotional distance and
unpredictability as reasons for the divorce. She was right, though Marcus struggled to articulate
why. Around age fifty-five, the symptoms intensified in ways he couldn't ignore. Loud noises in the
hallways at school—slamming lockers, a dropped tray in the cafeteria—send his heart racing. His body
reacts before his mind catches up, and he finds himself gripping the edge of a desk, breathing
carefully until the panic subsides.

Sleep has become unreliable. Most nights, Marcus lies awake until two or three in the morning, his
mind circling back to that day in ways he can't control. When he does sleep, he dreams of the
ambush, though the details shift and morph in the way of nightmares. Sometimes he's unable to warn
his friends. Sometimes he's the one who doesn't make it out. He wakes with his sheets soaked
through, his heart pounding, and the disorientation of not immediately knowing where he is.

His relationships with colleagues have grown strained. He's become hypervigilant, noticing exits in
every room, assessing threats that don't exist. A colleague's raised voice during a staff meeting
left him shaken for hours. He's aware that he's withdrawn from the social life of the school,
declining invitations to happy hours and departmental celebrations. The effort of managing his
internal experience in public has become exhausting.

The classroom remains his sanctuary in some ways. The predictability helps. His students—many of
them traumatized themselves, struggling with their own invisible wounds—seem to understand something
in his quietness, his refusal to judge. But even there, the weight of unprocessed grief and fear
increasingly presses down on him. He's considering retirement, though the prospect of losing the
structure and purpose the job provides frightens him nearly as much as the symptoms themselves.

Marcus has begun to recognize that the war never quite left him.
