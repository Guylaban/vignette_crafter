# Sarah

Sarah sits in the waiting room with her hands folded tightly in her lap, her chaplain's collar
visible beneath her cardigan. At forty-four, she carries herself with the composed bearing of
someone trained to be present for others' crises—yet something in her posture suggests she's been
bracing against an invisible force for a long time.

Three years ago, Sarah was serving as a hospital chaplain in a busy urban medical center. She'd been
working there for eight years, finding deep meaning in her role moving between patient rooms,
offering spiritual comfort to the dying and their families. She was good at it—colleagues described
her as steady, compassionate, unflinching in the face of suffering. On a Tuesday afternoon in March,
a man entered the hospital carrying a rifle. He opened fire in the emergency department, killing six
people and wounding twelve others before police arrived. Sarah was in a patient's room on that
floor. She heard the gunshots, the screaming, the chaos that followed. She hid in a supply closet
with two nurses for forty-seven minutes, uncertain whether the shooter was still in the building.

Since that day, Sarah hasn't returned to the hospital. She took a medical leave that became a
resignation. She now works remotely as a chaplain for a hospice organization, which feels safer
somehow—she can control her environment, work from home most days. But the safety feels fragile,
contingent on her ability to avoid crowds, to avoid the places where she used to move so freely.

The nightmares started immediately. She dreams she's still in the supply closet, that the door
opens, that the gunshots resume. She wakes gasping, her heart racing, sometimes unsure for several
seconds where she is. She's learned to sleep with her bedroom door locked and her phone on the
nightstand. Sleep itself has become a negotiation—she often lies awake until exhaustion overwhelms
her hypervigilance.

When she's awake, the world feels unpredictable in new ways. She startles at loud noises—a car
backfiring, a door slamming, someone's raised voice in a neighboring apartment. Her body reacts
before her mind catches up; she feels the adrenaline spike, the tightness in her chest. She's become
acutely aware of exits in every room she enters. When she does venture out—grocery shopping,
attending her sister's birthday dinner—she positions herself near doorways, mentally mapping escape
routes. She notices people's hands, the bulges in jackets, the way strangers move through space.

The isolation has deepened over time. She declined invitations to her church's community events. She
stopped attending her weekly book club. Her friends have largely stopped calling; she suspects
they've interpreted her repeated cancellations as rejection rather than fear. She tells herself
she's protecting them from her unpredictability, from the heaviness she carries. The irony isn't
lost on her—a chaplain trained to sit with suffering, now unable to sit with others at all.

What troubles Sarah most is a persistent sense of betrayal. The hospital was supposed to be a place
of healing. She was supposed to be the one offering comfort, the steady presence. Instead, she'd
been terrified and helpless, hiding in the dark. She questions whether she can trust her instincts
anymore, whether the safety she thought she'd cultivated was ever real. She worries that she's
become someone she doesn't recognize—someone afraid, someone isolated, someone who can no longer do
the work that gave her life purpose. She sits in the waiting room wondering if she'll ever feel at
home in the world again.
