# The Weight of Silence

Marcus sits in the studio he built forty years ago, hands resting on the clay wheel that hasn't
turned in three months. The morning light filters through the north-facing windows the way he always
intended, illuminating dust particles that dance above shelves lined with finished pieces—bowls,
vessels, and vases that represent a lifetime of practice. He runs his thumb across a crack in the
wheel's rim, a flaw he would have corrected immediately in his previous life. Now he leaves it.

His wife, Ellen, found him in this same studio on a Tuesday afternoon last September. She'd been
upstairs when their son David called from the emergency room. Their daughter-in-law's voice had been
strange on the phone—tight, wrong. David had collapsed at work without warning. Massive stroke. He
was forty-three years old.

Marcus remembers the drive to the hospital as a series of disconnected moments: Ellen's hand
gripping his thigh, the fluorescent hallway lights, the particular smell of antiseptic that will now
forever mean loss. David remained unconscious for four days before his brain activity ceased. Marcus
never got to say goodbye in any way that mattered.

Since then, Marcus has developed an elaborate system of avoidance. He cannot walk past the pottery
supply store on Main Street—he takes the long route through residential neighborhoods, even in rain.
The wheel itself has become impossible. He's tried twice. Both times, the moment the clay began to
spin, his breathing became shallow and fast, his hands started shaking, and he felt certain
something catastrophic was about to happen. The third time, he didn't try.

At night, Marcus lies awake beside Ellen, who has learned not to touch him unexpectedly. He startles
at the slightest sound—the refrigerator cycling, a car passing outside, Ellen's breathing changing
rhythm. His mind insists that something is wrong, that danger is imminent, even though the rational
part of him knows this makes no sense. He's taken to sleeping in the guest room, telling Ellen it's
because of his snoring, though they both know that's not true.

What troubles him most is the intrusive quality of his thoughts. Random moments trigger vivid
recollections of David as a boy, watching his father work clay, wanting to try the wheel himself.
Marcus can see David's small hands, feel the exact pressure of his son's body leaning against him.
These memories arrive without warning—while eating breakfast, while driving, while sitting in the
garden—and they're accompanied by a crushing sense of injustice and a rage he doesn't recognize in
himself. Last week, he threw a coffee mug against the kitchen wall. Ellen had simply cleaned it up
without comment.

He's stopped attending his pottery guild meetings. The thought of seeing his friends, of them asking
how he's doing, makes his stomach clench. He's stopped accepting commissions. He's stopped taking
orders for the holiday pieces that once sustained his business. Instead, he spends hours in the
studio doing nothing, sitting on his stool, watching dust move through light, waiting for some shift
that doesn't come.

Ellen has suggested he talk to someone. She's left business cards on the counter—a therapist who
specializes in grief. Marcus hasn't called. Part of him believes he doesn't deserve to feel better,
that moving forward somehow betrays David. Another part simply cannot imagine explaining any of this
to a stranger, cannot imagine the vulnerability required to speak these things aloud.

He picks up a piece of clay, holds it in his palm, then sets it down again.
