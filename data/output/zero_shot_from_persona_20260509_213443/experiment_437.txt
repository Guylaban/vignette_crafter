# Robert

Robert has been delivering packages across the city for forty-two years. He knows the neighborhoods
better than most postal workers, remembers the names of regular customers, and takes pride in his
reliability—the kind of man who has never called in sick, who still drives his route five days a
week even though he's well past traditional retirement age. His wife Margaret says he'd go stir-
crazy at home, and Robert agrees. The routine, the independence, the sense of purpose—these things
matter to him.

Three months ago, on a Tuesday afternoon in October, that life fractured.

Robert was making his usual stop at an apartment building in a neighborhood he's serviced for
decades. The lobby was empty, as it often is mid-afternoon. He didn't notice the man waiting until
it was too late. The assault was brief but brutal—a robbery, the police later determined, though
Robert had nothing of real value. The man wanted his wallet and watch. What Robert remembers most
vividly isn't the physical pain but the shock of powerlessness, the way his sixty-year-old body
suddenly felt fragile in a way he'd never experienced before.

Since that day, Robert hasn't returned to work. Margaret found him sitting in the driveway the next
morning, keys in his lap, unable to make himself go inside the building. He's tried several times.
He gets as far as the lobby door and his chest tightens. His hands shake. Sometimes he feels the
phantom grip of hands from behind, though logically he knows no one is there. He's started avoiding
the neighborhood entirely, taking detours that add forty minutes to any drive. Last week, he
couldn't bring himself to go to the grocery store because it's in that same area of the city.

At night, Robert lies awake replaying the moments before the assault—wondering what he missed, what
signs he should have seen. He wakes at three in the morning, drenched in sweat, his heart racing
from dreams he can't quite remember but that leave him shaken. Margaret has stopped trying to
comfort him; she says it makes him tense up, and she's right. He feels her concern as pressure,
another thing he's failing at.

The physical injuries healed weeks ago, but Robert feels fundamentally changed. He's always been the
capable one, the man who could handle whatever came his way. Now he startles at unexpected sounds.
When someone approaches him from behind—at the mailbox, in a store—panic floods through him before
reason catches up. He's stopped going to his Tuesday morning coffee group because the parking lot
makes him anxious. He's withdrawn from the volunteer work he and Margaret did at the community
center.

What troubles Robert most is the shame. He was assaulted, yes, but he's alive and relatively
unharmed. He tells himself he should be over this by now, that he's being weak. Yet each time he
considers going back to work, his body rebels with a certainty he can't override. The identity he
built over seventy-seven years—reliable Robert, steady Robert—feels like it's slipping away, and he
doesn't know who he is without it.

Margaret watches her husband withdraw further each week, and Robert watches himself disappear too,
unable to bridge the gap between who he was and whoever he's becoming.
