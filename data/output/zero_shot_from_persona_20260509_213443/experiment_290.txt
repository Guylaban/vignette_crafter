# Margaret's Careful World

Margaret sits in the waiting room with her hands folded in her lap, her posture impeccable. She's a
woman accustomed to precision—in her work examining crime scenes, in how she arranges her home, in
the way she speaks. But there's a brittleness to her composure, as though she's holding herself
together by force of will.

Three years ago, her younger brother James died suddenly of a ruptured aneurysm. He was 39, fit,
with two young children. Margaret had spoken to him on the phone that morning. He'd called about
their mother's upcoming birthday party. Nothing seemed wrong. By evening, he was gone.

Margaret remembers the moment the hospital called. She remembers her hands shaking so badly she
couldn't hold the phone. She remembers thinking—with the analytical mind that usually serves her
well in the morgue—that this couldn't be real. People didn't just stop existing. There were
explanations. There were causes. There had to be something that could have been prevented, detected,
known.

She threw herself into understanding what happened. She requested the autopsy report. She read the
neuropathology findings multiple times, as if repetition might change the outcome or reveal
something the medical examiners had missed. Her colleagues at work noticed she was spending longer
hours examining cases, cross-referencing, verifying. She couldn't let anything slip through. Not
anymore. Not when something so catastrophic had gone undetected in her own family.

Now, Margaret finds herself unable to relax. She checks on her husband repeatedly throughout the
day, texting him at work to make sure he's okay. When he doesn't respond immediately, her chest
tightens and her mind races through worst-case scenarios. She's become hypervigilant about
health—she reads articles about sudden cardiac death, aneurysms, strokes, memorizing symptoms she
might have missed in James. She's convinced something is wrong with her own body. Some mornings she
wakes with her heart pounding, convinced she's dying, only to realize nothing has actually happened.

Sleep is difficult. She lies awake most nights, her mind cycling through the same thoughts: What if
she'd called James more often? What if she'd insisted he get a physical? What if she'd recognized
some subtle sign she'd missed? The guilt is relentless, even though the rational part of her—the
forensic analyst—knows that no one could have predicted his death.

At work, she's become even more meticulous, though her supervisor has gently mentioned concerns.
Margaret notices she's withdrawn from her colleagues. She skips the usual lunch conversations. She
can't engage in small talk about weekend plans or family gatherings. When someone mentions their
brother or a health scare, she feels her throat constrict. Last week, a junior analyst mentioned her
father had chest pain, and Margaret found herself in the bathroom unable to breathe.

She avoids the places she and James used to go. She hasn't visited their mother in months, though
they live only forty minutes apart. She tells herself it's because of work, but the truth is more
complicated. Being in her mother's house, seeing the empty chair at the dinner table, seeing how her
mother's hands shake now—it's unbearable.

Margaret's husband has suggested therapy several times. She's resisted, believing she should be able
to manage this herself. She's trained to observe suffering in others, to document it professionally,
to remain objective. But she's beginning to understand that her own suffering isn't something she
can analyze away. The loss sits in her chest, heavy and immovable, and all the precision in the
world cannot undo what happened on that ordinary morning three years ago.
