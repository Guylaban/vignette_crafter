# Marcus

Marcus sits in the waiting room with his laptop open, though he hasn't typed anything in the past
twenty minutes. At forty, he's built a successful career as a software engineer at a mid-sized tech
firm, the kind of person colleagues describe as steady and reliable. His wife, Layla, had to
practically insist he come to this appointment. He doesn't think talking will help, but she's tired
of watching him deteriorate.

Three years ago, Marcus and his family were visiting his parents in Lebanon during what should have
been a peaceful summer. The earthquake hit at 4:17 in the morning. He remembers the exact time
because he'd checked his phone, confused about the rumbling, thinking it was thunder. Then the
building began to move in ways buildings shouldn't move. He grabbed Layla and their two young
children, pulling them toward the doorframe as plaster cracked and fell around them. They survived.
His parents' apartment was damaged but structurally sound. Many others weren't as fortunate.

Since that morning, Marcus has been unable to sleep through the night. He lies in bed, hyperaware of
every sound—the settling of the house, wind against the windows, his daughter shifting in her sleep
down the hall. Around 4 a.m., he invariably wakes in a panic, his heart racing as if the ground is
moving again. Layla has learned not to touch him when he wakes this way; his startle response is
severe. He often finds himself on the couch by dawn, scrolling through earthquake monitoring
websites or reading about building codes, as if knowledge might somehow prevent the unpredictable.

At work, Marcus has noticed a troubling shift in his concentration. He's a detail-oriented
engineer—his code has always been meticulous, his problem-solving methodical. Now he finds himself
rereading the same lines of code three, four times without comprehension. During team meetings, he
struggles to focus on what's being discussed, his mind drifting to worst-case scenarios. Last month,
a colleague joked about the building swaying slightly in high wind. Marcus left the meeting without
explanation and spent two hours in the bathroom stall, breathing heavily, convinced the structure
was unstable.

He's become hypervigilant about his family's safety in ways that frustrate and worry Layla. He
insists on knowing exactly where the children are at all times. He's researched earthquake-safe
rooms in their home and rearranged furniture obsessively. When his daughter wanted to attend a
sleepover at a friend's house, Marcus found himself unable to give permission, imagining scenarios
he couldn't control. The argument that followed left him feeling ashamed and trapped.

Avoidance has become his default strategy. He refuses to watch news coverage of natural disasters,
yet finds himself unable to stop searching for it online. He's declined invitations to visit his
parents again, though they're aging and his mother has asked multiple times. The guilt compounds the
anxiety. He tells himself he's protecting his family, but he knows something deeper is happening—a
fear so pervasive it's begun to suffocate his life.

Layla mentioned that he seems angry more often now, irritable over small things. He snapped at his
son yesterday for leaving a toy on the stairs. He knows it wasn't about the toy. Everything feels
fragile. Everything feels dangerous. And despite three years passing, despite being safe in his
suburban home thousands of miles away, Marcus cannot convince his body that the shaking has stopped.
