# Robert's Story

Robert is a fifty-five-year-old counselor who has spent the last twenty-eight years helping others
navigate their most difficult moments. He works in a community mental health center, specializing in
substance abuse counseling, and his colleagues describe him as steady, compassionate, and deeply
committed to his clients. He lives alone in a modest apartment near the clinic, and his life has
always revolved around his work—it is both his identity and his anchor.

Three years ago, Robert was attending a professional conference in a downtown hotel. He'd taken a
day off from the clinic to participate in workshops on trauma-informed care, a topic he found
increasingly important in his practice. He was in a workshop on the third floor when the explosion
occurred. A gas leak in the building's basement had gone undetected for months. The blast was sudden
and violent, collapsing portions of the structure and trapping people in the rubble for hours.
Robert was thrown against a wall, his leg trapped beneath fallen drywall and metal framing. He
remembers the darkness, the dust choking his lungs, and the sounds of people screaming around him.
He was rescued after four hours. Physically, he recovered—a fractured tibia, some deep lacerations,
and a concussion. But something else fractured that day, something he couldn't quite name at first.

When Robert returned to work six weeks later, he felt different. His clients noticed it, though they
didn't always articulate it. He would sit across from them in sessions, and his mind would suddenly
drift to the weight of the debris, the smell of concrete dust, the moment before the blast when he'd
been thinking about whether to grab coffee. He'd catch himself mid-sentence, losing his thread, and
would have to ask clients to repeat themselves. This embarrassed him deeply.

The nightmares started within days of returning to work. Robert would wake at two or three in the
morning, his heart pounding, convinced the building was collapsing around him again. He stopped
sleeping well, and the exhaustion compounded everything else. He began avoiding the downtown area
entirely, taking long detours to get anywhere, which added forty minutes to his commute. He told
himself it was just practical, but he knew the truth—driving past that area made his chest tight and
his hands shake on the steering wheel.

What troubled Robert most was his hypervigilance in the clinic. He found himself constantly scanning
the ceiling for cracks, noticing structural details he'd never paid attention to before. During
sessions, he would sometimes interrupt clients to ask if they'd heard that sound, or if they felt
the building shift. His clients were confused. Some of them, particularly those struggling with
anxiety, seemed to absorb his worry, and he could see it reflected back in their own agitation.

Robert began avoiding social situations. He'd always been a quiet man, but now he actively declined
invitations from colleagues. He stopped attending the weekly team lunches. When his sister called to
check in, he found himself irritable and short with her. He couldn't explain why. He didn't want to
talk about the explosion—it felt like admitting something was wrong, that he wasn't the stable,
capable person he'd always been.

What Robert struggles with most is the sense that he's lost something essential about himself. He's
a counselor, someone trained to sit with suffering and help others process trauma. Yet he finds
himself unable to do the very thing he's spent his career teaching others to do. The irony is not
lost on him. He sits in his apartment on weekend evenings, looking at his reflection in the dark
television screen, wondering who he's become and whether he'll ever feel like himself again.
