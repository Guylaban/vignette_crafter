# Marcus

Marcus is a nineteen-year-old counselor at a youth mental health nonprofit who carries himself with
a quiet maturity that seems unusual for his age. He speaks thoughtfully during sessions, choosing
his words with care, and there's a gentleness to his manner that suggests he's spent considerable
time thinking about how to help others feel heard. Yet beneath this composed exterior, he's caught
in a cycle of hypervigilance and avoidance that has intensified over the past eight months.

At fourteen, Marcus was diagnosed with acute leukemia. The diagnosis arrived suddenly—a routine
blood test during a school physical revealed something catastrophically wrong. He remembers the
oncologist's face, the way the doctor seemed to choose each word carefully, as if language itself
might shatter under the weight of what needed to be said. For the next three years, Marcus endured
chemotherapy, spinal taps, bone marrow biopsies, and hospitalizations. He lost his hair, his
strength, his sense of normalcy. He watched other patients die. He watched their families collapse.
By sixteen, he was in remission, and by seventeen, he was declared cancer-free.

But freedom, Marcus has learned, is not the absence of the disease. It's the constant, exhausting
vigilance against its return.

Now, when he wakes in the morning, his first conscious thought is often a body scan—checking for
lumps, for swelling, for any sign of wrongness. He finds himself researching leukemia statistics
late at night, reading about relapse rates and five-year survival curves until his chest tightens
and he has to close the laptop. His girlfriend has noticed that he sometimes goes rigid when she
touches his neck or his armpits, those places where lymph nodes live. He can't quite explain it to
her, the way a casual touch in those areas sends a spike of panic through him.

At work, Marcus is remarkably skilled with the adolescents he counsels. He listens without judgment,
validates their struggles, and seems to understand their pain in a way that transcends typical
training. His supervisor has commented on his unusual maturity and empathy. But Marcus knows the
truth: he counsels others partly because he cannot counsel himself. When a teenage girl describes
her anxiety about her health, Marcus feels her terror as his own. When a boy talks about the
unfairness of suffering, Marcus's throat closes. He goes home after these sessions feeling hollowed
out, and he lies awake replaying conversations, wondering if he said the right thing, wondering if
his presence somehow transmitted his own unresolved trauma.

He's begun avoiding medical appointments. A routine dental cleaning becomes an ordeal he postpones
for months. When his primary care doctor's office calls for his annual physical, Marcus feels a
nauseating dread. Intellectually, he knows that avoidance strengthens fear, but knowing this doesn't
change the fact that sitting in a waiting room surrounded by sick people activates a primal sense of
danger. He's also started withdrawing from his girlfriend, not because he doesn't love her, but
because he fears burdening her with the weight of his internal experience. She's noticed his
distance, and their conversations about it have become tense.

Marcus is caught between two identities: the young man who survived something devastating and wants
to help others do the same, and the frightened adolescent still trapped in the memory of those three
years. He functions well enough on the surface, but underneath, he's exhausted from the constant
work of managing his body's betrayal and his mind's refusal to accept that he's actually safe.
