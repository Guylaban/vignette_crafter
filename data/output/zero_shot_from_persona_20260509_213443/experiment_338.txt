# Marcus

Marcus is a 41-year-old dancer and choreographer who spent three months as a relief worker in a
conflict zone in Southeast Asia eight years ago. He sits in the therapy office with careful posture,
the kind of alignment that comes from decades of training his body to move with intention. Yet
there's a rigidity to it now, a holding rather than a grounded ease.

He describes his decision to volunteer with a humanitarian organization as natural at the time. He
had achieved success in his dance career—touring internationally, teaching at prestigious
academies—and felt called to use his skills differently. He taught dance and movement to children in
a displaced persons camp, believing the work was meaningful, even redemptive. His wife, Sophia,
supported the decision, though she worried about his safety.

Three weeks into his placement, the camp was attacked. Marcus was teaching a group of children in a
tent when the first explosions sounded. He remembers the specific quality of the light—dust
particles suddenly visible in the afternoon sun streaming through the tent fabric. He remembers the
children's faces, the way their bodies went still before fear registered. He helped evacuate them,
moved them to a concrete shelter, but two children from his class were killed in the attack. He knew
their names, their favorite movements, the way one girl always arrived early to help him set up.

In the days that followed, Marcus continued working. He told himself this was necessary, that
leaving would be abandonment. But something in him had fractured. He experienced the world
differently now—hyperalert to sounds, to exits, to potential threats. He finished his contract and
returned home.

The years since have been a slow unraveling. Marcus still dances, but it feels different. Where
movement once felt like dialogue between his body and music, it now feels like performance,
something he watches himself doing from outside his body. He struggles to be present in choreography
sessions. His mind goes to the camp unpredictably—during rehearsals, lying in bed, while teaching
students. When he hears loud noises, his body reacts before his mind catches up: his heart races,
his muscles tense, sometimes his legs feel weak.

His marriage has become strained. Sophia describes him as distant, emotionally unavailable. He
doesn't like to talk about what happened, and he becomes irritable when she tries to ask about his
day or make plans. He's stopped going to social gatherings. He avoids crowds, finding them
overwhelming. The sounds of the dance studio—music, footsteps, voices—sometimes trigger a sense of
dread he can't explain to others. He's considered quitting, but dance is who he is; without it, he
feels he might disappear entirely.

Sleep eludes him most nights. He lies awake cataloging dangers, replaying the attack with
variations—scenarios where he couldn't get the children to safety, where he failed them further.
When he does sleep, he wakes gasping, sheets damp with sweat. Sophia has started sleeping in another
room.

Marcus describes feeling numb much of the time, as though he's moving through life behind glass. Yet
this numbness can shatter without warning into intense guilt and anger. He feels responsible for the
children who died, though logically he knows he couldn't have prevented the attack. He carries an
image of one girl's mother's face when she learned her daughter was gone. He sees it when he closes
his eyes.

He came to therapy because Sophia gave him an ultimatum: get help or she would leave. He doesn't
believe therapy can change what happened, but he's here, trying.
