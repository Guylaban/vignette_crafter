# Arjun's Story

Arjun sits across from me with his laptop closed on his knee, a habit he's developed over the past
eight months. He's a twenty-year-old writer—the kind who'd been publishing short stories in online
literary magazines since high school, already building a modest following. His parents had been
proud, his mother especially. She'd saved every published piece in a folder on her phone.

Last March, his mother collapsed while cooking dinner. Arjun was in his room working on a story
about grief—he remembers the cruel irony of this now, how he'd been writing about loss abstractly
while his mother was dying downstairs. His father's screams brought him running. The ambulance took
forty minutes to arrive. She was pronounced dead at the hospital before Arjun finished filling out
the admission forms.

He hasn't written anything since that day.

The first few weeks felt unreal. Arjun moved through the funeral rituals mechanically, accepting
condolences from relatives he barely knew, their words sliding past him without landing. But around
week three, something shifted. He tried to return to his manuscript—a collection of interconnected
stories he'd been developing for months. He opened the document and stared at the opening line for
three hours without reading a single word. His chest felt tight. His hands started shaking. He
closed the laptop and didn't open it again for two weeks.

Now, whenever he tries to write, panic arrives uninvited. His heart races. He feels nauseated.
Sometimes he gets a sensation of unreality, as if he's watching himself from outside his body,
floating above his desk. He's convinced himself that writing somehow caused this—that his
preoccupation with his fictional story while his mother was dying was a form of neglect, a cosmic
punishment that manifested her death. The logic doesn't hold up in daylight, but at night, it feels
absolutely true.

The avoidance has expanded beyond writing. He's withdrawn from his online writing community,
ignoring messages from editors and fellow writers. He's barely left his room except for classes,
which he attends sporadically. His friends have stopped asking him to hang out. His father seems to
be managing by working longer hours, leaving Arjun alone in a house that feels too quiet.

Sleep has become treacherous. Most nights, he lies awake until three or four in the morning, his
mind cycling through the events of that day in excruciating detail. When he does sleep, he dreams
he's back in the hospital waiting room, searching frantically through hallways for his mother,
knowing he's too late. He wakes gasping, his sheets soaked through.

What troubles him most is the numbness between the panic attacks. He feels hollowed out, incapable
of feeling anything about anything. He scrolls through his phone mindlessly for hours. Food tastes
like nothing. He can't remember the last time he laughed. His therapist from high school had once
told him that writing was his superpower—his way of making sense of the world. Now it feels like
that power has turned against him, like the very thing that made him feel alive is now inextricably
linked to the worst moment of his life.

He wants to write again. He misses it desperately. But the gap between wanting and doing feels
impossible to cross. Every time he approaches his laptop, he's not sitting at his desk anymore—he's
standing in that hospital hallway, and his mother is still dying, and there's nothing he can write
that will change it.
