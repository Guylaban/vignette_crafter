# Marcus

Marcus has spent forty years chasing stories across three continents, building a reputation as a
dogged investigative journalist willing to go where others wouldn't. His wife, Amara, had always
supported his work, understanding that his drive to uncover truth came from something deeper than
ambition. They'd raised two children together, attended university events, built a life that felt
solid despite the frequent absences and the dangers inherent to his profession.

The phone call came on a Tuesday afternoon in March, two years ago. Amara had been visiting her
sister in Lagos for three weeks—a trip Marcus had encouraged, knowing she needed time away from his
work stress. A car accident on the expressway. She was conscious when the ambulance arrived, but the
internal bleeding was extensive. By the time Marcus reached the hospital forty-eight hours later,
she had already undergone two surgeries and was in intensive care.

What followed was a month of sitting in hospital corridors, watching his wife's body respond
unpredictably to treatment, holding her hand during moments of lucidity when she seemed to not quite
recognize him. Marcus had covered wars, interviewed torture survivors, documented atrocities—but
nothing had prepared him for the helplessness of watching the person he loved most slip
incrementally away. Amara survived, but the woman who returned home was changed. The stroke that
followed her recovery left her with partial paralysis on her left side and significant cognitive
difficulties. She struggles with memory now, sometimes forgetting conversations they had that
morning.

Marcus took leave from his newspaper. Then he took early retirement. He tells himself this was the
right decision, but the narrative doesn't feel entirely true. What he doesn't say aloud is that he
cannot write anymore. He sits at his desk—the same desk where he'd composed hundreds of articles—and
the words feel obscene. How could he care about political corruption or corporate malfeasance when
Amara struggles to remember their daughter's name some days?

At night, Marcus lies awake replaying the accident in his mind, though he wasn't there. He
constructs it in detail: the exact moment of impact, the sound of metal and glass, the seconds
before the ambulance arrived. Sometimes he's furious at Amara for going to Lagos, though he knows
this is irrational. More often, he's furious at himself for not being there, as if his presence
could have somehow prevented the unpredictable.

He's withdrawn from colleagues, declined social invitations. When he does leave the house, he's
hypervigilant—tensing at the sound of sirens, gripping the car door during drives, calculating risks
in ways that feel both reasonable and excessive. He's lost fifteen pounds. His sleep is fragmented;
he wakes at 3 a.m. with his heart racing, sometimes unsure whether he's waking from a nightmare or
from the weight of simply remembering.

Amara has made remarkable progress in her rehabilitation. She's returned to some activities, and
there are moments when Marcus glimpses the woman he married—a quick laugh, a familiar gesture. But
he cannot quite rejoin her in those moments. Instead, he watches from a distance, terrified that any
hope he allows himself will be followed by another collapse, another loss. The man who spent decades
bearing witness to others' trauma now finds himself unable to witness his own wife's recovery.
