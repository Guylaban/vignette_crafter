# Marcus

Marcus is a twenty-one-year-old bus driver who joined the military at eighteen, eager to serve and
find direction. He completed his basic training without incident and was deployed to a conflict zone
for eight months with a transport unit. Three months ago, his convoy was ambushed while moving
supplies between bases. An improvised explosive device detonated near his vehicle, killing two
soldiers instantly and injuring several others. Marcus was physically unharmed but was the first to
reach one of the wounded—a friend from his unit—and held him as he died.

He was medically discharged two months later and took a job driving a city bus route, thinking the
structure and civilian normalcy would help. He needed the work and the paycheck, and driving felt
familiar—something he could do. But Marcus is struggling more now than he did in those immediate
weeks after the explosion.

He sits in the waiting room with his shoulders hunched, fingers picking at the hem of his jacket.
When he speaks, his voice is quiet and measured, as though he's rationing his words. He describes
his mornings as a series of small battles. He wakes at four-thirty, before his five-forty-five
shift, because sleeping past that time triggers something—a feeling of dread that tightens his
chest. He lies in bed in the dark, waiting, sometimes for an hour, for the anxiety to become
manageable enough to move.

The bus itself has become a minefield of triggers. Loud noises—a backfiring car, a passenger
slamming a door, construction noise near the route—send electricity through his body. His hands grip
the steering wheel tighter, and he has to consciously remind himself he's in the city, not in a
convoy. Twice in the past month, he's pulled over because his vision blurred and his heart wouldn't
slow down. He told his supervisor he was unwell; he didn't mention that he couldn't catch his breath
or that he was convinced something terrible was about to happen.

He avoids his apartment on days off. Being alone with his thoughts feels dangerous. He drives
aimlessly or sits in his car in parking lots, watching people, but not really seeing them. At night,
he drinks—usually two or three beers, sometimes more—to quiet the noise in his head. He tells
himself it's just to relax, but he knows it's to sleep without the dreams. When the dreams come
anyway, he's back in the vehicle, smelling diesel fuel and dust, feeling the heat of the blast,
seeing his friend's face. He wakes gasping, drenched in sweat, and sometimes doesn't sleep again
that night.

His few friendships have withered. He declined invitations to go out, made excuses, and eventually
stopped answering texts. His mother calls weekly, and he keeps the conversations brief, assuring her
he's fine. He's not fine. He feels disconnected from everything—the passengers on his bus, the city
streets he drives, his own life. There's a numbness underneath the anxiety, as though he's watching
himself from outside his body.

He came to therapy because his mother insisted, and because part of him recognizes that something is
fundamentally wrong. He describes his current life as existing rather than living, going through
motions that feel increasingly hollow. He wants to feel normal again, though he's not sure what that
means anymore or if it's even possible.
