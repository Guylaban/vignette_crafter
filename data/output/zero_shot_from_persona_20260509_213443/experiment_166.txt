# Marcus

Marcus sits in the accounting office of a mid-sized firm, his desk organized with the precision that
has come to define him. At thirty-six, he is competent and reliable—the kind of employee who files
quarterly reports without error and stays late when deadlines approach. His colleagues respect him,
though few know him well. He prefers it this way.

The trauma that shaped Marcus occurred when he was eight years old. His father, a man whose moods
could shift like weather, came home one evening in a rage. Marcus remembers the sound first—his
father's voice, loud and accusatory, directed at his mother. He remembers hiding in the hallway,
watching through a crack in the kitchen doorway as his father shoved his mother against the counter.
The fear he felt then—the helplessness of being small and unable to stop what was happening—has
never fully left him.

His parents divorced two years later, and Marcus learned to make himself small and quiet, believing
that if he caused no trouble, no one would hurt anyone. He became the peacekeeper, the one who
anticipated needs and avoided conflict. This strategy worked well enough through adolescence and
into adulthood, though it came at a cost he didn't fully understand until his own marriage began to
deteriorate.

Now, at thirty-six, Marcus is hypervigilant in ways that have become automatic. When he hears a
raised voice in the office—even casual irritation between colleagues—his body tenses. His heart rate
spikes. He finds himself monitoring the tone of conversations around him, reading danger into
ordinary workplace friction. Last month, when his manager spoke sharply to a junior accountant about
a missed deadline, Marcus felt his palms sweat. He excused himself to the bathroom and sat in a
stall for ten minutes, breathing deliberately, waiting for his nervous system to settle.

His marriage ended three years ago, though the relationship had been deteriorating for much longer.
His ex-wife said he was emotionally distant, that he seemed afraid of her anger even when her
frustration was reasonable and directed at situations, not at him. She was right. Marcus couldn't
distinguish between normal conflict and danger. Every disagreement felt like the precursor to
violence. He would withdraw, become silent, try to manage her emotions before they could escalate.
Eventually, she left.

Sleep has become difficult. Marcus lies awake most nights, his mind cycling through worst-case
scenarios. He worries about his sister, who is married with two children, though she has never
mentioned problems in her relationship. He finds himself calling her more often than feels natural,
listening carefully to the tone of her voice, trying to detect unhappiness or fear. She has started
to seem annoyed by these calls.

At work, Marcus has begun to make small mistakes—uncharacteristic for him. He missed a detail in a
client's tax return last week, something he would normally catch immediately. His concentration
fractures easily, especially when there is tension in the office. He has started taking longer lunch
breaks, sitting alone in his car in the parking lot, sometimes just staring at nothing.

He doesn't think of himself as traumatized. He thinks of himself as careful, as someone who
understands that the world can turn dangerous quickly. But the vigilance is exhausting. The fear
that lurks beneath his competence and control is becoming harder to ignore. He is beginning to
wonder if the way he moves through the world—always braced for impact—is sustainable.
