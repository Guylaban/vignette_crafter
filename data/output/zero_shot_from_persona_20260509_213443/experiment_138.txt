# Maya

Maya sits in the leather chair across from me with her notebook balanced on her knee—a habit from
years of conducting interviews. She's meticulous about details, her pen poised, ready. But today,
three months into our work together, she's learning to sit without documenting everything. Her hands
still fidget.

She was thirty years old when her younger brother Rohan died. It was sudden—a car accident on a
Tuesday morning that no one saw coming. Maya had been in the newsroom when her mother called, her
voice fractured in a way Maya had never heard before. She remembers the exact time: 9:47 a.m. She
remembers the fluorescent lights humming overhead, the click of keyboards around her, the way her
editor's face blurred as she stood up and walked toward the exit.

The funeral was five days later. Maya moved through it like she was reporting on someone else's
tragedy, her mind splitting into the part that grieved and the part that observed. She noticed the
flowers—too many lilies. She noticed her father's hands shaking as he lit incense. She noticed the
way people whispered, as if their voices might disturb Rohan's rest. But she couldn't feel any of
it. Not yet.

Two weeks after the funeral, she returned to work. Her editor offered her time off; she refused. She
had a story to chase about municipal corruption, and the distraction felt necessary, like a
tourniquet on a wound. For six weeks, she threw herself into investigations, late nights at the
office, interviews with officials and whistleblowers. She was doing what she'd always done: making
sense of chaos through narrative and evidence.

Then came the nightmares. They started without warning—vivid, visceral scenes of the accident that
she hadn't witnessed but had imagined too many times. In the dreams, she's in the passenger seat.
She sees the truck approaching. She can't warn him. She wakes gasping, her heart racing so fast she
thinks it might break through her ribs.

The intrusions didn't stay in sleep. During interviews, she'd suddenly see twisted metal instead of
the person across from her. While writing, her mind would drift to Rohan's last moments. She started
avoiding the highway where the accident happened, taking circuitous routes that added forty minutes
to her commute. Then she began avoiding driving altogether, taking taxis, then avoiding taxis
because sitting in the back seat made her anxious. The world kept shrinking.

Her ex-husband had left eighteen months before Rohan died—their marriage had quietly dissolved into
polite distance—and she'd convinced herself she was fine with it, that she was independent and
resilient. After Rohan's death, she couldn't imagine being close to anyone. When colleagues tried to
check on her, she deflected with humor. When her mother called, she kept conversations brief and
factual. She moved through days in a fog, her body present but her mind somewhere else, replaying
that Tuesday morning, imagining different outcomes.

Six months after his death, Maya realized she hadn't written anything substantial. The corruption
story had stalled. She'd missed deadlines. Her editor had gently suggested she take leave. Instead,
she pushed harder, working weekends, caffeinating through the nights. But the words felt hollow. She
couldn't access the part of her that cared about uncovering truth. Everything felt muted, as if she
were experiencing life through thick glass.

Now, sitting with me, she admits she doesn't recognize herself anymore. The journalist who thrived
on piecing together complex narratives can't seem to construct meaning from her own story. She's
here because her mother finally said what Maya has been afraid to acknowledge: that grief has become
something darker and more consuming, and that she can't do this alone.
