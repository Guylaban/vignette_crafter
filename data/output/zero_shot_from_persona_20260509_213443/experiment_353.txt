# Dr. Héctor's Vigil

Dr. Héctor Morales sits in his office at the clinic, reviewing patient charts with the same
meticulous attention he has maintained for forty-two years. His reading glasses slip down his nose
as he works through the stack, but his hands tremble slightly as he turns each page. At sixty-seven,
he has trained himself to notice these small betrayals of his body—the tremor, the way his jaw
tightens when the afternoon light hits his desk at a certain angle, how his chest sometimes feels
too small to hold all the air he needs.

Twenty-three months ago, his daughter María called him at work. She was crying in a way he had never
heard before. His grandson, David—eight years old, the boy who wanted to be a soccer player like his
grandfather once was—had been rushed to the hospital with meningitis. The words felt impossible,
like they were being spoken in a language Héctor no longer understood. He left the clinic
immediately, driving through traffic with a clarity that frightened him, his physician's mind
already calculating mortality rates and complications while his heart refused to accept any of it.

David spent seventeen days in the pediatric intensive care unit. Héctor remembers every detail with
the precision of trauma: the exact shade of blue on the monitors, the particular way the IV pump
beeped at 2:47 a.m., the smell of antiseptic that seemed to embed itself in his clothes no matter
how many times he showered. He sat beside his grandson's bed, a doctor who suddenly understood
nothing. He had spent decades teaching medical students about the clinical presentation of
meningitis, but watching it happen to David—watching the fever spike, the rash appear, the
seizures—shattered something fundamental in how he understood his own knowledge.

David survived. He recovered fully, with no lasting neurological damage. This is what Héctor tells
people when they ask, and he means it. David is healthy. David plays soccer again. And yet.

Now, when Héctor sees a child in his clinic with a fever, something in his chest locks. His
breathing becomes shallow. He finds himself ordering tests he knows are unnecessary, his clinical
judgment clouded by a static he cannot quiet. He has stopped attending the weekly medical rounds he
once led. The thought of sitting in a conference room while colleagues present cases of pediatric
illness makes his skin feel too tight. He calls in sick more often than he would like to admit.

His sleep is fractured. He lies awake at three in the morning, his mind replaying scenarios that
didn't happen—David not waking up, the antibiotics failing, the infection spreading. When he does
sleep, he dreams of hospital corridors that stretch endlessly. He wakes with his heart racing,
certain something is wrong, before remembering that David is fine. This remembering happens every
single time, and it exhausts him.

Héctor has always been a solitary man. His marriage ended thirty years ago, and he has built a life
centered on his work and his family. But now, the thought of being around people—even his
daughter—feels overwhelming. When María calls to invite him to David's soccer games, he makes
excuses. He cannot bear to watch his grandson play without being flooded by the memory of those
seventeen days when David could barely move.

He sits at his desk, the patient charts blurring slightly, and wonders when he stopped being the
doctor and became simply a man afraid of losing everything.
