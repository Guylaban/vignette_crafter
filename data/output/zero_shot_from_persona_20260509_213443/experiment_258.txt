# Rajesh

Rajesh has worked as a housemaid for nearly thirty years. He moves through the homes of wealthy
families with the quiet efficiency of someone who has learned to be invisible—someone who
understands that his presence should be felt only in the gleam of polished floors and the fresh
scent of laundered linens. He takes pride in this work, though he rarely says so aloud. His hands
know the weight of every bucket, the angle that makes a mop most effective, the precise pressure
needed to clean glass without leaving streaks.

Three years ago, while working at the residence of a prominent family, Rajesh was mopping the marble
corridor outside the master bedroom when the youngest son, a boy of twelve, came running down the
hallway. Rajesh had just finished waxing the floor—it was slick and gleaming. He called out a
warning, but the child was moving too quickly. The boy's feet went out from under him. His head
struck the corner of the mahogany table at the bottom of the stairs with a sound Rajesh has never
been able to unhear.

Everything after that moment exists in fragments. The screaming. The blood on the marble. His own
hands shaking as he called for help. The ambulance. The parents' faces—first confused, then angry,
then something worse than anger. The boy spent two weeks in the hospital. He recovered, mostly,
though he walks with a slight limp now and sometimes forgets words mid-sentence.

Rajesh was not prosecuted. The family did not sue. But they also did not ask him to return.

Since then, Rajesh has not been able to work steadily. He takes jobs when he can find them, but
something has shifted in him. His hands, which once moved with unconscious grace, now feel
dangerous. He finds himself replaying that moment constantly—not just remembering it, but living it
again. He will be mopping a floor and suddenly see the boy's face, see the trajectory of the fall,
feel the weight of responsibility crushing down on his chest. His heart races. His palms sweat.
Sometimes he has to sit down, right there in someone's home, and wait for the sensation to pass.

At night, he lies awake in his small room, running through the moment endlessly, searching for what
he could have done differently. If he had finished mopping earlier. If he had stood in the hallway
instead. If he had been faster, stronger, more alert. The guilt is a constant, physical presence—a
tightness in his chest that never fully releases.

He has become hypervigilant in a way that makes him unsuitable for the work he has always known. He
startles at sudden movements. When children are present in a home, he becomes so focused on
monitoring them, so anxious about potential accidents, that he cannot concentrate on his tasks. Last
month, a woman hired him for a day and let him go after two hours because he kept asking about her
children's whereabouts, kept warning about hazards, kept hovering with a tension that made everyone
uncomfortable.

His ex-wife says he has become a different man—withdrawn, irritable, consumed by something she
cannot reach. His few friends have noticed that he declines invitations, that he seems distant even
when present. He drinks more than he used to, though he tells himself it is just in the evenings,
just to sleep.

Rajesh is fifty-two years old and no longer knows who he is without his work. The thing he was most
skilled at, the thing that gave him purpose and identity, has become something he fears. He carries
the weight of that boy's injury like a stone in his chest, and he cannot imagine how to set it down.
