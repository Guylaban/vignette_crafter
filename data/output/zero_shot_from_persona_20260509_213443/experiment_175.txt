# James Patel: A Paramedic's Reckoning

James Patel sits in the waiting room with his hands folded neatly in his lap, a habit formed over
thirty-seven years of paramedicine. At sixty-one, he's still fit, still sharp—or so he tells
himself. His wife Priya insisted he come, said she was worried, though James struggles to articulate
exactly what she should be worried about. He's functioning. He goes to work. He pays the bills.

Four months ago, James responded to a call at an industrial facility. A ruptured tank had released
chlorine gas into the warehouse. He and his partner arrived early, before full evacuation protocols
were in place. James remembers the acrid smell, the way his eyes burned immediately, the chemical
taste coating his throat. He remembers thinking clearly about the work—assessing, triaging, moving
people to fresh air. He was professional. He did his job.

But something shifted that day.

For the first few weeks, James experienced the expected physical symptoms: coughing, chest
tightness, occasional shortness of breath. The occupational health clinic cleared him after six
weeks. "Chemical exposure, mild exposure," they said. "You'll be fine." He returned to work.

The problem is that James isn't fine, though he's become adept at performing fineness. At the
station, he jokes with younger paramedics, completes his shifts without incident. But driving home,
his chest tightens in ways unrelated to any chemical. When the smell of cleaning products hits
him—bleach, ammonia, anything sharp—his body floods with a panic he can't quite explain. His heart
races. His hands sweat. He has to pull over sometimes, convinced he can't breathe, though rationally
he knows the air is fine.

Sleep has become complicated. James lies awake most nights, his mind cycling through scenarios. What
if he'd arrived earlier? What if he hadn't been so focused on the work that he missed something
about his own exposure? What if there's damage he can't see, something developing silently inside
him? He wakes at three in the morning, drenched, from dreams he can't quite remember but that leave
him feeling unsafe in his own bedroom.

Priya notices everything. She notices that he flinches when she uses the spray bottle to clean the
bathroom. She notices that he's stopped going to his weekly cricket match with friends. She notices
that he checks his oxygen saturation on his pulse oximeter multiple times a day—a habit that started
as reasonable caution and has become ritualistic. She notices that he's quieter, more withdrawn,
that he seems to be disappearing into himself even as he sits beside her.

At work, James has started making subtle changes. He volunteers less for certain calls, finds
reasons to let younger paramedics take the lead. He's never done this before. His reputation has
always been steady, reliable, the guy who stays calm when everything is chaos. Now he's aware of a
low-level anxiety that accompanies every shift, a hypervigilance about air quality and safety
protocols that his colleagues don't seem to share. He wonders if they think he's getting soft, if
they've noticed his hesitation.

The worst part is the shame. James has worked in emergency medicine for decades. He's seen real
trauma—people who've lost everything, who've suffered genuine catastrophe. He was exposed to a
chemical for perhaps five minutes. He was cleared medically. Yet he can't seem to move past it. His
body won't cooperate with his mind's assessment that he's fine. This disconnect troubles him more
than any physical symptom ever could.
