# Margaret

Margaret sits in the waiting room of the community mental health center, her hands folded tightly in
her lap. She's a woman who has spent the last twenty-eight years working in municipal
administration—first as a planning officer, then as a senior policy advisor for the city council.
Her colleagues have always known her as someone unflappable, organized, the person who could manage
competing demands without losing her composure. She still dresses the part: tailored blazer,
sensible shoes, hair carefully arranged. But something in her posture suggests the effort this
takes.

Three years ago, Margaret was in the basement archives of the municipal building where she'd worked
for over two decades. The building was being renovated, and she'd gone down to retrieve some
historical planning documents for a presentation. She noticed a chemical smell—sharp and acrid—but
thought nothing of it initially. The contractor had mentioned some work on old pipes. Within
minutes, her eyes began to water intensely. Her throat tightened. She remembers the panic of not
being able to breathe properly, the confusion as her vision blurred, the humiliation of being
carried out by emergency responders while her colleagues watched from the hallway.

The diagnosis came quickly: toxic exposure to industrial solvents that had leaked from deteriorating
storage containers in the basement. Margaret spent two nights in hospital for observation.
Physically, she recovered. The doctors said she was fortunate—her exposure was brief, her symptoms
resolved within days. She returned to work after a week, determined to move forward.

But something shifted that day in the basement. Margaret finds herself unable to enter the building
now without her heart beginning to race uncontrollably. She can feel it starting even in the car
park—a tightening in her chest, a sense of dread that makes her palms sweat. She's arranged to work
from home three days a week, telling her supervisor it's a scheduling preference. The truth is more
complicated. On the days she must go in, she arrives early, when fewer people are around, and she
avoids the basement entirely, taking the longer route through the east corridor even when it makes
her late for meetings.

The smell haunts her. She encounters it everywhere now—in cleaning products, in the parking garage,
sometimes in her own home when her husband uses certain detergents. Each time, her body reacts as
though she's back in that basement. Her heart pounds. She feels dizzy. She's convinced something
terrible is about to happen, though she can't articulate what. She's stopped using most cleaning
supplies in the house, which frustrates her husband. She's also stopped going to the office café,
where the smell of certain industrial cleaners lingers in the bathrooms.

Sleep has become difficult. Margaret lies awake, her mind replaying the moment over and over—the
smell, the panic, the feeling of her lungs closing. When she does sleep, she dreams of being trapped
in confined spaces, unable to escape. She wakes gasping, her nightclothes damp with sweat. Her
husband tries to comfort her, but she finds his presence both necessary and irritating; she can't
explain why.

Her work performance, once exemplary, has suffered. She misses details now. She finds it hard to
concentrate in meetings. She's considered early retirement, though she's not yet fifty-five and
loves her work—or loved it, before the basement. She feels as though a part of her competence has
been stolen, and she doesn't know how to reclaim it.
