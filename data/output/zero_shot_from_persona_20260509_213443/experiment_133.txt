# Professor Samuel's Vigil

Samuel sits in his office during office hours, grading papers with the same meticulous care he has
applied to his work for thirty-four years. His students know him as unfailingly present, generous
with feedback, the kind of professor who remembers their names and asks about their families. What
they don't see is how his hand trembles slightly as he writes comments in the margins, or how he has
to reread the same sentence three times because his mind keeps drifting elsewhere.

Eighteen months ago, his younger brother Kofi collapsed on a street in Lagos during a business trip.
A stroke, massive and unexpected. Samuel received the call at 2 a.m. and spent the next seventy-two
hours in a state of fractured consciousness—booking flights, making arrangements, calling family
members scattered across three continents. He arrived to find Kofi in the ICU, half his face slack,
his right side unresponsive. His brother, the one who had always been the vibrant one, the one who
laughed loudest at family gatherings, was trapped in a body that no longer obeyed him.

The weeks that followed blurred together. Samuel took leave from the university and stayed in Lagos,
sitting beside Kofi's hospital bed, talking to him even when there was no indication that Kofi could
hear. He read him passages from novels they had loved as boys. He held his hand during the difficult
physical therapy sessions where Kofi's eyes would fill with tears of frustration. He watched his
brother's wife try to hold everything together while their mother wept in the corner of the room.

When Samuel returned to teaching six months later, people told him he was brave. He had nodded,
accepted their sympathy, and returned to his routine. But something had fractured inside him that
didn't mend the way people expected.

Now, at night, Samuel lies awake in his quiet apartment, his mind replaying moments he cannot
change. He remembers the particular shade of gray in Kofi's eyes when he first woke in the
hospital—that moment when consciousness returned but agency had not. He remembers the sound of his
mother's voice on the phone last week when she called to say Kofi had had another small stroke.
Samuel had been preparing a lecture on contemporary African literature, and he had simply sat there,
phone pressed to his ear, unable to speak.

He has taken to avoiding social gatherings. His colleagues have stopped inviting him to departmental
dinners. His sister-in-law sends him updates about Kofi's slow, grinding progress—the small
victories of regaining movement in his fingers, the setbacks when infections develop. Samuel reads
these messages with a sensation like drowning. He wants to feel hope. Instead, he feels the weight
of helplessness, that particular gravity that comes from loving someone whose suffering he cannot
fix.

In the classroom, Samuel is still present. He lectures on the resilience of the human spirit in
literature, on how characters endure impossible circumstances. His voice carries authority and
warmth. But when a student raises their hand to ask about a character's motivations, Samuel
sometimes stares at them blankly for a moment too long, his mind elsewhere in a hospital room in
Lagos, watching someone he loves fight a battle against his own body that may never be won.
