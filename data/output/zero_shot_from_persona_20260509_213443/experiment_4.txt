# Marcus

Marcus sits with his shoulders drawn inward, hands folded tightly in his lap. At nineteen, he
carries himself with the careful precision of someone who has learned to take up as little space as
possible. He is newly married—just eight months—and works as an environmental scientist at a
nonprofit focused on water quality assessment. It's work he finds meaningful, though lately even
that has become complicated.

His childhood unfolded in a household where his father's anger was as unpredictable as the weather.
Marcus learned early to read the subtle shifts in tone, the particular way his father's footsteps
sounded on the stairs. When those signs appeared, Marcus would make himself small, move quietly, try
to anticipate what might prevent the next blow. His father's discipline was harsh and
frequent—sometimes triggered by something Marcus had done, often triggered by nothing at all. By the
time Marcus was twelve, the physical violence had become a constant undercurrent of his existence,
something he organized his entire day around avoiding.

The abuse stopped when Marcus was fifteen, when his father left the family. By then, the damage was
already woven through him.

Now, at nineteen, Marcus is trying to build a normal life. He excels at his work—his attention to
detail and ability to anticipate problems serves him well in environmental assessment. But he finds
himself hypervigilant in ways that don't match his current reality. When his wife, Leila, raises her
voice during an argument about household finances, Marcus's body floods with adrenaline. His chest
tightens. His mind goes blank except for a single imperative: escape or defend. He leaves the
apartment and walks for hours, unable to explain to Leila why a normal disagreement feels like a
threat to his survival.

Sleep has become increasingly difficult. Marcus lies awake at night, his mind cycling through worst-
case scenarios. What if he becomes angry like his father? What if he hurts Leila? The fear is so
visceral it wakes him in the night, drenched in sweat. Some nights he sleeps on the couch, telling
himself it's to avoid disturbing her, though the truth is more complicated. He doesn't trust himself
in the dark.

At work, Marcus has begun to withdraw. He used to collaborate easily with his team, but lately he
finds himself unable to concentrate during meetings. When his supervisor speaks to him in a direct
tone about a report deadline, Marcus feels his face flush with shame and something darker—a rage
that surprises him with its intensity. He apologizes immediately, profusely, even when no apology is
necessary. His supervisor has noticed the change, though she hasn't commented on it.

Marcus experiences intrusive memories without warning. He'll be analyzing water samples and suddenly
he's eight years old again, hearing his father's voice, feeling the sting of a belt across his
shoulders. The memory is so vivid it takes him several minutes to reorient himself to the present.
When this happens, he feels simultaneously disconnected from his body and trapped inside it—numb yet
burning.

He hasn't told Leila the full extent of what his childhood was like. He's mentioned his father was
strict, but the details feel too shameful, too exposing. He worries that if she really knew him, if
she saw the damage underneath, she would leave. So he maintains a careful distance, even as he
desperately needs closeness. This contradiction exhausts him.

Marcus came to therapy because Leila gently suggested it. He didn't want to come at first, but he's
beginning to recognize that something inside him is fractured in ways he cannot fix alone.
