# Maria's Story

Maria has worked the morning shift at the same fast-food restaurant for nearly fifteen years. She
knows every regular customer by name, remembers their orders without writing them down, and takes
pride in the efficiency of her station. At fifty-two, she is often the steadying presence during the
breakfast rush, the one newer employees turn to when they're confused. Her coworkers respect her
competence, though lately they've noticed she seems different—more withdrawn, quicker to startle at
sudden noises.

Three years ago, Maria's life fractured during the typhoon season. She and her husband lived in a
modest house in a low-lying neighborhood, a place they'd called home for decades. When the warnings
came, Maria wasn't particularly worried. She'd weathered storms before. But this one was different.
The water came faster than anyone predicted, rising through their streets with terrifying speed.
Within hours, their neighborhood was underwater.

Maria remembers the moment the water breached their front door. She remembers her husband grabbing
her arm, pulling her toward the attic, the way the water was so cold and moved with such force. She
remembers the sound—a roaring that seemed to come from everywhere at once. They made it to safety,
but neighbors didn't. She thinks about Mrs. Chen from two doors down, who'd been too slow to reach
higher ground. The body was recovered three days later.

In the months that followed, Maria returned to work because her family needed the income. But
something had shifted inside her. Loud noises—the hiss of the deep fryer, the crash of metal pans,
even the beeping of the register—send her heart racing. Last month, a delivery truck backfired in
the parking lot, and Maria found herself unable to move for several minutes, her hands shaking so
badly she couldn't hold a coffee cup. She told her supervisor she'd felt dizzy and went home early.

She sleeps poorly. Most nights, she wakes around three in the morning, her sheets soaked with sweat,
her mind still in that rising water. Sometimes she dreams she's back in her house, watching the
water climb the walls, knowing she can't stop it. She's tried to explain this to her husband, but he
seems to want to move forward, to not talk about it anymore. He's rebuilt their house, replanted the
garden. He wants things to return to normal.

But Maria can't seem to find normal. She avoids the news, especially during storm season. She's
become hypervigilant about weather forecasts, checking them obsessively on her phone during her
breaks. When it rains heavily, even now, her chest tightens and she feels the old panic rising.
She's considered taking medication but hasn't followed through. Part of her believes she should just
be stronger, should be over this by now.

At work, she's become more irritable, though she tries to hide it. A customer's raised voice over a
wrong order triggered a disproportionate wave of anxiety last week. She found herself near tears in
the bathroom, unable to explain why such a small frustration had upset her so deeply. Her coworkers
have started asking if everything is okay at home. Maria smiles and says yes, everything is fine.
But she's aware that she's not fine, that something inside her remains fractured, still caught in
that moment when the water was rising and the world felt utterly beyond her control.
