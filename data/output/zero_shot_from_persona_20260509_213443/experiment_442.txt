# The Weight of Witness

Marcus sits in the leather chair across from me, his tailored suit impeccable despite the tremor in
his hands as he reaches for water. At sixty-four, he carries himself with the practiced composure of
someone accustomed to public scrutiny, yet something fractures beneath that polish when he speaks
about the years he spent in Syria.

It was 2012 when Marcus first went. He was a different man then—still married, still believing that
his medical background and humanitarian impulse could make a tangible difference. He'd taken a leave
from his position as a city councilman, telling colleagues it was a sabbatical for personal
reflection. The truth was more complicated. He needed to do something that mattered beyond zoning
ordinances and budget allocations. He needed to feel like he was saving lives.

For eight months, he worked in a makeshift clinic in Aleppo, treating injuries that grew
progressively worse as the conflict intensified. Marcus describes this period with clinical
detachment at first—the types of wounds, the improvised surgical techniques, the shortage of
antibiotics. But then his voice changes. He remembers a girl, maybe nine years old, brought in by
her father after a building collapse. Her left arm was crushed beyond saving. Marcus had to
amputate. She survived the procedure, but he can still see her face when he closes his eyes at
night.

When he returned home, his wife said she didn't recognize him anymore. He'd lost thirty pounds. He
wasn't sleeping, or rather, he was sleeping twelve hours and still waking exhausted, his shirt
soaked through with sweat. The nightmares came in waves—not always about specific moments, but about
the ambient horror, the constant low-level dread of knowing that people were dying and he couldn't
stop it. His marriage dissolved over the next three years, quietly and without drama, as if they
were both too depleted to fight for it.

Marcus threw himself into politics with renewed intensity, eventually being elected to the state
legislature. His colleagues saw ambition; he was running from ghosts. He took on committees related
to refugee policy and international humanitarian law, positioning himself as an expert, which in
some ways he was. But the work felt hollow. He'd sit in committee meetings discussing aid
distribution while remembering the woman who'd bled out on his table because they didn't have enough
blood for transfusions.

Now, at sixty-four, Marcus is considering retirement. He tells me this almost apologetically, as if
stepping away from public life is a failure. What he doesn't say directly—but what emerges as we
talk—is that he's become increasingly isolated. He avoids social events where people want to discuss
his "inspiring" humanitarian work. He's hypervigilant in crowds, scanning for threats, his body
tense. He drinks more than he admits, usually alone in his apartment, watching news coverage of
ongoing conflicts with a grim fascination he can't explain.

Sleep remains elusive. He lies awake cataloging his failures—the patients he couldn't save, the
resources he couldn't procure, the girl with the crushed arm who is now, if she's alive, a teenager
he'll never know. Sometimes he wakes gasping, convinced he's back in that clinic, the smell of
infection and desperation overwhelming him even though he's in his climate-controlled bedroom in a
safe American city.

Marcus is caught between two identities: the decorated public servant and the man who witnessed
suffering he couldn't prevent. The distance between these versions of himself feels unbridgeable,
and the weight of that distance is what brings him to my office every Thursday afternoon.
