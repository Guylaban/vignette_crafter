# Marcus

Marcus moves through his studio with the practiced efficiency of someone who has spent twenty-five
years understanding fabric and form. At forty-eight, he maintains the lean physique of his younger
years, though his face carries a heaviness that no amount of sleep seems to lift. He runs his
fingers across a bolt of Italian silk, feeling its weight, its promise. This tactile work—the only
thing that still feels real to him—occupies the hours between dawn and the moment he forces himself
to leave.

Three years ago, Marcus was in Beirut. He'd returned to his childhood city to oversee the opening of
his first flagship boutique, a dream he'd carried since emigrating to the States as a young man. On
August 4th, 2020, he was in a nearby café, reviewing final sketches with his business partner, when
the port exploded. The blast threw him backward through a window. He remembers the sound first—not a
boom but a compression, as if the air itself had turned solid and was pushing down on his chest.
Then the silence. Then the screaming.

He woke in a hospital with lacerations across his left side and a fractured collarbone. The physical
injuries healed within months. The other damage has proven more persistent.

Now, three years later, Marcus still cannot tolerate loud noises. The sound of a siren sends him
into his bathroom, where he sits on the tile floor with his hands over his ears, waiting for his
heart to stop racing. Last month, when his neighbor's car alarm activated in the parking garage,
Marcus found himself unable to drive for a week. His ex-wife, Layla, had tried to understand this.
She'd tried for two years. She finally stopped trying.

The worst part isn't the startle response or the insomnia—though he averages perhaps four hours a
night, his mind replaying the moment of impact in obsessive detail. The worst part is how the world
has fractured into before and after. Colors seem muted now. He can design beautiful things—his
recent collection received critical acclaim—but he feels distant from the beauty itself, as though
he's executing a technical skill rather than creating from passion. His team at the studio notices
his detachment, the way he approves designs with a nod rather than the animated enthusiasm he once
brought to every project.

Marcus has stopped attending social events. Crowds feel dangerous, unpredictable. He avoids
restaurants with open windows and parking structures. He's developed an elaborate mental map of his
city, noting which routes have fewer people, fewer potential hazards. His friends have largely
stopped calling. His family in Lebanon has stopped asking when he'll visit again.

The intrusive memories come without warning. He'll be selecting thread colors and suddenly he's back
in that café, tasting dust and blood. He'll see news footage of a building collapse and his body
will respond as though the ground is moving beneath him, even as his mind knows he's standing safely
in his studio in Los Angeles.

What keeps him going, he tells himself, is the work. The discipline of measuring, cutting,
constructing something from nothing. There's control in fashion that life no longer offers. His
hands know what to do even when his mind cannot find solid ground. Each garment is a small act of
creation in a world that has shown itself capable of sudden, inexplicable destruction.

He arrives at his studio before dawn, as always, and begins again.
