# The Studio

Margaret sits in her studio most mornings now, though the work comes differently than it used to.
Her hands still know the clay—that muscle memory hasn't abandoned her—but something between
intention and execution has fractured. She's sixty-one, a sculptor for nearly forty years, and the
weight of her tools feels heavier than it did six months ago.

It started on an ordinary Tuesday at the community arts center where she teaches twice a week.
Margaret had just finished demonstrating hand-building techniques to her evening class when she
heard the screech of tires, then the terrible, unmistakable sound of impact from the street outside.
Without thinking, she moved toward the windows with several students. A delivery truck had struck a
cyclist—a young man, probably in his twenties—who lay motionless on the asphalt. Margaret watched as
his leg bent at an angle that legs shouldn't bend. She watched his face, slack and still. She
watched until the ambulance arrived, and even then, the image didn't leave. It's still there,
crystalline and intrusive, appearing without warning in the middle of her workday.

Her husband, David, noticed the change almost immediately. Margaret stopped coming to their weekly
dinners with friends. She'd always been social, animated when talking about her work or her
students, but now she finds herself making excuses. The thought of sitting across from people,
maintaining conversation, feels impossible. What if they ask how she's doing? What if she has to
explain that she can't stop seeing that young man's face? Instead, she tells David she's tired, that
she wants to focus on a new commission.

The commission exists, but it isn't progressing. Margaret has started three pieces in the past
months and abandoned each one. She'll spend hours at the wheel or with clay in her hands, and then
something will trigger the memory—a particular angle of light, a sound from the street, the feeling
of vulnerability that comes with focusing intently on something. Her mind floods with the image, and
her hands go numb. She can't continue. She steps away, sometimes for hours, sometimes for days.

Sleep has become negotiable. Margaret lies awake beside David, her body tense, waiting for something
she can't name. When she does sleep, she dreams of the accident in variations—sometimes she's the
one struck, sometimes it's David, sometimes it's a student. She wakes with her heart racing, damp
with sweat, and doesn't wake David. Instead, she goes to the kitchen and makes tea she doesn't
drink.

Loud noises startle her now. The garbage truck outside sends her into a state of hypervigilance that
takes an hour to settle. She's become hyperaware of traffic, of the fragility of bodies moving
through space. When she drives, she's cautious to the point of paralysis, gripping the wheel so
tightly her knuckles blanch.

What troubles Margaret most is the guilt. The young man survived—she learned this from a student
whose cousin knew his family—but Margaret can't shake the feeling that she did something wrong by
witnessing. By watching. By being unable to look away, and then unable to stop seeing it. Her
students have noticed her distraction. One asked last week if she was okay, and Margaret nearly wept
at the simple kindness of the question.

David keeps suggesting she talk to someone, that what she experienced was shocking and that it's
normal to be affected. Margaret knows this intellectually. She's lived long enough to understand
trauma in others. But applying that understanding to herself feels like a luxury she hasn't earned.
Still, she finds herself considering it—not for healing exactly, but for the possibility of
returning to her studio and finding her hands capable again.
