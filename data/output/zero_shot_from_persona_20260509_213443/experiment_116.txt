# Marcus

Marcus sits in the office with his shoulders squared, the habit of twenty years in the Navy still
evident in his posture. At thirty-four, he looks older than his years—there's a tightness around his
eyes that doesn't quite match his controlled demeanor. He's been married to Jennifer for eight
years, and they live near the naval base in San Diego. He agreed to come in after Jennifer finally
told him that his night terrors were affecting her sleep, that she was worried about him, that
something needed to change.

The trauma happened two years ago. Marcus was stationed in the Philippines as part of a humanitarian
deployment when Typhoon Haiyan made landfall. He was part of the initial response team, one of the
first to move into the devastated areas. He describes the experience methodically at first—the scale
of destruction, the logistics of coordinating rescue efforts, the coordination with other military
units. But then his voice changes slightly when he talks about the third day, when his team found a
family of four in the rubble of what had been a school. Two of them were already gone. The other two
were alive but barely. He had to make decisions about triage, about resource allocation, about who
got help first. He was twenty minutes away from the nearest medical facility. One of them didn't
make it.

For the first several months after returning home, Marcus seemed fine. He was promoted to Lieutenant
Commander. He and Jennifer talked about starting a family. He threw himself into his work with the
same dedication he always had. But gradually, things began to shift. He started having trouble
sleeping, waking up in a cold sweat multiple times a night. Jennifer says he sometimes calls out in
his sleep, and once he grabbed her arm so hard he left bruises because he was dreaming he was
pulling someone from collapsed concrete.

Now, two years later, Marcus avoids anything related to natural disasters. He won't watch the news
during hurricane season. He's had to decline several social invitations because they're near the
beach—being near water has become unbearable, even though he's spent his entire adult life around
the ocean. When he does go to work, he's hypervigilant in a way his colleagues don't seem to
understand. He's constantly scanning for problems, for things that could go wrong. His commanding
officer has noticed that he's become more rigid in his command style, less collaborative than he
used to be.

The worst part, Marcus says, is that he can't quite explain it to Jennifer. He'll be fine one
moment, and then something small—a weather alert on his phone, a news story about flooding—will send
him spiraling. His heart races. His mind floods with images from the typhoon. He feels like he's
back there, like the decision he made about that family is happening again, right now, in real time.
These episodes last for hours sometimes. He withdraws, becomes irritable, pushes Jennifer away even
though he knows that's not what he wants. She's tried to help, but he's not sure how to let her.

He describes himself as someone who has always been able to handle difficult situations. That's what
he was trained for. That's who he is as a naval officer. The fact that he can't seem to move past
this, that he's still having these reactions after two years, feels like a personal failure. He's
caught between the identity that has sustained him—the capable officer, the problem-solver—and the
reality of what his mind and body are doing to him now.
