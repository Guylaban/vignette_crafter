# A Life Interrupted

Layla arrives at her Thursday afternoon appointment carrying the weight of the day in her shoulders.
At thirty-eight, she moves with the careful deliberation of someone who has learned to occupy space
quietly. She has worked as a housekeeper for nearly fifteen years, moving through the homes and
offices of others with practiced efficiency, making herself nearly invisible—a skill that once
served her well.

Three years ago, everything fractured.

She had been cleaning the downtown office building where she worked the evening shift, a job she
chose specifically because it meant working alone, after the regular business hours ended. On a
Tuesday night in March, a man she didn't know followed her into the supply closet. What happened
there lasted only minutes, but those minutes have somehow stretched across every day since. He was
never caught. The police took her statement, but there were no witnesses, no clear evidence. Life
moved forward for everyone except Layla.

The assault itself was violent. She remembers the shock of it, the way her body betrayed her by
going rigid rather than fighting back. She remembers his hands, his voice, the smell of his cologne
mixed with something acrid and chemical. She remembers the fluorescent light humming overhead and
thinking, absurdly, that she needed to finish cleaning the bathrooms before she left. She has
replayed those minutes countless times since, each replay feeling as immediate and terrifying as the
first.

For months afterward, Layla couldn't return to that building. She lost that job, and her family's
income suffered. Her husband Reza tried to be supportive, but she could feel his frustration
underneath his patience. They needed her income. Eventually, she found work at a hotel, cleaning
rooms during the day shift. But the hotel rooms triggered something different—the closed doors, the
locks, the vulnerability of entering spaces where she couldn't control who might be inside. Her
hands would shake as she knocked and called out before entering. Guests would complain about her
slowness, her hesitation.

Now, three years later, Layla has developed an intricate system of avoidance and hypervigilance that
structures her entire life. She no longer works the evening shift anywhere. She insists on cleaning
rooms in strict numerical order, always checking closets and bathrooms before fully entering. She
keeps her phone in her hand constantly, her location shared with Reza at all times. She has
memorized the layout of every room in the hotel, every exit, every potential weapon. She sleeps
poorly, waking multiple times each night, sometimes unsure of where she is. When she does sleep, she
has nightmares so vivid that Reza has learned to wake her gently, speaking softly from across the
room so as not to startle her further.

The most insidious part is how the trauma has colonized her thinking. Layla finds herself scanning
faces in crowds, assessing threat levels in strangers. She interprets ambiguous interactions as
hostile. When a guest speaks to her, she assumes they are angry. When a male coworker holds a door
open, she feels a spike of panic. She has become exhausted by her own mind's constant vigilance, by
the way her body remains perpetually braced for danger that isn't coming.

Reza has suggested she see someone, and she has finally agreed. She sits across from her therapist
now, hands folded in her lap, and begins to speak about the Tuesday that changed everything. She
speaks quietly, as she does with most things, but there is something different in her voice
today—perhaps the smallest opening, a willingness to be seen.
