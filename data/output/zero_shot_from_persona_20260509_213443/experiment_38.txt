# Margaret's Careful Mornings

Margaret sits at her kitchen table with her coffee growing cold, watching the garden through the
window. At sixty-six, she has learned that mornings require a particular kind of vigilance. She
needs to be awake for at least twenty minutes before she tries to do anything that matters—before
she opens her laptop to work on the curriculum materials due to her team by noon, before she calls
her daughter, before she allows herself to believe the day might unfold normally.

Fifty-three years have passed since she served as a combat medic in the Pacific theater, but her
body hasn't seemed to notice the decades. The dreams still come most nights—the smell of blood and
diesel fuel, the weight of a soldier's leg in her hands as she applied a tourniquet, the sound of
helicopters that never quite landed. She wakes sometimes gasping, her heart moving so fast she has
to sit on the edge of the bed and count her breaths until her hands stop shaking.

Her work as a curriculum designer suits her in ways she didn't expect when she took the position
eight years ago. There's something calming about structuring learning, about building frameworks
that help people understand complex ideas. She designs online courses for healthcare workers, and
there's an irony she doesn't discuss with her colleagues—she's designing training modules for people
who will face emergencies she knows intimately. Last month, she spent three weeks developing a
module on trauma response for nurses. She rewrote the section on emotional regulation five times,
each version drawing from knowledge she's accumulated through her own relentless effort to stay
functional.

But structure only holds so much. Last Tuesday, a notification sound on her computer—a sharp
electronic beep she'd never heard before—sent her into a state of such acute panic that she had to
excuse herself from a video meeting. She sat in her bedroom for forty minutes, unable to explain to
her team why she'd disappeared. The sound had triggered something primal, something that insisted
danger was immediate and real. She knows intellectually that a notification isn't a warning siren,
but her nervous system disagrees with a certainty that no amount of reasoning can touch.

Her ex-husband used to say she was "difficult to live with," which was his way of describing the
nights she'd wake him with her thrashing, or the afternoons when she'd snap at him over something
trivial because she was already running at half-capacity just managing the background noise of her
own anxiety. The divorce was finalized fourteen years ago. She doesn't blame him anymore, though she
used to.

Margaret has learned to build her life around the unpredictability of her own mind. She takes the
same route to the grocery store because unfamiliar environments require too much cognitive energy.
She keeps her schedule light and avoids crowds. She has three friends she trusts, and she sees them
individually, never in groups. She's learned which restaurants have exits she can see from her
table. She's learned to recognize the feeling of dissociation before it fully arrives—a kind of
cottony distance from her own hands—and she has techniques to ground herself: touching something
cold, naming five things she can see.

The coffee is definitely cold now. Margaret pours it down the sink and makes a fresh cup. Her team
is expecting the curriculum module by noon, and she's nearly finished. She opens her laptop, takes a
breath, and begins to type. The work steadies her. It always does. She has learned, over these long
decades, that productivity and presence are not the same thing as healing, but they are something.
They are enough to keep her moving forward, even on mornings when forward feels like the hardest
direction to go.
