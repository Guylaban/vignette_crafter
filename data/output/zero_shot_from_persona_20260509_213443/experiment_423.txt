# Sarah's Story

Sarah sits in the waiting room with her hands folded in her lap, her fingers picking at a loose
thread on her sweater. At thirty-three, she looks younger—or perhaps just tired. Her dark blonde
hair is pulled back severely, and she wears large sunglasses despite the overcast afternoon outside.
She removes them as she settles into the chair across from me, revealing dark circles that makeup
doesn't quite conceal.

She's been a graphic designer for nearly a decade, working primarily from home for the past three
years. She tells me this matter-of-factly, but there's something in the way she mentions the
transition that suggests it wasn't entirely by choice.

Two years ago, Sarah received a direct message on Instagram from someone claiming to be an old
acquaintance. The messages seemed harmless at first—compliments about her design work, which she
shared on her professional portfolio. But within weeks, they became personal, then obsessive. The
person knew details about her daily routine: which coffee shop she visited on Tuesday mornings, the
gym she attended, the route she took to her husband's office. Sarah's chest tightens as she recounts
how she eventually discovered that this person had created multiple fake accounts, had shown up at
her workplace, and had been following her car.

The police became involved. A restraining order was obtained. The stalker was eventually arrested
and prosecuted. By any measure, the situation was resolved. But Sarah's body hasn't quite caught up
with that resolution.

She describes her current life in fragments. She doesn't leave the house alone anymore. Her husband,
Marcus, drives her to appointments, to the grocery store, everywhere. She's stopped going to the gym
entirely. She quit her office job and negotiated remote work with her design clients, something that
felt like a failure even though it was practical. Now she works from her home office with the blinds
drawn and the door locked, even during the day.

Her sleep is fragmented. She wakes at 2 or 3 a.m., her heart racing, convinced she's heard a noise
outside. Sometimes she gets up and checks the windows and doors, even though she already knows
they're locked. Marcus has stopped trying to reassure her; he simply lies awake beside her now, his
presence a small comfort.

When she's designing—her one remaining anchor to normalcy—she finds herself unable to concentrate.
Her mind fractures into hypervigilance. A notification on her phone sends adrenaline through her
system. An unfamiliar car parked on their street sends her spiraling. Last week, someone knocked on
the door for a package delivery, and she had a panic attack in the bathroom while Marcus answered
it.

She describes moments of detachment, too. Sometimes she's sitting with Marcus on the couch, and she
realizes she hasn't heard a word he's said for the past ten minutes. She's there but not there, her
mind replaying moments from two years ago—the moment she realized someone was watching her, the sick
feeling of violation, the way her sense of safety simply evaporated.

What strikes me most is not what she's lost—though there's clearly been loss—but the way she speaks
about the future. She doesn't quite believe in one yet. When I ask what she hopes for, she pauses
for a long time, and then simply says, "I just want to feel like myself again. I don't remember what
that was like."
