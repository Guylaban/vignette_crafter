# Marcus

Marcus sits in the leather chair across from the therapist, his posture rigid despite the chair's
inviting design. At fifty-one, he carries himself with the careful precision of someone accustomed
to being in charge—shoulders back, hands folded on his lap. But there's a brittleness to this
composure, a sense that he might shatter if pressed too hard.

Three years ago, Marcus was diagnosed with stage three pancreatic cancer. He describes the moment
his oncologist delivered the news with clinical detachment, as though reciting facts about someone
else's life. "She said the five-year survival rate was thirty percent," he recalls, his jaw
tightening. "I did the math immediately. Seventy percent don't make it. I was already doing the
math." He pauses, then adds quietly, "I still do the math."

The diagnosis came at a time when Marcus felt invincible. He'd been principal of Lincoln High School
for twelve years, respected by staff and students alike. His wife, Catherine, had passed away five
years earlier from a stroke—sudden, unexpected, devastating—but he'd rebuilt his life through work,
channeling his grief into mentoring troubled students and creating programs that actually made a
difference. He'd felt, finally, like he was on solid ground again.

The cancer changed everything. Marcus underwent aggressive chemotherapy and surgery, spending months
in a fog of nausea, pain, and existential terror. He took a leave of absence from the school,
something he'd never done before. The uncertainty was suffocating. Every scan result felt like a
verdict. Every ache in his body sent him spiraling into catastrophic thinking. He began researching
survival statistics obsessively, reading medical journals late into the night, his mind racing with
worst-case scenarios.

He recovered physically. The cancer went into remission. By objective measures, Marcus is a
survivor. But something fractured inside him during those months, and it hasn't fully healed.

He returned to work fourteen months after his diagnosis, determined to reclaim his life. But the
school feels different now—smaller, less real. He moves through his days on autopilot, attending
meetings and reviewing lesson plans while part of his mind remains tethered to the hospital, to the
moment he thought he was dying. He's hypervigilant about his health, noticing every twinge, every
irregularity. He's had three additional cancer screenings in the past year, each one a cycle of
dread and temporary relief.

Sleep is elusive. Marcus lies awake at night, his mind replaying conversations with doctors,
imagining recurrence, calculating the years he's already lost to treatment and recovery. When he
does sleep, he has vivid nightmares—he's back in the oncology clinic, but the doctor's face keeps
changing, and the diagnosis keeps shifting. He wakes gasping, his heart pounding, convinced that
something is wrong with his body.

His relationships have suffered. His adult daughter has expressed concern about his constant health
preoccupation, but he finds himself unable to reassure her—or himself. Colleagues have noticed his
withdrawal, his difficulty concentrating during important meetings. He's become irritable, snapping
at staff over minor issues, then immediately feeling guilty. He attends a grief support group for
people who've lost spouses, but he finds himself unable to focus on others' pain. His own terror is
too consuming.

Marcus knows, intellectually, that he survived. The evidence is there: clear scans, restored energy,
the ability to walk without pain. But he cannot shake the feeling that death is still waiting for
him, that his reprieve is temporary, that he's living on borrowed time. He exists in a perpetual
state of anticipation, waiting for the other shoe to drop, unable to fully believe that he gets to
continue living.
