# A Matter of Control

Marcus sits in the waiting room with his phone face-down on his lap, a habit he's developed over the
past eight months. He's dressed impeccably—charcoal suit, burgundy tie, gold cufflinks—the uniform
of someone who has spent thirty-seven years managing other people's money and, by extension,
managing the impression he gives the world. At sixty-two, he still arrives at his office on Wall
Street by 6:30 a.m., still reviews market reports obsessively, still maintains the discipline that
built his reputation. But something fundamental has shifted, and the tailored suit no longer quite
contains him.

His daughter Amara was thirty-four when she died last March. She was a pediatric nurse,
conscientious and warm, the kind of person who called her father every Sunday without fail. She had
two children—seven and nine—and a husband who loved her. She had plans. She had a life that made
sense, that followed a logical progression. And then, on a Tuesday evening, she collapsed while
making dinner. Aneurysm. By the time Marcus received the call, she was already gone.

He describes the moment he learned of her death as a kind of fracture. The world simply split into
before and after, and he has been living in the after ever since, trying to understand how the laws
of probability he'd spent his career calculating had suddenly betrayed him so completely.

For the first three months, Marcus functioned on autopilot. He managed the funeral arrangements with
the same meticulous attention he brought to portfolio management. He supported his ex-wife, whom
he'd divorced fifteen years earlier but never stopped caring for. He was present for his
grandchildren in ways he sometimes wishes he had been present for Amara herself—he'd been a
demanding father, focused on achievement, perhaps not always attuned to what she actually needed
from him.

But around June, something changed. The autopilot failed. Marcus began experiencing intrusive
thoughts about Amara's final moments—images he'd never witnessed but his mind constructed anyway.
He'd be reviewing a client's quarterly earnings, and suddenly he'd be seized by the conviction that
he should have called her that day, that his failure to do so somehow contributed to her death. The
logic was faulty, but logic no longer seemed to govern his thinking.

Sleep became impossible. He'd lie awake at three in the morning, his mind cataloging all the ways
he'd failed her—missed recitals, forgotten birthdays, the time he'd criticized her choice of husband
because the man wasn't ambitious enough. His chest would tighten, and he'd convince himself he was
having a heart attack, that he was dying too. He'd check his pulse repeatedly, sit on the edge of
the bed in the dark, waiting for catastrophe.

At work, his concentration fractured. He'd lose his place in conversations. Clients began to notice.
His business partner gently suggested he take time off. Marcus refused. Taking time off felt like
surrender, like admitting that Amara's death had the power to dismantle him. He'd built a life on
the principle that discipline and control could protect you from randomness, from loss. Amara's
death had exposed that principle as an illusion.

Now, in his office, he finds himself unable to make decisions—something that had always come
naturally. He second-guesses himself constantly, paralyzed by the recognition that his judgment
cannot be trusted, that the future cannot be predicted or controlled. He avoids the places where
they used to go together. He hasn't returned to his apartment in the Upper West Side, instead
staying in a hotel near his office, a temporary space that requires nothing of him.

His ex-wife called last week and said he sounded like a ghost. Marcus didn't disagree.
