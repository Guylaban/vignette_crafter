# Margaret's Story

Margaret sits in the chair across from me with her hands folded in her lap, a habit she's developed
over the past few months. She's a woman of sixty with silver threading through her dark hair, pulled
back neatly as it always is. She tells me she's been working at the call center for twelve
years—long enough that she knows every script, every protocol, every way to de-escalate an angry
customer. But nothing in those twelve years prepared her for what happened eight months ago.

Her younger brother, David, called her at work on a Tuesday afternoon. Margaret remembers this
clearly because she was on a break, sitting in the employee lounge with a cup of instant noodles
she'd brought from home. David sounded strange—not quite himself. He said he wasn't feeling well,
that he was going to lie down. Margaret told him to drink water, to rest. She didn't hear the
urgency in his voice, or perhaps she didn't want to. She was thinking about her next call queue.
David was forty-eight. He'd always been the healthy one in the family.

When her husband called two hours later, Margaret was already back at her station, her headset on,
her voice steady and professional as she walked a customer through a billing issue. The words her
husband spoke didn't make sense at first. Massive heart attack. David had collapsed in his kitchen.
By the time the ambulance arrived, it was too late.

The funeral happened in a blur. Margaret remembers standing in front of David's photograph, unable
to cry, unable to feel much of anything. Her relatives embraced her, spoke to her, but their voices
seemed to come from very far away. She returned to work after two weeks because staying home felt
worse—the silence in the house, the weight of it pressing down on her chest.

But work has become something different now. Margaret sits at her desk with her headset on, and
sometimes, without warning, she's back in that break room, holding her cold noodles, hearing David's
voice saying he wasn't feeling well. A customer's tone of voice—something slightly off, slightly
strained—can trigger it. Her heart races. Her hands shake. She's convinced the person on the line is
about to tell her someone has died. Once, she had to disconnect a call and excuse herself to the
bathroom, where she sat in a stall for twenty minutes, unable to breathe properly.

At night, Margaret lies awake next to her husband, her mind cycling through the conversation with
David. What if she'd asked more questions? What if she'd told him to go to the hospital immediately?
What if she'd taken that call more seriously instead of thinking about her metrics and her queue
time? She knows, intellectually, that she couldn't have changed anything. But her body doesn't
believe this. Her body believes she failed him.

She's started avoiding things. She doesn't answer her phone as quickly anymore—that split-second of
terror that it will be bad news is sometimes too much. She's called in sick to work three times in
the past month, unable to face the headset, unable to sit in that chair and maintain her
professional composure. Her husband has noticed she's withdrawn, that she flinches at sudden sounds.
Margaret notices it too. She notices everything now—how fragile everything is, how quickly a Tuesday
afternoon can become the worst day of your life.

She tells me she feels trapped between two worlds: the one where she was before, and the one she's
in now.
