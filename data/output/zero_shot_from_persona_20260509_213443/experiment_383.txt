# Dr. Linh's Practice

Dr. Linh has been practicing dentistry for twenty-three years. Her small practice sits in a quiet
strip mall, tucked between a dry cleaner and a tax preparation office. She arrives each morning at
7:15 to review patient charts and prepare the operatory, a ritual that has always steadied her. Her
hygienists know her as meticulous, unflappable—the kind of dentist who notices a millimeter of
recession on a molar and remembers every patient's fear of needles without being told twice.

Four years ago, a man began appearing in her waiting room. He would schedule appointments, then
cancel. He would sit in his car in the parking lot. He left notes in her mailbox—not threatening
exactly, but insistent. He knew details about her routine: when she arrived, when she left, that she
always bought coffee from the café two blocks away on Thursdays. He knew her husband's name. He knew
her daughter's school.

Dr. Linh reported him to the police. She obtained a restraining order. The stalking eventually
stopped, but something in her didn't.

Now, three years later, she still arrives at 7:15, but not because she finds comfort in the ritual
anymore. She arrives early because she needs to check the parking lot first—to walk the perimeter,
to look under the cars, to make sure the building is empty. Some mornings, her hands shake as she
unlocks the door. She tells herself it's just caffeine.

During patient appointments, she finds herself unable to fully concentrate. A man came in last week
for a crown preparation, and she kept losing her train of thought. She couldn't remember if she'd
already anesthetized the tooth. She had to excuse herself and check her notes. Her hygienist
noticed. Everyone notices when you're not yourself.

The evenings are worse. Her husband, Marcus, will suggest they go out for dinner, and her chest
tightens immediately. A restaurant means windows, means being visible, means not controlling who
might walk through the door. She makes excuses. She stays home. She keeps the curtains drawn and
checks the locks multiple times before bed—sometimes four or five times, even though she checked
five minutes earlier.

At night, she dreams she's in the operatory with a patient, and when she looks up, the man is
standing in the doorway. She wakes gasping, her heart throwing itself against her ribs. Marcus
reaches for her in the darkness, but she flinches away. She doesn't mean to. She knows it's him. But
her body doesn't believe it.

The worst part is the hypervigilance that follows her everywhere. In the grocery store, she
positions herself so she can see the entrance. At the bank, she counts how many people are around
her. Driving, she checks her mirrors constantly, convinced that the same car has been behind her for
too long. She knows, logically, that he hasn't contacted her in three years. She knows the
restraining order is still in place. She knows the police took it seriously.

But her nervous system doesn't know these things. It only knows that once, a stranger decided she
was his, and that the world is not as safe as she believed it to be.

She continues to work. She prepares root canals and fits crowns and explains periodontal disease to
anxious patients. She smiles. She is professional. But inside, she is always listening for the door
to open, always bracing for something terrible that may never come.
