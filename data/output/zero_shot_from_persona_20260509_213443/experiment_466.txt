# Maria's Story

Maria sits in the leather chair with her back straight, the way she's learned to sit—the way pilots
sit. At forty-five, she carries herself with the practiced composure of someone accustomed to making
split-second decisions while others' lives depend on it. She's been flying combat missions for over
two decades, and until three years ago, she felt invincible in the cockpit.

The attack happened during what should have been a routine reconnaissance mission over contested
territory. Maria was leading a formation of four fighter jets when the surface-to-air missile came
without warning. She saw the bloom of smoke on the ground and heard the warning systems screaming.
Her wingman's jet—piloted by her closest friend, Thomas—took the hit. She watched his aircraft
cartwheel through the sky before disappearing into the desert below. The radio went silent. She
completed the mission, landed safely, filed her report, and walked away. Thomas didn't walk away.

For the first year afterward, Maria told herself she was fine. She returned to flying, passed all
her psychological evaluations, maintained her perfect record. But her body was keeping score in ways
her mind wouldn't acknowledge. She started waking at three in the morning, drenched in sweat, her
heart hammering as though the missile was still coming. She'd jolt awake with her hands already
reaching for controls that weren't there. She stopped sleeping in her bed, instead falling asleep in
the armchair in her living room, where she could see the door, where she could see the windows.

The hypervigilance crept into her waking hours too. During preflight checks, she'd find herself
checking the same instrument panel three, four, five times, unable to trust what her eyes were
telling her. In the grocery store, she'd position herself with her back to the wall, scanning the
other shoppers, noting exits. A car backfiring sent her diving behind a parked vehicle last month.
She'd laughed it off to the concerned onlooker, but her hands shook for an hour afterward.

Maria began avoiding the squadron's social events. The camaraderie that once sustained her now felt
suffocating. When colleagues mentioned Thomas—when they talked about his family, his memorial
service, the empty seat in the briefing room—she felt something fracture inside her. She'd stop
answering texts. She'd make excuses not to attend dinners. The guilt was relentless: she'd survived,
Thomas hadn't, and somehow that felt like a betrayal.

Flying, which had always been her sanctuary, became increasingly complicated. She could still
execute maneuvers flawlessly, could still lead her team with precision. But in the moments before
takeoff, a suffocating dread would settle in her chest. What if today was the day? What if she'd
miss the warning signs? What if someone else didn't come home? The intrusive thoughts would flash
without warning—sudden, vivid images of Thomas's jet spiraling down, of her own cockpit filling with
smoke, of the moment she realized she was powerless to save him.

Now, sitting in the therapist's office, Maria describes these experiences with the same detached
professionalism she uses to debrief combat missions. But her leg bounces slightly, and her fingers
drum against her thigh in a pattern that matches her heartbeat. She hasn't slept through a full
night in thirty-six months. She hasn't attended a squadron event in eighteen months. She's still
flying, still performing, still excelling—but something essential has fractured, and she's not sure
how to put it back together.
