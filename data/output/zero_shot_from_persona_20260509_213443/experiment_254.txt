# A Quiet Man in the Stacks

Marcus sits in my office with the careful posture of someone who has learned to take up as little
space as possible. At seventy-eight, his frame is still strong—he worked as a librarian for forty-
three years before retiring five years ago—but there is a brittleness to him now, as though he might
shatter if someone raised their voice.

He came to see me because his wife, Amara, finally insisted. She says he has changed since the
bombing in Nairobi three years ago. She is right.

Marcus was visiting his sister in Kenya, a trip he took every other year without fail. He had just
stepped out of the university library where he'd spent the morning researching his family
genealogy—a project that had consumed him since retirement. He was crossing the street to meet his
sister for lunch when the explosion fractured the afternoon. He remembers the sound first: not a
bang but a deep, terrible crack that seemed to split the world open. Then the heat. Then nothing
clear until he was on the ground, ears ringing, surrounded by dust and the smell of burning.

He was not badly injured—a lacerated arm, a concussion, some hearing loss in his left ear. But the
woman next to him did not survive. He knows this because he remembers her face turning toward his in
the moment before, and he remembers the paramedics covering her with a sheet.

Now, three years later, Marcus cannot enter a library.

This is what troubles him most, and what he struggles to articulate to me. Libraries had been his
sanctuary for nearly half a century. The smell of aging paper and leather bindings, the particular
silence of the reading room, the way light fell through tall windows onto wooden tables—these things
had structured his life and given it meaning. He had trained young librarians, curated special
collections, and restored rare books with the care of a surgeon. The library was not just where he
worked; it was where he felt most like himself.

After the bombing, he tried to return to the library near his home. He made it through the entrance,
but the moment the doors closed behind him, his heart began to race so violently he thought he was
dying. The silence that once comforted him now felt like a held breath, like waiting for something
terrible to happen. He left immediately and has not returned.

Instead, he sits at home. Amara describes him as withdrawn, but it is more complicated than that. He
is hypervigilant in ways that exhaust both of them. He startles at unexpected sounds. He checks the
locks on their doors multiple times each evening. He no longer enjoys the news, which he once read
religiously. He has stopped attending his book club. When Amara plays music—something she loves—he
asks her to turn it down because he cannot hear approaching danger over it.

At night, he dreams of the library. In these dreams, he is shelving books when the walls begin to
crack. He tries to warn people, but no sound comes from his mouth. He wakes gasping, and it takes
him several minutes to remember that he is safe, that he is in his own bed in his own home in a city
where no bomb has fallen.

What strikes me most is the profound loss he is grieving. It is not simply the loss of his sense of
safety—though that is real and devastating. It is the loss of the one place where he felt entirely
himself, where his mind was most alive. He has become a man separated from the thing that made him
feel most human.
