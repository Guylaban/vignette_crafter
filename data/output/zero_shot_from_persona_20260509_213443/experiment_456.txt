# Rabbi Sarah's Unraveling Thread

Sarah sits in the leather chair with her hands folded in her lap, a posture that speaks of someone
accustomed to holding herself together. At forty-eight, she carries herself with the composed
dignity expected of a rabbi—shoulders back, voice measured—but there is something fragile beneath
the surface, like a carefully maintained facade that threatens to crack.

Three years ago, Sarah was leading a community Shabbat service at her synagogue in Cape Town when
the roof partially collapsed during a severe storm. She had been standing at the bimah, mid-prayer,
when the first beam came down. The sound was apocalyptic—a splitting, groaning tear followed by a
shower of plaster and wood. Twenty-three people were injured; one elderly congregant died. Sarah was
physically unharmed, though a piece of debris grazed her shoulder. That detail seems almost to
embarrass her now, as though her survival without serious injury is somehow a betrayal of the
gravity of what happened.

For the first few months, Sarah moved through her duties mechanically. She visited the injured in
hospitals, sat shiva with the family of the deceased, coordinated repairs to the building. Her
community needed her to be strong, and she was—or appeared to be. But something had fractured inside
her that day, something more fundamental than the sanctuary's architecture.

The nightmares began around month four. She would wake at three or four in the morning, her body
drenched in sweat, her heart racing, certain that the ceiling was collapsing again. Her husband,
David, learned not to touch her suddenly when she startled awake; she would lash out in panic before
recognizing him. She stopped sleeping in their bedroom, moving to the guest room where she could
keep the lights on.

Her relationship with the synagogue—the place that had been the center of her life for twenty
years—became unbearable. Walking into the repaired sanctuary triggered a physical response: her
chest would tighten, her vision would narrow, and she would feel the urgent need to escape. She
began finding reasons to conduct services in the smaller chapel. When she had to lead in the main
sanctuary, she would position herself near the exit. She found herself scanning the ceiling
obsessively, checking for structural weakness, unable to concentrate on the prayers she was supposed
to be guiding others through.

The hypervigilance extended beyond the building. Loud noises—a car backfiring, construction sounds,
even thunder—would send her into a state of acute anxiety that could last for hours. She became
reluctant to drive, convinced that something catastrophic would happen. She stopped attending
community events, making excuses that felt thin even to her. Her colleagues began to notice her
withdrawal, the way she would decline invitations with increasingly vague explanations.

What troubles Sarah most is the sense of disconnection from her calling. She had become a rabbi
because she believed deeply in the power of spiritual community, in the sanctuary as a place where
people could feel held and safe. Now the word "sanctuary" feels like a cruel irony. She finds
herself unable to access the faith that once sustained her, unable to offer the comfort and
certainty her congregation expects from her. In quiet moments, she wonders if she should step down,
if her presence has become more burden than blessing.

David remains patient, though she can see the worry in his eyes. She continues to show up, to
perform her role, but increasingly she feels like she is inhabiting someone else's life—going
through the motions of being Rabbi Sarah while something inside her remains trapped beneath that
collapsing roof.
