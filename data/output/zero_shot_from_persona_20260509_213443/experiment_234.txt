# Marcus

Marcus sits in the waiting room of his office building every morning now, fifteen minutes before his
workday begins. He tells himself it's to review client files, but really he's watching the security
guard at the front desk. He knows the guard's shift rotation, knows which cameras point toward the
elevators, knows exactly how long it takes to reach the stairwell. This careful surveillance has
become as routine as brushing his teeth.

Three years ago, Marcus was leaving a late-night dinner with colleagues when police stopped him two
blocks from the restaurant. He was driving a rental car—his own was in the shop—and officers said it
matched a description from a nearby robbery. Marcus remembers the confusion more than anything else
at first. He pulled over immediately, hands visible. He remembers thinking this would be resolved
quickly, that there was simply a misunderstanding.

What happened next exists in his mind as fragments. The officer's hand on his car door. His own hand
reaching for his wallet to show identification. The sudden, explosive pain across his ribs. He was
pulled from the vehicle, thrown against the hood. His glasses fell. Without them, everything became
a blur of uniforms and shouting. He felt hands everywhere—on his arms, his legs, his neck. He
couldn't see their faces clearly. He still can't, which somehow makes it worse.

The charges were dropped within hours. Wrong car. Wrong person. An apology came from the police
department's legal department, not from the officers themselves. Marcus received a settlement check
eighteen months later. The money sits in a separate account. He hasn't touched it.

Since then, Marcus has reorganized his entire life around avoidance. He no longer drives at night.
He takes different routes to his office, varying them unpredictably. When he passes a police car,
his heart rate spikes and his hands go numb on the steering wheel. Last month, a siren wailed three
blocks away, and Marcus found himself unable to leave his apartment for two days. He told his boss
he had the flu.

His work has suffered, though he's managed to hide the worst of it. Tax consulting requires
precision and attention to detail—qualities Marcus once prided himself on. Now he finds himself
reading the same paragraph in a client's financial statement five, sometimes ten times before the
words stick. He's made small errors that are unlike him, mistakes that have required awkward
corrections. His supervisor has noticed. There have been gentle questions about whether everything
is okay at home.

At night, Marcus lies awake replaying the moment his hand moved toward his wallet. He imagines
different choices: keeping his hands on the wheel, not reaching for his ID, speaking differently. In
these mental loops, he searches for something he could have done differently, as though the assault
was somehow a consequence of his own action. Rationally, he knows this isn't true. But the knowing
and the feeling exist in separate chambers.

He hasn't told many people. His ex-wife knows—they were still in contact when it happened—but she
grew tired of his hypervigilance, his inability to relax, the way he'd wake gasping from nightmares.
His adult children know the basic facts but not the details. Marcus is a private man, and shame has
wrapped itself around the trauma like a second skin. He feels foolish for being afraid, ashamed that
a man his age, a professional, is undone by what happened.

Marcus comes to therapy because his doctor insisted. He's not entirely convinced talking about it
will help, but he's running out of other options. He wants his life back—or at least some version of
it that doesn't involve constant vigilance and the persistent sensation that danger is always
seconds away.
