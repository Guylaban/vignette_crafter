# Michael's Story

Michael sits across from me with his shoulders drawn inward, fingers drumming a restless rhythm
against his thigh. At twenty-nine, he has the polished appearance of someone trained to present
well—crisp shirt, neat hair—but his eyes tell a different story. They dart toward the window
whenever a siren passes, and there's a tremor in his voice when he begins to speak about the
flooding.

It happened eighteen months ago during the spring storms that swept through the region. Michael was
working a sales conference at a downtown hotel when the river rose faster than anyone predicted. He
remembers the moment the water started seeping under the ballroom doors—a thin, dark line that
seemed almost innocent at first. Then came the announcement that guests should move to upper floors.
He made it to the third floor with a group of colleagues, but the water kept rising. For six hours,
they watched from the windows as the street below transformed into a churning current. Cars floated
past. He saw a man clinging to a lamppost, screaming. Michael doesn't talk much about what happened
to that man.

The hotel staff eventually moved everyone to the roof. Michael spent the night there, soaked and
shivering, listening to the sound of water destroying everything below. When rescue came the next
morning, he felt almost nothing—just a strange numbness as the helicopter lifted him away.

Now, Michael struggles with the most basic aspects of his job. He's a salesperson for a commercial
equipment company, someone who used to thrive on client meetings and presentations. But something
has shifted. Heavy rain triggers an immediate panic response—his heart races, his breathing becomes
shallow, and he finds himself scanning for exits and higher ground, even in his apartment on the
fourth floor. He's called in sick on rainy days more times than he can count. His manager has
started asking questions about his reliability.

What troubles Michael most is the unpredictability of his reactions. Last week, a client spilled a
glass of water during a meeting, and Michael felt his chest tighten so severely he had to excuse
himself. He sat in the bathroom stall for fifteen minutes, trying to slow his breathing. He's
embarrassed by this, ashamed that he can't simply move forward like other people seem to do.

Sleep has become elusive. Most nights, Michael lies awake, his mind cycling through scenarios—what
if he hadn't made it to the third floor, what if the rescue had been delayed, what if the water had
kept rising. When he does sleep, he dreams of drowning in slow motion. He wakes gasping, tangled in
sheets soaked with sweat.

His apartment is on high ground now—a deliberate choice—but he finds himself obsessively checking
weather forecasts, reading flood reports, tracking rainfall. He's researched escape routes from his
building and keeps a go-bag packed in his closet. His friends have stopped inviting him out because
he often cancels or leaves early, citing fatigue. The truth is more complicated: crowds make him
anxious, and he finds it increasingly difficult to maintain the social mask his job requires.

Michael wants to return to who he was before—the confident salesman who closed deals and genuinely
enjoyed client interaction. But between the intrusive memories, the hypervigilance, and the
exhaustion of fighting his own nervous system, he's struggling to recognize that version of himself.
He sits here now, hoping that talking about it might somehow make the water stop rising in his mind.
