# A Veterinarian's Persistent Vigilance

Alex moves through their clinic with practiced efficiency, checking on the recovery kennels and
updating charts. They've been a veterinarian for three years, and they love the work—the problem-
solving, the animals' trust, the tangible relief in owners' eyes when their pets improve. But
lately, something has shifted in how they navigate even this familiar space.

Six months ago, Alex was walking home from a late shift around 9 PM. They'd taken this route
countless times, through a relatively quiet neighborhood they'd considered safe. Two blocks from
their apartment, someone grabbed them from behind, demanding their wallet and phone. Alex complied
immediately, but the person shoved them hard against a brick wall anyway. The impact knocked the
wind out of them. It was over in seconds—the attacker ran, Alex stood trembling on the sidewalk, and
a neighbor called the police. No serious physical injuries. Alex reported it, gave a statement, and
tried to move forward.

Except moving forward hasn't happened the way they expected.

Now, six months later, Alex finds themselves hyperaware of every person who enters the clinic.
During appointments, they notice themselves tensing when clients stand too close, even when they're
clearly just trying to see their pet better. Yesterday, a man reached toward the counter quickly to
grab a pen, and Alex's entire body went rigid. Their hands trembled for twenty minutes afterward.

The evenings are hardest. Alex's partner, Morgan, has noticed the changes. They've stopped their
evening walks together, which they used to love. Instead, Alex drives everywhere now, even to places
within walking distance. They've rearranged their schedule to avoid leaving work after dark, asking
colleagues to cover late shifts. When they do have to stay late, they park directly under the
clinic's lights and sit in their car for several minutes, scanning the parking lot before getting
out—a ritual that feels both necessary and exhausting.

Sleep has become complicated. Alex lies awake, running through scenarios: What if it happens again?
What if they're somewhere without their phone? What if they can't comply fast enough? They've
started sleeping with their keys on the nightstand and a small flashlight under their pillow. Morgan
tries to be patient, but Alex can feel the strain. Morgan wants to help, but there's nothing to help
with—the danger feels everywhere and nowhere simultaneously.

At work, Alex's focus fractures unpredictably. They'll be performing a routine examination when
their mind suddenly floods with the memory of impact, the feeling of hands grabbing them. Their
heart races. Their breathing becomes shallow. They've had to excuse themselves from procedures twice
to collect themselves in the staff bathroom. Their colleague covered for them both times, but the
shame lingers. They're a professional. They should be able to handle this.

What troubles Alex most is the anger—a sharp, reactive irritability that surprises them. They
snapped at Morgan over something trivial last week. They've been curt with staff members. This isn't
who they are. They pride themselves on patience and compassion, qualities essential to both their
relationship and their work with animals and their anxious owners.

The logical part of Alex knows the mugging was random, that they did nothing wrong, that they're
safe now. But their body doesn't seem to have received that message. Every unexpected sound makes
them jump. Every crowded space feels like a potential threat. The person who grabbed them is still
out there somewhere, never caught, which somehow makes it worse. Alex feels suspended in a state of
constant preparation for danger that may never come.
