# Marcus

Marcus sits in the therapy office with his shoulders drawn inward, fingers picking at a loose thread
on his jeans. At thirty, he carries himself with the careful deliberation of someone who has learned
to make himself smaller. He's a curriculum designer for an educational nonprofit, work he describes
as "meaningful," though his voice flattens when he says it—as though meaning itself has become
difficult to access.

Two years ago, Marcus was in a relationship with David. They had been together for three years, and
by most external measures, it looked good. They traveled together, attended gallery openings, talked
about a future. What Marcus didn't tell anyone was how the relationship had gradually shifted. The
criticism came first—subtle observations about his appearance, his career choices, the way he spent
time with friends. David was charming about it, framing everything as concern. Marcus found himself
second-guessing his own perceptions.

The physical violence began on a Thursday evening after Marcus mentioned attending a work conference
in another city. David was drinking. Marcus remembers the shock of it more than the pain—David's
hand across his face, the force of it unexpected, almost unreal. When Marcus tried to leave the
apartment, David blocked the door. The evening fractured into fragments in Marcus's memory: raised
voices, his own body pressed against the wall, David's hands around his wrists, the specific smell
of David's cologne mixed with alcohol.

Afterward, David cried. He apologized profusely, called it a one-time mistake, blamed stress and
alcohol. Marcus believed him because he needed to believe him. But it happened again six weeks
later, and then again. Each time followed a similar pattern—David's remorse, his promises, Marcus's
hope that things would change. When Marcus finally left, it took him three months to actually go.

Now, two years later, Marcus still startles at unexpected touches. A colleague placing a hand on his
shoulder during a meeting sends adrenaline through his system. He finds himself unable to focus
during curriculum meetings, his mind suddenly elsewhere, replaying conversations searching for signs
he missed. He's become hyperaware of people's tone of voice, interpreting neutral comments as
criticism or threat.

Dating feels impossible. He's tried twice since the breakup, but when things begin to feel intimate,
panic rises in his chest. His body remembers what his mind tries to minimize. He's downloaded dating
apps and deleted them. He tells himself he's simply not ready, which is true, but there's something
deeper—a fundamental distrust of his own judgment that he can't shake.

His work has suffered too. Marcus prides himself on his creativity and attention to detail, but
lately he finds himself staring at curriculum frameworks without absorbing them. He's missed
deadlines, something that never happened before. His supervisor has been kind about it, but Marcus
feels the weight of his own decline. He used to stay late, enthusiastically tweaking lesson plans.
Now he leaves at five o'clock, goes home to his quiet apartment, and scrolls through his phone until
sleep comes.

What troubles Marcus most is the anger that surfaces unexpectedly. He'll be designing a unit on
resilience for high school students, and something about the language will trigger fury so intense
it frightens him. He's snapped at friends who didn't deserve it. He's had moments of rage toward
David that feel disproportionate, even though he knows they're not. The anger coexists with
shame—shame that he stayed, shame that he didn't see it coming, shame that he still sometimes
wonders if he overreacted.

He doesn't think of himself as traumatized. That word feels too big, too dramatic for what happened.
But something is broken in how he moves through the world now, and he's not sure how to repair it.
