# James

James sits with his hands folded tightly in his lap, his weathered fingers—calloused from decades of
roofing work—pressing into his palms with enough force to turn the skin white. He's a broad-
shouldered man who carries himself with the kind of physical competence that comes from a lifetime
of working heights, but today he seems smaller somehow, folded in on himself.

The assault happened three years ago on a job site. James had stayed late to finish flashing around
a chimney on a residential renovation. A subcontractor he'd worked with before—someone he'd
considered friendly—came back to the property after hours. What James thought was a casual
conversation turned violent. The man forced himself on James in the half-finished attic space, and
James, shocked and confused in those first moments, didn't fight back the way he thought he would
have. Afterward, the man left as though nothing had happened, and James sat alone in the darkening
attic for an hour, unable to move.

He never reported it. He told himself it was because the shame felt too large to speak aloud,
because he worried people wouldn't believe him, because naming it seemed to make it more real. He
went home to his wife Margaret that night and said nothing. For weeks, he told her he was stressed
about a difficult project.

Now, three years later, James can barely work. Heights—the thing that once made him feel alive and
capable—have become unbearable. He'll climb onto a roof and his heart begins racing before his feet
leave the ladder. His vision narrows. His hands shake. He's had to turn down jobs, telling
contractors he's thinking about retiring, though he's only forty-eight and roofing is all he knows.
The loss of identity is almost as painful as the trauma itself.

He wakes most nights drenched in sweat, his mind replaying the assault in fragmented, sensory-heavy
flashes. The smell of dust and old wood. The weight of another body. The particular quality of the
afternoon light through the attic window. These details ambush him at random moments—sometimes when
he's in the truck driving to a job, sometimes when he's lying next to Margaret, who has stopped
asking what's wrong because his silence has become its own answer.

Margaret knows something is profoundly wrong. She's noticed that James has become hypervigilant in
ways that don't match his character. He checks locks twice. He positions himself with his back to
the wall in restaurants. He startles violently at unexpected sounds. When she touches him without
warning, he flinches. Their intimate life has essentially ended; the thought of being vulnerable
with anyone, even his wife of twenty-two years, fills him with a dread he can't articulate.

He's started drinking more than he should—a beer or two most evenings, sometimes more. It helps him
sleep, or at least it helps him stop thinking for a while. He avoids social situations, declined an
invitation to his brother's birthday dinner, and has become increasingly isolated. His few close
friendships have withered from neglect.

James knows something is broken in him. He knows the way he's living—suspended between the man he
was and someone he doesn't recognize—isn't sustainable. But the thought of speaking about what
happened, of admitting vulnerability, of being defined by what was done to him, feels like a second
violation. Still, he's here now, in this office, because Margaret gave him an ultimatum: get help or
she can't stay. He loves her. He's terrified of losing her. So he's here, sitting with his hands
clenched, trying to find the words for something he's spent three years trying not to feel.
