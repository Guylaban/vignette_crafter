# Clinical Vignette: Amara

Amara is a 42-year-old woman who works the morning shift at a textile manufacturing plant, a job
she's held for eight years. She moves through her days with a practiced efficiency—arriving early,
completing her tasks at the cutting station, leaving promptly at three o'clock. Her coworkers know
her as quiet and reliable, though few know much about her life before she came to the factory.

Twenty-three years ago, Amara lived in a different country, in a city that descended into chaos
during political unrest. She was nineteen then, a university student studying literature, full of
ideas about social justice and the future. When she attended a student protest, she believed she was
exercising her right to speak. She was arrested that evening and taken to a detention facility.

What happened in that facility over the next six months remains difficult for Amara to articulate,
even now. She describes it in fragments—the sound of a door closing, the smell of the space, the
cold of the floor beneath her. She describes hands, voices, pain that seemed to have no end. The
interrogations blended together. The isolation was absolute. She learned quickly that her body was
not her own, that her voice would not be heard, that resistance only brought more suffering.

She was eventually released through an international intervention, and after years of displacement,
she managed to immigrate to build a new life. But the past did not stay behind.

Now, Amara lives alone in a small apartment. Her ex-husband left five years ago, unable to navigate
the terrain of her nightmares and emotional distance. She hasn't spoken to her family in her home
country in over a decade—the guilt of survival mixing with the pain of disconnection.

At the factory, Amara is triggered by specific sounds: the high-pitched alarm that signals shift
changes sends her heart racing, and she has to grip the edge of her workstation to steady herself.
The fluorescent lights and the confined space of the break room make her feel trapped, and she
prefers to eat alone in her car. When a new supervisor last month spoke sharply to a coworker, Amara
found herself unable to return to work for three days. The authority in his voice transported her
somewhere else entirely.

At night, she rarely sleeps more than two or three hours. Her mind replays scenes she cannot fully
remember—fragmented images of faces, the sensation of restraint, a voice giving orders. She wakes
gasping, her body drenched in sweat, her apartment suddenly feeling unsafe despite the locked doors
and windows. Some nights she simply sits in her kitchen with the lights on until dawn, drinking tea
and waiting for daylight to feel real again.

Amara has developed a careful system for managing her life. She avoids crowds, maintains minimal
social contact, and has structured her routines to minimize unpredictability. She drinks more
alcohol than she acknowledges, using it as a tool to quiet her mind enough to sleep. She experiences
persistent numbness—unable to feel joy, even in moments that should bring it. When her coworker
brought in photos of her newborn grandchild, Amara felt only a distant echo of what she imagined she
should feel.

She carries constant vigilance, scanning environments for threats, noting exits, assessing people's
intentions. Her body remains in a state of high alert, her nervous system never fully convinced of
safety. She startles easily and often feels irritable without understanding why.

Amara came to therapy because her supervisor suggested it—a gentle recommendation after she had a
panic response on the factory floor. She is uncertain whether talking about the past will help or
simply make it more real. She wants to believe that healing is possible, but mostly she wants the
trembling to stop and to sleep through one full night.
