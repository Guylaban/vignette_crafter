# Maya's Story

Maya sits in the waiting room of her therapist's office, her leg bouncing rhythmically against the
floor. At twenty-five, she carries herself with the careful composure of someone who has learned to
present as fine when she is anything but. She works as a counselor at a community mental health
center, helping others navigate their struggles—a job she once loved, though it feels increasingly
hollow now.

The trauma began eight months ago on a Tuesday evening. Maya had been driving home from work, tired
but satisfied with her day. She was pulled over for a broken taillight in a neighborhood she knew
well. What should have been a five-minute interaction became something else entirely. The officer
approached her window with unusual aggression, and when Maya—nervous, speaking quickly as she always
does when anxious—reached for her glove compartment to retrieve her registration, he misread the
movement. He yanked open her door, pulled her from the vehicle, and threw her against the hood. The
metal was warm from the engine. She remembers the shock more than the pain, remembers calling out
that she was complying, remembers his hands on her arms leaving bruises that took weeks to fade.

It was over in minutes. No arrest. No charges. Just a warning about the taillight and a shattered
sense of safety.

Now, Maya finds herself hypervigilant in ways she cannot control. When she drives, her hands grip
the steering wheel so tightly her knuckles blanch white. At red lights, her eyes dart constantly to
her mirrors, tracking every vehicle behind her. She has started taking longer routes home to avoid
the intersection where it happened, adding twenty minutes to her commute. Some nights she doesn't
drive at all, calling rideshares instead, though the thought of being trapped in a car with a
stranger—even a driver—makes her chest tighten.

At work, Maya notices the change most acutely. She sits across from clients who share their own
experiences of trauma, and she finds herself struggling to stay present. Her mind fragments.
Sometimes a client will mention police, and Maya's body floods with adrenaline—her heart races, her
palms sweat, and she has to excuse herself to the bathroom to collect herself. She has always been
good at compartmentalizing, at being the calm, steady presence her clients need. But lately, she
feels like a fraud. How can she help others heal when she cannot sleep through the night?

The insomnia is relentless. Maya lies awake replaying the interaction obsessively, imagining
different outcomes, torturing herself with what-ifs. What if she had kept her hands visible? What if
she had spoken more softly? What if she had simply accepted the ticket without reaching for
anything? She knows intellectually that none of this was her fault, yet her nervous system refuses
to believe it. Around 3 a.m., when sleep feels impossible, she scrolls through her phone, reading
news stories about police violence, each one tightening the knot in her chest.

Her family has noticed. Her mother calls frequently, and Maya can hear the worry beneath her careful
questions. Her father, who immigrated to this country decades ago and has always taught his daughter
to respect authority, seems confused by her fear. How can she explain that authority itself has
become a trigger? That seeing a police car now sends her into a state of panic she cannot always
manage?

Maya knows she needs help. She has been trained in trauma-informed care, has read the literature,
understands the neurobiology of what is happening to her. But knowing and experiencing are different
things. She is here because the weight of carrying this alone has become unbearable.
