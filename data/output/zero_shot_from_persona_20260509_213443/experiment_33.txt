# Maria's Story

Maria sits in the office with her hands folded in her lap, her posture rigid despite the comfortable
chair. At thirty-nine, she carries herself with the precision of someone accustomed to discipline
and control. She's been a soldier for nearly two decades—a decorated staff sergeant who has served
in multiple deployments and earned commendations for her leadership. But it's the disaster that
happened eighteen months ago that has brought her here, to a civilian therapist's office, where she
struggles to find the words.

The hurricane came without warning to the base where Maria was stationed. She had weathered storms
before, but this one was different. She remembers the moment the roof of the barracks began to fail,
the sound like nothing she'd heard in combat. While others panicked, Maria's training kicked in. She
organized evacuations, counted heads, made decisions. She was doing what she did best—leading,
protecting, commanding. But then came the moment she couldn't control: the wall of water that swept
through the lower levels where some of the newer recruits were sheltering. She had told them to move
to higher ground, but the evacuation routes flooded faster than anyone anticipated. Three of them
didn't make it out.

Maria's voice becomes barely audible when she speaks about this. She tells her therapist that she
replays those moments constantly—not in the way people casually remember things, but in vivid,
intrusive flashes that ambush her without warning. Last week, she was reviewing supply manifests at
her desk when the sound of heavy rain triggered a complete sensory re-experiencing. She found
herself back in that moment, her heart racing, her palms sweating, convinced that the water was
rising around her again. It took her twenty minutes to reorient herself to the present.

Sleep has become nearly impossible. Maria lies awake beside her husband, Daniel, cataloging
everything she could have done differently. She's hypervigilant to weather reports—checking them
obsessively, sometimes three or four times an hour. When clouds darken, she feels a surge of dread
that she rationally knows is disproportionate. Her body doesn't listen to reason. Her muscles tense,
her breathing becomes shallow, and she's consumed by an urgent need to move, to prepare, to prevent
the disaster from happening again.

What troubles Maria most is how she's withdrawn from the things that once sustained her. She took a
desk position after the disaster—unable to face returning to active duty in the field. Daniel tries
to encourage her to do things she used to enjoy, but she finds no pleasure in them anymore. Dinner
with friends feels exhausting. Her extended family's gatherings, once something she looked forward
to, now feel overwhelming and impossible to navigate. She feels fundamentally disconnected from the
people around her, as though they exist on one side of an invisible barrier and she exists alone on
the other.

At work, Maria functions. She completes her tasks with the same competence she's always brought to
her role. But colleagues have noticed she's different—quieter, more withdrawn, less likely to
participate in the informal camaraderie of the office. She blames herself for the three soldiers who
died, though the investigation concluded that the evacuation procedures were overwhelmed by
circumstances beyond anyone's control. Intellectually, she understands this. Emotionally, she cannot
release the weight of it.

She tells her therapist that she feels like she's failing—at being a soldier, at being a wife, at
being herself. The guilt is suffocating, and the hypervigilance is exhausting. She came to therapy
because Daniel insisted, because she recognized that something fundamental has shifted in how she
moves through the world, and because she no longer trusts herself to manage this alone.
