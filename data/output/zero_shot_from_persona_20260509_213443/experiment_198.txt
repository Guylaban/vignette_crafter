# Marcus

Marcus is a seventy-three-year-old retired journalist who spent forty-five years covering stories
that mattered. He's the kind of man who built his identity around bearing witness—someone who
believed that being present at the worst moments meant something, that documentation itself was a
form of justice. He sits across from me with the posture of someone accustomed to observation rather
than being observed, his notebook-thin frame wrapped in a cardigan despite the warm afternoon.

In 2019, at sixty-eight, Marcus was sent to cover a chemical plant explosion in a neighboring state.
He'd covered industrial accidents before, natural disasters, even a factory fire in the eighties
that had earned him recognition. This assignment seemed routine to him—another story, another chance
to tell the truth. He arrived within hours, while the smoke still hung thick over the facility and
emergency crews were still pulling bodies from the rubble.

What happened next remains fractured in his memory. Marcus describes the moment a secondary
explosion occurred while he was interviewing a paramedic—a violent, unexpected eruption that threw
him backward against a concrete barrier. He lost consciousness briefly. When he came to, there was
blood on his face and the sound of sirens had become unbearable. He was evacuated with a concussion
and several cracked ribs. The physical injuries healed within months.

The other injuries haven't.

Marcus stopped working a year later. He tells me he couldn't focus on stories anymore, couldn't sit
with sources without feeling his chest tighten. He'd been divorced for twenty years by then, his
marriage a casualty of his career's demands. He had no children. What he had was his work, and when
that became impossible, he had very little else.

Now, four years later, Marcus doesn't sleep through the night. He lies awake around three in the
morning, his mind replaying the explosion—not just the sound but the particular smell of it, the
chemical burn in his nostrils. He's developed a habit of checking windows and doors multiple times
before bed, something he's aware is irrational but can't seem to stop. When he hears loud noises—a
car backfiring, construction work—his body responds before his mind catches up. His heart races. His
hands shake.

He's become hypervigilant in ways that confuse him. He avoids crowds now, which is strange for a man
who once thrived in the chaos of breaking news. He stopped attending his weekly coffee meetup with
other retired journalists because the coffee shop is near a construction site. The unpredictable
sounds make him feel trapped. He's started drinking more—a glass of whiskey most nights, sometimes
two.

Marcus has become isolated. He tells me he feels like a ghost in his own apartment, going through
days that blur together. He reads the news obsessively, searching for stories about industrial
safety, chemical regulations, disaster preparedness. It's as though he's trying to understand what
happened through research, as though knowledge might somehow undo the explosion or make it make
sense.

What troubles him most is the sense that he's become a different person—someone diminished, someone
who can no longer do the thing that made him feel alive. He looks at me with tired eyes and says he
doesn't recognize himself anymore, that the man who went to that plant site never really came back.
