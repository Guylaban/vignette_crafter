# Marcus

Marcus is a fifty-year-old plumber who has spent the last three decades building a reputation for
reliability and craftsmanship in his community. He and his wife, Patricia, have been married for
twenty-two years and live in the same neighborhood where he grew up. He takes pride in his work—the
kind of pride that comes from doing something well and having people trust you to do it. Until
eighteen months ago, his life followed a predictable rhythm: early mornings, honest labor, coming
home to Patricia, weekends spent on small projects around the house.

The assault happened on a Tuesday evening in February. Marcus had been called to an emergency job at
a commercial building downtown—a burst pipe in the basement that needed immediate attention. He
arrived around seven o'clock, when the building was mostly empty. A man confronted him in the
stairwell, demanded his wallet and tools. Marcus, who has never been one to back down, refused. What
followed was violent and sudden. The man beat him with a crowbar, fracturing two ribs and leaving
him with a concussion and deep lacerations across his back and shoulders. Marcus spent three nights
in the hospital.

The physical injuries healed within weeks. The rest did not.

Now, eighteen months later, Marcus moves through his days like someone perpetually braced for
impact. Patricia notices that he no longer goes to jobs alone—he insists on bringing his apprentice,
even when it's inefficient or costs him money. He checks and rechecks the locks on their doors
multiple times each evening, sometimes getting out of bed at two in the morning to verify they're
secure. Patricia has learned not to touch him unexpectedly; the startle response is violent and
frightening for them both.

Work, which once grounded him, has become a minefield. He still takes plumbing jobs, but certain
situations trigger an almost unbearable anxiety. Basement work makes his chest tight and his
breathing shallow. Enclosed spaces feel suffocating. When he's in someone's home and they move
suddenly in his peripheral vision, his body floods with adrenaline—his hands shake, sweat beads on
his forehead, and he has to excuse himself to the bathroom to regain composure. He's turned down
jobs, something he never did before, and his income has suffered. More painful than the financial
impact is the erosion of his identity. He's always been the man people called when they needed
something fixed. Now he's the man who can't handle a basement job.

Sleep is fragmented and unreliable. He lies awake replaying the assault—the sound of the crowbar,
the smell of his own blood, the feeling of helplessness. When he does sleep, he dreams of being
trapped, unable to move or call out. He wakes gasping, sometimes finding himself on the floor,
uncertain how he got there.

What troubles Marcus most is the anger. It comes in waves, disproportionate and terrifying. Patricia
mentioned once that she was thinking of taking a different route home from work, and Marcus
erupted—not at her, but the rage was there, raw and consuming. He apologized immediately, but the
look on her face told him she was frightened. He frightens himself. This isn't who he is. He's
always been steady, dependable, calm. Now he feels like a stranger in his own skin, reactive and
volatile, trapped in a body that won't let him forget what happened on that Tuesday in February.

He sits in the therapy office with his shoulders slightly hunched, hands folded tightly in his lap,
waiting for someone to help him find his way back to himself.
