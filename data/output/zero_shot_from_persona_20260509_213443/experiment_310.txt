# Marcus

Marcus has worked retail for fourteen years, moving from stock associate to assistant manager at a
mid-sized electronics store. He takes pride in knowing his inventory, training new employees, and
maintaining the store's systems. His wife, Catherine, says he's always been steady—the kind of
person who shows up, does the work, doesn't complain. They've been married for eight years, no
children yet, though they'd talked about it before last winter.

On a February afternoon, a gas line ruptured in the shopping center. Marcus was in the back office
reviewing inventory when the explosion happened. The force threw him against a filing cabinet. His
ears rang. When he could move, he crawled toward the sales floor through smoke so thick he could
barely see. Two of his employees were already outside; he found the third, Derek, collapsed near the
registers. Marcus dragged him out. Derek survived. So did Marcus.

But something fractured that day that Marcus still can't quite name.

For weeks afterward, Marcus couldn't sleep without the television on. Catherine would find him at 3
a.m. sitting in the kitchen with all the lights blazing, unable to explain why darkness felt
dangerous. When he did sleep, he'd wake gasping, convinced he was still in that smoke. He'd shower
at odd hours—sometimes four times a day—as if he could wash something off that wouldn't come clean.

He returned to work after two weeks. The store had reopened in a temporary location. Marcus thought
returning would help, that normalcy would settle him. Instead, he found himself unable to
concentrate on the simplest tasks. He'd stand at the register and suddenly forget what he was doing
mid-transaction. His hands would shake. He'd snap at employees over minor mistakes, then feel
ashamed. One evening, a customer dropped a heavy box near the stockroom, and Marcus found himself on
the floor, heart racing, before he even realized what had startled him.

Catherine noticed he'd withdrawn. He'd come home and sit in silence, staring at his phone without
looking at it. She'd ask about his day, and he'd give one-word answers. When she tried to touch
him—to hold his hand or rest her head on his shoulder—he'd tense. He couldn't explain why her
closeness felt suffocating. Sex became impossible; his body simply wouldn't respond. Catherine
eventually stopped trying, and that hurt him more than the rejection, because it meant he was
failing her too.

The hypervigilance exhausts him most. Loud noises send him into a state of alert that takes hours to
subside. He notices every exit when he enters a room. He monitors the smell of the air. At the
store, he finds himself checking the building's structural integrity, tracing where the gas lines
might run, calculating escape routes. His manager has suggested he might need to take more time off,
but Marcus resists. He feels like if he leaves, he's admitting something is truly wrong with him.

What troubles Marcus most is the sense that he's watching his own life from outside it. He goes
through the motions—manages the store, drives home, sits with Catherine—but he feels disconnected,
as though there's glass between him and everything he used to care about. He remembers loving his
job, enjoying the problem-solving of retail management. Now it all feels hollow.

He hasn't told anyone the full weight of what he carries. Catherine knows he's struggling. His
manager knows he's not himself. But Marcus hasn't found the words to articulate how fundamentally
the world has changed, how unsafe everything feels, or how he's become a stranger to himself.
