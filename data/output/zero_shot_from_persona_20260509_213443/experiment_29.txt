# Marcus

Marcus is nineteen years old, lean and restless, with the kind of dark circles under his eyes that
speak to nights spent awake rather than asleep. He works the morning shift at a McDonald's in a
strip mall outside the city, a job he's held for just over a year. He's good at what he
does—efficient at the register, reliable about showing up—but lately his manager has noticed he's
become jumpy around the kitchen, particularly when the exhaust hood kicks on with its sudden roar.

Eight months ago, Marcus was visiting his grandmother in a small town in the Midwest when a tornado
touched down without much warning. He'd been helping her rake leaves in her backyard, something he
did every fall, when the sky turned a color he'd never seen before—a sickly greenish-gray that
seemed to swallow the light. His grandmother had called him inside, and they'd made it to the
basement just as the sound began. Marcus describes it as if the world was being torn apart, a noise
so loud and so total that it obliterated everything else. The house shook. Debris crashed through
the walls upstairs. It lasted maybe two minutes, though Marcus says it felt like hours.

When it was over, their house was destroyed. His grandmother was physically unharmed, but Marcus had
a gash on his shoulder from flying glass. They were lucky—three people died two blocks away. Marcus
helped with the cleanup, helped his grandmother file insurance claims, and drove back to his
apartment in the city two weeks later. He thought he was fine. He told himself he was fine.

He wasn't fine.

Now, when unexpected loud noises happen—a car backfiring, a door slamming, even the ice machine
cycling on at work—Marcus's body floods with adrenaline. His heart races. His hands shake. For
several seconds, he's back in that basement, waiting for the walls to collapse. He's called in sick
to work three times in the past month after having what he calls "episodes" in the morning before
his shift. He lies in bed and can't seem to move, his chest tight, convinced that something terrible
is about to happen.

Sleep has become nearly impossible. Marcus lies awake most nights, his mind cycling through what-
ifs. What if they hadn't made it to the basement in time? What if his grandmother had been outside?
He finds himself checking weather apps obsessively, watching the radar for any hint of a storm. When
clouds roll in, he feels a creeping dread that builds throughout the day. He's started avoiding his
grandmother's calls because talking to her reminds him of what happened, and he feels guilty about
that avoidance, which only makes the anxiety worse.

At work, Marcus has become hypervigilant about the building's structural integrity. He's noticed
cracks in the ceiling tiles that probably aren't new. He's thought about where he'd go if something
happened during his shift. He's considered quitting, but he needs the money for rent, and part of
him knows that running away won't help.

What troubles him most is the feeling that the world has become fundamentally unsafe. Before the
tornado, Marcus moved through his days without much thought. Now, he feels like he's constantly
bracing for impact, waiting for the next disaster that he's convinced is coming.
