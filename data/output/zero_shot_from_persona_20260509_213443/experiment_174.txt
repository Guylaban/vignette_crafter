# Margaret's Story

Margaret sits in the office with her hands folded tightly in her lap, her posture rigid despite the
comfortable chair. At sixty-six, she carries herself with the precision she's brought to network
systems for forty years—everything in its place, everything controlled. But control has become
difficult lately.

Three years ago, Margaret was assaulted in the parking garage beneath her office building. She'd
stayed late to troubleshoot a server issue, something she'd done hundreds of times before. The
garage was nearly empty when she walked to her car. A man grabbed her from behind, dragged her
between two vehicles, and attacked her. A security guard's arrival interrupted what might have been
worse, but Margaret was left with broken ribs, lacerations, and something she couldn't quite name—a
fracture in how she understood the world.

She describes that night methodically, as if recounting a network topology. But her voice flattens
when she reaches the moment of impact. Her hands, usually expressive, go still. She doesn't cry.
Margaret rarely cries anymore.

The weeks after the assault were a blur of police statements and medical appointments. She returned
to work after six weeks, determined to resume her routines. She'd always been someone who solved
problems, who built systems that protected data and kept operations running. Surely she could
engineer her way through this too. But something had shifted in the architecture of her mind.

Now, three years later, Margaret describes her life in terms of avoidance. She no longer uses the
parking garage—she parks on the street a block away, even in rain, even when her knees ache from the
walk. She's stopped attending the industry conferences she once loved, the ones where she'd
reconnect with colleagues and stay current with new technologies. Crowds feel dangerous now. Open
spaces behind her feel dangerous. She's taken an early retirement from the firm where she'd built
her reputation, moving to a remote consulting role that allows her to work from home.

At night, she lies awake replaying the assault, though the details shift and morph. Sometimes the
man's face is clear; other times it's a blur that somehow feels more threatening. She jolts awake
several times a week, her heart racing, her body drenched in sweat. Her late husband, David, would
have held her through these nights. They were married for thirty-eight years before he died of
cancer four years ago—just one year before the assault. She was still learning how to be alone when
the attack happened.

Margaret describes hypervigilance as a constant background hum. When she's out, she monitors exits,
watches for movement in peripheral vision, positions herself with her back to walls. At home, she's
installed additional locks and cameras. She checks them compulsively, sometimes five or six times
before bed. Her neighbors probably think she's paranoid. She thinks she's realistic.

What troubles her most is the numbness. She doesn't feel joy anymore, not really. She goes through
the motions with her two adult children when they call, asking about their lives, but the
conversations feel scripted. She used to lose herself in complex engineering problems, in the
elegant logic of systems design. Now work feels hollow—she completes tasks competently but without
the satisfaction that once sustained her.

She's aware that something fundamental has broken. The woman who built networks that connected
thousands of people now isolates herself from most of them. The woman who trusted her instincts and
her ability to navigate the world now questions everything—every decision, every moment, every dark
corner.

Margaret wants to feel like herself again. She's just not sure that person still exists.
