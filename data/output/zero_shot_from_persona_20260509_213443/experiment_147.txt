# Marcus

Marcus sits with his hands folded tightly in his lap, the kind of stillness that suggests he's
practiced holding himself together. At forty-one, he has the weathered look of someone who has spent
decades outdoors—deep lines around his eyes, sun-darkened skin—but there's something else there too,
a tension that doesn't quite belong to someone whose profession is the quiet management of natural
spaces.

He's been a park ranger for nineteen years, a job he loves with the kind of devotion that comes from
genuinely believing in its purpose. He knows every trail in the state park he oversees, can identify
birds by their calls, and takes pride in the way he educates visitors about conservation. His
partner of seven years, Deepak, has always admired this about him—the steadiness, the competence,
the way Marcus seems most at peace when he's alone in the forest with his radio and his
responsibilities.

Three years ago, that changed. Marcus was doing a routine solo patrol in a remote section of the
park when he encountered two men illegally harvesting timber. What should have been a
straightforward confrontation escalated quickly. They had weapons. They had decided, apparently in
that moment, that Marcus was a problem that needed solving. They bound his hands and feet, locked
him in a maintenance shed on park grounds, and left him there while they continued their work.

He was there for eighteen hours.

Marcus doesn't talk much about those hours, not yet. What he does talk about is what came after—the
way his body seized up when search and rescue finally found him, the way he threw up for three days
straight, the way he couldn't sleep without the lights on for months. Deepak had been patient at
first, but patience wears thin when your partner won't leave the house, when he insists on checking
all the locks five times before bed, when he flinches at unexpected sounds and sometimes doesn't
seem to recognize you for a moment when you come around a corner.

The job became impossible almost immediately. Marcus tried returning to work after his medical
leave, but the forest—his forest, his sanctuary—had transformed into something untrustworthy. He'd
be on a routine patrol and suddenly his heart would be racing, his hands shaking, convinced that
something terrible was about to happen. He couldn't be alone without panic crawling up his spine. He
couldn't trust that other people in the park weren't a threat. Within six months, he took early
retirement on medical grounds, a decision that still seems to haunt him.

Now he spends most days at home. He's become hypervigilant about home security, installing cameras
and motion sensors that Deepak finds excessive but has learned not to comment on. When Deepak comes
home from work, Marcus is often anxious until he's physically in the house and accounted for. He has
trouble concentrating, trouble sleeping even with medication, trouble believing that he's actually
safe despite all logical evidence to the contrary.

What troubles Marcus most, though, is the guilt. He feels responsible for what happened, as if he
should have handled it differently, seen it coming, prevented it somehow. He also grieves the person
he was before—the confident ranger who belonged in the wilderness, who had a clear sense of purpose.
That person feels very far away now, replaced by someone he barely recognizes, someone afraid in a
way that doesn't seem to have an off switch.
