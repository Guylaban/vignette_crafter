# Sister María's Vigil

Sister María sits in the small chapel of the convent where she has lived for the past eighteen
years, her hands folded in her lap. The afternoon light filters through the stained glass, casting
colored shadows across the stone floor. She is fifty-four now, though some days she feels much
older. She has been a nun for over two decades, having entered religious life after her divorce at
thirty-six—seeking, she once told a spiritual director, the kind of peace that comes from certainty
and purpose.

She found that peace for a long time. Her work as a community health educator took her into the
poorest neighborhoods of the city, where she taught women about maternal health and nutrition. She
loved this work with a fierceness that surprised even her. The women called her "Hermana María," and
she knew their names, their children's names, their struggles and their small victories.

On March 14th, three years ago, she was at the community center where she held weekly clinics. It
was a Tuesday afternoon. She remembers the smell of the coffee someone had brought, the sound of a
baby crying in the waiting area, the way sunlight was coming through the windows at that particular
angle that made her think of God. Then came the sound—not quite an explosion, but something that
seemed to shake the building from within. Then screaming. Then chaos that her mind still cannot
quite organize into a coherent sequence.

The bombing killed seven people that day. Sister María was not among them, though she sustained
injuries—a fractured arm, lacerations, a concussion. But the physical wounds healed relatively
quickly. The other injuries have proven more persistent.

Now, three years later, she still cannot enter the community center. She has tried several times.
Once she made it as far as the front steps before her chest tightened so severely she thought she
was having a heart attack. Her hands shook for hours afterward. She has been reassigned to
administrative work at the convent—scheduling, records, correspondence—tasks that keep her behind a
desk in a familiar, controlled space.

The nightmares come most nights. She wakes at 2 or 3 a.m., her heart racing, her body drenched in
sweat despite the cool chapel air. Often she cannot remember the specific content of the dreams,
only the feeling of being trapped, of needing to escape but being unable to move. She has taken to
sleeping with her light on, which the other sisters pretend not to notice.

During the day, she is hypervigilant in ways that exhaust her. Loud noises—a door slamming, a car
backfiring on the street outside—send her into a state of high alert that takes hours to settle. She
has stopped attending community meals because the dining hall feels too exposed, too unpredictable.
She eats alone in her room now, which she tells herself is fine, is perhaps even more contemplative.

What troubles her most is the numbness. Prayer, which once anchored her entire existence, now feels
hollow. She goes through the motions—the liturgy, the rosary, the daily office—but the sense of
connection she once felt, that intimate presence she experienced in prayer, has become distant and
unreliable. She worries sometimes that her faith itself has been fractured, and this worry keeps her
awake even on nights when the nightmares don't come.

She continues to show up each day, to do her work, to wear her habit. But something essential in her
has shifted, and she moves through her days now with a kind of careful, exhausted vigilance, as
though waiting for the ground to shake again.
