# Sarah

Sarah is a twenty-eight-year-old high school English teacher who carries herself with the careful
precision of someone used to being observed. She speaks clearly, choosing her words as though each
one might be scrutinized, and there's a practiced quality to her smile—the kind she uses with
students who need reassurance. But beneath this professional composure, there's a brittleness that
becomes apparent once she begins to talk about the past eighteen months.

It started small. A former student—someone from three years ago, when she first began teaching—began
leaving comments on her social media posts. They were complimentary at first, then increasingly
personal. He'd mention things she'd said in class, observations about her life that she'd never
shared publicly. Sarah initially dismissed it as the kind of boundary-crossing that sometimes
happens with adolescents who develop crushes on teachers. She blocked him.

But he created new accounts. Different names, same pattern. He'd message her through platforms she
didn't even use regularly. Then he showed up at the coffee shop near her apartment on a Saturday
morning—a place she'd mentioned once, in passing, to a colleague. He was sitting at a table facing
the door when she walked in. He smiled and waved. She left without ordering.

The fear didn't announce itself gradually. It arrived suddenly, like a door slamming shut in her
chest. Sarah began checking her locks multiple times before bed, a ritual that now takes fifteen
minutes because she has to verify each one at least three times. She varies her route to school,
never leaving at the same time twice. Her relationship with her boyfriend has become strained—he
doesn't fully understand why she needs him to walk her to her car after work, why she jumps when her
phone buzzes, why she's started teaching with her classroom door locked even during lessons.

At school, Sarah finds herself hypervigilant in ways that bleed into her teaching. She notices every
unfamiliar face in the hallway, every car that lingers in the parking lot. During her lessons, she
loses focus mid-sentence, her mind jumping to worst-case scenarios. She's started avoiding the staff
room because the windows face the parking lot, and she can't sit with her back to the door. Her
principal has noticed her distraction, the way she seems to be somewhere else even when she's
physically present. She's considered taking leave but fears that stepping away might mean losing
control of the situation somehow, as if her vigilance is the only thing keeping her safe.

The stalker was eventually identified and a restraining order was issued four months ago, but Sarah
hasn't been able to shake the feeling that he's still watching. She checks her mirrors obsessively
while driving. She's installed a security system in her apartment, and she reviews the footage each
evening, looking for anything out of place. Some nights she doesn't sleep at all, lying awake and
listening for sounds that never come.

What troubles her most is the way the fear has changed her. She used to love her job, loved the
creative energy of the classroom. Now teaching feels like performing while standing in a spotlight
she never asked for. She's aware that her hypervigilance is affecting her students—they sense her
tension—but she can't seem to dial it back. Even knowing that the restraining order is in place,
even knowing logically that she's taken every reasonable precaution, the fear remains, solid and
present, like something living inside her that won't leave.
