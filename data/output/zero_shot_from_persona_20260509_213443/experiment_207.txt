# Marcus

Marcus is a sturdy man in his late forties with calloused hands and the practical bearing of someone
who has spent thirty years fixing other people's problems. He sits in the therapist's office with
the same posture he uses when crouched beneath a sink—present but braced, ready to move if needed.

He met David fifteen years ago at a bar downtown. It was a different era of his life, one where he
felt permitted to explore parts of himself he'd kept hidden. David was charming, attentive, made him
feel seen in ways he hadn't experienced before. Within six months, they were living together in a
modest house on the north side. Marcus thought he'd finally found something real.

The first time David hit him, it was over a misplaced set of keys. Marcus had come home late from a
job site, exhausted, and couldn't immediately locate them. David's face transformed—something Marcus
had learned to recognize as the moment before impact. The punch came fast, and Marcus fell against
the kitchen counter. Afterward, David cried and apologized profusely, brought him ice, held him. It
felt almost like tenderness.

But the hitting became routine. A forgotten grocery item. A work call that ran late. The way Marcus
had laughed at something his brother said. Each incident followed the same arc: David's rage, the
violence, then remorse and reconciliation. Marcus found himself trying to prevent the triggers,
monitoring his own behavior obsessively. At work, he became withdrawn, snapping at apprentices,
making careless mistakes that weren't like him. His business partner eventually asked if everything
was okay. Marcus said he was just tired.

He stayed for seven more years. He told himself he loved David, that he could manage it, that
leaving would mean admitting failure. His mother had always said he was stubborn. Eventually, during
an argument about nothing Marcus can now quite remember, David's violence escalated. There was a
lamp, broken glass, and Marcus realized with sudden clarity that he might not survive another year.
He left that night with a duffel bag and his truck.

That was four years ago. He's been married to his current wife, Susan, for two years. She's kind and
stable, a nurse who works long shifts at the hospital. She loves him genuinely, and he loves her,
but something in him remains fractured.

Now, when he's under a sink in someone's basement, he sometimes freezes. A sound—a door closing
upstairs, footsteps—and his heart accelerates beyond reason. He has to consciously remind himself
where he is. At night, he wakes in a cold sweat, his body convinced David is in the bedroom. Susan
has learned not to touch him when he's sleeping. He startles violently, and there's always a moment
where he doesn't recognize her, where his fist is already clenched.

He doesn't talk much about what happened. Men don't, he thinks. Especially men who were supposed to
be the ones doing the protecting. He carries shame alongside the fear, a complicated weight that
makes him feel smaller than his frame suggests.

What brings him to therapy now is Susan's gentle persistence and his own recognition that he's
disappearing. He's drinking more than he should. He's stopped seeing his brother. Work feels like
moving through water. He describes all of this matter-of-factly, as though discussing a faulty pipe,
but his leg bounces continuously as he speaks, and his eyes keep returning to the door.
