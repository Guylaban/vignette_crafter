# A Teacher's Burden

Priya sits in the classroom after the last bell, grading papers at her desk while afternoon sunlight
filters through the windows. She's a seventh-grade English teacher at a middle school in the
suburbs, a position she's held for six years. Her classroom walls are decorated with student poetry
and motivational quotes—a space she once loved. Now it feels like a stage where she performs
normalcy each day.

Three years ago, during a field trip to a local nature preserve, everything changed. Priya was
supervising a group of students near a hiking trail. She was distracted—checking her phone for a
message from her husband—when she didn't notice that Marcus, a quiet boy in her class, had wandered
off the marked path. He'd slipped on loose rocks, falling down an embankment and fracturing his
tibia and fibula badly. The image of his twisted leg and the sound of his scream play in her mind
almost daily, often without warning.

The guilt has never fully lifted. Marcus recovered physically, though he limped for months. His
parents were kind, never blamed her explicitly, but Priya blamed herself relentlessly. She replayed
those moments obsessively: if she'd looked up two seconds earlier, if she hadn't checked her phone,
if she'd positioned herself differently. The "ifs" became a endless loop in her mind.

In the months following the incident, Priya noticed her body responding in ways she couldn't
control. Her heart would race unexpectedly during field trips or when she saw Marcus in the hallway.
She began avoiding outdoor activities with her classes altogether, citing curriculum changes. Her
principal didn't question it. Her colleagues assumed she'd simply become more cautious.

Sleep became difficult. Priya would lie awake beside her husband, Arjun, replaying the accident or
imagining worse outcomes—what if Marcus had hit his head, what if she hadn't heard him crying. When
she did sleep, she'd wake gasping from nightmares where the injuries were catastrophic. Arjun would
find her on the couch at three in the morning, scrolling through her phone, unable to return to bed.

The hypervigilance has become exhausting. During lessons, part of her attention is always monitoring
the room—checking that no student is in an unsafe position, that the furniture is arranged to
prevent accidents. She's reorganized her classroom three times to eliminate potential hazards. Her
body remains tense throughout the day, and by evening, she's depleted. She's lost fifteen pounds
without trying.

What troubles her most is the emotional numbness that's crept in alongside the anxiety. She loves
teaching, or she did, but now she feels detached from her students. She goes through the
motions—delivering lessons, providing feedback, attending staff meetings—but the joy is gone. Arjun
has noticed. He's gently suggested that maybe she should talk to someone, but Priya hasn't known how
to explain what's happening. How could she articulate that she's terrified of harming someone else,
that she questions her competence as a teacher, that she sometimes thinks her students would be
safer with someone else?

She's considered leaving education entirely. Her parents, who sacrificed much to help her become the
first in their family to earn a master's degree, would be devastated. Teaching was supposed to be
her calling. Instead, it's become a daily reminder of her failure to protect a child in her care.

Today, as she grades papers, she notices her hands trembling slightly. She sets down her pen and
stares at the stack of essays. Tomorrow, she'll return to the classroom and smile at her students.
But tonight, she'll lie awake again, waiting for sleep that may not come.
