# Layla

Layla sits in the consultation room with her hands folded precisely in her lap, her posture almost
architectural in its geometry—shoulders squared, spine aligned, as though she is constructing
herself into something immovable. She is eighteen, recently accepted into a competitive architecture
program, and she speaks about her work with genuine passion. Buildings, she explains, follow rules.
They have logic. They don't surprise you if you understand their structure.

Two years ago, when Layla was sixteen, she was walking home from her part-time job at an
architecture firm where she interned after school. She had spent the afternoon drafting floor plans,
thinking about spatial flow and how people move through space. On a busy street in her neighborhood,
she witnessed a car strike a pedestrian—a woman about her mother's age. The impact was violent and
immediate. Layla watched the woman's body crumple. She watched people gathering, heard the sirens,
watched the paramedics work. She stayed there, frozen, until a police officer gently moved her away.

She didn't know the woman. But she couldn't stop seeing it.

In the months that followed, Layla developed a system. She mapped out every route she could take—to
school, to the firm, to her family's home. She calculated which streets had the fewest cars, which
times had the least traffic. She created alternatives to her alternatives. Her family noticed she
was taking longer to get places, but she didn't explain. The routes had to be safe. They had to be
predictable. She needed to see everything coming.

By the time she turned seventeen, Layla had narrowed her world considerably. She stopped walking
anywhere unfamiliar. She avoided busy streets entirely, even if it meant adding forty minutes to her
commute. When she did venture out, she moved with hypervigilance—her eyes tracking every vehicle,
every movement in her peripheral vision. Sounds startled her intensely. A car backfiring once sent
her into the bathroom at school where she sat for twenty minutes trying to slow her breathing.

Her architecture internship became both refuge and torment. The office was controlled, enclosed,
predictable. But the drive there terrified her. She began asking her father to drive her, then
asking him to wait in the parking lot. She couldn't explain why. The rational part of her mind—the
part that understood structural integrity and load-bearing walls—knew this didn't make sense. A
parking lot wasn't dangerous. But her body disagreed.

Sleep became fragmented. She would lie awake, her mind replaying the moment in high definition, or
she would wake gasping from dreams where she couldn't move, where the car kept coming. Some nights
she didn't sleep at all. She'd sit at her desk instead, working on her designs, creating perfect
spaces where nothing unexpected could happen.

Now, in her first semester of university, Layla is struggling. She excels in her studio courses—her
professors praise her technical skill and her innovative spatial thinking. But she's missing classes
because the walk across campus feels impossible. She's declined social invitations because they
might involve unfamiliar routes or crowded streets. She's thought about dropping out, about finding
a way to study architecture remotely, where the world can be contained within a screen.

She describes all of this calmly, logically, as though narrating the specifications of a building.
But when she mentions the sound of the impact, her hands tighten. When she talks about the woman's
face, her voice becomes very small. She says she knows it's irrational, that she understands
probability, that statistically she's safer than most people. But understanding something
intellectually and feeling safe are, she's discovered, two entirely different structures that don't
align.
