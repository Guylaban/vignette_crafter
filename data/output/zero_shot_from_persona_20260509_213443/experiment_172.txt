# Rabbi David's Unfinished Journey

David is a sixty-three-year-old rabbi with silver hair and thoughtful eyes that seem to look through
people rather than at them. He has led his congregation for nearly thirty years, and his sermons are
known for their depth and humanity. Yet beneath his composed exterior lives a man still haunted by
events that occurred more than five decades ago.

David was born in Budapest in 1961, though he rarely speaks of his earliest years. When he was three
years old, his family fled Hungary during the revolution, joining a wave of refugees desperate to
escape Soviet tanks and political persecution. What David carries with him—what he has never fully
articulated until recently—are fragmented sensory memories of that flight: the cold, the darkness of
hiding places, his mother's trembling hands, the sounds of soldiers' boots on cobblestone streets.
He remembers being separated from his father for three days, a separation that was eventually
resolved but left an imprint that decades have not erased.

The family eventually settled in Toronto, and David's parents built a careful, quiet life. They
rarely discussed what they had left behind. David became an exceptional student, eventually
attending rabbinical school, where he found meaning in studying texts about suffering, redemption,
and the human capacity to endure. He married Ruth, a kind woman who has stood beside him through
their forty years together. They have two adult children and four grandchildren. By all external
measures, David has built a life of purpose and stability.

But something shifted about three years ago. David was preparing a sermon about refugees—a topic
increasingly present in the news—when he found himself unable to continue writing. His hands began
to shake. His breathing became shallow. That evening, he experienced what he would later learn to
call a panic attack, though he had no framework for understanding it at the time. Ruth found him in
his study, drenched in sweat, convinced something was medically wrong with him.

Since then, David's life has slowly constricted. He experiences intrusive images—not full memories,
but fragments: a child's shoe on pavement, a woman's face he cannot quite see, the sensation of
being crushed in a crowd. These images arrive without warning, sometimes triggered by news reports,
sometimes by nothing he can identify. When they come, his body responds as though the danger is
present: his heart races, his muscles tense, and he feels an overwhelming urge to escape, to run, to
protect those around him.

David has begun avoiding situations that might trigger these episodes. He no longer watches the
news. He declined to lead a community forum on immigration policy. He finds himself hypervigilant
during congregational events, positioning himself near exits, noting who is present, assessing
potential threats. Ruth has gently mentioned that he seems distant, that he startles easily, that he
sometimes wakes in the night with his fists clenched.

The irony is not lost on David. He is a man devoted to helping others find meaning in their
suffering, yet he cannot name or understand his own. He continues to lead services, to counsel
members of his congregation, to appear composed and present. But privately, he feels increasingly
exhausted by the effort of holding everything together. Some nights, lying beside Ruth in the
darkness, he wonders whether the past he thought he had transcended was simply waiting, patient and
persistent, for a moment when his defenses might lower enough to finally be felt.
