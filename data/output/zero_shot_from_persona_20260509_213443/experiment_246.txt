# Marcus

Marcus sits in the waiting room with his hands folded tightly in his lap, his right leg bouncing
with a rhythm that seems to have no off switch. At thirty-three, he looks older—there's a weariness
around his eyes that doesn't quite match his face. He's dressed in clothes that are impeccably
tailored, the seams perfectly aligned, the fabric hanging with the kind of precision that only comes
from someone who understands garment construction intimately. It's his work, his identity, the thing
he's done since he was a boy learning the craft from his father in Lagos.

Five years ago, Marcus was working in a busy tailor shop in Abuja, a space he'd built himself with
savings and hope. The shop sat on a busy street, its windows displaying the vibrant fabrics he
loved—rich indigos, bold patterns, the colors of home. He was there on an ordinary Tuesday morning,
hemming trousers for a wedding, when the blast came. He doesn't talk about the explosion itself
much, but his body remembers everything. The sound still finds him in quiet moments. The way the
light changed. The smell. The feeling of being thrown against the cutting table, his hands
instinctively reaching for fabric that was no longer there.

Three people died in the shop that day. Marcus survived with a fractured left arm and cuts across
his chest and shoulders. The physical injuries healed within months. The other injuries didn't.

Now, five years later, Marcus lives in a small flat in London with his sister. He hasn't worked as a
tailor since the explosion. The thought of it paralyzes him—not the physical act of sewing, but the
vulnerability of being in an enclosed space, of having his back to a door, of the sudden sounds that
come without warning. Last month, a car backfired outside his flat, and he found himself on the
kitchen floor, hands shaking, convinced something had happened. It took him twenty minutes to
convince himself it was just a car.

He avoids crowded places. He avoids shops. He avoids the news, which means he avoids knowing what's
happening in the world, which means he feels disconnected from everything. His sister tries to
include him in family gatherings, but he often makes excuses. When he does attend, he positions
himself near exits. He watches people's hands. He notices every door, every window, every potential
threat. His body is constantly scanning, constantly preparing for something terrible to happen
again.

Sleep is fragmented. He goes to bed around midnight and wakes at three, his heart racing from dreams
he can't quite remember but can certainly feel. The dreams are abstract—falling, drowning, being
trapped—but they leave him gasping. Some nights he doesn't try to sleep at all. He'll sit in his
kitchen with the lights on, scrolling through his phone, waiting for morning.

His sister suggested he see someone, and after months of resistance, he agreed. He's not sure what
he expects—he's not sure anything can undo what happened. But there's a part of him, a small part
that still remembers the man who loved his work, who took pride in making something beautiful with
his hands, that wonders if there might be a way back to that.
