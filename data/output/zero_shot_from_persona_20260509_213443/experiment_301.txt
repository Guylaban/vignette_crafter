# Case Vignette: Marcus

Marcus sits across from me with his shoulders drawn inward, his fingers working absently at the cuff
of his shirt. At thirty-four, he carries himself with the careful restraint of someone perpetually
braced for bad news. He is a publisher—a career that once brought him genuine satisfaction—but these
days, his voice flattens when he speaks about work, as though describing someone else's life
entirely.

His wife, Layla, was hit by a car fourteen months ago while crossing the street near their home. She
was struck by a driver running a red light. Marcus wasn't there. He was in his office downtown,
reviewing manuscripts, when his phone rang with a number he didn't recognize. The emergency room
doctor's voice on the other end remains the pivot point of his life: before and after, divided
cleanly into two distinct periods.

Layla survived. This fact should be a relief, and in the rational part of his mind, Marcus knows it
is. She spent three weeks in the hospital with a fractured femur, internal bleeding, and a traumatic
brain injury. She recovered. She walks now, though with a slight limp. She has returned to teaching
part-time. By any measure, they were fortunate.

But Marcus cannot live in that rational space anymore.

He describes the hospital visits with a flatness that seems almost mechanical—the smell of
antiseptic, the beeping monitors, the way Layla's face looked when she regained consciousness and
didn't recognize him for several long minutes. He remembers standing in the hallway while a
neurosurgeon explained the extent of her injuries, and he remembers feeling as though he were
watching the scene from outside his body, unable to fully inhabit the moment even as it was
happening.

What troubles him most now is the unpredictability of his own mind. He will be working at his desk,
reading a submission about a car accident, and suddenly his chest tightens. His vision narrows. He
finds himself unable to breathe properly, convinced that something terrible is about to happen.
These episodes come without warning—sometimes three or four times a week, sometimes stretching
across several days without incident, which somehow makes them worse because he never knows when the
next one will arrive.

He has become hypervigilant in ways that embarrass him. When Layla leaves for work, he texts her
periodically throughout the day. Not to check in naturally, but to verify she is alive. He tracks
her location on his phone. When she is late coming home, he catastrophizes immediately—imagining
accidents, injuries, scenarios in which she doesn't return. Layla has begun to resent these
behaviors, though she tries to hide it. He can see it in the tightness around her eyes when he asks
her to text him when she arrives at school.

His sleep has become fragmented. He lies awake for hours, his mind cycling through variations of the
accident—different outcomes, different severities, different ways it could have been worse. When he
does sleep, he dreams of the phone call, the doctor's voice, the moment of impact he never witnessed
but has constructed in meticulous, horrifying detail.

At work, his colleagues have noticed the change. He used to be decisive, engaged with the
manuscripts that crossed his desk. Now he struggles to concentrate. A submission that should take
two hours to review stretches across a week. He has missed deadlines. His boss has asked gently if
everything is all right at home.

Marcus doesn't know how to answer. Everything is all right at home. Layla is alive. She is
recovering. He should be grateful. Instead, he carries a constant, low-grade dread that the universe
has already collected its debt once and might demand payment again. He cannot shake the feeling that
safety is an illusion, that the ground beneath his feet is far more unstable than he ever realized.
