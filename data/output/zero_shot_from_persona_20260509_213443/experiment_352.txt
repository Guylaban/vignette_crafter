# Margaret's Persistent Watchfulness

Margaret sits in the therapy office with her hands folded precisely in her lap, a habit formed over
fifty years of professional auditing. At seventy-five, she maintains the meticulous posture and
careful speech patterns that once made her an exemplary financial reviewer. But her eyes betray
something else—a constant scanning of the room, a slight tension in her jaw that never quite
releases.

She was thirty-two when the civil conflict erupted in her homeland. She had been living in the
capital with her young family, working for a government ministry, conducting routine audits of
departmental accounts. The work was steady, predictable, the kind of precision that suited her
temperament. Then the fighting came suddenly, not to the outskirts first, but directly into the city
center where she worked.

Margaret remembers the sound most vividly—not the explosions themselves, but the silence that
followed them, the way the world seemed to hold its breath. She was in the office building when the
first shell hit nearby. The fluorescent lights swayed. Papers scattered everywhere, and she found
herself crawling under her desk, gripping a ledger as if the numbers on its pages might protect her.
Colleagues screamed. Someone's blood was on her blouse. She never learned whose.

For three days, she sheltered in that building with thirty other people, moving between the basement
and the ground floor as the fighting shifted direction. She rationed water from the bathroom taps.
She checked the office supplies obsessively—counting pens, organizing papers into neat
stacks—because the counting kept her mind from thinking about what might happen next. When rescue
finally came, she walked out into a city she no longer recognized.

Now, fifty-three years later, Margaret has built a life. She emigrated, married a kind man named
David, raised two children, maintained her career until retirement. She appears, to most observers,
to have moved forward entirely. But she has not.

The hypervigilance never left. Margaret cannot sit with her back to a window or a door. At
restaurants, she positions herself to face the entrance, which her husband has long since stopped
questioning. She startles at unexpected sounds—a car backfiring, a child's shout—her body moving
before her mind catches up. Last month, when David dropped a stack of plates while cooking, Margaret
found herself on the floor, her heart racing uncontrollably for twenty minutes afterward. She felt
ashamed, though David was patient.

Sleep remains fractured. She wakes most nights between two and four in the morning, her mind
suddenly alert, her body damp with sweat. She lies beside David, listening to him breathe, counting
his breaths the way she once counted office supplies. Sometimes she gets up and checks the locks on
all the doors and windows, though she locked them before bed. The checking provides only momentary
relief before the anxiety creeps back.

She experiences intrusive images without warning—a flash of the building's collapsed corner, the
face of a woman crying, the particular shade of dust in the air. These images interrupt her day
unexpectedly: while reading the newspaper, while gardening, while sitting with her grandchildren.
When they come, she feels momentarily displaced from the present, as though she is back in that
basement, vulnerable and waiting.

Margaret tells her therapist that she has been "managing fine" for decades. But lately, at seventy-
five, she feels the weight of it differently. She is tired in a way that sleep does not touch. She
wonders sometimes if this vigilance, this endless watching and waiting, is simply who she has
become—not a symptom, but a permanent part of her architecture.
