# Marcus

Marcus sits with his hands clasped tightly in his lap, his posture rigid despite the comfortable
chair. At fifty-six, he carries himself with the careful precision of someone who has spent a
lifetime controlling his body—a professional dancer whose career spanned three decades on stages
across Europe. Yet something fundamental has shifted in how he inhabits that body.

Eight months ago, during a contemporary piece at the National Theatre, Marcus performed a lift he
had executed thousands of times. His partner, Elena, a dancer he had worked with for five years, was
supposed to land on the stage after the aerial maneuver. Instead, Marcus's hands slipped. Elena fell
hard, her arm breaking in two places, her shoulder dislocating. The silence that followed—the
absence of the music continuing, the gasps from the audience, Elena's cry—lives in him still.

He remembers standing there, his arms still positioned as though holding her weight. The paramedics
arrived. He remembers Elena's face twisted in pain, and worse, the moment her eyes met his and he
saw her fear transform into something else—understanding that this was his fault. He had been her
partner. He was supposed to keep her safe.

The weeks that followed were a blur of hospital visits, investigation by the theatre's safety
committee, and Elena's long rehabilitation. She recovered well enough physically, though she hasn't
returned to dancing. Marcus learned this through mutual friends; Elena doesn't return his messages.
Her silence feels like a verdict.

Now, eight months later, Marcus struggles to sleep. When he does, he dreams of that moment—sometimes
Elena falls differently, sometimes he catches her but his arms shatter like glass, sometimes he's
watching from the audience unable to move. He wakes gasping, his heart racing, often finding himself
standing in the kitchen at three in the morning, unsure how he got there.

His wife, Catherine, has been patient, but he sees the exhaustion in her face. She reaches for him
at night and he flinches. Not because he doesn't want her touch, but because his body feels
dangerous, unreliable. The hands that once moved with such confidence now sometimes shake when he's
simply holding a coffee cup.

He tried returning to teaching—he'd begun mentoring younger dancers before the accident—but he
couldn't do it. Standing before his students, watching them move through combinations, he became
hypervigilant, scanning for mistakes, for moments where someone might fall. He found himself
gripping the barre so hard his knuckles went white. He quit after two weeks.

The worst part is the intrusive thoughts. He'll be doing something ordinary—washing dishes, reading
the newspaper—and suddenly he's back in that moment, feeling the slip of his hands, the terrible
weightlessness as he lost her. His body floods with adrenaline, his breathing becomes shallow.
Sometimes these episodes last minutes; sometimes they stretch into hours where he simply sits,
unable to do anything but wait for the panic to subside.

Marcus has started avoiding mirrors. He can't bear to look at his hands, the instruments of his
life's work, now the source of his deepest shame. Catherine suggested he might talk to someone, and
here he is, finally, because the weight of what he did—what his body did—feels too heavy to carry
alone anymore.
