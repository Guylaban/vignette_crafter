# Marcus

Marcus sits in the waiting room with his uniform still on, having come directly from his shift. At
eighteen, he's one of the youngest officers on the force, having joined the academy straight out of
high school. He carries himself with the stiffness of someone who's learned to keep everything
controlled, everything contained.

He grew up in a household where his father's temper was the weather system everyone learned to
predict. Marcus's mother would tense when she heard the car door slam. His younger sister would
retreat to her room. Marcus, even as a child, would position himself in the hallway between his
parents' bedroom and the rest of the house, as if his body could be a barrier. He remembers the
sounds more than the specific words—his father's voice rising like a storm, his mother's pleas
becoming smaller and smaller, the crash of something breaking. He was nine the first time he called
the police himself. He was eleven when he stopped trying to intervene physically.

By fifteen, Marcus had decided he would become a police officer. It made perfect sense to him then:
he would be the one with authority, the one who arrived when things fell apart, the one who could
stop it. The academy felt like purpose. His instructors praised his discipline, his focus, his
ability to remain calm under pressure.

Everything changed six months into his work on patrol.

He responded to a domestic violence call in a neighborhood not unlike the one where he grew up. The
address came through dispatch, and Marcus felt his chest tighten, but he pushed it
aside—professional distance, he told himself. When he arrived, a man was holding his wife against
the kitchen counter. The scene is still vivid to Marcus: the woman's face, the way her body had gone
rigid, the man's hand around her throat. It wasn't his parents, but it was exactly his parents. It
was every night he'd lain in bed listening through the walls.

Marcus doesn't talk much about what happened after that. He made the arrest. He did his job. But
something fractured inside him that he can't quite name.

Now, months later, Marcus is caught in a loop he doesn't know how to break. When he's off duty, he
finds himself replaying the call obsessively, wondering if he could have arrived faster, if there
was something more he should have done. He sleeps poorly, his mind alert even when his body is
exhausted. Loud noises make him flinch—a car backfiring, a door slamming, his roommate raising his
voice on a phone call. He finds himself scanning rooms automatically, noting exits and threats,
unable to simply exist in a space without assessing it.

Worse is the shame. He's supposed to be trained for this. He's supposed to handle violence without
it affecting him. Instead, he feels hypervigilant during his shifts, jumping at movements, his hand
drifting toward his belt too quickly. He's started requesting desk duty, telling his sergeant he's
considering a career change, but the real reason is that he can't trust himself in the field
anymore. He's terrified he'll overreact, that he'll become the thing he's trained to stop.

Marcus has started to wonder if choosing this job was about protecting others or protecting himself
from what he witnessed as a child. And he's beginning to understand that some doors, once opened,
can't be closed by simply walking through them in a uniform.
