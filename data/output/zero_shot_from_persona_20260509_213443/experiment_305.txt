# Sarah

Sarah sits carefully in the leather chair across from the therapist's desk, her posture precise and
controlled. At forty-three, she maintains the composed demeanor of someone accustomed to managing
details—a quality that has served her well during her twenty years as a pharmacist. She arranges her
hands in her lap, fingers interlaced, and begins to recount a history she has carried silently for
decades.

Her childhood unfolded in a household where her father's unpredictability shaped every interaction.
She describes him as a man whose moods could shift without warning, whose hands moved faster than
his words. The beatings began when she was seven, triggered by infractions both real and imagined—a
poor grade, a question asked at the wrong moment, the way she looked at him across the dinner table.
Her mother, caught in her own survival, would disappear into the kitchen or into silence. Sarah
learned early that stillness and obedience were her only tools for self-preservation.

By adolescence, she had developed an almost preternatural awareness of her father's breathing
patterns, the particular angle of his shoulders that preceded violence. She could read the room the
way a pharmacist reads a prescription—identifying the active ingredients of danger in small, precise
details. This hypervigilance, once a survival mechanism, has become her constant companion into
adulthood.

Sarah's career in pharmacy emerged partly from this need for control and order. The precise
measurements, the careful documentation, the responsibility of ensuring that medications are
correctly dispensed—these elements aligned with her psychological architecture. For years, her
colleagues knew her as meticulous, reliable, almost unnaturally calm under pressure. What they did
not see were the nights when her body would jolt awake at three in the morning, her heart racing,
convinced she had heard her father's footsteps. What they did not witness was how she would
sometimes freeze mid-conversation, her mind suddenly transported to a moment thirty-five years in
the past.

Her marriage to David, a kind and patient man, seemed to offer sanctuary. Yet even within this safe
relationship, the trauma persists in ways both obvious and subtle. When David raises his voice—even
in jest, even in passion—Sarah's body floods with adrenaline. She finds herself bracing, calculating
exits, preparing for impact that never comes. David has learned to move slowly around her, to
announce his presence when entering a room, to speak softly. He does this not with resentment but
with love, though Sarah carries the guilt of requiring these accommodations.

The intrusive memories have intensified in recent years. She will be counting pills at the pharmacy
counter when suddenly she is eight years old again, hearing her father's voice, feeling the weight
of his hand. These episodes last only seconds, but they leave her shaken, requiring her to excuse
herself to the stockroom where she sits among the carefully organized bottles, trying to ground
herself back to the present.

Sleep remains elusive. She avoids certain sounds, certain smells—the particular cologne her father
wore, the smell of alcohol on someone's breath. She has become hyperaware of her own body, sometimes
feeling disconnected from it, as though she is observing herself from a distance rather than
inhabiting her own skin. Loud noises cause her to startle violently. She avoids crowds, preferring
the controlled environment of the pharmacy where she can predict the day's rhythm.

Sarah has never spoken about the abuse to anyone until now. The shame and the silence have been her
faithful companions, as familiar as her own reflection. She presents these truths with clinical
precision, as though describing a medication's side effects, but occasionally her voice wavers—a
small crack in the facade that suggests the depth of what she carries.
