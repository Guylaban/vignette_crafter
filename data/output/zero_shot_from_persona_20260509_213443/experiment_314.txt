# Marcus

Marcus sits with his shoulders curved inward, his tailored navy suit somehow making him look smaller
rather than more professional. At eighteen, he has already built a reputation at the mid-sized
accounting firm where he works as a junior tax consultant—the kind of young man who arrives early,
stays late, and catches errors others miss. His colleagues describe him as quiet but brilliant,
someone who will likely make partner by thirty. What they don't know is that he hasn't slept through
a single night in four months.

It started in June, after a networking event at a hotel downtown. Marcus had been encouraged by his
supervisor to attend, to build relationships in the industry. He was excited—proud, even, that
someone his age was being taken seriously in professional spaces. He'd networked for two hours, made
several business cards, and was heading back to his car when a man he'd been talking to offered to
walk him to the parking garage. Marcus remembers the elevator. He remembers saying no, very clearly.
He remembers the shock of being stronger than he expected but not strong enough.

The weeks immediately after blur together. Marcus reported it to police, endured the examination,
answered questions that felt like accusations. He returned to work after ten days because staying
home felt worse—at least at the office, his mind could focus on tax codes and client portfolios. But
something fundamental had shifted. His body no longer felt like his own. In client meetings, he
finds himself tracking the other person's movements, noting exits, calculating distances. His leg
bounces constantly. His hands shake when he types.

Sleep has become a battlefield. He lies awake until three or four in the morning, his mind replaying
fragments he can't control—the smell of cologne, the weight of an arm across his chest, the
particular quality of darkness in the garage. When he does sleep, he wakes gasping, tangled in
sweat-soaked sheets. He's taken to sleeping in the office sometimes, on the couch in the break room,
because his apartment feels too isolated, too quiet, too full of corners where something might be
waiting.

The hypervigilance has begun affecting his work. Last week, a client's sudden movement made Marcus
flinch so violently he knocked over his coffee. He'd excused himself, cleaned it up, returned to the
meeting with an apology he didn't feel. His supervisor has started asking if everything is alright.
Marcus has become adept at saying yes while his hands remain clenched, while his jaw stays tight
enough to ache.

Food tastes like nothing. He's lost twelve pounds. His mother calls from Lagos, and he tells her
he's simply working hard, that the weight loss is from dedication to his career. It's easier than
the truth. He hasn't told anyone what happened—not his family, not his few close friends, not anyone
at work. There's a particular shame that sits in his chest, one he can't quite name or explain. He
wonders if people can see it on him, if it broadcasts somehow that he is no longer intact.

What keeps him going is routine. Tax codes are logical. Numbers don't lie or hurt. If he can just
keep working, keep performing competence, maybe he can outrun what happened. Maybe if he's
successful enough, productive enough, the night in the garage will matter less. Maybe he can become
someone it didn't happen to.

But each night, the garage finds him anyway.
