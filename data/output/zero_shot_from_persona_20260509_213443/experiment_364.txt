# Captain James Moana

James sits in the consulting room with his hands folded carefully in his lap, a habit born from
decades of controlled precision. At sixty-three, he carries himself with the upright posture of
someone who has spent forty years in cockpits, but there's a brittleness to it now, as if the
structure might crack if examined too closely.

He flew combat missions in 1982, during a brief but intense conflict in the South Atlantic. He
doesn't volunteer this information readily. When he does speak about it, his voice becomes measured
and distant, the same tone he once used to communicate with air traffic control during emergencies.
He was a young pilot then, twenty-three years old, sharp and confident. He flew reconnaissance
missions over hostile territory, sometimes twice in a single day. He watched another pilot's plane
take fire and spiral downward. He never learned what happened to that pilot—not then, not in the
forty years since.

For decades, James managed. He built a career with a major international airline, rose to captain,
flew transpacific routes with steady hands and perfect safety records. His wife, Patricia, says he
was always somewhat distant, but she attributed it to the demands of his profession. He was a good
provider. He was reliable. He came home between rotations and mowed the lawn. They had two children
who grew up largely without him, which seemed normal enough for an airline family.

Three years ago, James retired. Patricia had been looking forward to this—travel, time together,
grandchildren visiting. But retirement unraveled something in him. Without the structure of flight
schedules and pre-flight checks, without the narrow focus required to manage an aircraft, his mind
began to wander into places he'd kept locked away for forty years.

He started having nightmares almost immediately. Not every night, but often enough that he began
sleeping in the guest room so his thrashing wouldn't disturb Patricia. In these dreams, he's back in
the cockpit, but something is always wrong—the instruments won't respond, or the ground is rising up
too fast, or he realizes mid-flight that he's forgotten something critical. He wakes gasping, his
heart racing, convinced for several disorienting seconds that he's about to crash.

During the day, certain sounds trigger something sharp and automatic in his chest. The high-pitched
whine of a jet engine on television makes him flinch. Last month, when a car backfired in the
supermarket car park, he dropped the shopping bags and crouched behind a vehicle before he
understood what had happened. A young woman asked if he was all right. He couldn't explain.

Loud, unexpected noises have become a minefield he navigates constantly. He avoids crowded
places—airports are impossible now, which carries its own bitter irony. He's become hypervigilant in
ways he doesn't fully recognize in himself. When Patricia comes up behind him unexpectedly, he
startles violently. He finds himself scanning crowds for exits and threats. He's stopped going to
the local pub with his friends because the noise and unpredictability overwhelm him.

What troubles him most is the emotional numbness that has settled over everything else. Patricia
reaches for his hand, and he feels it physically but not emotionally. His grandchildren visit, and
he goes through the motions of engagement while feeling oddly removed, as if observing himself from
outside his body. He watches television but doesn't absorb what he's watching. Food tastes like
nothing.

He's started drinking more—a glass of whiskey most evenings, sometimes two. It helps him sleep, or
at least makes the nightmares less vivid. Patricia has noticed. She's suggested, carefully, that
perhaps he should talk to someone. The idea frightens him more than he's willing to admit. Talking
means opening doors he's spent four decades keeping sealed.
