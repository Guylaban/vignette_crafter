# Dr. Priya Mehta

Dr. Priya Mehta sits in the waiting room with her hands folded in her lap, her posture rigid despite
the comfortable chair. At eighty, she carries herself with the precision of someone who spent fifty
years working in millimeters—a dentist's discipline evident in how she holds her jaw, how she
positions her shoulders. Her silver hair is arranged carefully, her cardigan impeccable. She looks
composed, but her eyes scan the room in a way that suggests she has already identified the exits.

It was 2008 when everything changed. Priya was attending a dental conference in Mumbai, staying at
the Oberoi Hotel with colleagues. She had been looking forward to the trip—three days away from her
practice in Toronto, a chance to reconnect with her professional community. She remembers the
evening clearly: she was in her room on the nineteenth floor, reviewing a presentation she was
giving the next morning. The first explosion came around 9:30 p.m. The building shook. The lights
flickered. She thought it was an earthquake.

The second explosion came minutes later, closer. Then the screaming started.

She remembers the acrid smell of smoke seeping under her door. She remembers calling her ex-husband
in Canada, her voice steady even as her hands trembled, telling him she loved him and that she
didn't know what was happening. The line went dead. She spent the next fourteen hours barricaded in
her bathroom with the lights off, sitting on the tile floor, listening to sirens and gunfire and the
sounds of people dying in the hallway outside her room.

Sixteen years have passed since those three days of terror in November. Priya returned to Toronto.
She returned to her practice. She continued working, seeing patients five days a week until she
retired three years ago. But the hotel never truly released her.

Now, she cannot stay in hotels. A conference invitation, a family trip—she declines them all. When
she must travel, she drives, staying in her car if necessary, unable to tolerate being enclosed in a
room above the ground floor. She stopped flying to visit her sister in Vancouver. She stopped
attending dental conferences, despite the invitations, despite her reputation in the field.

Her sleep is fractured. Most nights, she lies awake until 2 or 3 a.m., her mind cataloging escape
routes from her bedroom. She has installed additional locks on her doors. She keeps her car keys on
her nightstand. When she does sleep, she jolts awake at loud noises—a siren, a car backfiring, the
neighbor's dog barking—her heart racing as if the explosions are happening now.

The worst part is the unpredictability of it. Some days are manageable. But then she will catch the
smell of something burning, or she will see a news report about a bombing somewhere in the world,
and the years collapse. She is back in that bathroom. Her hands shake. Her breathing becomes
shallow. She has to sit down, sometimes for hours, waiting for her nervous system to remember that
she is safe.

Her dental practice was her identity for so long. The precision, the control, the ability to help
people—it grounded her. But she cannot go back to that work. The thought of being trapped in a small
room with a patient, of not being able to leave quickly, creates a panic she cannot manage. She
retired early, telling colleagues it was time, that she had earned the rest.

What she doesn't tell people is that retirement has left her unmoored. Without the structure of her
practice, the days are longer. The memories have more space to occupy.
