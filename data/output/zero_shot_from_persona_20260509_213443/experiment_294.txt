# The Numbers Don't Add Up

Margaret sits in the waiting room with her hands folded precisely in her lap, her posture impeccable
despite the tremor that runs through her fingers. At sixty-three, she carries herself with the
disciplined bearing of someone who has spent forty years analyzing spreadsheets and quarterly
reports. Her suit is pressed, her hair arranged carefully, and yet there is something fragile
beneath this order—a brittleness that suggests the structure holding her together requires constant,
exhausting maintenance.

She came to the United States in 1989, fleeing Rwanda as violence began to fracture her country. She
was twenty-four then, a young woman with a degree in mathematics and a fiancé she would never see
again. She remembers the smell of smoke that morning, the sound of her mother's voice telling her to
run, the weight of a single suitcase containing everything she could carry. She does not often speak
about what she saw before she left, or what she learned had happened to those she loved after she
was gone.

Margaret built a new life through precision and control. She found work at a financial services
firm, where her gift for numbers and patterns was immediately recognized. She was methodical,
reliable, and she never missed a deadline. Over the decades, she moved up, became senior analyst,
managed teams, built a reputation for catching discrepancies that others missed. The work suited
her—there was comfort in the certainty of mathematics, in the way numbers could be balanced,
reconciled, made to make sense. The real world, by contrast, had never made sense, so she lived in
the world of data.

But in the past year, something has shifted. Margaret finds herself unable to concentrate on the
spreadsheets that once absorbed her completely. Numbers blur and rearrange themselves on her screen.
She catches herself staring at a column of figures for twenty minutes without comprehending a single
entry. Her manager has noticed. There have been conversations about performance, about whether she
might consider early retirement.

The insomnia started six months ago. She lies awake at three in the morning, her mind cycling
through catastrophes—not current ones, but old ones, the ones she survived. She thinks about her
apartment building, about the sound of machetes, about her sister's daughter whose birth
announcement she received in 1992 from a cousin in Belgium, a child she never met. Sometimes she
cannot remember whether these memories are things she witnessed or things she imagined during those
early days when the information was fragmented and terrible.

During the day, she is hypervigilant in ways she cannot quite explain. A car alarm on her street
sends her heart racing. She finds herself tracking exits in restaurants, noting which colleagues
seem irritable or withdrawn, interpreting neutral comments as veiled hostility. Last month, a
coworker raised his voice during a meeting about budget projections, and Margaret felt something
break open inside her—a sensation of falling, of danger materializing from nowhere. She excused
herself and spent forty minutes in the bathroom stall, breathing deliberately, trying to remember
where she was.

She has always prided herself on self-sufficiency. She has never married, never allowed herself the
vulnerability of depending on another person. She has friends, colleagues, but maintains careful
distance. Now, increasingly, she finds herself avoiding social situations altogether. She cancelled
her book club membership. She stopped attending church. The world feels unpredictable in a way it
did not feel when she was younger, when she was simply trying to survive. Now that she is supposed
to be safe, supposed to be settled, the terror seems to have finally caught up with her.

She sits with these contradictions—the woman who mastered financial analysis unable to master her
own mind, the survivor who thought she had already paid the price now discovering the bill is still
coming due.
