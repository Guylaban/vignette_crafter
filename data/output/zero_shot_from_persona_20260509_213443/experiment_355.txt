# Sarah's Story

Sarah is twenty-one years old, with an easy smile that doesn't quite reach her eyes anymore. She
works as a counselor at a youth center in the city, helping teenagers navigate school pressures and
family conflicts. Her colleagues describe her as thoughtful and patient with the kids, someone who
listens without judgment. What they don't see is how much effort it takes for her to sit still in
those sessions, how she positions herself near the door of her office, or how she sometimes loses
track of what a teenager is saying because she's listening for sounds in the hallway.

Three years ago, Sarah was serving as a soldier in a peacekeeping operation in West Africa. She was
twenty-three then—or felt like it, anyway. The Sarah who enlisted seems like a different person now.
She had wanted to serve her heritage country, to do something meaningful. The first months were
manageable. She was trained, disciplined, part of something larger than herself. But nothing truly
prepares you for the moment everything changes.

It happened during a routine patrol in a region that was supposed to be secure. Sarah was riding in
the second vehicle of a convoy when the first vehicle ahead detonated an improvised explosive
device. The blast was so loud it seemed to come from inside her own chest. She remembers the
smell—acrid and wrong. She remembers screaming, though she's not certain if it was her voice or
someone else's. She remembers running toward the burning vehicle, her training overriding her
instinct to flee, and finding her sergeant—a man she'd known for two years—burned beyond
recognition.

The evacuation, the hospital, the investigation—these blur together now. She was physically
unharmed, which somehow made everything worse. People kept telling her she was lucky. She didn't
feel lucky. She felt hollowed out, as though the blast had extracted something essential and left
only a shell that looked like Sarah.

She finished her deployment, completed her service, and came home because that's what soldiers do.
For a while, she thought she was fine. She enrolled in a counseling program, got her certification,
started working with young people. But fine was a performance, not a reality.

Now, nearly two years after returning home, the symptoms have become impossible to ignore. Sudden
noises send her heart racing—a car backfiring, a door slamming, the pop of a balloon at a birthday
party at the youth center. Her body reacts before her mind catches up, flooding with adrenaline and
the certainty that danger is imminent. She has trouble sleeping, not from insomnia exactly, but from
nightmares so vivid she wakes gasping, her sheets soaked with sweat. In the dreams, she's back in
that convoy, but the endings change. Sometimes she's the one burning. Sometimes she can't reach her
sergeant in time. Sometimes she watches her colleagues die, one after another, and she's powerless
to stop it.

During the day, she feels numb, detached from the world around her. When she's counseling a teenager
about their problems, she sometimes feels irritated by what seems like their minor concerns, then
immediately feels ashamed of that irritation. She's hypervigilant in ways she can't control—scanning
rooms for exits, noting who's behind her, tensing when someone approaches from her blind side. She's
withdrawn from friends, finding social gatherings exhausting and triggering. She drinks more than
she used to, mostly in the evenings when the memories are loudest.

Sarah knows something is wrong. She knows the person she is now is not the person she was before.
But she's trained to help others, not to ask for help herself, and that gap between knowing and
acting feels impossibly wide.
