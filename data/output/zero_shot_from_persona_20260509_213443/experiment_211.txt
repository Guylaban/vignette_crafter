# Sister Margaret

Sister Margaret sits in the small counseling room with her hands folded in her lap, a habit
cultivated over thirty-seven years of religious life. She is a woman of deliberate composure, her
gray-streaked hair pinned neatly beneath her veil, her voice measured and soft. Yet something in the
tightness around her eyes suggests a fracture beneath the surface.

Three years ago, her younger brother Michael was struck by a drunk driver while crossing the street
near his home. Margaret was not there. She learned about it through a phone call from the hospital—a
clinical voice informing her that Michael had suffered severe head trauma and was being rushed into
emergency surgery. She remembers standing in the convent kitchen, the phone pressed to her ear,
unable to move or speak. The other sisters eventually found her there, still holding the receiver.

Michael survived the initial surgery but remained in a coma for two weeks. Margaret was at his
bedside nearly every day, watching his chest rise and fall, watching machines beep and hiss, praying
with an intensity that frightened her. When he woke, he was not the brother she knew. The vibrant,
quick-witted man who had always made her laugh was now struggling to form sentences, his left side
partially paralyzed, his personality fundamentally altered by the injury.

Since then, Margaret has found herself unable to settle into the rhythms that once sustained her.
She leads morning prayers with the community, but her mind fractures into fragments—images of
Michael's hospital room, the sound of the ventilator, the smell of antiseptic. During the Eucharist,
she sometimes finds her hands shaking so badly she can barely hold the chalice. She has had to step
back from serving as the convent's spiritual director, a role she held for fifteen years.

At night, she lies awake in her small room, her body tense despite the quiet darkness. She rehearses
catastrophic scenarios: Michael having another stroke, falling and hitting his head again, being
alone without someone to help him. When she finally does sleep, she dreams of the accident
itself—though she never witnessed it, her mind has constructed vivid, horrifying versions. She wakes
gasping, her heart racing, uncertain for several seconds where she is.

Margaret has become hypervigilant about safety in ways that perplex her. She finds herself checking
on the other sisters compulsively, making sure they are not in danger. She has reorganized the
convent's kitchen three times to minimize accident risks. Driving to visit Michael has become
increasingly difficult; she grips the steering wheel tightly and feels a wave of panic whenever she
sees a car approaching from an intersection.

What troubles her most is a creeping sense of isolation. She feels she should be stronger, that her
faith should shield her from this kind of suffering. The other sisters are kind, but she senses
their discomfort with her distress. They seem to expect her to find solace in prayer, as she always
has. Instead, prayer has become another source of anxiety—she finds herself bargaining with God,
begging for guarantees that Michael will be safe, that no one else she loves will be taken from her.

She visits Michael every other week. He has made slow progress, but he will never fully recover.
During these visits, Margaret oscillates between deep sadness and an almost suffocating sense of
helplessness. She watches him struggle to speak, to move, and she is seized by a guilt she cannot
name—guilt that she was not there, guilt that she is healthy while he suffers, guilt that she
sometimes wishes the accident had never happened, even though she knows this wish is selfish and
wrong.
