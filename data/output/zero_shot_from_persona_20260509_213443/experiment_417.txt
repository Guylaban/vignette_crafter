# A Matter of Principle

Margaret has been a public defender for fifty-two years. She carries herself with the kind of quiet
authority that comes from decades spent advocating for people society has largely written off. Her
office walls are lined with case files and photographs of clients she's helped—some released from
wrongful convictions, others spared harsher sentences through her meticulous work. At seventy-eight,
she's reduced her caseload but hasn't retired. She says the work still matters.

The assault happened eighteen months ago on a Tuesday evening in the courthouse parking garage.
Margaret was walking to her car after reviewing case files for a client scheduled to appear the
following morning. A man she'd successfully defended five years earlier—someone whose charges she'd
gotten reduced through a plea agreement—approached her from behind. He'd been drinking. He blamed
her for his subsequent struggles, for what he saw as a hollow victory that had left him without
closure or real help. He pushed her against a concrete pillar, striking her face and shoulder before
a security guard intervened. The physical injuries healed within weeks: a fractured cheekbone,
bruised ribs, a concussion.

The other injuries have proven more persistent.

Margaret doesn't go to the parking garage anymore. Instead, her husband drives her to the courthouse
entrance and picks her up at the main doors. She tells people it's because she's getting older, that
she prefers the convenience. The truth is more complicated. The garage has become a place where her
mind plays tricks on her. Even during daylight hours, she finds herself scanning for movement in her
peripheral vision, her body tensing at the sound of footsteps echoing off concrete. She's taken the
elevator twice since the assault. Both times, she experienced what her daughter—a physician—gently
called a panic attack: her heart racing, her breath shallow, a certainty that something terrible was
about to happen.

What troubles Margaret most is how the assault has infiltrated her work. She's spent five decades
believing in the redemptive potential of the legal system, in the possibility of second chances.
She's defended people accused of terrible things because everyone deserves representation, because
the system only works when it works for everyone. But now, when she sits across from clients in
interview rooms, she sometimes catches herself wondering: Could this person hurt me? Has this person
already hurt someone? The thoughts arrive unbidden and unwelcome, contradicting everything she
believes about presumption of innocence and human capacity for change.

She's stopped working late. She used to spend evenings at her office, reviewing transcripts and
preparing arguments. Now she leaves by four o'clock, before the light fades and the building
empties. Her colleagues have noticed her reduced presence, though she hasn't explained why. She's
always been private about her personal life.

Her husband notices how she startles at unexpected sounds, how she sometimes wakes in the night with
her fists clenched. She has trouble concentrating on books she once loved. The news—especially
stories about violence—makes her anxious and irritable in ways that surprise them both. She's
considered retiring, which would have been unthinkable two years ago. The work that defined her
identity for half a century now feels complicated in ways she doesn't quite know how to untangle.

Margaret sits in the consultation room with a steady gaze, her hands folded in her lap. She speaks
with precision about the assault, the way she might describe a case. But beneath her composed
exterior, something has shifted. She's beginning to wonder whether the person she was before the
parking garage still exists, or whether she's become someone different—someone more afraid, less
certain, more questioning of the very principles that have guided her life's work.
