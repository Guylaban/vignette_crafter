# Maya

Maya sits in the worn leather chair across from the therapist's office, fingers tracing the frayed
edge of their jeans. At twenty-seven, they have the careful posture of someone who has learned to
take up less space than they naturally would.

Three years ago, Maya was thriving as a special education teacher at Riverside Primary School. They
loved their work—the incremental victories with their students, the way a struggling reader's face
would light up when something finally clicked. Maya had just come out as non-binary the year before
and felt genuinely settled in their identity for the first time. Their relationship with Jordan,
their partner of four years, was solid. Life felt like it was moving in the right direction.

Then came the messages. At first, they seemed like professional interest—a parent from the previous
year's class asking for advice about their child's progress. But the tone shifted quickly. The
messages became personal, arriving late at night, increasingly intimate despite Maya's polite
deflections. When Maya didn't respond, they escalated. The person showed up at the school's parking
lot. They found Maya's social media accounts and began commenting obsessively. They sent gifts to
the school addressed to Maya, each one more unsettling than the last.

The school administration told Maya to document everything but seemed reluctant to take further
action. The police said there wasn't enough for a restraining order yet. Maya felt untethered,
exposed. The person knew where they worked, where they lived. They knew things about Maya's daily
routine—what time they arrived at school, which coffee shop they visited on Fridays.

Three years later, Maya still teaches at Riverside, but something fundamental has shifted. They
arrive at school forty minutes early now, scanning the parking lot from their car before getting
out. During lessons, their attention fractures unexpectedly. A knock on the classroom door sends
their heart racing. Last month, a student asked why their voice had gotten shaky during a lesson
about feelings and safety. Maya couldn't explain that they'd seen someone in the hallway who
resembled their stalker.

Sleep has become a negotiation. Maya lies awake listening for sounds, checking their phone
compulsively even though they know the stalking stopped eighteen months ago. Jordan tries to be
patient, but Maya notices the tension when they ask Jordan to check the locks again, or when they
insist on keeping their location shared at all times. Jordan suggested they might feel safer if they
moved away, started fresh somewhere new, but Maya can't quite imagine leaving the job they love,
even though it now feels like the job is slowly leaving them.

At work, Maya has become hypervigilant about their students' safety, which makes sense given their
training, but colleagues have gently noted that Maya seems to be the only teacher who keeps their
classroom door locked at all times. Maya notices things other people miss—exits, potential threats,
the way strangers look at them. Sometimes they're not sure if they're being careful or if fear has
simply become their baseline.

The stalking stopped. The person was identified, contacted by police, and moved away. But Maya
carries the experience like a stone in their chest. They still love teaching, still feel that spark
when they connect with a struggling student. But now there's always a shadow underneath it—a
persistent awareness that safety is fragile, that their body and routine and presence can become
objects of someone else's obsession.
