# Marcus's Story

Marcus sits in the waiting room of the clinic, his leg bouncing with a restless energy he can't
quite control. At twenty-five, he has the sharp eyes of someone trained to observe detail, to catch
the story others miss. But lately, those eyes have been haunted by something he can't quite report.

Two years ago, Marcus was covering a protest in his city as a freelance journalist. He'd done this
dozens of times before—he knew the protocols, the safety measures, the way to position himself to
get the shot without becoming part of the story. That day felt different, though he couldn't have
articulated why. The crowd was tense. The police presence was heavy. He was wearing his press
credentials clearly, his camera around his neck, his notebook visible. He was a journalist. He was
supposed to be protected.

When the order came to disperse, it came without warning. Marcus was moving away, already, but not
fast enough. An officer grabbed him from behind, and in the confusion—whether it was intentional or
not, Marcus still doesn't know—he was slammed against a wall. His camera broke. His ribs bruised.
His sense of safety shattered completely.

What haunts him most isn't the physical pain. It's the feeling of powerlessness, of his identity—his
press credentials, his role as an observer—meaning nothing. He was supposed to be neutral.
Protected. Instead, he was just another body to the officer who grabbed him.

Since that day, Marcus has struggled to work. He still goes to assignments, still carries his
camera, but something fundamental has shifted. Loud noises make him flinch violently—a car
backfiring sends him into an immediate panic. Last month, a colleague touched his shoulder
unexpectedly to get his attention, and Marcus reacted so defensively that he knocked over an entire
coffee setup at the newsroom. He was humiliated. His girlfriend, Amara, had to leave work to pick
him up because he couldn't stop shaking.

At night, Marcus lies awake replaying those seconds. He wonders if he could have moved faster, if he
should have positioned himself differently, if he'd somehow provoked the officer's response. Amara
tells him it wasn't his fault, but her reassurance doesn't stick. His mind loops through the
scenario endlessly, searching for something he could have done differently—as if there was some
magical action that would have prevented it.

The worst part is what this has done to his journalism. Marcus has always defined himself through
his work, through his ability to bear witness, to tell stories that matter. Now, when he's at an
assignment, he's hypervigilant in a way that makes reporting nearly impossible. He scans for threats
constantly, positioning himself near exits, unable to focus on the actual story unfolding before
him. He's missed critical moments because his attention was consumed by monitoring his environment.
His editor has noticed his output declining, though Marcus hasn't told him why.

He's also withdrawn from Amara in ways he doesn't fully understand. She wants to help, but he feels
ashamed—ashamed that he's afraid, ashamed that he can't do his job, ashamed that a man he's never
seen again has taken up so much space in his mind. Some nights, when she tries to hold him, he feels
trapped rather than comforted.

Marcus came to the clinic because Amara finally insisted, because he recognized he wasn't
functioning the way he used to, because he needs to understand why his body still feels like it's
being slammed against that wall.
