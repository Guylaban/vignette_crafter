# Marcus

Marcus is a forty-two-year-old electrician from Samoa who has worked in residential and commercial
wiring for the past eighteen years. He's been with his partner David for six years, and they live
together in a modest home where Marcus has meticulously rewired the entire electrical system
himself. He takes pride in his work—the precision, the problem-solving, the tangible way he can
improve people's homes. At least, that's who he was before.

Three months ago, Marcus was called to a job site to troubleshoot faulty wiring in a small office
building downtown. He arrived around 6 p.m., after most staff had left. As he was working in the
basement, a man he'd never seen before approached him asking for directions. Marcus, naturally
friendly, stopped what he was doing to help. The man suddenly became aggressive, demanding Marcus's
wallet. When Marcus hesitated—more confused than fearful in that first moment—the man struck him
repeatedly with a crowbar he'd apparently brought for the purpose. Marcus remembers falling, trying
to protect his head with his arms, and then nothing until he woke in the hospital with a fractured
radius, three broken ribs, and a concussion.

The assault lasted perhaps ninety seconds. The man was never caught.

Now, three months later, Marcus barely leaves the house. He's taken leave from his job, telling his
supervisor he needed time to recover from the physical injuries. The ribs have healed. His arm is
strong again. But something else remains fractured. He lies awake most nights, his mind cycling
through variations of the attack—sometimes he fights back successfully, sometimes he doesn't,
sometimes the man returns. When he does sleep, he wakes gasping, his heart hammering, convinced he's
still in that basement. David has learned not to touch him suddenly in the dark.

The thought of returning to job sites fills Marcus with a dread so consuming he can barely
articulate it. He's not afraid of being attacked again, exactly. It's more fundamental than that—a
sense that the world is fundamentally unsafe, that he cannot trust his own judgment about people or
situations. The man had seemed normal. That's what Marcus keeps returning to. He helped someone who
was about to hurt him.

When he does venture out—to the grocery store, to his partner's family gathering last month—Marcus
finds himself scanning faces, noting exits, calculating threats. His shoulders stay tensed. Loud
noises make him flinch violently. A car backfired last week and he found himself on the ground
before he'd consciously decided to move. David had to help him up, embarrassed and shaking.

He's started drinking more than he used to, a beer or two in the evening becoming three or four. It
helps quiet the vigilance for a few hours, though it always returns worse by morning. He feels
distant from David, irritable, exhausted by the effort of pretending he's managing. His partner has
gently suggested he talk to someone, and Marcus knows he should. But he also feels ashamed—ashamed
that he's afraid, ashamed that he can't just move past this, ashamed that a man who works with his
hands, who solves problems for a living, now feels helpless.

He still has his tools in the garage. Sometimes he looks at them, remembering the satisfaction of
work, wondering if that version of himself—competent, useful, unafraid—still exists somewhere, or if
he's been rewired in some fundamental way he cannot repair.
