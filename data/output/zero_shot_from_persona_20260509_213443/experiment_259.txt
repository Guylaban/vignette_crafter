# Margaret's Vigil

Margaret sits in the waiting room of her cardiologist's office, her hands folded in her lap with the
precision of someone accustomed to control. At seventy-seven, she still carries herself with the
bearing of someone who has spent five decades making life-and-death decisions in operating theatres.
Her grey hair is immaculately styled, her clothing carefully chosen—a pale blue blouse, pressed
linen trousers. Yet her leg bounces slightly beneath the table, a rhythm she seems unaware of.

Five years ago, Margaret experienced what she describes as "the moment everything changed." During a
routine colonoscopy—a procedure she'd undergone before without incident—she suffered a massive
myocardial infarction. The cardiologist had been inserting the scope when Margaret felt a crushing
pressure in her chest, as if her ribs were being compressed by invisible hands. She remembers the
sudden chaos, the urgent voices of the medical staff, the sense of her body betraying her. She was
rushed to the cardiac unit, where she spent three days uncertain whether she would survive.

The physical recovery took months. The psychological recovery, she now understands, may never be
complete.

Margaret retired from surgery two years after the event, though not by choice. She'd attempted to
return to the operating theatre six months post-incident, believing that confronting the environment
would help her regain her sense of mastery. Instead, standing across from an anaesthetized patient,
her hands began to tremble uncontrollably. Her vision narrowed. She could feel her heart
accelerating—that same sensation of losing control that had seized her during the colonoscopy. She'd
had to leave mid-procedure, something she'd never done in her entire career.

Now, in her retirement, Margaret finds herself trapped in a peculiar inversion of her former life.
She spent decades being the one who remained calm while others panicked, the one who made critical
decisions under pressure. Now she cannot sit through a medical appointment without her entire
nervous system becoming hypervigilant. Every specialist visit triggers a cascade of intrusive
thoughts: images of the colonoscopy room, the smell of antiseptic, the sensation of her chest
tightening. She knows, intellectually, that these sensations are memories, not current threats. But
her body doesn't listen to reason.

The waiting rooms are particularly difficult. Margaret sits among other patients, many younger than
her, and she feels acutely aware of the fragility of existence. She watches people on their phones,
seemingly unconcerned about their mortality, and feels a strange disconnection. Don't they
understand how quickly everything can be taken away? How a routine procedure can become a brush with
death?

Her sleep has become fractured. She lies awake at three in the morning, her heart rate elevated, her
mind cycling through scenarios. What if it happens again? What if she's alone when it does? She
lives by herself—her marriage ended fifteen years ago, and her children live overseas. She's become
reluctant to be alone for extended periods, yet simultaneously withdrawn from social contact. Her
friends have stopped calling as frequently; she suspects they've tired of her cancellations.

Margaret's mind, once her greatest asset—sharp, analytical, capable of holding multiple complex
variables simultaneously—now feels like a liability. It endlessly generates worst-case scenarios,
loops through medical information she wishes she didn't know, and fixates on bodily sensations she
cannot ignore. The very knowledge that made her an excellent surgeon—intimate understanding of what
can go wrong inside the human body—now haunts her.

She sits in the waiting room, waiting for her name to be called, and wonders if she will ever feel
safe in her own body again.
