# Marcus

Marcus is a twenty-five-year-old housekeeper who moves through his work with practiced efficiency,
arriving early and leaving late. His supervisor describes him as reliable and thorough—the kind of
employee who notices details others miss, who reorganizes supply closets without being asked, who
seems more comfortable when he has a task to focus on. What his supervisor doesn't see is the way
Marcus's jaw tightens when someone raises their voice in the hallway, or how he positions himself in
rooms so he can see the door.

The physical abuse began when Marcus was seven. His stepfather's violence was
unpredictable—sometimes triggered by spilled milk or a bad day at work, sometimes for no apparent
reason Marcus could identify. His mother, overwhelmed and frightened herself, would sometimes
intervene and sometimes withdraw. By adolescence, Marcus had learned to read the smallest shifts in
his stepfather's mood, to anticipate danger, to make himself smaller. He developed an almost
preternatural ability to sense tension in a room, to notice when someone's breathing changed or
their posture shifted. These skills kept him safer then. Now, at twenty-five, they exhaust him.

Marcus lives alone in a studio apartment, which he keeps immaculately organized. He has few close
relationships. His coworkers like him but don't really know him—he eats lunch in his car, declines
after-work drinks, maintains a polite distance. Dating feels impossible. He tried once in his early
twenties, but when his girlfriend touched him unexpectedly while he was concentrating, he flinched
so violently he knocked over a lamp. She didn't understand. He couldn't explain that his body was
still protecting him from a man who hasn't been in his life for eight years.

Sleep is complicated. Marcus often lies awake for hours, his mind running through scenarios—what if
someone breaks in, what if he's not alert enough, what if he misses a warning sign. When he does
sleep, he dreams of his stepfather's face, of being trapped, of his voice. He wakes gasping, his
heart hammering, and it takes him twenty minutes to remember where he is and that he's safe. Some
nights he doesn't bother trying to sleep at all; instead, he cleans his apartment or scrolls through
his phone until exhaustion finally wins.

During the day, he's hypervigilant in ways he doesn't fully recognize. Loud noises make him jump—a
door slamming, a car backfiring, raised voices from neighboring apartments. His shoulders are
perpetually tense, knotted between his shoulder blades. He experiences sudden waves of anger that
feel disproportionate to whatever triggered them: a guest leaving a mess feels like a personal
violation; a supervisor's constructive feedback feels like criticism that pierces something deep.

There are also the intrusive moments. He'll be cleaning a bathroom and suddenly smell
something—cologne, sweat, something indefinable—and he's seven again, bracing for impact. His hands
shake. His breathing becomes shallow. The moment passes, but it leaves him rattled for the rest of
the day.

Marcus doesn't talk about any of this. He's learned that people either minimize the past or pity
him, and neither feels bearable. He tells himself he's fine, that he's moved on, that his childhood
doesn't affect him anymore. But the evidence surrounds him: the way he can't relax, the
relationships he sabotages before they can hurt him, the constant low-level dread that something bad
is coming. He's built a life of control and safety, but the walls are exhausting to maintain.
