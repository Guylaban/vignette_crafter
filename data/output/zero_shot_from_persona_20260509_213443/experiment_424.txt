# Marcus

Marcus runs his import business from a modest office in downtown Miami, the same one he's operated
for twenty-three years. He arrives early each morning, reviews invoices, manages his small staff. To
anyone watching, he appears composed, professional—a man who has built something solid. But Marcus
hasn't slept through a single night in eight years.

It started when he was fifty-two. After his youngest daughter graduated from college, Marcus felt a
restlessness he couldn't name. His business was stable, his marriage intact, his life comfortable.
So when a nonprofit reached out asking for his help—he spoke Spanish, had business acumen, could
manage logistics in a conflict zone—he volunteered for six months in Central America. He told
himself it was meaningful work. His wife, Elena, worried but didn't stop him.

The first three months were manageable. Marcus coordinated supply deliveries to medical clinics in
areas affected by gang violence. He met with local officials, navigated checkpoints, solved
problems. He felt useful in a way his business never quite provided. Then one afternoon, while
waiting outside a clinic, gunfire erupted. A stray bullet struck a young boy standing near
Marcus—maybe eight years old. Marcus held him while people screamed. The boy died before the
ambulance arrived.

Marcus finished his contract. He came home. He didn't tell Elena about the boy for months.

Now, eight years later, Marcus still wakes at 2 or 3 a.m., his heart hammering. Sometimes he's back
at that clinic. Sometimes he's driving toward it, knowing something terrible is about to happen but
unable to stop the car. He lies beside Elena in the dark, afraid to move, afraid to wake her. He
gets up, goes to his office—even at three in the morning—and works until dawn.

His employees have learned not to comment on his presence. Marcus is meticulous, exacting, sometimes
sharp in ways that seem disconnected from the moment. A mistake in shipping paperwork triggers an
intensity of anger that surprises everyone, including himself. He's aware of it happening, watches
himself react, but can't seem to stop the surge. Later he feels ashamed, apologizes, returns to his
usual controlled demeanor.

Loud noises—a car backfiring, a dropped box in the warehouse—send electricity through his body. His
hands shake. His mind goes blank for a few seconds. He's learned to excuse himself, to breathe in
the bathroom until it passes. He tells people he's fine, just tired.

Elena has stopped asking him to go out in the evenings. Restaurants feel dangerous to him; he
positions himself facing the door, scans for exits, notices everyone who enters. He's aware of how
this looks, how it affects her, but the vigilance feels necessary. The world feels like that
clinic—something terrible could happen any moment.

He drinks more than he used to. A glass of rum in the evening helps quiet his mind, helps him sleep
for a few hours. He tells himself it's just a habit, nothing serious. He's a man who handles his
responsibilities. His business runs smoothly. He pays his bills. He comes home to his wife.

But Marcus hasn't felt truly present in his own life since that afternoon. He moves through his days
as if watching himself from outside his body, managing tasks, managing symptoms, managing the weight
of a boy he couldn't save and a world that suddenly revealed itself as fragile and violent and real.
