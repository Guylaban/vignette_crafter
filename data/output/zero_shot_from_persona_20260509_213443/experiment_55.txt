# Margaret's Story

Margaret is a 47-year-old plumber who has spent the last twenty-five years building a reputation for
reliable, meticulous work throughout her city. She speaks with quiet confidence about her trade—the
way she can diagnose a problem by listening to pipes, the satisfaction of fixing what others cannot.
Yet beneath this professional competence lives a woman still haunted by a childhood she rarely
discusses.

She was seven when her grandmother began raising her. Margaret's mother had struggled with
addiction, and her grandmother—stern, unpredictable, and deeply religious—took control with an iron
hand. The beatings started over small infractions: a shirt worn incorrectly, a question asked at the
wrong moment, a look that seemed disrespectful. Margaret learned quickly to move carefully through
the house, to anticipate her grandmother's moods, to make herself small. By adolescence, she had
internalized the message that her body was something to be controlled, punished, corrected.

Now, decades later, Margaret sits in her kitchen—a space she has meticulously maintained, every tool
in its place—and describes the physical sensations that ambush her without warning. A client raising
their voice about a repair job sends her stomach into knots. When her husband touches her shoulder
unexpectedly, her body tenses before her mind catches up. She finds herself holding her breath in
conversations, waiting for criticism that may never come.

The hypervigilance has become part of her work routine. Margaret arrives at job sites early,
assessing the space, noting exits, observing the homeowner's demeanor. She is excellent at reading
people—a skill honed through childhood survival—but it exhausts her. She comes home from work
depleted, her muscles aching not from the physical labor but from the constant state of alert. Her
husband notices that she sleeps poorly, sometimes waking in a sweat at three in the morning, her
heart racing. She cannot always remember what she was dreaming about, only the feeling of being
trapped.

Certain smells trigger her—the particular scent of a cleaning product reminds her of her
grandmother's house, and Margaret finds herself unable to finish a job if a client uses it. She has
begun making excuses, referring those jobs to colleagues, silently ashamed of her own limitations.
She tells herself she is simply becoming more selective about her clients, but she knows the truth.

Relationships remain complicated. Margaret loves her husband deeply, yet she struggles to receive
affection without her nervous system interpreting it as a threat. She apologizes frequently,
sometimes for things she has not done, a remnant of her childhood strategy of preemptive contrition.
She has difficulty accepting praise for her work, quick to point out flaws others cannot see. When
her husband suggests she might benefit from talking to someone, she initially dismisses the idea.
Talking about the past feels dangerous, like opening a door that cannot be closed.

What brings Margaret to seek help now is not a single crisis but the accumulation of small
surrenders. She is tired of jumping at sounds. She is tired of the weight in her chest that arrives
without explanation. She is tired of the distance she maintains from her own life, observing it from
behind a protective barrier. She wants to understand why her body still responds as though she is
seven years old, why safety remains so elusive even in a home she has built with her own hands.
