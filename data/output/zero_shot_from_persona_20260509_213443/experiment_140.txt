# Marcus

Marcus sits in the consultation room with his hands folded in his lap, a habit that seems to settle
him. At sixty-seven, he has the measured composure of someone who has spent decades thinking about
space, proportion, and how objects relate to one another. His silver hair is carefully groomed, and
he wears a linen shirt in a soft taupe—colors he would choose for a client's living room. Yet there
is something held in his shoulders, a tension that no amount of good posture can quite dissolve.

Thirty-two years ago, Marcus was working as a junior designer for a boutique firm in Port Moresby.
He was on a site visit to a residential project in the highlands when armed men entered the
compound. What was meant to be a two-hour consultation became something else entirely. He and two
colleagues were taken into a storage building and held for eleven days. The men demanded money, made
threats that shifted hourly, and kept them in a space with minimal light and no certainty about what
would happen next. Marcus remembers the smell of diesel fuel and rust. He remembers the sound of his
own breathing, which seemed impossibly loud in the darkness.

The experience ended when police negotiators secured their release, but Marcus never quite left that
building. For the first few years after, he could work, though he moved through his designs like
someone underwater. His wife, Patricia, was patient and steady—she would find him at three in the
morning, standing in their kitchen, unable to explain why he couldn't sleep. By the time their
children were grown, Marcus had learned to function. He developed routines. He became successful,
known for creating serene, carefully controlled interior spaces. Clients appreciated his attention
to detail, his insistence on proper lighting and clear sightlines in every room.

Patricia died four years ago. Without her presence anchoring him, without the structure of their
shared life, something shifted. The captivity began to feel recent again.

Now, Marcus describes his current life in measured terms, but the weight beneath his words is
evident. He still works—he cannot imagine not working—but he has become increasingly selective about
projects. He will not take jobs in buildings without multiple exits. He struggles with basement
spaces and interior rooms without windows. Recently, he turned down a lucrative contract because the
client wanted to renovate a basement wine cellar. He couldn't bring himself to explain why.

Sleep remains elusive. He lies awake most nights, his mind cycling through scenarios: what if the
negotiators hadn't come, what if the men had decided differently, what if, what if. When he does
sleep, he wakes gasping, sometimes unsure whether he is in his bedroom in 2024 or back in that
building. The nightmares are always the same—he is looking for a door that doesn't exist.

He has become isolated in ways that surprise him. He declines dinner invitations, finds crowds
overwhelming. His hands shake sometimes when he is reviewing design plans, particularly when
discussing confined spaces with clients. He has stopped going to his club, stopped attending the
design conferences he once enjoyed. His apartment, which he designed himself with meticulous care,
feels like both sanctuary and prison. He knows every angle, every exit, every source of natural
light—but he is aware that he chose these things not for beauty anymore, but for safety.

Marcus speaks about all of this with the careful precision of someone describing someone else's
life. Yet his eyes betray him. They are the eyes of a man still waiting for the door to open.
