# Priya's Reckoning

Priya sits across from me with her hands folded in her lap, a posture of practiced stillness. At
twenty-three, she has the composed bearing of someone far older—the kind of composure that doesn't
come from confidence but from the exhausting work of holding oneself together. She's a financial
auditor at a mid-sized firm, a position she secured two years ago through a combination of
intelligence and determination. Numbers have always made sense to her in a way people don't. They
are orderly, verifiable, controllable. This made her good at her job. It also, she suspects, is why
she struggles with everything else.

Two years ago, Priya was in Lahore visiting her maternal grandmother when escalating tensions in the
region erupted into violence. She had planned a three-week trip. On the second week, armed conflict
spread closer to the residential neighborhoods where her family lived. She remembers the sound
first—not the gunfire itself, but the way it made the windows rattle in their frames, a rhythmic
percussion that interrupted the afternoon. Her grandmother had pulled her away from the kitchen
window, and they'd huddled in an interior room with Priya's aunt and cousins. They stayed there for
six hours. The electricity cut out around hour four. In the darkness, Priya could hear her eight-
year-old cousin crying softly, and she'd held her hand, whispering reassurances she didn't believe.

When the immediate danger passed, Priya's family managed to get her on a flight back to the States.
She arrived home feeling oddly untethered, as though the person who had boarded the plane to visit
her grandmother was someone else entirely.

For the first several months after returning, Priya threw herself into work. Her firm was in the
midst of a major audit, and she volunteered for extra hours. The spreadsheets and financial
statements were a refuge—they required her complete attention, and in that attention, she could
quiet the intrusive images that arrived without warning. A car backfiring on her commute would send
her heart into her throat, convinced it was gunfire. She'd find herself in the parking garage at
work, unable to remember how she got there, her hands shaking against the steering wheel.

Sleep became fragmented. She'd wake at 2 or 3 a.m., her body drenched in sweat, her mind still half-
convinced she was in that dark room in Lahore. She stopped going out with colleagues after work.
Restaurants with too many exits made her anxious. Crowded spaces felt dangerous in a way she
couldn't articulate. She told people at work she was simply focused on her career.

The hypervigilance has become her baseline. In meetings, she positions herself where she can see the
door. In her apartment, she finds herself listening—not for anything specific, but listening
nonetheless, as though danger might announce itself if she's paying close enough attention. She's
acutely aware of every person around her, their movements, their intentions. It's exhausting, this
constant assessment.

What troubles her most are the gaps in her memory and the moments when her mind seems to fracture.
She'll be reviewing an audit trail and suddenly lose ten minutes—she'll look up and realize time has
passed, but she has no recollection of it. Sometimes she catches herself holding her breath without
knowing when she started. Her body remembers things her mind is trying to forget.

Priya is high-functioning by most measures. She still goes to work, still submits her reports on
time, still performs at a level that satisfies her supervisors. But there's a cost to this
functioning, a kind of internal depletion that grows more acute with each passing month. She's begun
to wonder if this is simply who she is now—someone who survived, but who cannot quite return to
living.
