# Sarah's Story

Sarah sits with her hands folded in her lap, her posture upright and composed—a habit formed over
thirty-five years of clinical practice. At fifty-seven, she remains one of the most experienced
midwives at the regional hospital, known for her steady presence during complicated deliveries. But
lately, steadiness has become a performance.

The assault happened seven months ago, on a night she'd stayed late to complete paperwork after a
double shift. She'd been alone in the staff locker room when a man she didn't recognize—later
identified as a former patient's relative—forced himself on her. The attack lasted perhaps five
minutes, though Sarah describes it as both instantaneous and endless. She fought back, screamed, and
he fled when another staff member arrived. The police were called. An investigation followed.
Nothing has been resolved.

What Sarah remembers most vividly is not the violence itself, but the moment immediately
after—standing in the bathroom, unable to recognize the woman in the mirror. That face belonged to
someone who had been invaded, compromised, unmade. Even now, she cannot shower without that same
disorientation, that sense of watching herself from outside her body. She washes mechanically,
efficiently, her mind somewhere else entirely.

Her husband, Marcus, has been patient, though she can see the strain in how he moves around
her—careful, tentative, as if she might shatter. They haven't been intimate since the assault. She
wants to want that closeness, but her body has become a landscape of unpredictability. Sometimes a
simple touch on her shoulder sends her into a state of hyperarousal that lasts for hours. Other
times, she feels nothing at all, just a distant numbness that frightens her more than the panic
does.

Work has become increasingly difficult. She's always loved the privilege of being present at
births—that sacred moment when a new person enters the world. But now, when she's assisting at
delivery, her mind fractures. She's simultaneously in the sterile hospital room and back in that
locker room. She finds herself gripping the edge of the bed too tightly, her breathing shallow and
rapid. Just last week, a laboring mother asked if she was all right, and Sarah realized she'd been
staring blankly at the wall for several minutes. She's begun requesting fewer shifts, citing
fatigue, though her colleagues have noticed the change in her.

Sleep has become a battlefield. She lies awake until two or three in the morning, her body locked in
a state of vigilance. When sleep finally comes, it brings nightmares—not always of the attack
itself, but of variations, of scenarios where she couldn't escape, where no one came. She wakes
gasping, sometimes unsure of where she is. Marcus often finds her sitting in the kitchen at dawn, a
cold cup of tea beside her.

The hypervigilance extends everywhere. In the hospital corridors, she finds herself scanning faces,
tracking exits, positioning herself with her back to the wall. Crowded spaces feel suffocating.
She's stopped attending her weekly gathering with other midwives—a tradition she'd maintained for
two decades—because the noise and proximity feel unbearable. Her sister-in-law invited her to a
family celebration last month, and she declined without explanation, unable to articulate that the
thought of being surrounded by people, of having to pretend normalcy, felt like drowning.

There are moments when Sarah catches herself in the mirror and sees that same unrecognizable woman
from the locker room. She wonders if she'll ever feel like herself again, or if this fractured
version is permanent. She still goes to work, still delivers babies, still performs the role of the
competent, calm midwife. But inside, she exists in a constant state of not-knowing—not knowing when
her body will betray her with panic, not knowing when a sound or smell will transport her backward,
not knowing who she is anymore.
