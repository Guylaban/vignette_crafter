# Marcus

Marcus is a sixty-six-year-old software engineer who retired from a major tech company three years
ago. He sits in the office with a measured composure, speaking thoughtfully about his life, though
there's a careful distance in how he discusses his past. His wife, Patricia, encouraged him to seek
therapy after noticing patterns she couldn't ignore anymore.

The trauma began when Marcus was seven years old. His father, a volatile man who struggled with
alcoholism, would beat him regularly—sometimes with a belt, sometimes with his hands. These weren't
isolated incidents. They were woven into the fabric of Marcus's childhood, unpredictable and severe.
His mother was largely absent, either emotionally or physically, leaving young Marcus to navigate
his father's rage alone. By the time Marcus was twelve, his father had left the family entirely, but
the damage was already done.

For decades, Marcus functioned. He was intelligent, driven, and channeled his energy into his
career. Programming appealed to him because it was logical, controllable, and rewarded precision. He
could spend hours debugging code, finding satisfaction in solving problems that had clear
solutions—unlike the chaos of his childhood. He married Patricia at thirty-five, and they built a
quiet life together. He was a devoted husband, careful never to raise his voice, never to lose
control. But Patricia knew something was beneath the surface.

The symptoms became more apparent after retirement. Without the structure of work, without the
mental absorption that coding provided, Marcus found himself alone with his thoughts. He started
having intrusive memories—vivid, unwanted flashbacks of his father's face, the sound of his voice,
the feeling of being trapped and helpless. These episodes would strike without warning: a raised
voice on television, the smell of alcohol on someone passing by, even certain weather patterns that
reminded him of the season when the worst beatings occurred.

Marcus developed hypervigilance without fully recognizing it. He monitors his environment
constantly, noting exits, assessing threats. When Patricia touches him unexpectedly, his body
tenses. He's aware of this reaction and feels ashamed by it, but he cannot seem to control it. At
restaurants, he positions himself so he can see the door. At family gatherings, he sits near the
perimeter of rooms. His sleep is fragmented—he wakes at three or four in the morning, his heart
racing, sometimes unable to remember what he was dreaming about, only knowing that danger felt
imminent.

There's an emotional numbness that troubles him more than he initially admits. He loves Patricia,
but he struggles to feel the warmth he knows he should. He avoids conversations about his childhood,
and Patricia has learned not to push. He's withdrawn from friendships that once mattered, finding
social interaction increasingly taxing. Small talk feels impossible when his mind is occupied with
managing internal threat detection.

What makes Marcus's presentation complex is how well he's compensated. He's articulate, intelligent,
and capable of discussing his experiences with clinical precision. But beneath this articulation is
a man still trying to survive an attack that happened nearly sixty years ago. His body hasn't
received the message that he's safe. His nervous system remains on high alert, scanning for the
danger that once defined his world.
