# Mr. Nguyen's Vigil

Mr. Nguyen sits in the waiting room with his hands folded in his lap, his silver-streaked hair
neatly combed, his tie perfectly knotted. At sixty-eight, he carries himself with the composed
bearing of someone who has spent forty years arguing cases in front of judges. But when he speaks
about his wife, his voice becomes very small.

It was three years ago when his daughter called him at the courthouse. He was preparing for a
closing argument—a case he'd been working on for months, a young man accused of armed robbery. Mr.
Nguyen had been about to prove the evidence was circumstantial. Instead, he found himself in a
hospital parking lot, his hands shaking so badly he could barely hold his phone.

His wife, Linh, had suffered a massive stroke while gardening. She was sixty-five. By the time the
ambulance arrived, she had already lost consciousness. Mr. Nguyen spent eighteen days watching her
lie in a hospital bed, her right side completely paralyzed, unable to speak. On the nineteenth day,
she stopped breathing.

The funeral was large. Linh had been a beloved elementary school teacher for thirty-five years. Her
former students came. Their families came. Mr. Nguyen moved through it all as if underwater,
accepting condolences, receiving casseroles he would never eat, performing the role of the grieving
widower with the same precision he brought to his legal work.

But somewhere between the funeral and now, something fractured.

Three years later, Mr. Nguyen still wakes at 3 a.m., his heart racing, convinced that Linh is
calling for help. He lies in bed, listening to the silence of their house—their house, still full of
her things—and the panic doesn't subside until dawn. When it does, exhaustion replaces it. He has
stopped taking cases that require evening preparation. His colleagues have noticed.

He no longer gardens. The sight of their backyard, where Linh was kneeling when the stroke happened,
makes his chest tighten. He hired someone to maintain it, but he keeps the curtains drawn on that
side of the house. Sometimes he drives past the hospital where she died and has to pull over because
his vision blurs and his hands won't stop shaking.

What troubles him most is the intrusive thoughts. During oral arguments, when he should be focused,
he suddenly sees Linh's face as it was in that hospital bed—slack, unresponsive. He loses his train
of thought. Last month, he forgot a statute he's cited a hundred times. The judge had to remind him.

He has stopped attending his weekly dinner group with other retired judges. He tells himself he is
simply tired, but the truth is more complicated. When people ask how he is doing, he feels a surge
of rage that frightens him. He wants to shout that he is not doing, that nothing is doing, that his
wife is dead and he was not there when it happened, that he was arguing about evidence and procedure
while she was collapsing in their garden.

He doesn't shout. Instead, he nods politely and excuses himself.

At night, he sits in his study with a glass of whiskey he doesn't finish, looking at photographs of
Linh. In these moments, he feels the weight of three years pressing down on him like a physical
thing. He wonders if this heaviness is simply what the rest of his life will feel like.
