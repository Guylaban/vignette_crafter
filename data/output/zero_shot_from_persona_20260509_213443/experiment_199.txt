# Margaret's Story

Margaret sits in the waiting room each week with her hands folded in her lap, always arriving ten
minutes early. At fifty-six, she carries herself with the careful composure of someone who has spent
decades learning to hold things together. Her work as a social ministry worker has defined much of
her adult life—she coordinates support programs for vulnerable families in her community, a role
she's held with quiet dedication for nearly twenty years.

Three years ago, Margaret's world fractured. She was working late at the office, finalizing
paperwork for a client intake, when a man she'd never met before entered through the unlocked back
door. What happened in the next forty minutes exists in her memory as something fragmented and
surreal—moments of vivid clarity interrupted by stretches of dissociation she still cannot fully
account for. When it was over, he left as suddenly as he'd come. Margaret sat on the floor of her
office for an hour before she could move.

She reported it to police. She told her husband, Robert. She returned to work after two weeks,
believing that resuming her routine would restore some sense of normalcy. Instead, the trauma
followed her into every aspect of her life.

The hypervigilance came first. Margaret now locks and relocks doors compulsively—the back entrance
at work, the car doors, the front door at home. She counts the locks sometimes, losing track and
starting over. When clients arrive for appointments, she positions herself near the exit. Her body
tenses at unexpected sounds. Last month, a colleague approached her from behind to ask a question,
and Margaret's startle response was so severe that she knocked over her coffee and spent twenty
minutes in the bathroom trying to calm her racing heart.

Sleep has become a battleground. Margaret lies awake until midnight most nights, her mind cycling
through intrusive images she cannot control. When she does sleep, nightmares jolt her
awake—sometimes she's back in the office, sometimes the assault happens in different locations,
sometimes it's Robert's face she sees instead of the stranger's. She wakes gasping, her body
drenched in sweat. Robert has learned not to touch her when she wakes; even his gentle hand on her
shoulder triggers panic.

The emotional numbness troubles her more than she admits. Margaret goes through the motions of her
work with mechanical efficiency, but the empathy that once came naturally now feels distant and
forced. She sits across from clients describing their trauma, and she hears herself offering the
right words, but something essential feels absent inside her. The guilt about this is crushing—she
feels like a fraud, unable to genuinely help others when she cannot help herself.

Concentration has become nearly impossible. Margaret reads the same paragraph three times without
retaining it. In team meetings, she loses track of conversations. She's made small mistakes at
work—missed follow-ups, duplicated referrals—things that never happened before. She worries
constantly that her colleagues will notice, that they'll question her competence.

What troubles Margaret most is the way she's withdrawn from life. She and Robert rarely go out
anymore. She's stopped attending her book club. She avoids the grocery store during busy hours,
avoiding the crowds and the vulnerability of public spaces. Robert tries to encourage her to do
things, but the thought of leaving the house often feels insurmountable. She can see the worry in
his eyes, the way he's learned to move carefully around her, and this awareness adds another layer
of shame to what she already carries.

Margaret continues showing up to work, continues being the person others rely on. But inside, she is
living in a state of constant threat, unable to trust her own mind or body, struggling to find her
way back to herself.
