# Margaret's Persistent Vigilance

Margaret sits in the leather chair across from me with her hands folded neatly in her lap, a posture
she maintains throughout most sessions. At seventy-three, she carries herself with the careful
precision that has defined her forty-year career as a quality assurance engineer—the same meticulous
attention to detail that once made her invaluable in semiconductor manufacturing now manifests in
how she monitors her own breathing, counts the tiles on the ceiling, and positions herself where she
can see both the door and the window.

She begins by describing her childhood home in rural Austria, where her father's unpredictability
shaped every waking moment. His anger would erupt without warning—over a misplaced tool, a child's
mistake, a tone he perceived as disrespectful. Margaret learned to read the smallest shifts in his
expression, the particular way he cleared his throat before striking. She became hypervigilant
before she had words for what hypervigilance meant. At seven years old, she could detect tension in
a room the way other children detected candy.

The physical abuse was severe and frequent. Her father would use his hands, his belt, whatever was
nearest. Margaret describes these moments with clinical detachment, as though narrating someone
else's history, though her jaw tightens and her breathing becomes shallow as she speaks. She recalls
hiding in the cellar, waiting for the storm to pass, her small body pressed against concrete walls
that never quite felt cold enough to numb her completely.

What strikes me is how seamlessly this vigilance transferred into her professional identity.
Margaret became an engineer precisely because engineering demanded what her trauma had trained her
to do: spot defects before they became catastrophic, maintain absolute control over variables, trust
nothing that hadn't been verified multiple times. Her colleagues described her as meticulous, almost
obsessive—a woman who caught problems that others missed entirely. For decades, her hypervigilance
was an asset. It paid her mortgage. It earned her respect.

But now, at seventy-three, after retiring five years ago, Margaret has nowhere to direct this
vigilance. Without the structured environment of quality control, without the legitimate outlet for
her need to monitor and detect and prevent disaster, her nervous system has begun consuming itself.
She lies awake at night performing catastrophic mental audits of her life—reviewing conversations
for hidden slights, scanning her body for signs of illness, checking her front door lock repeatedly
even though she knows it's secure. Her husband of forty-eight years has learned not to approach her
suddenly from behind; twice, she has struck him reflexively, her body responding before her mind
catches up.

She startles at sudden noises—the doorbell sends adrenaline coursing through her. She avoids crowds
because the unpredictability terrifies her. Sitting in restaurants with her back to the door creates
an anxiety so intense she cannot eat. She's developed insomnia so profound that she sometimes goes
three nights without sleep, her mind cycling through worst-case scenarios with mechanical precision.

Margaret describes her current life with a kind of resigned exhaustion. She knows her reactions
don't match the actual danger present in her safe, orderly home. She knows her husband isn't her
father. She knows the world isn't the cellar. But knowing and feeling remain stubbornly
disconnected. Her body still believes it's seven years old, still waiting for the next blow, still
preparing for a threat that lives only in the past.
