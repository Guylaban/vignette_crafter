# Clinical Vignette: Dr. Margaret

Margaret sits in the chair across from me with her hands folded tightly in her lap, her posture
rigid despite the softness of the upholstered seat. At fifty-eight, she carries herself with the
composed authority of someone accustomed to being the expert in the room—a habit formed over thirty
years of psychiatric practice. Yet something in her eyes suggests she has come here precisely
because she can no longer maintain that stance alone.

Three years ago, Margaret was driving home from the hospital after a long shift. She'd been a
psychiatrist for most of her adult life, working in both private practice and at a teaching hospital
in Auckland, where she specialized in trauma and dissociative disorders. The irony is not lost on
her. She remembers the road was wet. She remembers the truck's headlights appearing suddenly in her
peripheral vision. She does not remember the impact itself—there is a gap, a blank space where the
collision should be—but she remembers waking in the hospital with a fractured pelvis, three broken
ribs, and a concussion that would keep her off work for six months.

The physical injuries healed. The pelvis mended. She returned to work, to her practice, to the
familiar rhythm of listening to others describe their worst moments. But something shifted in those
months of recovery, something she couldn't quite diagnose in herself.

Now, when she drives, her hands grip the steering wheel so tightly her knuckles turn white. She
finds herself scanning the road obsessively, checking mirrors, anticipating threats that haven't
materialized. Twice she has pulled over on the motorway convinced she was about to lose control.
She's started taking the back roads instead, adding forty minutes to her commute, and even then she
arrives at work with her heart racing and her shirt damp with sweat. She tells herself it's
reasonable caution, professional awareness—but she knows the difference between prudence and
hypervigilance.

The nightmares began within weeks of the accident. In them, she is always the passenger, never the
driver, watching helplessly as the vehicle spins. Sometimes the truck hits them again and again,
each collision worse than the last. She wakes gasping, her bedsheets soaked, and often cannot fall
back asleep. She's tried sleep hygiene, melatonin, and once even prescribed herself a low dose of
trazodone, which she discontinued after a week because she felt she should be able to manage this
herself.

What troubles her most is something quieter and more insidious. She has become withdrawn from her
colleagues. She cancels social engagements with vague excuses. Her ex-husband, whom she remains
cordial with, has noticed she sounds different on the phone—distant, flat. She sits in her office
with patients and sometimes finds her mind drifting to the blank space where the impact should be,
wondering what her brain is protecting her from. When a patient describes their own accident trauma,
she feels a tightness in her chest that makes it difficult to maintain her clinical presence.

She has not told anyone at the hospital. In her field, she knows what people assume about
psychiatrists who struggle—that they are compromised, that their judgment is questioned, that
admitting vulnerability is professional suicide. So she has carried this alone, a woman who has
spent her career helping others process their worst experiences, now unable to process her own.

She comes to see me because she is running out of ways to manage, and because somewhere beneath the
professional armor, she knows that the person who sits with traumatized patients cannot herself
remain trapped in this half-healed place.
