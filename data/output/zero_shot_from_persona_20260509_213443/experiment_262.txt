# Case Vignette: Marcus

Marcus sits in the office with his shoulders slightly hunched, fingers interlaced tightly in his
lap. At twenty-one, he carries himself with the careful deliberation of someone much older. He
speaks about his work as a public defender with genuine passion—he believes in defending the
vulnerable—yet there's a brittleness beneath his commitment that becomes apparent as he talks.

He was eight when his family fled. Marcus doesn't remember deciding to leave their home in Bosnia;
he remembers his mother's hands shaking as she packed a single suitcase, remembers the sound of
distant gunfire that didn't seem distant at all. The journey to the refugee camp in Croatia is
fragmented in his mind—three days crammed in the back of a truck with thirty other people, the smell
of unwashed bodies and fear, an old woman next to him who stopped moving somewhere around hour
forty. His father tried to shield him from seeing, but Marcus saw anyway.

The camp itself exists in his memory as a series of vivid, disconnected moments. The color of the
tents. The taste of thin soup. A boy his age who befriended him on the second day and disappeared on
the fourth. When aid workers came through distributing supplies, Marcus learned to make himself
small, invisible. He was good at it. Too good.

His family was eventually resettled in a European city when he was ten. They rebuilt—his parents
found work, Marcus went to school, learned the language fluently. By all measures, the resettlement
was successful. Marcus became a high-achieving student, driven by something he couldn't quite name.
In university, he gravitated toward law, toward justice, toward helping people who had no one. His
professors noted his intensity, his almost obsessive preparation for cases. He passed his bar exam
at twenty and landed a position as a public defender immediately.

But the past two years have been increasingly difficult. Marcus finds himself hypervigilant in ways
that don't match his current reality. When a client raises their voice during a consultation, his
chest tightens and his mind goes blank—he's eight again, waiting for something terrible to happen.
He's started having nightmares where he's back in the camp, searching for people he can't name. He
wakes with his heart racing, drenched in sweat, and lies awake for hours.

In the courthouse, Marcus is meticulous and effective, but colleagues have noticed he's become
withdrawn. He declines social events, claims he's too busy preparing cases. His girlfriend, Emma,
has gently mentioned that he seems distant lately, that sometimes when she touches him unexpectedly,
he flinches. He knows this isn't fair to her, but he can't seem to soften the vigilance that's
become his constant companion.

Recently, Marcus represented a young refugee client facing deportation. During their meetings, the
client described fleeing violence, described camps and uncertainty. Marcus found himself unable to
maintain professional distance. He worked the case obsessively, stayed up nights researching
precedents, and when the judge ruled against his client, Marcus felt something crack inside him. He
called in sick for three days.

He's begun to wonder if his drive to help is actually helping, or if he's just running from
something he can't outrun. He notices now that he chooses cases involving vulnerable populations
almost exclusively, as though he's trying to save the eight-year-old boy he once was. The intrusive
memories are becoming more frequent. Loud noises make him jump. He's started avoiding the courthouse
cafeteria because it reminds him of the camp's communal eating areas. Emma asked him yesterday if he
was okay, and for the first time, he said no.
