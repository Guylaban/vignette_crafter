# A Disrupted Blueprint

Marcus sits in the design studio where he works, his dual monitors casting a pale glow across his
face. On the left screen, a wireframe for a mobile app interface. On the right, a browser tab he's
had open for three days—a live feed from the weather service. He refreshes it compulsively, even
though he knows the forecast won't change for hours. His colleagues have stopped asking why he does
this.

Two years ago, Marcus was visiting his grandmother in the coastal province where she'd lived her
entire life. He'd taken a week off from his junior position at the design firm, planning to help her
with some repairs around the house and finally teach her how to use the smartphone he'd gifted her.
On the fourth day, the typhoon warnings began. His grandmother had weathered dozens of storms; she
insisted they were fine. Marcus believed her. He'd grown up in the city, attended university there,
built his life around concrete and glass. Typhoons were something that happened to other people.

The storm arrived with a violence he couldn't have imagined. The sound was what he remembers most
vividly—not the wind itself, but the way it made everything scream. The roof began to fail around
midnight. Marcus and his grandmother huddled in the interior bathroom as water poured through the
ceiling. He held her hand and tried to keep her calm, though his own heart was hammering so hard he
thought it might break through his ribs. When the water started rising, he made a decision that
still wakes him at three in the morning: he helped his grandmother to the roof, then went back
inside to find documents, money, anything that might matter. The house collapsed while he was
inside. He was pulled from the rubble six hours later by rescue workers.

His grandmother did not survive.

Marcus returned to the city three weeks later. He moved back into his apartment, returned to work
after a month, and told people he was fine. He wasn't fine. He still isn't.

The hypervigilance is constant now. Weather reports consume him. He's memorized the names of typhoon
seasons, wind speed classifications, early warning systems. When it rains, he struggles to stay in
his apartment. The sound of wind against the windows sends adrenaline flooding through his system.
Last month, a colleague mentioned a storm system forming over the Pacific. Marcus left work at two
in the afternoon, his hands shaking, unable to explain why.

His work suffers in ways he can't quite articulate to his manager. He designs interfaces meant to be
intuitive, to guide users smoothly through digital experiences. But lately, he finds himself
designing escape routes into every project—extra navigation buttons, redundant pathways, multiple
ways out. His designs have become cluttered, inefficient. He's received feedback that his recent
work lacks the clean elegance his team values.

Sleep is fragmented. He dreams of rising water, of his grandmother's face, of being trapped in dark
spaces. He wakes gasping, sometimes unsure which room he's in. Some nights he doesn't bother trying
to sleep at all, instead scrolling through disaster preparedness websites until dawn.

He hasn't told anyone at work about his grandmother. When people ask how his family is doing, he
changes the subject. He hasn't returned to the province. He can't imagine ever going back.

Marcus knows something is wrong with him. He knows the way he lives now—the constant scanning for
danger, the exhaustion, the way his chest tightens whenever his phone buzzes with a weather alert—is
not sustainable. But naming it, seeking help, admitting that a week in August has rewritten his
entire nervous system: these things feel impossible.
