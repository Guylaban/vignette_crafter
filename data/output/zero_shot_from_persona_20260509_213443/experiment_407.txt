# Marcus

Marcus has been driving the same downtown bus route for nineteen years. He knows the regulars by
name, remembers which passengers prefer the front seats, and takes pride in running on schedule. His
coworkers describe him as reliable—the kind of driver management calls when they need someone
steady. But something fundamental shifted three months ago, and Marcus struggles to recognize
himself in the person he's become.

It happened on a Tuesday afternoon during the 2:45 run. Marcus was stopped at a red light when
police officers pulled over a car two vehicles ahead. What started as a routine traffic stop
escalated quickly. Marcus watched in his side mirror as officers approached the vehicle with weapons
drawn. The driver exited suddenly, and within seconds, gunfire erupted. The driver collapsed on the
street. Passengers screamed. Marcus froze at the wheel, unable to move, unable to look away from the
scene unfolding just thirty feet from his windshield.

The aftermath was chaos—emergency vehicles, investigators, statements to police. Marcus was told
he'd done nothing wrong, that he was a witness, not involved. But his body didn't accept that
distinction. For days afterward, he couldn't sleep. When he finally did, he dreamed of the moment
the driver fell, except in the dreams it was Marcus himself lying on the pavement. He'd wake
gasping, his heart hammering so hard he'd have to sit up and press his palm against his chest to
convince himself he was breathing.

He tried to return to work after a week. The first time he approached the intersection where it
happened, his hands began shaking so violently he had to pull over. His vision narrowed. His chest
tightened. He sat there for twenty minutes before radioing dispatch that he wasn't feeling well. He
hasn't driven that route since.

Now Marcus works the evening shift on the outer routes, but anxiety has become his constant
passenger. He's hypervigilant in ways that exhaust him—scanning the street for police activity,
monitoring other drivers obsessively, checking mirrors every few seconds. When he sees a patrol car,
his stomach drops and his breathing becomes shallow. He's started avoiding certain neighborhoods
entirely, taking longer routes to sidestep areas where he might encounter police presence.

Off-duty, Marcus has largely withdrawn. He lives alone and has become more isolated. He used to grab
coffee with other drivers after their shifts; now he drives straight home. His apartment has become
a refuge, though even there he's restless. He keeps his curtains drawn and finds himself listening
for sirens, his body tensing whenever he hears them. He's lost interest in things he once
enjoyed—his weekly poker game with friends, the occasional movie, even reading, which used to calm
him.

The worst part, Marcus says, is feeling like a coward. He's a man who's always been capable,
reliable, someone others depend on. Now he feels fragile and unpredictable. Some mornings he wakes
determined to push through, to reclaim his route and his life. Other mornings, the thought of
getting behind that wheel fills him with a dread so overwhelming that he calls in sick. He's taken
more absences in the past three months than in his entire nineteen-year career. He worries
constantly about losing his job, but the anxiety about returning feels even worse.

Marcus describes himself as "stuck"—caught between the life he had and the life he's living now,
unable to move in either direction.
