# James

James sits in the leather chair across from the therapist's desk, his posture rigid despite the
relative comfort of the office. At fifty-two, he carries himself with the practiced authority of
someone accustomed to commanding respect—shoulders back, jaw set, the bearing of a man who has spent
twenty-eight years in education. But there's a brittleness to it now, a performance that seems to
cost him something.

Three years ago, James was leaving his school after a late evening of budget meetings. The parking
lot was nearly empty. A former student—someone he'd disciplined years earlier for repeated
behavioral violations—approached him with a knife. The assault lasted perhaps ninety seconds. James
sustained deep lacerations to his forearm and shoulder, and a puncture wound to his ribs that missed
vital organs by centimeters. He survived. He recovered physically within weeks.

The rest has taken longer.

For the first six months after the attack, James forced himself back into the principal's office. He
told himself this was necessary, that showing fear would undermine his authority. His wife,
Margaret, watched him prepare for work each morning with growing concern. She noticed he'd begun
arriving at school earlier and earlier—now he's there by six in the morning, before anyone else,
walking the hallways with his keys in his hand like a talisman. He checks the parking lot from his
office window constantly, particularly in the late afternoon. He's installed additional security
cameras, changed the lock on his office door twice, and keeps his phone in his hand during the
school day.

The hypervigilance has become part of his routine, so integrated into his day that he barely
acknowledges it anymore. What he does acknowledge is the anger. It arrives without warning—a parent
questions a discipline decision, a teacher arrives five minutes late to a meeting, a student speaks
back in the hallway—and James finds himself responding with an intensity that surprises even him.
Last month, he snapped at his secretary over a misfiled form and immediately saw the hurt in her
face. He apologized, but the shame lingered for days.

At night, James lies awake beside Margaret, or he sleeps fitfully and wakes gasping, his shirt
soaked through. The dreams are vivid: he's back in that parking lot, but the knife keeps coming, and
this time he can't move. Or he's at school and suddenly he can't find the exits. Margaret has
stopped trying to comfort him during these episodes. She's learned that his body doesn't accept
touch after a nightmare—he flinches away instinctively, and the rejection in both directions leaves
them both wounded.

He's stopped attending the monthly principals' association meetings. The thought of sitting in a
conference room, his back to the door, makes his chest tight. He's also stopped going to the grocery
store at certain hours, stopped taking his usual evening walks through the neighborhood. Margaret
goes alone now, and James stays home, telling himself he's catching up on work. The truth is he
feels safer inside, with the doors locked and the windows covered.

What troubles James most is that he no longer recognizes himself. The man who once loved the
unexpected interactions of school life—the spontaneous conversations with students, the openness of
his office door—has become someone who plans every moment, controls every variable he can. He's a
good principal still, technically. The school runs smoothly. But the joy has drained from it,
replaced by a grinding vigilance that exhausts him.

He tells the therapist he just wants to feel normal again. To trust that the world is safe. To stop
jumping at shadows in his own building.
