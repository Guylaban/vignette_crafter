# Margaret

Margaret sits with her hands folded in her lap, her posture impeccable despite the weight she
carries. At fifty-two, she maintains the composed demeanor that has served her well on the bench for
the past fifteen years—chin level, shoulders back, a careful neutrality in her expression. But
there's a brittleness to this composure now, a sense that it might shatter if examined too closely.

Twelve years ago, Margaret took a leave of absence from her judicial position. Her marriage had
recently ended, and she felt untethered in a way that both frightened and propelled her toward
something meaningful. She joined a humanitarian organization and spent fourteen months in a conflict
zone in Southeast Asia, providing legal assistance to displaced populations and documenting human
rights violations. She was fifty years old and believed she was finally doing work that mattered.

The organization's compound was struck by artillery fire on a Tuesday morning in March. Margaret was
in the documentation center with three colleagues when the first explosion shattered the windows.
She remembers the sound—not like anything she'd heard before, a pressure that seemed to come from
inside her own chest as much as from outside. She remembers the dust, the ringing in her ears, and
then the blood on her colleague's face. Two of them died in the initial strike. A third, a young
woman named Aisha whom Margaret had been mentoring, died three hours later despite their attempts to
stop the bleeding.

Margaret was extracted the following day. She returned to her position on the bench six months
later, telling herself that she had processed the experience adequately. She had attended the
memorial service. She had written letters to the families. She had resumed her life.

But the resumption was incomplete. Margaret finds herself hypervigilant in her courtroom now,
acutely aware of exits and potential threats. When someone raises their voice during
proceedings—even a passionate attorney making an argument—her body floods with adrenaline. Her hands
sometimes tremble as she reviews case files, particularly those involving violence or loss of life.
She has presided over three murder trials since returning, and each one has left her depleted in
ways she struggles to articulate.

Sleep has become fragmented. She lies awake at three in the morning replaying details that feel
urgent and unresolved: the exact shade of Aisha's shirt, the specific words she spoke in the final
hours, whether there was something more she could have done. When she does sleep, the dreams are
vivid and disorienting—she's back in the compound, but it's also her childhood home, and the people
she's trying to save keep changing faces.

Margaret has withdrawn from her social circles. She declined the judicial conference in June, made
excuses about a heavy caseload. She hasn't had dinner with her sister in four months. Her ex-husband
reached out recently, concerned, and she responded with a curt email. There's a distance she
maintains now, a protective barrier that keeps others at arm's length. She tells herself it's
practical—she has demanding work, after all—but she recognizes it as something else: a fear that if
she lets anyone close, they might see how fragmented she's become.

What troubles her most is the guilt. She survived when Aisha didn't. She returned to a position of
comfort and authority while families in that region continued to grieve. This guilt sits beneath
everything else, a constant undertone that colors her decisions from the bench and her interactions
with those around her. She wonders sometimes if she should return to that work, as though suffering
might balance the scales. Other times she wonders if she's fundamentally broken, incapable of the
emotional regulation her position demands.
