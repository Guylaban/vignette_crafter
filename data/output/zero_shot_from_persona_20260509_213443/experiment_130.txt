# Marcus's Story

Marcus has worked in HVAC maintenance for nearly thirty years. He knows the systems that keep
buildings breathing—the ducts, the filters, the intricate networks most people never think about. He
takes pride in his work, in being the person businesses call when something breaks down. His hands
are calloused and capable, marked by decades of crawling through attics and basements,
troubleshooting problems others can't solve.

Three years ago, on an ordinary Tuesday morning, Marcus was called to a commercial office building
downtown to investigate a chemical smell reported by tenants. The building manager seemed
unconcerned—probably just a cleaning crew, he'd said. Marcus went to the basement mechanical room as
he'd done hundreds of times before. He didn't wear a respirator. There seemed no reason to.

The smell hit him immediately, acrid and wrong. Before he could retreat, his throat closed. His eyes
burned so intensely he thought they might split open. His lungs seized, and he collapsed onto the
concrete floor, gasping for air that felt like swallowing fire. Emergency responders found him
minutes later. The investigation later revealed a pesticide storage container had ruptured in an
adjacent room—a chemical not meant for indoor use, not meant for human exposure.

Marcus spent four days in the hospital. The doctors said his lungs would heal. Physically, they did.
But something else didn't.

Now, when Marcus enters a building, his body doesn't trust him anymore. Walking into a client's
basement, he finds his heart racing before he's even descended the stairs. His breath becomes
shallow and rapid, as though his lungs remember that day more vividly than his mind does. He's
started carrying a respirator everywhere, checking it obsessively—straps intact, filters sealed,
always accessible. His colleagues have noticed him becoming more cautious, sometimes overly so,
insisting on ventilation checks that seem unnecessary to them.

The worst part is that Marcus has begun avoiding jobs. When the dispatcher calls with a basement
assignment, something in his chest tightens. He makes excuses—his back is bothering him, he's
overbooked. His supervisor, patient at first, has grown frustrated. Marcus knows he's letting his
team down, knows that his avoidance is becoming a pattern he can't break.

At night, he doesn't sleep well. His wife, Linh, has mentioned it gently—how he wakes suddenly,
sometimes gasping, sometimes calling out. He dreams of that basement, of the smell, of not being
able to breathe. Some nights he just lies awake, his mind refusing to quiet, replaying the moment
again and again, searching for what he should have done differently.

During the day, unexpected smells trigger him—a cleaning product at the grocery store, someone's
perfume, exhaust fumes. His body floods with adrenaline before his mind catches up. He becomes
irritable with Linh over small things, then feels guilty for his anger. She doesn't deserve his
fear.

Marcus still goes to work. He still shows up, still does his job. But there's a fragility to it now,
a constant negotiation between what he knows he's capable of and what his body insists he can't do.
He's always been someone who solves problems, who fixes things. Now he feels broken in a way he
doesn't know how to repair.
