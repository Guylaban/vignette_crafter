# Detective Maria Reyes

Maria sits across from me with her hands folded tightly in her lap, a habit born from thirty years
of controlling her composure in interrogation rooms. At fifty-seven, she still carries herself with
the bearing of someone accustomed to authority, though lately that authority feels like a costume
she can no longer quite button correctly.

Four years ago, Maria responded to a call about an abandoned warehouse in the industrial district.
What dispatch described as a possible methamphetamine lab turned out to be something far worse—a
chemical storage facility that had been illegally dumped with industrial solvents and pesticides.
When the structure's compromised roof collapsed inward during the investigation, Maria was inside.
She inhaled deeply of the toxic cloud that billowed through the space before her partner pulled her
out. She remembers the acrid taste, the burning in her lungs, the way the world seemed to tilt as
they stumbled into fresh air.

The acute physical symptoms resolved within weeks. The doctors said she was fortunate. But Maria
knows that fortune is relative.

She tells me that sleep has become a minefield. Most nights, she lies awake until two or three in
the morning, her mind cataloging the chemicals she was exposed to—benzene, toluene, chlorinated
hydrocarbons—as if memorizing their names might somehow prevent them from damaging her. When sleep
finally comes, she dreams of that warehouse: the smell returns with olfactory vividness that jolts
her awake gasping, convinced her lungs are burning again. Her ex-husband left five years before the
exposure, but she suspects the years of her hypervigilance and emotional distance made their
marriage unsustainable long before the divorce papers were signed.

What troubles Maria most is that she no longer trusts her body to tell her the truth. She
experiences waves of physical symptoms—chest tightness, dizziness, a persistent sensation that she
cannot quite fill her lungs completely—and cannot distinguish between real physiological effects of
the exposure and her mind's interpretation of threat. She has seen three different cardiologists.
All her tests come back normal. This does not comfort her. Instead, it deepens her conviction that
something is fundamentally wrong, something the tests cannot detect.

She remains on active duty, though she has requested reassignment away from scene investigations.
Her captain accommodated this without comment, though she caught the look of concern in his eyes.
Her colleagues have stopped inviting her to the detective's bar after shifts. She knows they mean
well, but their casual banter about cases and the gallows humor that once sustained her now feels
like a language she no longer speaks.

Maria describes heightened startle responses—the sound of a car backfiring sends her into a state of
acute alarm that takes minutes to subside. She avoids the industrial district entirely, taking
circuitous routes home from the precinct. She has stopped attending the community events she once
enjoyed. Her daughter, who lives two hours away, has gently suggested that her mother seems
different now, more withdrawn, less present even when they are together.

What strikes me most is Maria's fundamental disorientation. She is a woman trained to read evidence,
to construct narratives from facts, to understand cause and effect. But her trauma has fractured
this ability. She cannot understand why her body betrays her with symptoms that medical science
cannot confirm. She cannot reconcile the detective she was—capable, controlled, trusted—with the
person she has become: uncertain, hypervigilant, imprisoned by a threat that may or may not be real.
