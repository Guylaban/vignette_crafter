# Marcus

Marcus sits in the session with his hands folded in his lap, his leg bouncing slightly beneath the
table. At fifty, he carries the weight of the past six years like a physical thing—something that
has settled into his shoulders and never quite lifted. His wife, Priya, sometimes catches him
staring at nothing, and he'll snap back to awareness as if surfacing from underwater.

It began when his daughter Anjali was fourteen. She developed a persistent fever that wouldn't
break, followed by a rash that spread across her body in the night. Marcus remembers the emergency
room vividly—the harsh fluorescent lights, the way the doctor's face changed when they said
"meningitis." He remembers Priya's hand gripping his so hard it left marks. For three weeks, Anjali
was in the pediatric ICU, and Marcus existed in a state of suspended animation, moving through hours
that felt like days.

She survived. She recovered completely, even. But Marcus did not.

In the years since, he finds himself caught in loops of vigilance that exhaust him. When Anjali
sniffles, his chest tightens. He checks on her at night, sometimes twice, listening for her
breathing. He's researched rare diseases until 2 a.m. more times than he can count. Priya has gently
suggested this might be excessive, but he cannot seem to stop himself. The thought that something
could happen to her again—that he might miss the warning signs—creates an anxiety so acute it feels
like a physical pressure behind his ribs.

His work as an animator has suffered. For twenty-five years, Marcus has found joy in creating
movement, in bringing stillness to life frame by frame. But now, sitting at his workstation, he
struggles to concentrate. His mind fractures. He'll be sketching a character's expression when
suddenly he's back in the ICU, watching Anjali's small body hooked to machines. His hands shake. He
has to stop, step away, breathe. His colleagues have noticed his productivity has dropped. He's
missed deadlines. His creative director, concerned, has suggested he take time off.

Sleep has become complicated. Marcus lies awake beside Priya, his mind generating scenarios—What if
symptoms return? What if he's not paying attention? What if it happens again and he's asleep? When
he does sleep, he dreams of the hospital. He wakes gasping, his shirt damp with sweat. Priya wakes
too, and she holds him while he tries to explain that it felt real, that Anjali was in danger, that
he couldn't reach her.

He startles easily now. A car horn makes his heart race. When his phone rings unexpectedly, he feels
a surge of panic before he can answer. He avoids hospitals entirely, even taking a different route
to work to bypass the medical complex. Being near one triggers a cascade of memories—the antiseptic
smell, the beeping monitors, the helplessness.

Marcus loves his daughter fiercely. He knows, intellectually, that she is well. But his nervous
system doesn't believe it. His body remains convinced that catastrophe is imminent, that vigilance
is the only thing standing between her and disaster. Even now, in this quiet office, part of him is
listening—for his phone, for a text, for some sign that something is wrong. The exhaustion of this
constant scanning has become his baseline. He has forgotten what it feels like to simply be calm.
