# Dr. Marcus Chen

Marcus sits in the leather chair across from me, his posture rigid despite its obvious comfort. At
fifty-eight, he carries himself with the careful precision of someone accustomed to control—a habit
formed over thirty-five years of dentistry, where millimeters matter and steadiness is everything.
Today, his hands rest on his thighs, and I notice they're trembling slightly.

Six months ago, Marcus was pulled over while driving home from his practice. He'd been checking his
phone at a red light in a neighborhood he'd driven through a thousand times. The officer approached
rapidly, hand near his weapon, shouting commands that Marcus, startled and partially deaf in one ear
from a childhood illness, couldn't fully hear. In his confusion, Marcus reached toward the glove
compartment for his registration. The officer interpreted this as a threat. What followed was a blur
of violence that Marcus describes in fragmented pieces—the officer's hands on him, his face pressed
against the hot pavement, the weight of a knee on his back, the taste of blood. He was released
after twenty minutes with a warning about "suspicious behavior." No charges were filed. No apology
was offered.

Since that afternoon, Marcus has been unable to return to his practice. The thought of sitting in
the dental chair—that position of vulnerability, reclined and exposed—makes his chest tight. He's
taken medical leave, telling his staff he has a back injury. The truth feels too complicated to
explain, even to himself. His business partner has begun asking questions about his return date.
Marcus doesn't have an answer.

His relationship with Patricia, his partner of eight years, has become strained. She finds him awake
at three in the morning, standing in the kitchen, sometimes not remembering how he got there. He's
begun sleeping in the guest room, claiming he doesn't want to disturb her, though the real reason is
that he feels safer with his back against a wall, away from windows. When she touches him
unexpectedly, he flinches. Last week, she approached him from behind while he was washing dishes,
and he spun around so violently he nearly hit her. The look on her face—fear mixed with hurt—has
haunted him since.

Loud noises send him into a state of hyperalertness that can last for hours. A car backfire on the
street sends him to the floor. Sirens make his heart race uncontrollably. He's begun avoiding his
neighborhood entirely, taking circuitous routes when he must drive, always scanning for police
vehicles. He's stopped going to the grocery store during busy times, preferring empty aisles where
he can maintain sight lines. His world has become smaller, more controlled, yet paradoxically less
safe.

What troubles Marcus most is the shame. He's a man who has spent his life in a position of authority
and expertise, someone patients trust with their mouths open and their vulnerability exposed. The
fact that he couldn't control what happened to him, that he was rendered powerless by someone with a
badge and a gun, sits in his chest like a stone. He replays the moment he reached for his
registration obsessively, wondering if he could have done something differently, said something
different, been someone different. He knows intellectually that he did nothing wrong, yet he cannot
shake the feeling that somehow, he failed.

His hands shake when he thinks about returning to work. His mind races with scenarios he cannot
control. And in the quiet hours before dawn, Marcus lies awake in the guest room, listening to the
sounds of the city, waiting for the next threat he cannot predict and cannot prevent.
