# Margaret's Unfinished Projects

Margaret sits in the waiting room with her hands folded in her lap, her posture perfect—the same
posture she's maintained for forty years in corporate offices. She's sixty-three now, still sharp,
still capable, though something has shifted beneath the surface. When she speaks about the past
eighteen months, her voice remains steady, but her fingers betray her, curling and uncurling against
the fabric of her skirt.

Three years ago, Margaret was diagnosed with stage three ovarian cancer. She remembers the moment
the radiologist said the word—cancer—with perfect clarity, though she wishes she didn't. She was in
the middle of managing the transition of a major infrastructure project, her calendar packed with
meetings, her inbox overflowing. She'd been experiencing fatigue and occasional pain, but she
attributed it to stress, to aging, to the demands of her role. When the diagnosis came, it felt like
the ground had simply vanished.

The treatment was brutal. Chemotherapy, surgery, radiation—a series of assaults on her body that she
endured with the same methodical determination she brought to her work. She made spreadsheets
tracking her appointments, her medications, her side effects. Control through documentation. But
control, she learned, was an illusion. Her body belonged to the disease and the doctors now, not to
her.

Margaret recovered, technically. The cancer went into remission. She returned to work after six
months, determined to reclaim her life, to prove that nothing had fundamentally changed. But
everything had changed.

Now, eighteen months into remission, she finds herself unable to settle. At night, she lies awake
beside her husband, David, her mind spiraling through worst-case scenarios. The cancer is
returning—she's certain of it. Every ache, every fatigue, every minor symptom becomes evidence of
recurrence. She's scheduled four additional screening appointments beyond what her oncologist
recommended, and she still doesn't feel reassured.

At work, she's become hypervigilant in a different way. She struggles to focus on her projects,
though she's been a project manager for twenty-two years. During meetings, her mind drifts to her
mortality, to the fragility of her body. She's missed deadlines—something that never happened
before—because she's spent hours researching cancer statistics and survival rates instead of
working. Her team has noticed her distraction, though they're kind enough not to mention it
directly.

She avoids certain situations now. She skips the office birthday celebrations because the casual
cheerfulness feels obscene when she's so acutely aware that life can be stolen away. She declined a
promotion that would have required travel; the thought of being away from her familiar doctors, her
established routine, sent her into a panic that surprised her with its intensity.

Margaret experiences sudden moments of overwhelming dread, usually in the afternoon around the time
she used to receive her test results. Her heart races, her breathing becomes shallow, and she has to
excuse herself from meetings. She's learned to hide these episodes, but they're becoming more
frequent.

David tries to help. He reminds her that she's healthy now, that the scans are clear, that she's
survived. But his reassurance, however well-intentioned, doesn't penetrate the deep knowing in her
body that danger is still present, that it's always present. She knows logically that he's right,
but logic feels like a tool that no longer works.

Margaret has kept working, kept functioning, kept her life running smoothly on the surface. But
underneath, something remains fractured—a fundamental trust in her own safety that cancer took from
her and hasn't returned.
