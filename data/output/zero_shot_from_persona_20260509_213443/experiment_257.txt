# A Quiet Morning

Margaret sits in her kitchen with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea that has long gone cold. The
morning light filters through the lace curtains she hung herself—a small act of reclaiming beauty in
a space that once felt dangerous. At sixty-eight, she has learned that healing is not linear, that
some mornings still arrive heavy with the weight of what happened.

For forty-three years, Margaret was a surgical nurse. She spent her career moving with purpose
through operating rooms, her hands steady, her mind sharp. She was the nurse other nurses called
when complications arose—calm, competent, unshakeable. That identity sustained her through her
marriage to David, through the early years when his drinking seemed manageable, through the moment
it stopped being manageable at all.

The escalation happened gradually, the way such things often do. A shove when dinner was late. A
backhand when she questioned his spending. By the time Margaret was fifty-five, she had become
expert at reading his moods, at making herself smaller, quieter, less likely to provoke. She told no
one at the hospital. She wore long sleeves in summer. She became someone who flinched at sudden
movements.

The final incident occurred on a Tuesday evening in March, thirteen years ago. Margaret had
mentioned—casually, she thought—that she was thinking of visiting her sister in Lagos. David's
response was swift and brutal. He threw her against the kitchen counter, his hands around her
throat, his voice in her ear saying things she has trained herself not to remember. When it ended,
Margaret lay on the floor, gasping, and something in her broke in a way that all the previous
violence had not quite accomplished.

She left him the next morning. She filed for divorce. She pressed charges, though the case moved
slowly through the system. And then she retired from nursing, unable to return to the operating room
where her hands no longer felt reliable, where the fluorescent lights triggered a sensation of being
trapped, where she could no longer trust her own body to function under pressure.

Now, thirteen years later, Margaret still wakes sometimes at three in the morning, her heart racing,
her body convinced that David is coming through the door. She has learned the names for these
experiences—hypervigilance, flashbacks, intrusive memories—though naming them does not diminish
their power. When her husband Bernard (whom she married eight years ago, after a long period of
rebuilding) comes up behind her unexpectedly, she still startles violently, her nervous system
flooding with adrenaline before her rational mind can catch up and remind her that she is safe.

The television news triggers her. Certain songs trigger her. The smell of whiskey triggers her. She
has become a student of her own avoidance, noticing how she no longer goes to the grocery store at
certain times, how she has rearranged her life around invisible boundaries that David still,
somehow, still controls.

What Margaret does not talk about much is the grief. The grief of the career she loved and could not
return to. The grief of the years she lost to fear. The grief of the woman she was before—the
steady, confident surgical nurse—and the awareness that she will never fully be that woman again.

Some mornings, like this one, Margaret sits with these losses while her tea grows cold. Bernard is
at work. The house is quiet. She is safe. And still, some part of her remains suspended in that
March evening, waiting for the sound of breaking glass.
