# Mrs. Ama Mensah

Mrs. Ama Mensah is an eighty-year-old woman who has spent the last fifty-three years as a midwife.
She carries herself with the quiet authority of someone who has guided hundreds of women through
their most vulnerable moments, who has held newborns slick with vernix and placed them into
trembling arms. Her hands are still steady, though lately she finds them shaking for reasons that
have nothing to do with age.

Three years ago, Ama was driving to a clinic in the early morning when a delivery truck ran a red
light and struck her vehicle on the driver's side. The impact was violent and sudden. She remembers
the sound—a terrible crashing that seemed to come from everywhere at once—and then the sensation of
the car folding around her like paper. She was trapped for forty-seven minutes before emergency
services extracted her. She sustained a fractured pelvis, two broken ribs, and a severe laceration
across her left shoulder. The physical injuries healed, more or less. The other injuries have not.

In the months following the accident, Ama tried to return to her work. She had delivered babies
through wars, through personal loss, through her own health crises. Surely she could manage this.
But the moment she sat in a car, her heart would begin to race in a way that felt dangerous, as
though it might burst through her ribs. Her breathing would become shallow and quick. She would grip
the steering wheel so tightly her knuckles would blanch white. She stopped driving. Her husband,
Kwame, began driving her everywhere, though the resentment in his silence was palpable.

At the clinic, things were worse. Ama would be examining a patient in labor, focused on the work she
loved, when suddenly she would be back in the car—hearing that crash, feeling the metal crushing
inward. Her vision would narrow. Her hands would tremble. Once, she had to excuse herself mid-
examination. Another time, a patient's scream during labor sent Ama into such a state of panic that
a younger colleague had to take over. The humiliation burned deeper than any physical pain.

Now, three years later, Ama has retired from midwifery, though she never imagined her career would
end this way. The nightmares come three or four times a week—she is trapped in the car, or she is
trapped in a delivery room, or the boundaries between the two blur together. She wakes gasping, her
nightgown soaked through with sweat. Kwame has learned to sleep in the guest room. She has not told
him this breaks her heart.

Ama finds herself avoiding anything that reminds her of the accident. She refuses to ride in cars
except for essential appointments. She avoids watching news reports of traffic accidents, which
means she avoids the news almost entirely. She has stopped attending church because the parking lot
triggers her anxiety. Her children worry that she is becoming reclusive, but they do not understand
what it feels like to have your body betray you so completely, to have your mind insist you are
still in danger when you are sitting safely in your living room.

There is a particular cruelty in this, she thinks. After decades of helping others through their
darkest hours, she finds herself unable to help herself. The confidence that once defined her—the
certainty in her clinical knowledge, in her ability to manage crisis—has been replaced by a
constant, gnawing sense of threat. Her body remains vigilant for a danger that has already passed,
though some part of her is not convinced it has passed at all.
