# Claire

Claire sits in the office with her hands folded tightly in her lap, her shoulders drawn up toward
her ears. At thirty-three, she has the lean, muscular build of someone accustomed to physical
demands, but there's a brittleness to her posture now, as though she might shatter if touched.

She's been a paramedic for eleven years. She loves the work—or she did. The structured chaos of
emergency response, the knowledge that she was saving lives, the camaraderie of her station crew.
These things anchored her.

Four months ago, everything fractured.

It was a routine call on a Tuesday afternoon. A sixty-seven-year-old man had fallen at home. Claire
and her partner arrived within minutes. The patient was conscious, alert, complaining of chest pain.
They lifted him onto the stretcher with practiced efficiency. Claire had done this thousands of
times. But as they were loading him into the ambulance, the stretcher suddenly lurched—her grip
slipped, or perhaps the latch hadn't engaged properly. The patient fell, his head striking the
ambulance's metal frame with a sound Claire can still hear in the quiet moments before sleep.

The man suffered a severe subdural hematoma. He survived the initial injury but spent two weeks in
intensive care. He has permanent cognitive deficits now. He doesn't recognize his own daughter.

Claire knows intellectually that accidents happen, that she followed protocol, that the
investigation cleared her of negligence. But knowing this and believing it are different things
entirely.

The first weeks were the worst. She called in sick repeatedly, something she'd never done before.
When she finally returned to work, her hands shook during patient transfers. She second-guessed
every movement, every decision. A week ago, she requested a desk assignment. She couldn't face the
ambulance anymore.

Her wife, Sarah, tries to help. They've been married for six years, and Sarah knows Claire's
rhythms, her tells. But Claire has become someone Sarah doesn't quite recognize. She wakes at three
in the morning, her heart racing, replaying the moment of the fall in excruciating detail. Sometimes
she's convinced it's happening again. Other times she lies awake, unable to sleep at all, her mind
cycling through catastrophic scenarios—what if she'd held on tighter, what if she'd checked the
latch, what if, what if, what if.

During the day, Claire moves through their house like a ghost. She's stopped going to the gym,
stopped meeting friends for coffee. She watches the news obsessively, reading stories about medical
errors and patient deaths, as though punishing herself with each article. When Sarah touches her
unexpectedly, Claire flinches. She snaps at small frustrations—a broken coffee mug, a delayed
train—with a fury that shocks them both.

The guilt is suffocating. Claire knows this man, knows his name is Robert, knows he has three
grandchildren he may never remember. She's looked at his daughter's social media, watched her life
fracture in real time. Sometimes Claire thinks it would be easier if she'd just quit, if she'd never
become a paramedic at all. Then this wouldn't have happened. Then she wouldn't be the person who
broke someone's life.

She doesn't sleep well. When she does sleep, she dreams of falling. In the dreams, it's always her
falling, always her hitting her head, always her waking up unable to remember who she is.

Sarah asked her to see someone. Claire resisted for weeks. But she's here now, hands folded, waiting
to talk about the day everything changed.
