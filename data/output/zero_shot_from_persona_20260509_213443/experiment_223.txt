# Marcus

Marcus sits in the office with his shoulders curved inward, a habit he's developed over the past
three years. At sixty-eight, he carries the weight of a man who has survived something he never
expected to survive, and the survival itself has become its own burden.

Twelve years ago, Marcus was diagnosed with stage three colon cancer. He was fifty-six then, still
working full-time as a policy analyst for the Department of Health, a position he'd held for nearly
thirty years. He remembers the colonoscopy results with crystalline clarity—the doctor's careful
words, the pamphlets about treatment options, the sensation of the room tilting slightly even though
he remained perfectly still. What he doesn't remember, with the same clarity, is much of the
chemotherapy that followed. That time exists in his mind as fragments: the chemical smell of the
infusion center, his wife Amara holding his hand, the toilet, the mirror showing a stranger with
hollowed cheeks.

He survived. The cancer went into remission. By most measures, Marcus's story should have been one
of triumph and gratitude.

But three years ago, a routine scan detected something. Not cancer—the radiologist was careful to
explain this—but a small mass that needed monitoring. The word "monitoring" became the axis around
which Marcus's life began to rotate. Every six months, another scan. Every six months, the same
suffocating wait for results, the same sensation of standing at the edge of an abyss. The mass never
grew. It never changed. Yet Marcus found himself unable to believe in its benignity.

His work as a policy analyst—work that had always engaged him, that required careful analysis of
health data and demographic trends—became increasingly difficult. He found himself unable to
concentrate on reports. Numbers that once made sense now seemed to mock him. He would sit at his
desk for hours, reading the same paragraph about healthcare access, his mind instead replaying
conversations with his oncologist, imagining scenarios where the next scan would bring bad news.

Sleep became elusive. Marcus would lie awake beside Amara, listening to her breathe, seized by the
conviction that he was dying, that he was wasting the time he had left by lying motionless in the
dark. When he did sleep, he had the same dream repeatedly: he was in the infusion chair, but the IV
was dripping something black, and he couldn't move or call for help.

Two years ago, Amara died suddenly of a stroke. Marcus was unprepared for this different death, this
one that required no warning scans or medical appointments. The shock of it seemed to crystallize
something in him. He retired from his position six months later, unable to imagine returning to the
office, unable to imagine the future at all.

Now, in his small apartment, Marcus moves through his days with a kind of suspended animation. He
avoids news stories about cancer. He cannot watch medical dramas. He has stopped attending his
church, finding the gathering of people—particularly older people—unbearable. The thought that
anyone around him might receive bad health news triggers a panic response so intense that his chest
tightens and his vision narrows.

He still receives his regular scans. The mass remains unchanged. But Marcus has learned to distrust
stability, to interpret the absence of bad news as simply a delay in its arrival. He is waiting,
always waiting, for the other shoe to drop. And in that waiting, he has become untethered from the
life he built, the work that gave him purpose, and the person he was before he learned that his body
could betray him.
