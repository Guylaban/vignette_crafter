# A Matter of Control

Robert sits in the leather chair with his hands folded precisely in his lap, a posture perfected
over forty years of public life. At sixty-eight, he carries the bearing of someone accustomed to
managing impressions, though today there's a brittleness beneath the polish. His wife insisted he
come. He wouldn't have, otherwise.

Eighteen months ago, Robert's car was struck by a delivery truck at an intersection near his
district office. The other vehicle ran a red light at what witnesses estimated was forty miles per
hour. Robert remembers the sound first—a metallic shriek that still wakes him at three in the
morning. He remembers the sensation of weightlessness as the car spun, then the crushing pressure as
metal folded around him. He was trapped for seventeen minutes before the fire department extracted
him. Three broken ribs, a fractured collarbone, and a concussion. By any measure, he was fortunate.

The physical injuries healed within months. The other part hasn't.

Robert returned to his legislative duties after six weeks, determined to demonstrate resilience.
This was his instinct—to move forward, to maintain appearances, to show constituents that nothing
could derail him. But something fundamental had fractured alongside his ribs. Now, when he drives to
the capitol building, his foot hovers anxiously over the brake. At intersections, he finds himself
holding his breath, scanning for threats. Twice, he's gripped the steering wheel so tightly that his
hands cramped for hours afterward. Last month, he asked his chief of staff to arrange for a driver.
He told colleagues it was about efficiency. It wasn't.

The hypervigilance has seeped into other spaces. During committee meetings, he finds his attention
fragmenting—one moment he's debating healthcare policy, the next he's tracking the emergency exit,
counting the bodies between himself and the door. His colleagues have noticed he's become more
irritable, quicker to anger over minor procedural disagreements. He's snapped at staff members who
didn't deserve it. This bothers him more than he admits.

Sleep is fractured. Robert lies beside his wife Catherine, who sleeps soundly, and he envies her
deeply. He drifts off around midnight, then jolts awake at 2 or 3 a.m., his heart racing, his shirt
damp with sweat. Sometimes he dreams he's still in the car, metal groaning around him, unable to
move. Other nights he simply lies there, mind cycling through scenarios—what if the truck had hit
the driver's side, what if he'd left the office two minutes earlier, what if, what if.

Catherine has been patient, but he can feel the strain. She reaches for him in bed and he flinches.
She suggests they take a weekend trip and he feels a surge of panic at the thought of being in a car
for hours. She's started sleeping in the guest room, though she frames it as needing better rest
before her book club meetings. They both know better.

The work that once energized him now feels hollow. Robert sits through legislative sessions and
experiences a strange disconnection, as though he's watching himself perform the role of politician
rather than inhabiting it. He's good at the performance—the speeches, the constituent meetings, the
careful navigation of party politics. But underneath, there's an exhaustion that sleep doesn't
touch, a sense that the ground beneath him is less solid than it once was.

He came to this office because Catherine was firm. Because he noticed himself reaching for whiskey
earlier each evening. Because he recognized, with the same analytical mind that serves him in
politics, that something has shifted and he doesn't know how to shift it back.
