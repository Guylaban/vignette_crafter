# Dr. Carlos Mendez

Carlos sits in the office with his hands folded in his lap, his wedding band catching the light as
he shifts slightly in his chair. At sixty-two, he carries himself with the measured composure of
someone accustomed to being in control—the kind of bearing you develop after thirty-five years of
holding people's lives in your hands as a surgeon. But today, there is something fragile beneath
that composure, something that cracks when he speaks about the accident.

It was eighteen months ago, a Sunday morning. Carlos had decided to drive up to the cabin in the
mountains that he and his wife, María, had owned for twenty years. He was looking forward to the
quiet, the solitude. The road was familiar—he'd driven it hundreds of times. He remembers the
weather was clear, the sun bright on the windshield. He remembers thinking about a patient he'd
operated on the day before, wondering how she was recovering. And then, nothing. A truck had crossed
the center line. The collision was violent and immediate.

When Carlos wakes up, he is in a hospital bed, and María is holding his hand. He has a fractured
collarbone, three broken ribs, and a laceration on his forehead that required twelve stitches. The
truck driver had fallen asleep at the wheel. For three weeks, Carlos thinks about nothing but the
image of the grille of that truck coming toward him, how large it seemed, how impossible it was to
avoid. He replays it. He counts the seconds. He wonders what would have happened if he'd left five
minutes earlier, five minutes later.

When he returns home, Carlos tries to resume his normal life. He returns to the hospital after eight
weeks, against his surgeon's recommendation to take longer. María notices he is different. He is
more irritable, quicker to anger over small things. He snaps at the residents during rounds in ways
he never has before. He finds himself unable to focus during consultations. His hands, which have
never trembled, now shake slightly when he's tired. He tells himself it's just the lingering effects
of the trauma, that time will heal it.

But the nightmares persist. Most nights, he dreams he is back in the car, and this time the impact
kills him. He wakes gasping, his heart racing, his shirt soaked with sweat. María has learned not to
touch him when he wakes—he startles violently, and once he nearly struck her without meaning to. Now
she simply sits up and waits for him to orient himself to the present.

Driving has become a different experience entirely. Carlos finds himself gripping the steering wheel
with white knuckles, his eyes fixed on the road ahead, hypervigilant to every movement of other
vehicles. A car changing lanes makes his heart spike. He avoids highways now, taking only surface
streets, even when it adds an hour to his commute. Twice, he has nearly caused accidents because he
braked suddenly when another vehicle came too close. He has begun making excuses to María about why
they should take her car, why she should drive.

At the hospital, Carlos notices he is becoming more withdrawn. He declines invitations to
departmental dinners. He finds himself irritable with colleagues, particularly younger surgeons who
seem to take unnecessary risks. He has started having doubts about returning to the operating room,
though he hasn't admitted this to anyone. His hands are steady when he operates, but his mind is
elsewhere—rehearsing the accident, imagining catastrophes, unable to fully inhabit the present
moment where he is supposed to be saving lives.

María watches her husband of thirty-eight years and sees someone she recognizes but doesn't quite
know anymore. He is here, but he is also somewhere else, trapped in those few seconds before impact.
