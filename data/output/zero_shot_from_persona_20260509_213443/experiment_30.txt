# Marcus

Marcus is a fifty-six-year-old classical violinist who has spent the last thirty years performing
with regional orchestras and teaching at a conservatory. He carries himself with the careful posture
of someone accustomed to holding an instrument, though lately his shoulders have begun to curve
inward, as if he's trying to make himself smaller. His wife, Catherine, whom he's been married to
for twenty-eight years, sits beside him during our sessions, her hand occasionally reaching over to
touch his arm—a gesture that sometimes makes him flinch.

Three years ago, Marcus attended a chamber music festival in another state. He was invited to
perform in a master class led by a renowned conductor, an honor he'd anticipated for months. After a
late rehearsal one evening, a senior festival administrator—someone with significant institutional
power—invited him back to his private residence under the pretense of discussing future performance
opportunities. Once there, the man sexually assaulted Marcus. The assault was brief but violent, and
Marcus left immediately, driving home through the night in a state of shock and confusion.

For the first six months, Marcus told no one. He continued teaching, continued performing, but
something fundamental had fractured. He would sit with his violin, an instrument he'd loved since
childhood, and feel nothing. The muscle memory remained—his fingers still found the notes—but the
joy had evaporated. He began canceling performances, citing illness. Catherine noticed his
withdrawal but didn't press him. When he finally told her what had happened, she wept, but Marcus
felt oddly detached from her response, as though he were describing something that had happened to
someone else.

The nightmares began almost immediately after disclosure. Marcus would wake in a cold sweat, his
body rigid, unable to distinguish between the dream and the memory. Some nights he doesn't sleep at
all, instead sitting in the dark living room with the television on for company. Catherine has
learned not to startle him when he's in these states; a sudden touch can send him into a panic that
takes hours to subside.

Crowds have become unbearable. He attended a concert at the conservatory last month—he'd promised
one of his students—and spent the entire performance in a state of hypervigilance, scanning faces,
tracking exits, convinced something terrible was about to happen. His heart rate remained elevated
for hours afterward. He no longer attends faculty meetings or social events. The thought of
returning to the festival, of potentially seeing the man again, creates a physical sensation of
dread that radiates through his chest.

What troubles Marcus most is his relationship with his violin. He still teaches, though he's reduced
his schedule to half-time. When he plays now, he's acutely aware of his body in space, of his
vulnerability. The instrument, which once felt like an extension of himself, now feels like a
reminder of exposure. He's stopped performing publicly entirely. His colleagues have stopped asking
why.

He describes a persistent sense of danger that doesn't seem to match reality. He checks locks
multiple times. He avoids being alone with male colleagues. He's hyperaware of his surroundings in
ways that exhaust him. There's a persistent numbness punctuated by sudden waves of anger—usually at
himself, for not fighting back, for not reporting sooner, for continuing to show up at work as
though nothing happened.

Catherine watches him struggle and feels helpless. Marcus watches himself disappear and feels
ashamed.
