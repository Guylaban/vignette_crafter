# Marcus

Marcus sits in the therapy office with his hands folded tightly in his lap, his calloused fingers
bearing the marks of eighteen years in carpentry. At thirty-one, he has the weathered look of
someone who has spent decades in the sun, though it has only been four months since the night that
changed everything.

He was working late at a residential renovation in the city's east side, finishing cabinet
installations for a client who needed the job completed before moving day. Around eleven p.m.,
Marcus stepped outside for air, still wearing his work apron. He was thinking about the mitered
joints he'd cut that afternoon, mentally reviewing whether they would fit flush when he returned to
the workshop the next morning. A police cruiser pulled up suddenly, and officers emerged with
weapons drawn, shouting commands that didn't match what Marcus was doing. He wasn't holding
anything. He was simply standing there, breathing in the cool night air.

What happened in the next ninety seconds has become the organizing principle of Marcus's life. He
was thrown to the ground, his face pressed against concrete. His arm was wrenched behind his back
with enough force that he heard something pop in his shoulder. He was screaming that he worked
there, that he was a carpenter, that he wasn't doing anything wrong. The officers didn't seem to
hear him. There was a case of mistaken identity—they were looking for someone else, someone who had
committed a robbery two blocks away. When they finally realized their mistake, they helped him up,
apologized briefly, and drove away.

Now, four months later, Marcus cannot work. He tells his wife, Nira, that his shoulder still hurts,
and it does, but that isn't why he isn't working. He hasn't returned to the renovation site. He
hasn't picked up his tools. The thought of being outside at dusk makes his chest tight. His hands
shake in ways they never did before, even when he's not thinking about that night.

He startles at sudden sounds—the neighbor's car door slamming, Nira's footsteps when she comes home
from her job at the bank. He lies awake most nights, his mind replaying the moment the officers
emerged from the car, the weight of a body he couldn't control pressing him down. When he does
sleep, he dreams he's still on the ground, that the officers haven't realized their mistake, that no
one is coming to help him.

Nira has stopped asking when he'll go back to work. She's taken on more hours at the bank to cover
the mortgage. Marcus knows this. He feels the weight of it constantly, another pressure added to the
ones already crushing him. He wants to work. He misses the smell of fresh-cut wood, the satisfying
click of a nail set perfectly. He misses feeling like himself—competent, useful, a man who built
things with his hands.

But when he imagines going back outside with his tools, when he pictures himself on a ladder or in
an open work space, his body floods with the same terror he felt on that concrete. His breath
becomes shallow. His mind splits between the present and that ninety seconds. He cannot seem to find
his way back to being the person he was before, the carpenter who loved his work and his life.
Instead, he sits in his living room in the middle of the day, waiting for something to change,
unable to move forward and unable to go back.
