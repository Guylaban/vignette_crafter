# Maria's Story

Maria manages the Riverside Hotel downtown, a mid-sized property where she's worked her way up from
front desk clerk over fifteen years. She's competent, organized, someone staff and guests rely on.
But for the past two years, she's been running on fumes, though few people know it.

It started during a late shift three years ago. She was closing out the business center around
midnight when a guest—a man who'd seemed unremarkable during check-in—followed her into the back
office. What happened next fractured something fundamental in how she moves through the world. He
pinned her against the filing cabinet. It was over in minutes, but those minutes have stretched into
years.

Maria didn't report it immediately. She showered at work, changed clothes, and finished her shift.
The next morning, she told herself it was survivable, that reporting would mean police interviews
and hotel investigations and her name attached to something ugly. She'd built her career on being
reliable, unshakeable. Instead, she became someone who checks door locks three times and listens for
footsteps.

Now, when she's alone in the office—which is often in her role—her chest tightens. She positions
herself near the door. Sometimes she asks a front desk clerk to wait nearby, inventing reasons. Her
staff has noticed she's different about security protocols, more rigid, implementing new procedures
that seem excessive. She doesn't tell them why.

The hotel environment, once her domain, has become a landscape of threats. Guest complaints that she
would have handled smoothly now send her into spiraling worry. A raised voice in the hallway makes
her freeze. She's hyperaware of men's movements, their proximity, their intentions. She finds
herself studying faces, trying to predict danger. The man who assaulted her was never prosecuted—he
checked out the next day—and she sometimes scans news websites, irrationally afraid she'll see him
again.

Her relationship with her partner has strained under the weight of what she can't quite explain. He
notices she startles at touch, that she's withdrawn, that she sometimes goes rigid during intimacy.
Maria wants to be close to him but her body has other ideas. She lies awake at night, her mind
cycling through what-ifs and worst-case scenarios. Sleep comes in fragments. She wakes gasping,
sometimes unsure where she is.

Work has become both her anchor and her torment. She needs the structure and control it provides,
yet every shift carries an undercurrent of dread. She's called in sick more often than she'd like,
and on days she does go in, she's exhausted in a way that sleep doesn't fix. She's considered
leaving hospitality entirely, but that would mean admitting that what happened changed her, that it
took something from her that she can't reclaim.

Maria hasn't told her family. In her culture, there's a weight to these things—a shame she
intellectually knows isn't hers to carry, but carries anyway. She's also never formally told anyone
what happened, not a therapist, not a doctor. Naming it aloud feels like giving it more power, like
stepping into a victim identity she's been resisting.

She functions. She shows up. She manages the hotel with the same attention to detail she always has.
But underneath, she's vigilant and afraid, cycling between numbness and panic, trying to outrun a
memory that lives in her body whether she acknowledges it or not.
