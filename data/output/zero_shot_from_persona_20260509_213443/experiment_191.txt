# Marcus

Marcus has been driving a city bus for twenty-eight years. He knows the routes by heart—the timing
of lights, the particular dip in the road on Maple Street, where the afternoon sun hits the
windshield just right. His regulars greet him by name. He takes pride in the job, in getting people
where they need to go safely.

Three years ago, Marcus worked the late shift. It was a Tuesday night in October, around eleven
o'clock, when he locked up the depot and walked toward the employee parking lot. The lot was poorly
lit—something he'd mentioned to management before, though nothing had changed. A man approached him
asking for directions. Marcus, helpful by nature, stopped to answer. Within seconds, two others
emerged from the darkness. They forced him into a van parked nearby.

What happened over the next two hours exists in his mind as fragments and gaps. He remembers the
smell of the van's interior, the cold metal of a tool against his ribs, voices that seemed to come
from underwater. He remembers the pain and the overwhelming sensation of helplessness—of his body no
longer being his own. He was released near the highway, badly injured. A passing driver found him.

The physical wounds healed within months. The other injuries have not.

Marcus returned to work after six weeks of medical leave. His wife, Jennifer, thought it would
help—that the routine would ground him. For the first few weeks, he managed. But then the
hypervigilance began. Every passenger who boarded suddenly felt like a threat. He'd notice a man's
hands, the way someone stood too close, the tone of a voice. His heart would race without warning.
He started checking the back of the bus obsessively before starting his route, looking under seats,
in the storage areas. Once, he pulled over mid-route because a passenger's cologne reminded him of
the van.

The intrusive thoughts came next. At random moments—stopped at a red light, eating dinner with
Jennifer, lying in bed at three in the morning—he'd be back there. Not remembering it; living it.
His body would respond as if it were happening again: his muscles tensing, sweat pooling, his
breathing becoming shallow and panicked. Jennifer would find him sitting in the dark in the garage,
unable to explain what had happened.

Sleep became impossible. He'd lie awake for hours, then drift off only to wake in terror from
nightmares he couldn't quite remember but whose emotional residue clung to him through the morning.
He stopped going to the gym, stopped meeting friends for beer on Fridays. The world felt
fundamentally different—less safe, less knowable. He couldn't trust his own judgment anymore. The
man who'd seemed friendly, helpful, open—that man had been vulnerable in the worst way possible.

Now, at fifty-three, Marcus still drives his bus, though some days feel like an act of will. He's
called in sick more often than he'd like to admit. Jennifer notices how he checks the locks on the
house multiple times before bed, how he positions himself in restaurants facing the door, how he
flinches when she touches him unexpectedly from behind. She's suggested therapy several times. He's
considered it, though part of him believes that what happened is simply something he'll have to
carry now—a permanent weight that's settled into his chest and won't lift.

He still knows the routes by heart. But the man who knows them has changed in ways he's still
struggling to understand.
