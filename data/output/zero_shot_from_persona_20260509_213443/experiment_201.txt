# María's Interrupted Thread

María sits in the afternoon light filtering through her studio windows, her hands resting on a bolt
of emerald silk that arrived this morning. At seventy, she still designs—smaller collections now,
mostly custom pieces for longtime clients who trust her eye. But the silk remains untouched. She's
been staring at it for twenty minutes, thinking about the last time she felt truly present with
fabric.

It was three years ago, during the riots that swept through the city. María was working late in her
studio downtown, hand-stitching beading onto a wedding gown for her granddaughter's ceremony. Around
eight in the evening, the sounds began—car alarms, breaking glass, shouting that grew closer. She
locked the studio door and moved away from the front windows, continuing to work in the back room as
the chaos grew louder outside.

Then came the screaming. A young man—couldn't have been more than twenty—crashed through her front
window, running from something or someone. He was bleeding heavily from his arm, his eyes wide and
terrified. María froze. She watched as he stumbled toward her back room, his hand leaving a trail of
blood across her white tile floor. She didn't move. She couldn't move. In those suspended seconds,
she made a choice—she stepped back, deeper into the storage room, and let him pass. She heard him
collapse somewhere beyond her sight. She heard his breathing change. She heard him die.

The police arrived fifteen minutes later. They found him. They found her, too, still holding the
needle and thread, still wearing her reading glasses, still covered in the beading she'd been sewing
when his life ended a few rooms away.

Now, three years later, María's hands shake when she threads a needle. Not always—sometimes she
forgets and works for an hour without noticing anything unusual. But then a car backfires outside,
or she hears raised voices from the street, and her hands betray her. The tremor returns. The needle
becomes impossible to control.

Sleep is fragmented. She lies beside her husband, Roberto, listening to the neighborhood sounds that
most people ignore. Every siren makes her body rigid. She's learned to slip out of bed without
waking him, to sit in the kitchen with coffee at three in the morning, waiting for daylight to make
her feel safer.

She hasn't returned to the original studio. Instead, she works from a small room in their home now,
on the second floor, away from street level. Her clients understand that she's retired from large
commissions, though she doesn't explain why. How would she explain that she can no longer be in a
room with large windows? That she flinches when someone moves too quickly near her?

The work itself has changed too. She still designs, but the joy has become something harder to
access. There are moments—usually in the early morning when the house is quiet—when she finds
herself absorbed in a pattern or a color combination, and she forgets. For those brief hours, she's
the María who loved nothing more than the problem-solving of a difficult seam, the satisfaction of
transforming a vision into something real.

But the emerald silk sits untouched. She's been afraid to begin, afraid of what might interrupt her
again, afraid of what it means that she let someone die while she stood frozen in her own space.
