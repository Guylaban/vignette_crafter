# James

James sits in the leather chair with his legs crossed, uncrossed, then crossed again. His fingers
worry at the cuff of his Comme des Garçons shirt—a piece he designed three years ago, back when his
hands still knew how to create without trembling. He's fifty-one now, though he looks older. The
silver at his temples has spread like spilled paint.

Two years and four months ago, his husband Marcus suffered a severe stroke while James was at his
studio in SoHo. James was in a design meeting when the hospital called. He remembers the way the
room tilted, how his assistant's voice sounded like it was coming through water. Marcus was thirty-
eight. There had been no warning signs.

The first weeks were a blur of ICU waiting rooms and machines that beeped in rhythms that still
invade James's sleep. Marcus survived, but the man who came home was fragmented—his right side
weakened, his speech halting and uncertain. James learned the word "aphasia" the way other people
learn their children's names. He learned it by living it every single day.

James stopped going to the studio. His partner initially understood, then gradually didn't. The
spring collection went to another designer. The autumn line was cancelled. His name, once
recognizable in certain circles, began to fade from the industry conversation. He tells himself he
doesn't care, but his hands know better. They shake when he tries to sketch. The familiar ache of
creation has become something else entirely—something that feels too much like abandonment.

At night, James lies awake while Marcus sleeps fitfully beside him. He listens to his husband's
breathing, waiting for it to stop. He has learned that the heart can betray you without warning,
that bodies are unreliable and terrible. Sometimes he gets up and checks Marcus's pulse at the
wrist, at the neck. He knows this is irrational. He does it anyway. Three, sometimes four times a
night.

The nightmares come in two varieties. In the first, James is back in that design meeting, and when
he finally reaches the hospital, Marcus is already gone. In the second, Marcus is awake but
completely unreachable—his eyes looking directly at James while his mouth forms words that never
arrive. James wakes from these drenched and gasping, his heart performing acrobatics in his chest.

He has stopped answering calls from colleagues. The emails pile up, unanswered. His therapist
suggested he might consider returning to design in some capacity, and James felt something between
rage and despair. How could he sit at a desk sketching hemlines and silhouettes when Marcus
struggles to form the word "water"? The guilt is suffocating—guilt that he survived intact, guilt
that he resents the caregiving that has consumed his life, guilt about the guilt itself.

Marcus tries. He works with a speech therapist twice a week, and there have been small
victories—words recovered, movements regained. James celebrates these moments with genuine joy that
feels increasingly foreign in his own chest. But the joy never lasts long. It's always eclipsed by
the knowledge of how fragile everything is, how quickly a Tuesday can become a before and after.

James's mother suggested he join a support group. He hasn't gone. He isn't sure what he would say to
other people who have watched their worlds collapse. He isn't sure he knows how to be anyone other
than the man he has become—vigilant, terrified, and utterly still.
