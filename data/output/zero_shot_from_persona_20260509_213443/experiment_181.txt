# Marcus

Marcus sits in the therapy office with his hands folded tightly in his lap, a habit he's developed
over the past eighteen months. At forty, he carries himself with the physical competence of someone
trained to manage crises—shoulders squared, posture alert—yet there's a brittleness underneath, as
though he might fracture if someone pressed too hard.

Seven years ago, Marcus's younger brother Karim was assaulted outside a nightclub. Marcus wasn't
there. He was working a night shift at the station, running routine calls, doing what he'd done for
fifteen years as a paramedic. The call came from his sister-in-law. Karim was at County Hospital.
Critical condition. When Marcus arrived, his brother had suffered severe head trauma and internal
bleeding. Karim died three hours later without regaining consciousness.

The first weeks after the funeral, Marcus functioned mechanically. He returned to work after two
weeks—his captain didn't push back, understanding that some people process grief through routine.
But something shifted in Marcus's mind during those early shifts. Every call became a potential
version of his brother's final moments. A motorcycle accident on the interstate, and Marcus found
himself unable to approach the patient without his hands trembling. A young man with a head wound,
and Marcus's vision narrowed, his breathing became shallow. He knew the anatomy too well. He knew
what internal bleeding looked like. He knew how quickly someone could slip away.

Within months, Marcus began experiencing intrusive images—not just of his brother, but of patients
he'd treated years earlier, now recontextualized through his trauma. He'd be at home with his wife,
Layla, and suddenly he'd be back at County Hospital, watching Karim's monitors flatline. The
transitions happened without warning. A song on the radio. A news report about violence. Even the
smell of antiseptic in a grocery store could trigger a cascade of panic that left him drenched in
sweat, convinced something terrible was about to happen.

His sleep deteriorated. Marcus would lie awake next to Layla, afraid to close his eyes, afraid of
the nightmares that came when he did. He started avoiding the station's break room, where colleagues
discussed difficult calls. He couldn't bear the casual discussions of trauma that had once been part
of his professional identity. The job that had defined him for fifteen years now felt like a
minefield.

Layla noticed the changes first—the way he'd flinch at sudden sounds, how he'd become hypervigilant
about their teenage daughter's whereabouts, checking his phone obsessively when she was out. She
noticed how he'd stopped attending family gatherings, how the mention of his brother's name could
send him into a withdrawn silence that lasted hours. She tried to reach him, but Marcus found
himself unable to articulate what was happening. How could he explain to his wife that his own
competence—his training, his experience, his ability to save lives—felt like a cruel joke when it
couldn't save the person who mattered most?

Now, eighteen months after beginning therapy, Marcus still struggles with the weight of what he
carries. He's taken medical leave from the station, a decision that felt like another loss. Some
days are better than others. He can sit with Layla for an entire evening without his mind
fragmenting. He's learning to recognize the early signs of hyperarousal, to breathe through the
panic before it consumes him. But the images remain vivid. The guilt persists—a whisper that he
should have been there, should have known, should have been able to fix it.
