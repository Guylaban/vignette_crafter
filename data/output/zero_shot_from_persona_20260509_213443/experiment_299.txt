# A Mother's Vigil

Mei sits in her small fabric shop near the edge of the city, arranging bolts of silk for the third
time this morning. The morning light catches the jewel tones—emerald, sapphire, crimson—colors that
should bring her joy. Instead, she finds herself checking her phone again. Her daughter Lin is
twenty-eight now, healthy and living two hours away, but Mei still needs to know she's okay.

Three years ago, Lin was admitted to the hospital with acute leukemia. Mei remembers the exact
moment the doctor said the word—how the fluorescent lights seemed to flicker and hum, how she
couldn't feel her legs. She was fifty-four then, still running the shop she'd opened twenty years
earlier, a place where neighborhood women came not just for fabric but for conversation, for
community. That life feels like it belonged to someone else.

The first months of Lin's treatment are a blur now—a succession of hospital corridors, beeping
machines, and a daughter whose hair fell out in clumps. Mei slept in a vinyl chair beside Lin's bed,
her back aching, her mind refusing to quiet. She would lie awake calculating survival rates,
imagining worst-case scenarios with a clarity that felt almost prophetic. What if the chemotherapy
didn't work? What if there was a relapse? The "what ifs" came in waves, each one more elaborate and
terrifying than the last.

She closed the shop for six months. Her husband David wanted her to rest, but rest felt impossible,
felt like abandonment. She needed to be present, to will Lin back to health through sheer force of
maternal attention. When Lin finally went into remission, Mei felt something shift—not relief
exactly, but a different kind of fear. A fear that had nowhere to go.

She reopened the shop last year, but it's not the same. Some days, Mei can barely focus on
customers. A woman will come in asking about cotton prints, and Mei's mind will suddenly snap to
memories of Lin's port catheter, the way the nurses would access it with practiced efficiency.
She'll excuse herself to the back room, where she sits among the storage boxes and tries to breathe.
Her hands shake. Her chest tightens. The fear that something will happen to Lin—that this remission
is temporary, that she's somehow jinxing her daughter by feeling even momentarily okay—sits beneath
everything like a stone.

At night, Mei lies awake while David sleeps beside her. She's learned to do this quietly, so as not
to disturb him. She rehearses emergency protocols, thinks through what she'd do if Lin called with
bad news, if the cancer came back. She's read medical journals at two in the morning. She's watched
videos of survivorship stories, mining them for hope and also for hidden warnings. Some nights she
gets four hours of sleep. Other nights, none.

The hypervigilance extends everywhere now. She notices every cough, every moment of fatigue in
herself and David. When Lin doesn't answer her phone immediately, Mei's heart races with a certainty
that something is wrong. She's called the hospital several times in the past year—ostensibly to ask
questions about follow-up care, really just to hear a voice confirm that Lin's latest scans were
clear.

Her body has become a stranger to her. She startles at sudden sounds in the shop. She's lost weight,
though she cooks the same meals as always. She's aware that she's not the person her customers
remember, the woman who used to laugh easily and recommend fabrics with an artist's passion. Now she
moves through the shop like she's underwater, present but distant, always listening for the phone,
always waiting for the next crisis that her mind insists is coming.
