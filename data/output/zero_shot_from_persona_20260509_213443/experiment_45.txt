# Marcus

Marcus sits in the waiting room with his shoulders slightly hunched, one leg bouncing with a steady
rhythm. At thirty-seven, he has the weathered look of someone who has given much of himself to
others—his face lined in ways that suggest more than just the passage of time. He works as a social
ministry worker at a large Catholic organization, coordinating support programs for vulnerable
families in the community. His colleagues describe him as dedicated, sometimes to the point of
exhaustion.

Three years ago, Marcus was in a relationship with Daniel, a man he believed he would build a life
with. They had been together for five years, and though there were tensions, Marcus had convinced
himself they would work through them. He was, after all, trained to help people navigate difficult
relationships. He understood conflict, understood trauma. He thought he could apply that knowledge
to his own life.

The night everything changed, they had argued about money—about Daniel's spending, about Marcus's
refusal to give him access to more funds. Daniel had been drinking. The argument escalated quickly,
in ways Marcus still struggles to articulate. What he remembers clearly is the moment Daniel's fist
connected with his face, the shock of it more than the pain. Then Daniel's hands around his throat,
pushing him against the kitchen counter. Marcus remembers thinking, distantly, that this wasn't
real, that this couldn't be happening to him.

Since that night, Marcus has not been the same.

He lies awake most nights, his body tense, waiting for something he cannot name. When he does sleep,
he wakes gasping, his sheets soaked with sweat, his heart pounding as though he is still pressed
against that counter. He has taken to sleeping with the bedroom door locked and a lamp on low in the
corner. Sometimes he wakes and forgets where he is, and the panic takes several minutes to recede.

At work, Marcus is hypervigilant in ways his colleagues don't fully understand. He startles at
sudden movements or loud voices. During a staff meeting last month, when a coworker stood up
suddenly to grab something from a shelf, Marcus flinched so visibly that the room fell silent. He
made a joke to cover it, but the shame lingered for hours. He finds himself scanning rooms when he
enters them, noting exits, assessing potential threats. It is exhausting and automatic.

He has withdrawn from the social aspects of his work—the team lunches, the evening volunteer
trainings. His supervisor has gently asked if everything is okay. Marcus says yes, that he is simply
focused on his caseload. The truth is that being around others, particularly in enclosed spaces,
triggers an overwhelming sense of dread. He worries about being trapped, about not being able to
escape. Crowds make his chest tight.

His ex-partner has moved away, remarried even, but Marcus still feels unsafe. He has caught himself
checking his car before getting in, looking over his shoulder on the street, tensing when someone
stands too close to him. Rationally, he knows Daniel is no longer a threat. But his body has not
received that message.

Marcus has also begun to question his own judgment in ways that haunt him. How did he not see this
coming? How did he, someone trained to recognize signs of abuse in others, miss them in his own
relationship? There is a deep shame that accompanies these questions, a feeling that he should have
known better, should have been smarter, stronger. He finds himself replaying conversations, looking
for the moment he should have left, the warning signs he ignored.

He is here, now, because a friend finally convinced him that he needed help—that what he is
experiencing is not weakness or failure, but a response to trauma that deserves attention.
