# Margaret

Margaret sits in the studio she shares with two other musicians, waiting for her turn to record a
cello part for a chamber ensemble's upcoming album. At forty-six, she has built a quiet reputation
as a reliable session musician and private instructor in her city. The studio is familiar—she has
been coming here for eight years—yet something about the fluorescent lights and the close quarters
makes her shoulders tense. She reminds herself this is just a recording session, the kind she has
done hundreds of times.

Three years ago, Margaret was rehearsing for a concert series when she began experiencing what she
thought was simple fatigue. Her energy drained week by week. Within a month, she could barely make
it through a full rehearsal without needing to sit down. The diagnosis came swiftly: acute leukemia.
The word itself felt like a note struck in a minor key, something discordant and wrong.

The chemotherapy started immediately. Margaret remembers the hospital room more vividly than she
remembers her own home from that period. She recalls the IV stands, the way her hands trembled when
she tried to hold a pencil, the absolute certainty one night at 3 a.m. that her body was simply
shutting down. Her husband sat beside her bed most nights, but she was alone in the particular
terror of feeling her own body betray her.

She survived. After eighteen months of treatment, the cancer went into remission. Medically, she was
cleared to return to normal life. She picked up her cello six months after her last chemotherapy
session.

But normal has not returned.

Now, three years later, Margaret finds herself hypervigilant in ways she cannot quite explain to
people who ask how she is doing. A small ache in her shoulder becomes a harbinger of relapse. She
monitors her body constantly, cataloging every sensation—a swollen lymph node, unusual fatigue, a
persistent cough. She has had multiple unnecessary blood tests because the anxiety becomes
unbearable without them. Her general practitioner knows her by name now, and though he is kind, she
sees the gentle concern in his eyes when she requests another screening.

The intrusive thoughts arrive without warning. Sometimes, sitting at a traffic light or in the
middle of teaching a student, Margaret is suddenly back in the hospital, tasting the metallic
quality of the chemotherapy drugs, feeling the cold of the IV line. Her heart races. She has to pull
over or excuse herself to the bathroom.

Sleep has become complicated. She lies awake cataloging her symptoms, or she has nightmares about
the hospital—dreams where the chemotherapy never ends, where the doctors tell her the cancer has
returned and spread. She wakes gasping, her nightshirt soaked through. Her husband has learned not
to touch her when she startles awake; it only makes the panic worse.

She has withdrawn from the chamber ensemble she played with for fifteen years. The rehearsals felt
suffocating—too many people, too much enclosed space, too many reminders that she had lost time,
lost certainty, lost the person she was before. Playing alone is safer. Teaching private students in
her home is manageable because she controls the environment.

Margaret knows intellectually that she is healthy now. The doctors have told her this repeatedly.
The cancer markers are stable. She should be grateful. She is grateful. But gratitude and terror are
not opposites; they exist together in her, tangled and inseparable. She picks up her cello and
begins to play, her fingers finding the familiar positions. The music is beautiful and technical and
distant—a conversation she is having with herself about survival, about a body that once betrayed
her, about the impossibility of ever feeling safe inside her own skin again.
