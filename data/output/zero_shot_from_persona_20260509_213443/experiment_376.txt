# Margaret

Margaret sits in the waiting room with her hands folded neatly in her lap, her posture rigid despite
the comfortable chair. At forty-eight, she has the composed appearance of someone accustomed to
presenting a careful version of herself to the world. She's a tutor—has been for nearly twenty
years—and she carries that professional demeanor like armor.

The trauma began seven years ago, though Margaret doesn't mark time that way. She marks it by before
and after. Before, she was someone who believed in second chances, who saw the best in people. Her
husband, David, was charming in the early years of their marriage. But alcohol changed him. Or
perhaps it revealed something that had always been there.

The night that fractured everything happened on a Tuesday. Margaret had corrected David about
something trivial—she can't even remember what now—and he'd grabbed her by the throat and slammed
her against the kitchen counter. The violence was sudden and complete. She remembers the shock more
than the pain, the way her mind couldn't reconcile what was happening with the person standing in
front of her. When he finally released her, she crumpled to the floor, gasping. He left the house.
She sat there for three hours, unable to move, unable to call anyone.

She stayed for another four years after that. There were apologies, promises, counseling sessions he
quit attending. There were long stretches of peace that made her believe things might change. But
the violence returned in cycles—sometimes months apart, sometimes weeks. Each time, Margaret would
tell herself it was her fault, that she'd provoked him, that she should have been more careful. She
told no one. Her family in Southeast Asia knew nothing. Her colleagues at the tutoring center saw
only the same competent, reserved woman who arrived on time and prepared her lessons meticulously.

Three years ago, after an incident that left her with a fractured wrist, Margaret finally left. The
divorce was quiet, settled without drama. She moved into a small apartment and returned to her work.
On the surface, everything appeared to resume. She tutors high school students in mathematics and
English, preparing them for exams with the same precision she brings to everything. Her students
like her. Parents request her specifically.

But Margaret has not returned to herself.

She startles at sudden movements. When a student drops a textbook, her heart races and her hands
shake. She's hypervigilant in ways she doesn't fully acknowledge—always aware of exits, of who is
behind her, of potential threats in ordinary spaces. Loud voices make her freeze. She's taken to
wearing her hair down to cover the thin scar on her neck, though she knows most people wouldn't
notice it.

Sleep is fragmented. She lies awake for hours, her mind cycling through the same memories, or she
wakes gasping from nightmares she can't quite recall but that leave her drenched in sweat. Some
nights she doesn't bother trying to sleep at all, instead grading papers or preparing lesson plans
in the quiet hours before dawn.

She's withdrawn from her husband in every way except legally—they remain married because divorce
felt like failure, and Margaret has always been someone who sees things through. But she flinches
when he touches her. She's become someone she doesn't recognize, someone smaller and more
frightened. She wonders sometimes if this is who she'll be forever: a woman going through the
motions of her life while something essential remains trapped in that kitchen, gasping for breath.
