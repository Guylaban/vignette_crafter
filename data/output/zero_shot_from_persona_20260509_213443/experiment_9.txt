# Marcus

Marcus sits in the waiting room each week with his laptop open, even though he's not working. He
tells himself he's staying productive, but really he's just trying to keep his hands busy. At
thirty-seven, he has the lean, slightly worn appearance of someone who hasn't quite settled into
middle age—his dark hair is beginning to gray at the temples, and there's a tension around his eyes
that doesn't fully release even when he smiles.

Three years ago, Marcus was diagnosed with stage three melanoma. The word "cancer" had never been
part of his vocabulary before, and even now it feels like something that happened to someone else,
even though the scar on his shoulder—a constant reminder—tells a different story.

He was working as a database administrator for a financial services company then, the kind of job
that exists in the background of most people's lives. Marcus liked it that way. He was good at
organizing systems, at making sense of complex data structures, at solving problems that had clear
solutions. His ex-wife used to joke that he loved databases more than he loved people, and maybe
there was some truth to that. But the cancer changed something fundamental about his ability to
control anything.

The surgery came first. Then the chemotherapy. Marcus remembers sitting in the infusion center,
watching the poison drip into his veins, and feeling like he was watching his own body betray him in
real time. His colleagues sent flowers. His ex-wife visited twice, then less frequently. The months
blurred together in a haze of nausea, hair loss, and a peculiar kind of fear that never fully went
away.

He's been in remission for two years now. The scans come back clear. His oncologist uses the word
"cured," though they both know it's more complicated than that. But Marcus can't seem to move past
it. At night, he lies awake convinced that the cancer is coming back, that he can feel it growing
somewhere in his body, invisible and waiting. Sometimes he gets out of bed at three in the morning
and searches his body for lumps, running his fingers across his skin until he's exhausted.

At work, he finds it harder to concentrate. The database systems that once felt like puzzles to
solve now feel meaningless. What does it matter if he optimizes a financial transaction when his own
body might be shutting down? He's taken more sick days in the past year than in the previous five
years combined. His manager has started to notice. There have been gentle conversations about
"getting back to normal."

But Marcus doesn't know what normal is anymore. He's hyperaware of his body in ways he never was
before—every headache is a tumor, every fatigue is the cancer returning. He startles easily. Loud
noises in the office send his heart racing. When he sees other cancer patients in public, he feels a
crushing sense of dread, as if their illness is contagious, as if proximity to their suffering means
he's doomed to return to his own.

The divorce finalized six months after his last chemotherapy treatment. His ex-wife said she loved
him but couldn't live with someone who was no longer present, even when he was physically there.
Marcus didn't argue. He wasn't sure he was present anymore either.

Now he comes to therapy because his doctor suggested it, because the anxiety is worse, because he's
starting to wonder if there's a way to live again that doesn't feel like waiting for the other shoe
to drop. He still goes to work, still maintains his systems, still shows up. But inside, part of him
remains in that infusion center, watching the poison drip, waiting for his body to betray him again.
