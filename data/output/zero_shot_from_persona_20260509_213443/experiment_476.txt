# Marcus

Marcus sits with his hands folded in his lap, his broad shoulders curved inward in a way that seems
at odds with the authority his parole officer's badge represents. At thirty-four, he carries himself
with the practiced composure of someone accustomed to managing difficult situations—de-escalating
conflicts, reading body language, maintaining control. But his eyes tell a different story. They
dart toward the door occasionally, and there's a tightness around them that suggests he hasn't slept
well in some time.

Three years ago, Marcus was in a relationship he thought would be permanent. His partner was
charming, ambitious, and for the first two years, they seemed to fit together well. Marcus had built
a solid career as a parole officer in the city, taking pride in his work helping men reintegrate
into society. He understood the complexity of human behavior, the ways trauma and circumstance
shaped choices. He thought he could navigate anything.

The first time his partner hit him, Marcus was shocked into silence. It happened during an argument
about money—something trivial that escalated unexpectedly. One moment they were talking; the next,
his partner's fist connected with his jaw. Marcus stood there, tasting blood, unable to process what
had just happened. His partner cried afterward, apologized profusely, blamed stress at work. Marcus
told himself it was an isolated incident. People had bad moments.

The violence became a pattern. His partner would be fine for weeks, sometimes months, and then
something would trigger an explosion. A comment that seemed innocent would be interpreted as
disrespect. A late arrival home would spark accusations of infidelity. And then there would be
hitting, pushing, objects thrown. Marcus found himself walking on eggshells, analyzing every word
before he spoke, trying to anticipate what might set things off. He told no one—not his family back
in Nigeria, not his colleagues at the department, not his few close friends. The shame was
suffocating.

The final incident happened in their apartment on a Thursday evening. Marcus had mentioned casually
that he was thinking about seeing a therapist to work through some stress. His partner interpreted
this as criticism, as though Marcus was saying something was wrong with their relationship. What
followed was the worst beating Marcus had endured. He left that night and never went back.

Now, eighteen months later, Marcus is still trying to rebuild. He's moved into a new apartment,
started therapy, and ended the relationship definitively. But his body hasn't caught up with his
mind. He startles at sudden noises—a car horn, a raised voice in the hallway, even the beep of his
work radio. Last month, a parolee he was interviewing raised his hand suddenly to scratch his head,
and Marcus flinched so violently he knocked over his coffee. He's hyperaware of exits in every room,
positioning himself strategically so he's never trapped. Sleep is fragmented; he wakes in the night
drenched in sweat, his heart racing, sometimes unable to remember if he's dreaming or remembering.

His current relationship, which began six months ago, is strained by his hypervigilance. His partner
is patient but confused by his reactions—the way he sometimes freezes when touched unexpectedly, the
way he needs reassurance that she's not angry when her tone shifts. Marcus knows intellectually that
she's safe, that she's not him, that lightning rarely strikes twice. But knowledge and nervous
system don't always align.

At work, Marcus finds himself struggling with cases that involve domestic violence. He reads reports
of men who've hurt their partners, and he feels a complicated mixture of recognition and revulsion.
He understands now, in a way he never did before, the invisible architecture of abuse—how it
isolates, how it distorts reality, how it convinces you that you're responsible for someone else's
violence.
