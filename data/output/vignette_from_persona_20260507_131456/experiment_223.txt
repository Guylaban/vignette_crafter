constant vigil. For three weeks, he lay tethered to equipment, each beep and hiss a potential

the unpredictability of his physiology; every time his chest hitched, he braced for another crisis.
By the time he was discharged, the acute danger had passed, but Malcolm had come to believe that the
threat remained. He noticed his shoulders had drawn up, tight and unyielding, as if still ready to
react to danger. Even inside his own home, where the doors were locked and the familiar objects
waited on their shelves, he felt on guard, scanning the hallway for any unfamiliar sound. He managed
the house alone since becoming widowed, and the quiet rooms, once a comfort, now felt like places
where he had to remain watchful. As the weeks turned into months, his days as a policy analyst
became a steady navigation of parallel experiences. Sitting at his drafting table, sorting through
legislative briefs and funding allocations, he had difficulty concentrating. The words on the page
remained clear while his attention drifted to the pulse in his wrist or the rhythm of his breathing.

stresses as emergencies. When a memory of the illness returned, it did so without the usual passage
of time; the memory felt like it was happening right now rather than something that was over.

a stranger. What frightened him most was the sudden shift between these two states: one moment he
would be reading a policy draft, detached and distant, and the next the emotional weight would crash
down, so overwhelming that he had to stop, set his pen down, and wait. These experiences solidified
into quiet convictions. Malcolm had come to believe he would never be able to feel normal emotions

longer trust his own body; a simple cough or a skipped heartbeat sent a sharp current of alarm

news of another health scare, and he avoided large meals, watching his stomach with steady
attention. At work, the constant tension and drifting focus ran alongside his withdrawal. He began
excusing himself from afternoon strategy meetings, where the fluorescent lights and overlapping
voices coincided with his concentration difficulties. His colleagues noted his quiet distance, and
Malcolm accepted it without protest, interpreting the fatigue as proof that he was not yet ready to
return to his full responsibilities. Now, at sixty-eight, Malcolm sits in his office chair each
morning, the morning briefs stacked beside him, aware of the body on alert and the muscles holding

where he realized he was living in a state of suspended warning. He does not expect the past to
disappear, nor does he fear it will. He hopes, perhaps, to find a way to let his emotions return to

for danger. He is ready to meet his days as they come, with the same careful attention he once
brought to policy, no longer certain of what will happen, but prepared to meet it.
