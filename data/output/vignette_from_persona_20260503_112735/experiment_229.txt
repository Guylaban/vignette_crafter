### Clinical Vignette: Thomas Thomas is a 49-year-old European American male, married, and works as
a fashion designer. His life took an abrupt turn when his daughter, Sarah, was critically injured in
a car accident three months ago. The event has left Thomas grappling with intense emotional turmoil
and a set of coping mechanisms that have disrupted his daily functioning. On the day of the
accident, Thomas had been at work, sketching designs for the upcoming fall collection. Around noon,
he received a frantic call from Sarah’s friend, informing him about the crash. Rushing to the
hospital in a daze, every detail of the scene—emergency lights flashing, medical staff rushing
around—remains vividly imprinted on his mind. The memory of seeing Sarah lying unconscious, her face
pale and bruised, is so overwhelming that he often finds himself pausing mid-action at work or home
whenever these moments resurface. Thomas has come to believe that if he had been there instead of at
the office, something could have prevented the accident. This guilt on recall haunts him daily,
making it almost impossible for him to engage in his usual routine without feeling intense emotional
distress. He tries hard not to think about the trauma but finds himself involuntarily triggered by
similar situations—a siren passing by, a car swerving nearby. Each trigger brings back the vivid
clarity of that traumatic day, leading him to suppress thoughts and keep his mind occupied all the
time. The overwhelming negative emotions he experiences—fear, horror, anger, guilt, and shame—are
exacerbated when memories resurface. He often finds himself unable to sleep due to these powerful
feelings, struggling to fall or stay asleep as the images from that day replay in his mind like a
never-ending loop. When something reminds him of the accident, he feels strong physical
reactions—his heart races, he sweats profusely, and sometimes even feels dizzy. To cope with this
emotional turmoil, Thomas has adopted various maladaptive strategies. He avoids making any future
plans, fearing that another tragedy might occur if he leaves Sarah alone or engages in activities
outside his immediate control. Instead of seeking support from friends or family, he keeps himself
occupied by working long hours at the studio or staying up late to watch TV shows and movies.
However, these distractions only offer temporary relief; they inevitably lead him back to intrusive
memories that feel so overwhelming he has to stop whatever he is doing. His wife, Karen, notices his
increasing isolation and stress. He’s been canceling social engagements and family events, pushing
away from the very connections that could provide comfort. Thomas feels trapped in this cycle,
knowing that these behaviors are not helping but feeling powerless to break free from them. What
frightens him most is the fear of never being able to move past his daughter’s accident and losing
control of his life. What finally brought Thomas to seek help was an incident at work where he had a
panic attack during a critical meeting, leading to a major project delay. His colleagues’ concern
and the realization that this disruption could jeopardize his career pushed him to consider
professional support. He is hopeful for a way out of this emotional turmoil, hoping to regain
control over his life without these overwhelming memories and debilitating emotions. Thomas’s
current state reflects a self-perpetuating cycle where intrusive memories lead to intense negative
feelings, which in turn prompt maladaptive coping strategies that further fuel the resurgence of
traumatic memories. His journey towards recovery will require addressing these interconnected
components to break this cycle and help him find a more adaptive way to manage his trauma.
