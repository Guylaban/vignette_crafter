deeply shaken, and she now finds herself trapped in a cycle of self-blame and heightened anxiety
that affects nearly every aspect of her life. The night of the accident still haunts Aisha vividly.
She remembers the sound of screeching tires and the sickening crash as her brother’s car hit a tree
on their way home from work. Despite being at an intersection where she had to wait for a traffic
light, she feels responsible because she was supposed to be driving that night but offered to let
him go first. This act of kindness now feels like a grave mistake. “If I had insisted on going with
him,” Aisha often thinks, “maybe it wouldn’t have happened.” The weight of this self-blame has led
her to ruminate incessantly over the events leading up to that night, replaying each moment in her
mind and questioning every decision. Aisha’s self-blame extends beyond just the incident itself. She
is certain that she could have done something differently, even though it was an accident caused by
another driver’s negligence. This sense of responsibility has made her overly critical of herself,
leading to a belief that others would have handled things better if they were in her shoes. “I
should have been able to prevent this,” she often tells herself, which only exacerbates her feelings
of guilt and inadequacy. The memory of the accident is fragmented and painful for Aisha. She finds
herself pushing it away before she can fully recall it, but whenever a detail resurfaces, it feels
as though the event is happening in the present rather than something that has already occurred.
This disjointed memory makes her feel like she’s trapped in a loop of reliving the trauma over and
over again. “I know what happened,” Aisha says, “but I can’t put it into words.” This inability to
articulate her experiences leads her to avoid media that might remind her of similar events, such as
watching TV programs or reading articles about car accidents. Aisha’s heightened sense of threat has
also taken a toll on her daily life. Whenever something takes longer than expected—like waiting for
an email response from work—her mind immediately jumps to the worst-case scenario. “What if this
delay means something bad is happening?” she worries, causing her body to react before she even
realizes what’s going on. Her heart races and her palms sweat as she anxiously anticipates danger
lurking around every corner. The combination of these symptoms has made Aisha increasingly irritable

even this doesn’t fully alleviate her distress. Aisha’s sleep problems have compounded her
difficulties, making it hard for her to stay focused during the day. She often lies awake at night,
ruminating over the details of her brother’s death and questioning why nothing has been done about
the injustice she feels she experienced. “What happened was deeply unfair,” she says, feeling that
no one truly understands or cares about what she went through. Over time, Aisha’s maladaptive
strategies have only deepened her negative appraisals. Her self-blame and sense of injustice make it
difficult for her to move forward from the trauma, leading her to further isolate herself and engage
in behaviors that provide temporary relief but ultimately exacerbate her distress. “I know these
aren’t healthy ways of coping,” she acknowledges, “but I don’t know how else to deal with what
happened.” The accumulation of these symptoms finally brought Aisha to seek help when a colleague

is most afraid that she will never be able to fully process her brother’s death or find peace again,
but she hopes that through therapy she can begin to understand and manage her reactions more
effectively. “I want to stop feeling like I’m living in a constant state of fear,” Aisha says,
expressing her desire for a life free from the overwhelming burden of trauma.
