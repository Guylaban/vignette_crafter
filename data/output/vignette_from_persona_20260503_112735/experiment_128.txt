Sara, an 80-year-old Middle Eastern woman and a retired social worker, has been struggling with the
aftermath of her son’s life-threatening illness. The trauma she experienced during that time
continues to shape her daily life in profound ways. The traumatic event itself was marked by
prolonged hospitalizations and intensive medical interventions. Sara vividly recalls the sterile
smell of antiseptic, the constant beeping of machines, and the sharp glint of needles and IVs—all
potent triggers for her now. The memory forces its way into her mind at unexpected moments, often
without any clear visual recall, only the overwhelming grief that comes with it. This uncontrolled
access to the memory is exacerbated by loss themes in conversations, such as when others discuss
their loved ones who have passed away. Sara’s triggers not only lead directly to these intrusive
memories but also fuel her negative appraisals about herself and the world. She feels violated
because she had to act in ways that went against her values during those difficult times—making
decisions under immense pressure, constantly worrying about whether she was doing enough for her
son. The memory of those events brings back a sense of permanent damage and helplessness; Sara
believes she has changed irrevocably and cannot prevent bad things from happening again. These
negative appraisals have driven Sara to adopt several maladaptive strategies that serve as coping
mechanisms, but ultimately reinforce the cycle of distress. She tries to suppress thoughts about her
son’s illness by pushing them out of her mind whenever they intrude. Additionally, she avoids going
out in public and refrains from any medical appointments or hospitals due to fear of encountering
cues that remind her of those painful times. The weight of these negative appraisals and the
uncontrolled access to memories has led Sara into a spiral where every small trigger can set off a
cascade of emotional responses. She feels overwhelmed by the grief on recall, which in turn leads
her to avoid medical cues even more vehemently. Her avoidance behaviors, such as staying away from
hospitals or not seeking necessary medical care, have real consequences: she is isolated and
hesitant to engage with others for fear that they might discuss loss themes. Sara’s retirement does
not shield her from these triggers either; in fact, it amplifies them. As a social worker, Sara was
used to helping others navigate difficult situations, but now those same conversations about grief
and loss can trigger her own trauma. She has come to believe that she cannot control the intensity
of her reactions and this sense of helplessness further reinforces her negative appraisals. Despite
these overwhelming feelings, something finally brought Sara to seek help. Her grandson’s recent
health scare was a tipping point; watching him go through medical treatments brought back all the
painful memories she had tried so hard to suppress. She is most afraid that she will not be able to
protect her loved ones from suffering again, but at the same time, she hopes for some relief from
these constant reminders and fears. In summary, Sara’s journey has been marked by a cycle where
triggers lead to intrusive memories and negative appraisals, which in turn reinforce maladaptive
coping strategies. This loop continues to tighten, making her daily life increasingly difficult. Her
current state is one of profound distress and hope for finding ways to break this vicious cycle and
regain some semblance of control over her emotional responses.
