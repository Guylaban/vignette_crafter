Narayan, an 80-year-old widowed South Asian man who served in the Coast Guard, has been haunted by a
traumatic event that occurred decades ago. On a routine rescue mission, he accidentally caused
serious injury to another crew member. Since then, Narayan has experienced a complex interplay of
symptoms that have progressively worsened over time. Narayan was on duty one stormy evening when the
distress call came in. As he navigated through turbulent seas, his mind raced with concern and
determination. In an effort to secure a rope, his hand slipped, causing it to strike another crew
member in a critical area, resulting in severe injury. The memory of this event is fragmented;
pieces of the incident come back to him in disconnected moments that feel completely real across all
his senses at once. When he tries to deliberately recall what happened, it becomes difficult to
access the full memory. The trauma has left Narayan with intense triggers. Being in a natural
environment similar to where the accident occurred, hearing sirens, and talking about the future can
all stir up deep distress. For example, when walking near the coast or hearing sirens on his radio,
he is immediately transported back to that fateful night. These triggers activate his sense of
threat, causing him to feel unsafe in unfamiliar places because he cannot assess the danger
effectively. This hypervigilance is a constant presence, as Narayan believes he has to be on guard
all the time. Narayan’s self-blame and feelings of being defective exacerbate his distress. He often
thinks, “There must be something wrong with me as a person” or that "the event happened because I am
the sort of person who makes such mistakes." These negative appraisals lead him to engage in
maladaptive strategies like magical thinking, where he tells himself the event did not really happen
or was not that bad. He also forces himself to think only positive thoughts and tries to push the
memories out of his mind. The cycle is perpetuating itself: when Narayan’s triggers activate his
threat response, it intensifies his fragmented and intrusive memories, which in turn reinforce his
negative appraisals. These negative beliefs then lead him to engage more heavily in maladaptive
strategies like thought suppression and positive reframing. For instance, after hearing sirens near
the coast, he tries desperately to push the images out of his mind or tell himself that it was just
a routine mission gone wrong. These coping mechanisms only provide temporary relief, often leaving
Narayan feeling worse afterward. The more he suppresses thoughts about the event, the harder it
becomes for him to recall and process what happened in a healthy manner. He finds himself avoiding
unfamiliar places and always choosing where to sit based on how safe or protected he feels, which
further isolates him from social activities. The fear of being out in nature or hearing sirens has
also affected his occupational environment. As a retiree who volunteers at the Coast Guard once a
week, Narayan often avoids outdoor tasks and stays indoors, even if it means missing out on
important responsibilities. This avoidance behavior is compounded by his belief that he might cause
harm again, leading him to take unnecessary risks in other areas as a form of self-punishment. What
frightens Narayan most is the possibility that his guilt will continue to grow and eventually
overwhelm him entirely. Despite these struggles, he is hopeful for some relief from his distress.
The mounting impact on his daily life finally prompted him to seek help, hoping to break free from
this vicious cycle and reclaim a sense of peace and control over his thoughts and actions.
