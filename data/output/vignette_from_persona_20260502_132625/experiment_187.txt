and a steady voice. But something had fractured in him nearly three years ago, and no amount of
routine or purpose had been able to knit it back together.

The accident happened on a Tuesday in late autumn. Tane had been driving home from a community
meeting, taking the back roads he knew well, when a delivery truck ran a red light. The collision
was catastrophic. His sedan crumpled like paper. He remembered the screech of metal, the sensation
of being inverted, the taste of blood in his mouth. He remembered the face of the paramedic who
pulled him from the wreckage—a young woman with a look of controlled urgency. But there were gaps.
He could not remember the moment of impact itself, though his body seemed to remember it perfectly.
He could not recall how long he lay there, or whether he had lost consciousness. When he tried to
reconstruct those missing moments, his mind went blank, as though a section of film had been removed
from the reel. What remained were islands of horrifying clarity: the weight of the steering wheel
against his ribs, the smell of gasoline, the sound of his own breathing, ragged and afraid. He had
spent months in the months after, lying in bed at night, trying to force the missing pieces into
place, but they would not come. The harder he pushed, the more elusive they became, and the more he
came to believe that his inability to remember was itself a sign that something was fundamentally
wrong with him—that he had failed in some crucial way during those lost seconds.

This conviction had taken root and grown. Tane had come to believe that the accident had happened
because of the way he had acted—some split-second decision, some lapse in attention that he could no
longer access but that he knew, with absolute certainty, had caused the collision. His body had been
damaged in the crash, yes, but what terrified him more was the sense that his body had become
unreliable, that it was still living in that moment of impact, still braced for danger even when he
was sitting safely at his desk or walking through the grocery store. His heart would race without
warning. His hands would shake. Sweat would pour down his back. He felt dead inside—a hollowed-out
version of himself—yet his nervous system remained hyperalert, as though it knew something he did
not.

The gaps in his memory had begun to warp how he understood his own role in the tragedy. When he
could not remember what he had been thinking or doing in those crucial moments, he filled the
silence with blame. He had been distracted. He had been careless. He had been the author of his own
near-death. This narrative had calcified into something unshakeable, and it colored every intrusive
thought that seized him without warning—unwanted images of the crash, of his body twisted in the
wreckage, of the paramedic's face. These thoughts came most often when he was alone, or when he was
tired, but they also came when he encountered certain triggers. When someone raised their voice in
anger—even calmly, even reasonably—something in Tane would fracture. The anger of others, no matter
how justified or mild, would send him spiraling into conviction that he deserved punishment, that he
had brought this upon himself. And when he saw a police uniform, or the dark blue of a military
jacket, his body would flood with dread. He was not conscious of why at first; it took him weeks to
trace the association back to the paramedics and police officers at the scene, to the sense that
authority figures were there to hold him accountable for what he had done.

The intrusive thoughts had become more frequent as his avoidance had deepened. He had stopped
attending community meetings where conflict might arise. He had begun to eat lunch alone in his
office rather than with colleagues, terrified that a casual disagreement might trigger a spiral. His
ex-wife, Mariana, had noticed his withdrawal and had tried to reach out, but he could not bear to
hear concern in her voice. The isolation had compounded everything. Without the buffer of
connection, his mind had become a closed loop: the gaps in his memory fed his self-blame, his self-
blame made him vulnerable to the intrusive thoughts, and the thoughts themselves seemed to confirm
that he was right—that he deserved to feel this way, that his body's betrayal was justified. He had
become a ghost in his own life, present but untethered, waiting for the next moment when his body
would remind him that he was still in danger, still paying for whatever he had done on that Tuesday
three years ago. It was only when his supervisor gently suggested that his withdrawn demeanor was
affecting his ability to lead, that something had finally given way. Tane had made an appointment,
and now he sat in a therapist's office, afraid to speak, afraid that saying the words aloud would
make the guilt real, afraid that no one would believe him when he said he could not remember.
