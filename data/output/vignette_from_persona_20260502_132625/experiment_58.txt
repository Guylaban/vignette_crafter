# Clinical Vignette: Vinh

Vinh was forty-four when the mugging happened, a night-shift nurse at a county hospital in a mid-
sized city, steady in his work and married to his wife of sixteen years. On a Tuesday in March, he
had finished his shift at eleven and decided to take a shortcut through the parking garage to his
car rather than use the main corridor. The garage was poorly lit, and as he walked toward his
vehicle on the third level, two men emerged from behind a concrete pillar. The first grabbed him
from behind, wrenching his left arm upward, while the second punched him in the ribs and face. They
took his wallet, his phone, his watch—the whole encounter lasted perhaps ninety seconds, though Vinh
would later describe it as feeling timeless. What stayed with him most viscerally was the moment he
saw the first man's face clearly in the fluorescent light as they fled: a flash of features, an
expression of cold calculation, burned into his mind with photographic precision. When the memory
returned—and it returned constantly in those first weeks—Vinh experienced it not as narrative but as
a series of still images, each one vivid and intrusive. He felt the phantom ache in his ribs where
the punch had landed, a dull throb that would bloom across his chest whenever the images surfaced,
as though his body were reliving the violence independently of his conscious mind.

In the weeks after the assault, Vinh became convinced that something about him had invited the
attack. He replayed the moment endlessly: had he walked too slowly, signaling vulnerability? Had
something in his bearing marked him as an easy target? He had come to believe that there was an
intrinsic fault in how he moved through the world, a visible flaw that predators could sense. This
conviction that he had somehow caused his own victimization began to reshape how he understood his
future. Nothing good can happen to me anymore, he told himself. The attack was not a random tragedy
but a revelation of a deeper truth about his life—that he was fundamentally unsafe, fundamentally
flawed, and that no amount of caution could undo what he now was. Because he was certain that danger
followed him, that his very presence attracted it, he began to adopt strategies designed to render
himself invisible or untraceable. He installed a mirror on the dashboard of his car and checked it
obsessively at red lights, watching for vehicles that might be tracking him. During his shifts at
the hospital, he positioned himself so he could see the entrance to every room, and when he sat in
the break room, he always chose the seat with the clearest view of the door and the fewest people
behind him—a calculation so automatic that his colleagues began to notice he was always the last to
arrive and the first to leave, never relaxing into the rhythm of shared meals.

The threat monitoring became relentless. Vinh found himself unable to sleep; his mind would race
through scenarios of re-attack, and when exhaustion finally pulled him under, he would jolt awake at
the slightest sound, his heart hammering. He frequently looked behind him when walking through
corridors or parking lots, a habit so ingrained that it felt less like a conscious check and more
like a necessary part of his sensory apparatus. To manage the anxiety that threatened to overwhelm
him, he kept his mind constantly occupied—listening to podcasts during his commute, watching videos
on his phone during breaks, anything to prevent the silence in which the images might surface. Yet
the more he tried to distract himself, the more insistently the memories returned. The photographic
vividness of the assault seemed to intensify whenever he was most vigilant, as though his heightened
threat detection was actually amplifying the sensory precision of the trauma. The images would come
unbidden, each one accompanied by the physical pain, and in those moments his conviction that he was
fundamentally marked for suffering would deepen. He had come to see his hypervigilance not as a
symptom but as a necessary adaptation to a world that had revealed itself to be irredeemably
hostile.

Six months after the mugging, Vinh's marriage had begun to fracture under the weight of his
vigilance. His wife found him distant, preoccupied, always checking mirrors and exits, never fully
present. He had taken to avoiding the hospital parking garage entirely, using a longer route that
added twenty minutes to his commute, and he had stopped going to the grocery store at night, asking
his wife to do the shopping instead. The avoidance had begun to spread: he no longer attended his
monthly poker game with friends, no longer took walks in the neighborhood where he and his wife had
lived for over a decade. What frightened him most was not the possibility of another attack but the
certainty he now carried that his life had fundamentally ended on that Tuesday night in March, and
that everything since had been merely the prolonged aftermath of a death that had already occurred.
It was only when his wife finally insisted that he see someone, when she told him she could no
longer bear to live with the ghost of the man she had married, that Vinh finally made an
appointment. He came to the first session exhausted, his eyes hollow, still checking the room's
exits before sitting down, and when asked what he hoped might change, he could only shake his head.
He was not certain that change was possible, or even that he deserved it.
