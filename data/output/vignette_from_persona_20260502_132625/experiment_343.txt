# Amara

Amara was thirty-four when her younger brother Kwame died. He had been driving home from his shift
at the port authority in Lagos—a route they had both travelled a hundred times—when a fuel tanker
crossed the median on the expressway. The collision was instantaneous. Kwame's sedan crumpled like
paper. The police told her later that he would have felt nothing, but Amara had spent weeks
imagining the moment in excruciating detail: the sound of metal screaming, the impossible angle of
the steering wheel, the terrible silence after. She had not been there. She was at home, preparing
dinner. That fact had become the hinge on which everything else turned.

For the first months after Kwame's funeral, Amara moved through her days in a kind of shock. She had
married Kofi two years earlier and lived with him in a small compound outside Accra, where she
worked as a fisherman, departing before dawn to tend nets in the lagoon. The rhythm of that work—the
repetitive motion, the cold water, the solitude—had always steadied her. But by autumn of that year,
something had shifted. She found herself unable to sleep. Each night she would lie awake beside
Kofi, her mind circling the same terrible loop: *If I had called him that afternoon. If I had
insisted he stay for dinner. If I had been paying attention to what was happening in the world
instead of thinking only of myself.* Around two or three in the morning, unable to bear the weight
of these thoughts any longer, she would rise and spend hours scrolling through her phone or sitting
in the darkened living room, sometimes purchasing things she did not need—a handbag, a piece of
jewelry, expensive fabric for a dress she would never wear—just to feel the momentary relief of the
transaction, the small hit of distraction. By dawn she would be exhausted, but sleep never came.

The anniversary of Kwame's death in March arrived like a physical blow. Amara woke that morning with
her heart racing, her skin clammy, a sense of dread so acute it felt like a presence in the room.
She called in sick to the fishing cooperative and spent the day alone, which only made things worse.
Every sound—a car horn in the distance, the rumble of a delivery truck—sent her into a state of
hypervigilance. She found herself thinking about the accident again with renewed intensity, and by
evening she was consumed with rage at herself, at the universe, at Kofi for not understanding why
she could not simply move past this. She snapped at him over something trivial, her voice sharp with
an anger that seemed to come from nowhere and everywhere at once. Later, ashamed, she tried to
apologize, but the words caught in her throat. How could she explain that she felt responsible for
her brother's death in a way that no amount of logic could touch?

The months that followed established a pattern that Amara had come to recognize but could not break.
Whenever someone mentioned a car accident—a colleague at the cooperative mentioning a fender-bender,
a news report on the radio about a highway collision—her body would respond before her mind caught
up. Her chest would tighten, her hands would shake, and she would feel a wave of fear and shame so
overwhelming that she would have to excuse herself. To manage these moments, she had begun staying
up later and later, sometimes until three or four in the morning, online shopping or watching
videos, anything to keep the intrusive thoughts at bay. She told herself she was being sensible,
that if she kept moving, kept her mind occupied, the memories could not catch her. But the
exhaustion was making her irritable, and her irritability was making everything worse. She had
snapped at Kofi twice this week alone, once over breakfast when he had mentioned seeing a traffic
accident on his way to work. The words had come out like venom: *Don't talk about that. I don't want
to hear it.* She had seen the hurt in his eyes, the confusion, and she had hated herself for it even
as the anger continued to burn in her chest.

What frightened Amara most was the thought that she had somehow caused Kwame's death by not being
vigilant enough, by not somehow sensing that danger was coming. She was certain that she should have
protected him, that a better sister would have found a way. The conviction was irrational, but it
lived in her body as surely as her own heartbeat. She had come to believe that she deserved to
suffer, that the sleeplessness and the rage were fitting punishments. It was only when Kofi finally
insisted, his voice gentle but firm, that she agreed to seek help. She was terrified of what talking
about it might unleash, but she was also terrified of losing him, of becoming someone so consumed by
guilt that she could no longer recognize herself.
