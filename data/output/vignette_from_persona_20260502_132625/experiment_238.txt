# Marcus

Marcus Volkov, forty-nine, sits in the leather chair across from the therapist with his shoulders
drawn inward, his hands folded tightly in his lap. He is a city council member in a mid-sized
Midwestern city—the kind of position that demands visibility, composure, a steady hand at community
meetings and ribbon cuttings. For the past eighteen months, he has been drowning.

It began on a patrol in Kandahar in 2009. Marcus was a captain then, leading a convoy through a
market district. The intelligence had been solid—or so they'd been told. He remembers the weight of
the body armor, the dust in his throat, the way the market had gone quiet all at once. Then the IED
detonated beneath the second Humvee. He remembers screaming, the smell, the shape of something that
might have been a leg. But when he tries to reconstruct the sequence—who was where, what he did in
the first seconds, whether he called for the medic or whether someone else did—the memory fractures.
Whole stretches are simply gone, replaced by a kind of white noise. Three soldiers died. He knows
this. He knows he was there. But the specifics slip away like water through his hands, and when he
tries to speak about it, even to himself in the privacy of his home, the words dissolve mid-sentence
and he is left gasping, the terror of that moment flooding back into his chest as though it were
happening now.

The terror is the worst part. When he forces himself to remember—when he sits alone and tries to
piece together what happened—his body responds as if the threat is immediate and real. His heart
races. His breathing becomes shallow. His muscles tense as though preparing to dive for cover.
Marcus had come to believe that his body was working against him, that it was fundamentally broken,
still wired for a danger that no longer existed. He was certain the world remained as lethal as it
had been in that market, that safety was an illusion people told themselves. The hypervigilance was
constant. He had to be watching, always, for the signs that something was about to go wrong. His
wife, Elena, had stopped trying to touch him months ago. When she reached for his arm during dinner
or placed a hand on his shoulder in the kitchen, he would flinch violently, his entire body
recoiling as though from a live wire. The look on her face each time—a mixture of hurt and
resignation—had become another thing he couldn't bear to remember.

To manage the intrusions, Marcus had developed a system. The moment a memory threatened to surface,
or when his thoughts began spiraling into catastrophic loops about what might happen if he let his
guard down, he would turn on the television or blast music through the speakers in his home office.
The noise was a wall. It kept the fragments at bay, filled the spaces where the memories might take
hold. At first it had worked—the distraction bought him hours of relative peace. But over time, the
strategy had begun to backfire. The sensory flooding seemed to intensify his sense that he couldn't
trust anything, that even in his own home he needed external noise to keep himself from dissolving.
The constant monitoring of his own thoughts—the exhausting surveillance of his own mind for signs of
breakdown—had become its own form of torture. He would catch himself spiraling and then begin
monitoring that spiral, watching himself for evidence of losing control, which only deepened his
conviction that his mind, like his body, was no longer reliable. When the noise stopped, when he was
forced into silence, the fragmented images would rush back with even more force, and his appraisals
about the world's danger would crystallize into certainty.

The sleep had gone first. He would lie awake until two or three in the morning, unable to shut off
the vigilance, unable to trust that the house was secure even though he had checked the locks four
times. Elena had moved to the guest room six months ago. At city council meetings, he found himself
struggling to concentrate, his mind half-listening to constituents while the other half monitored
the room for exits, for threats, for the moment when everything would fall apart. Two weeks ago,
during a public forum about zoning regulations, he had blanked mid-sentence, unable to find his way
back to what he had been saying. The silence had stretched. People had stared. He had excused
himself and driven home, where he sat in the dark with the television blaring, unable to move.

It was Elena who finally insisted he seek help. She had found him that evening, still in his coat,
still in the dark, and she had said something that cut through the noise: "I can't reach you
anymore." Marcus knows she is right. He is terrified that if he stops the distraction, stops the
monitoring, stops the avoidance, the memories will consume him entirely. But he is also beginning to
understand that the very things he has done to survive are now the things that are destroying his
life.
