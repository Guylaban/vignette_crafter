James Chen was fifty-two years old when the bomb went off at the civic center in Portland, a
building he had entered dozens of times as a journalist covering city council meetings and local

vividly—what plays in his mind with unbearable clarity even now, twenty-one years later—is the taste
of copper and ash in his mouth as he lay there, ears ringing, unable to move. He remembers the smell
of burnt concrete mixing with something chemical and wrong. Seventeen people died. He survived with
a fractured collarbone and lacerations across his left side. He was one of the lucky ones. Everyone
told him so.

For the first few months after the bombing, James returned to work. He filed stories, attended press
conferences, interviewed survivors. He appeared functional. But around the eighteen-month mark,
after his divorce from Margaret had been finalized—she had grown tired of his distance, his
irritability, his refusal to go anywhere public—he began to notice that certain things had started
to unravel. A photograph of the bombed building, printed in an archive he was researching, sent him
into a panic so acute he had to leave the newsroom. The image triggered an immediate spike in his
vigilance; he found himself listening for unusual sounds in his apartment for hours afterward,
scanning for threats that weren't there. To manage the anxiety that followed, he had impulsively
purchased an expensive leather chair that he positioned so his back was always against the wall,
allowing him to see the entire room at once. The purchase felt necessary, almost urgent, a way to
regain control after the photograph had shattered his sense of safety.

James had come to believe that if he allowed himself to truly think about what happened—to sit with
the memory rather than push it away—he would lose his grip entirely. The memory itself felt unreal
to him, a film so extreme that his mind sometimes rejected it as something that could have actually
occurred. Yet when the memory did break through, it felt like the bombing was happening again in
real time: the pressure of the blast, the copper taste flooding his mouth, the helplessness. This
conviction that thinking about the event would destroy him made him acutely sensitive to anything
that might trigger the memory. When a colleague mentioned an unpredictable schedule change, James
felt his threat system ignite; unpredictability itself had become dangerous because it meant he
could not prepare, could not anticipate, could not keep the memory at bay. He responded by spending
three thousand dollars on a security system he barely used, then another two thousand on a vacation
he cancelled. The spending was compulsive, a way to distract himself from the thought that he was
spiraling, that he would never feel normal again.

The more James tried not to think about the event, the more the memory replayed. When he sat in
restaurants, he found himself noticing potential weapons—a steak knife, a heavy glass, the proximity
of exits—and his body would go rigid, seized by a freezing response that made it impossible to
continue eating or speaking. His colleagues had stopped inviting him to lunch. The threat monitoring
had become constant: he listened obsessively for sounds that might mean someone was entering his
apartment, even though he lived on the fourth floor of a secure building. He could not relax, even
when he was objectively safe. The hypervigilance fed the conviction that something terrible was
coming, that he had lost the capacity to feel genuine emotion, that nothing held meaning anymore. A
specific taste—something acrid and metallic that sometimes appeared in his morning coffee—would
catapult him back into the memory, and the memory would confirm his worst fear: that he was trapped
in a cycle he could not escape.

At seventy-three, James has finally come to therapy. His retirement is approaching, and the thought
of having nothing but time to sit with his own mind terrifies him. What he fears most is that the
memory will consume him entirely, that the feelings he has been outrunning for two decades will
finally catch up. What he hopes for, though he barely dares to articulate it, is that he might feel
something genuine again—not happiness necessarily, but the possibility that his life could mean
something beyond the moment the world exploded.
