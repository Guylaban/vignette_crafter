# Aroha's Reckoning

Aroha had been working the night shift at the downtown parking garage for twelve years—a quiet,
methodical job that suited her temperament. At sixty-one, she had built a life of routine: the same
checkpoint each evening, the same fluorescent-lit corridors, the same faces cycling through. She had
never married, had no children, and her colleagues knew her as reliable, unflappable, the kind of
woman who noticed details others missed. That reputation made the evening of March 14th all the
harder to reconcile with what she had become.

She had been conducting her usual perimeter walk around 10 p.m. when a young man—no more than
twenty-five—had emerged from between two parked cars, moving erratically, his movements jerky and
unpredictable. Aroha had called out to him, her voice steady as it always was. She remembered that
much. But the rest unfolded in fragments she could not piece together no matter how many times she
tried. She knew she had moved toward him. She knew she had raised her hand—whether to steady him or
to signal for backup, she could not say with certainty. The next concrete image was his body on the
concrete, blood pooling near his temple, his left arm bent at an angle that made her stomach turn
even now. She had called the ambulance. She had given her statement. But the critical seconds—the
moment her hand made contact, the fall itself, the precise sequence—had simply vanished, leaving
behind only a void that terrified her more than the injury itself.

The gap in her memory had become unbearable. Aroha found herself returning to that night
obsessively, retracing her steps through the garage after her shifts, trying to reconstruct the
missing pieces. She would stand in the exact spot where it happened and attempt to recreate the
angle of her movement, the position of his body, anything that might fill the blank. She dwelled
endlessly on what she might have done differently—whether a softer voice, a different approach, a
moment's hesitation, might have changed everything. The self-blame was relentless: she had been
trained in de-escalation, had been hired precisely because she was careful, and yet somehow she had
hurt someone. The fact that the young man had been intoxicated and had a history of erratic behavior
meant nothing to Aroha. She had been the trained professional. The responsibility was hers alone.

But here was the cruel paradox: the more she ruminated, the more she tried to make sense of the
missing moments by analyzing every detail she could remember, the more alien the memory itself
became. When she forced herself to recall the event—when she sat in her apartment at night and
deliberately walked through what she could access—it felt as though she were watching a film of
someone else's actions. The woman in that memory was not quite her. The hand that had moved, the
voice that had called out, belonged to a stranger. Sometimes, when she managed to summon a visual of
the young man's face in those final seconds before impact, she felt nothing at all—no horror, no
guilt, just a flat, numb observation, as if she were reading about it in a report. This emotional
distance from her own experience only deepened her conviction that something was fundamentally wrong
with her, that she had some capacity for violence she had never known she possessed.

The investigation had cleared her of wrongdoing. The prosecutor had determined it an accident. But
Aroha could not accept this verdict because she could not access the truth of what had happened. The
missing pieces had become a kind of proof of her culpability—if she could not remember, how could
she be sure she had not been reckless, aggressive, careless? She had stopped working the night
shifts, citing back pain. She had stopped attending the small social gatherings her colleagues
occasionally organized. The garage itself had become impossible; even driving past it made her chest
tighten. She had begun to withdraw from the world, convinced that understanding the event—filling in
the blanks, making sense of the senseless—was the only path to redemption, yet each attempt to do so
only deepened her sense of unreality and dread. It was this contradiction, this exhausting loop of
seeking answers that left her feeling more fractured than before, that had finally driven her to
seek help.
