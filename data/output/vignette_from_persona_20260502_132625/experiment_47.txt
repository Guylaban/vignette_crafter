# Chukwu's Vigilance

Chukwu was nineteen when the stalking began, though he didn't recognize it as such at first. He had
been working as a UX designer at a mid-sized tech firm in Atlanta for just over a year, the kind of
role that let him stay quiet in meetings and pour his energy into wireframes and user flows—work
that made sense in a way most social interaction didn't. In March, a colleague named Marcus from the
marketing department had started messaging him on Slack after hours, asking about his weekend plans,
commenting on his Instagram stories within minutes of posting. Chukwu had been polite but distant, a
boundary he thought was clear. It wasn't.

By late April, Marcus had begun showing up at the coffee shop where Chukwu went on Tuesday mornings
before work. The first time felt like coincidence. The second time, Chukwu felt his stomach tighten.
By the fifth time, Marcus was sitting at a table that faced the door, waiting. When Chukwu tried to
leave early, Marcus followed him to his car. "I just want to talk," Marcus said, his voice
reasonable, almost hurt. "I don't understand why you're being cold." Chukwu drove away, hands
shaking on the wheel.

What happened next unfolded in increments that made it hard to know when to panic. Marcus created a
fake Instagram account to follow him. He began appearing outside Chukwu's apartment building on
random evenings, claiming he "happened to be in the neighborhood." He sent long, rambling messages
about how Chukwu had "led him on" and "owed him a chance." When Chukwu blocked him, Marcus found new
numbers, new accounts. The messages grew darker—not explicit threats, but close enough: *I know
where you live. I know your routine. Don't think you can just ignore me forever.* Chukwu reported it
to HR. Marcus was let go. For two weeks, there was silence, and Chukwu had allowed himself to
breathe.

Then Marcus appeared at the gym Chukwu had been going to since high school. He was sitting in the
lobby when Chukwu came out of the locker room, casual, almost smiling. "Thought I'd try this place,"
he said. Chukwu left without finishing his workout and never went back.

In the months that followed, Chukwu had come to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous
in ways he couldn't fully control or predict. He had replayed the early interactions with Marcus
over and over—the Slack messages, the coffee shop encounters—searching for the moment he had invited
this, the way he had acted that had somehow communicated openness or interest. He was certain he had
done something, said something, smiled at the wrong moment or been too polite, and that this was the
price of that mistake. The thought that he might have inadvertently encouraged Marcus's attention
was almost worse than the stalking itself, because it meant the threat came from inside his own
judgment.

Now Chukwu had to be especially careful because you never knew what could happen next. He had
stopped going to the coffee shop, the gym, the park where he used to sit with his laptop. He varied
his route to work, always checking mirrors and blind spots. At the office, he kept his headphones in
and his door—when he had one—closed. He had lost weight from the constant low-level anxiety, the way
his stomach stayed clenched. His mother had noticed and asked if he was sick. He couldn't explain
that he had begun seeing potential threats everywhere: the man at the grocery store who looked at
him too long, the car that seemed to slow down on his street, the way his phone buzzed with unknown
numbers.

Three months into this vigilance, Chukwu's work had started to suffer. His creative output had
flattened; he missed deadlines because he was spending hours checking social media for any sign of
Marcus, any new account, any hint that the surveillance was continuing. His manager had pulled him
aside, concerned. Chukwu couldn't tell him the truth. He had told no one at work about Marcus, about
the stalking, about any of it. The shame of it—the sense that he had somehow invited this through
his own carelessness—kept him silent.

He was nineteen and exhausted in a way that sleep didn't fix. When he finally called his mother and
told her everything, she insisted he see someone. What terrified Chukwu most was the possibility
that Marcus was still out there, still watching, still waiting for the moment when Chukwu's guard
dropped. And beneath that fear was something harder to name: the conviction that he deserved this,
that his own actions had set it all in motion, and that no amount of caution would ever be enough to
make him safe.
