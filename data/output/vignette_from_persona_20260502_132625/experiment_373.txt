# Arjun

Arjun was forty-three when the truck hit his car on the coastal highway north of Monterey. He had
been a fisherman for twenty years by then, a methodical man who rose before dawn, knew the tides by
heart, and had built a quiet life with his wife Meera in a small house overlooking the water. On
that October afternoon, he had left the docks early—a rare thing—to drive inland for a doctor's
appointment. The highway was clear. He remembered the sun on the windshield, the smell of salt still
clinging to his clothes. He did not remember the truck's horn, or the impact itself, only the
sensation of the car folding around him, metal screaming, the certainty that he was dying, and then
nothing until the ambulance.

Eighteen months had passed since that day. Arjun had healed, physically. The fractures had knitted.
He had returned to his boat. But something fundamental had fractured in him that remained unset, and
at fifty-eight, widowed now for two years—Meera had died of cancer while he was still learning to
walk again—he found himself a man trapped in a loop of his own making, unable to name it or break
free.

The triggers arrived without warning and with terrible precision. A conversation with a customer at
the fish market, someone he had never met, would send his heart racing; the stranger's casual
questions about his day felt like interrogation, and he would find himself fumbling for words,
certain that this person could somehow sense his fragility and meant to exploit it. Worse were the
moments when someone mentioned loss—a friend's death, a child moved away, a marriage ended—and the
casual way people spoke of these catastrophes as if they were survivable. Talk of accidents,
especially cars struck without warning, would crystallize something in his chest. He had stopped
watching the news. He could not look directly at the highway anymore.

What terrified Arjun most was the silence. Inside his own home, he kept the television on
constantly, unable to bear the quiet that seemed to gather like a predator in the corners. Even at
the dock, surrounded by the calls of gulls and the creak of boats, he found himself scanning the
water, the sky, the parking lot—always searching for the next threat, the thing about to shatter his
world. His body had become an enemy. His hands would shake without reason. His chest would tighten
as if the steering wheel were closing around him again. He had come to believe that his body was
fundamentally untrustworthy, that it would betray him at the moment he needed it most.

The memory itself was a living thing. When he tried to describe what happened—and he rarely did,
because the words simply dissolved before he could speak them—the event seemed to rush toward him as
if it were happening now, not nearly two years in the past. The terror was immediate and total, as
raw as the moment of impact. Each time he approached the memory, it dragged him backward into that
folding metal, that certainty of death. And because the memory felt so present, so alive, every
trigger that led him toward it felt genuinely dangerous. A stranger's voice could open the door to
that afternoon. Talk of loss could crack it wider. The sight of a car accident on the street would
slam it shut around him again, trapping him inside.

The memory's immediacy had transformed how he saw the world. He had come to believe that people were
not what they seemed—that beneath ordinary conversation lay hidden danger, that the world was
fundamentally unsafe, that he himself was no longer the man he had been. These convictions fed his
constant vigilance. Because he could not trust others or himself, he scanned relentlessly for signs
of threat. And when he found them—the unusual silence of an empty room, the way a stranger's eyes
lingered—his mind rushed toward the memory, toward the terror, and the cycle tightened further. He
had stopped accepting invitations. He had reduced his fishing trips. He had become a man living in a
state of perpetual emergency, waiting for the next impact, unable to believe that safety was
possible.

It was his sister who finally insisted he seek help. She had noticed that he no longer spoke, that
he had withdrawn entirely from the small community that had known him for decades. Arjun had agreed,
though he was not certain what help could offer a man whose body and mind had become the source of
the danger he feared most. What he wanted most was to be alone without feeling hunted, to speak to
another person without terror, to trust that the silence of his home was simply quiet and nothing
more.
