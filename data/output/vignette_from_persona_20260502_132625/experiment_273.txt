# Amir's Case

Amir was thirty-seven when the stalking began, a firefighter with twelve years on the job and a
marriage that had weathered its share of strain. In the spring of two years ago, a woman he had met
briefly at a community event became convinced they had a connection. What began as messages
escalated to appearances—outside the firehouse, near his home, at the grocery store where his wife
shopped. She knew details about his schedule, his routes, the places he frequented. For months, Amir
tried to be reasonable, to de-escalate, to assume there was some misunderstanding that could be
clarified. But the woman's obsession deepened. She followed him on his bicycle during his commute to
the station, maintaining a distance but close enough that he could hear her breathing, the scrape of
her shoes on the pavement. She waited in the parking lot at dawn. She called his home phone at three
in the morning. The police became involved. A restraining order was filed. And then, one evening in
August, she appeared at the back door of his house while his wife was upstairs. When Amir opened it,
she was holding a knife.

The police arrested her before anyone was hurt, but something in Amir fractured that night in a way
the physical world could not repair. In the weeks that followed, he returned to work, but he was not
the same man who had left. His colleagues noticed it immediately—the way he positioned himself in
the station so his back was never to a door, the way he flinched at sounds that would have barely
registered before. When an alarm sounded unexpectedly during a drill, Amir's heart would hammer so
violently he thought it might tear through his ribs. He had come to believe that his hypervigilance
was not a symptom but a necessity, that any moment of relaxation might be the one that cost him his
life. The woman was in custody, the restraining order ironclad, but Amir was certain that none of
this mattered—that danger was still coming, that he had simply not seen it arrive yet.

The bicycle became unbearable. During his commute, every sound behind him—a car door, a voice, the
whistle of wind—sent him into a spiral of scanning and checking. He would pull over, turn around,
convince himself that someone was tracking him. After three weeks, he stopped cycling altogether and
drove instead, though the car felt like a cage and the traffic lights like traps where he could be
cornered. When he entered the firehouse now, his eyes immediately found the exits, catalogued them,
measured distances. He watched his colleagues in a way that made them uncomfortable, searching their
faces and body language for signs of threat that he could not articulate but felt with absolute
certainty. The memory itself was fragmented and overwhelming—not a coherent narrative but a flood of
sensation and dread that would arrive without warning, triggered by something as small as a woman's
voice in the grocery store or the sight of someone on a bicycle. When it came, Amir would have to
leave, to find a bathroom or a quiet corner, because the emotion was so total, so consuming, that he
could not speak or function. He knew what had happened, but the words would not come; he could only
feel the terror and the shame of feeling it.

What frightened Amir most was what this fear said about him. He had always understood himself as
capable, as someone who ran into burning buildings while others ran out. Now he was a man who
scanned exits and watched strangers and could not ride a bicycle without terror. He had come to
believe that he had permanently changed for the worse, that something fundamental in him had broken
and could not be repaired. The hypervigilance, the way he monitored every sound and every face, felt
like evidence that he was losing his mind—that his reactions had become so disproportionate to the
actual threat that he was, in fact, going crazy. These convictions fed back into his vigilance;
because he believed himself weak and damaged, every moment of alertness felt necessary, every scan
of a room justified. The more he watched for danger, the more danger he seemed to find, and the more
convinced he became that he was unraveling. His wife had begun to sleep in the guest room. His
captain had quietly suggested that he consider leave. It was only when a colleague finally
confronted him directly—asking if he had talked to anyone about what happened—that Amir recognized
he could no longer manage this alone. He came to his first session pale and exhausted, describing
himself as broken, afraid that therapy itself might confirm what he already believed: that the man
he had been was gone, and that the frightened, hypervigilant stranger wearing his skin was all that
remained.
