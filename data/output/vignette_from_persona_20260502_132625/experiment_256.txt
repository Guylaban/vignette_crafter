# Marcus

Marcus was thirty-five when a man with a knife took his wallet on a darkened street corner three
blocks from his apartment. The mugging itself lasted perhaps ninety seconds—a figure emerging from
shadow, the blade catching streetlight, a voice that was almost conversational in its demand. What
Marcus remembered most vividly, what played back in his mind with the clarity of a photograph he
could not look away from, was the moment the man's hand closed around his neck. The pressure was
sudden and absolute. The attacker's face—thin, weathered, eyes that seemed to contain nothing—filled
Marcus's entire visual field. Then the shove that sent him sprawling, the sound of retreating
footsteps, the burning in his throat and the sharp, radiating pain in his neck where the man's
fingers had dug in. When Marcus tried to stand, his legs would not obey him. He sat on the cold
pavement for what felt like hours but was probably minutes, touching his neck, half-convinced he was
bleeding.

He was not bleeding. The police came. They asked questions in voices that were patient but clinical,
and Marcus answered them, though he could not quite hear himself speaking. In the weeks that
followed, the memory did not fade. Instead, it crystallized. Whenever something triggered the
recollection—a shout in the street, a sudden sound, the sight of a police uniform—the memory would
return with such photographic intensity that Marcus would feel the pressure on his neck all over
again, would smell the man's clothes, would see that empty face. His body would respond as if the
threat were happening in the present moment. The flashbacks came without warning. Once, a student in
his special education classroom raised his voice in frustration, and Marcus felt his breath catch,
felt his hands move instinctively to his throat. He had to excuse himself, locking himself in the
staff bathroom for twenty minutes while his heart hammered against his ribs.

The neck pain itself—a dull, persistent ache that radiated from where the man's fingers had
pressed—became another trigger. When he felt it, the memory surfaced. When the memory surfaced, the
pain seemed to intensify, as if his body were confirming the danger all over again. Marcus could not
relax, not even when he was alone in his apartment with the doors locked and the lights on. His mind
would not accept that he was safe. He began to scan every room he entered, noting exits, calculating
distances, assessing threat. In his classroom, he positioned himself near the door. In restaurants,
he sat facing the entrance. In the grocery store, his eyes moved constantly from aisle to aisle,
cataloging people, movements, anything that might be a threat.

What made this worse—what made the hypervigilance feel not like paranoia but like necessity—was the
conviction that had settled into his bones: that he was inadequate, that someone else would have
handled the situation differently. Somebody else would not have gotten into this situation in the
first place. He had been careless, walking that route at that hour, not paying attention. He was
weak, and the memory of his own helplessness—the way his body had failed him, the way he had sat on
the pavement unable to move—fed a rage that consumed him when he recalled the event. But alongside
the rage came guilt. He should have fought back. He should have been stronger. He should have been
better. These beliefs made the threat feel permanent and internal. He was not just in danger from
the world; he was inadequate to meet it. This sense of his own insufficiency meant he could never
truly relax, never believe himself safe, because the danger was partly him. His hypervigilance was
not irrational; it was the only rational response to living with someone fundamentally unprepared
for the world.

By month four, Marcus had stopped going out except for work and essential errands. He avoided the
neighborhood where the mugging occurred, then avoided the adjacent neighborhoods, then found reasons
not to travel anywhere unfamiliar. His colleagues noticed he was quieter, more withdrawn. He had
turned down the departmental happy hour three times. When a police officer visited the school for a
safety presentation, Marcus felt his chest tighten the moment the man entered the building, and he
spent the entire assembly staring at his hands. The threat was constant now—not episodic, but woven
into the texture of his daily life. It was this relentless exhaustion, this sense that he would
never be safe and never be adequate to the task of keeping himself safe, that finally brought him to
a therapist's office on a gray November afternoon. He sat in the waiting room, scanning the exits,
and wondered if talking about it would make the photographs in his mind finally fade, or whether he
was simply broken in a way that could not be repaired.
