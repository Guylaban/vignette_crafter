# Priya

Priya was twenty-three when the shell landed three blocks from the apartment where she and her
parents had sheltered for six weeks in Beirut. She had been standing at the kitchen window, watching
the street below—a habit born of numbness rather than vigilance—when the sound came: not quite an
explosion, but the sharp, metallic *bang* of impact followed by the percussion of collapse. The
building shook. Dust rose from the street like breath. What she saw in that moment fractured into
isolated, vivid frames: the bicycle that had been leaning against the shuttered shop across the way,
now twisted and airborne; a man's arm, detached at the shoulder, landing on the pavement with a
sound she could not name. The rest—the seconds before, the chaos after, the sequence of events that
should have formed a coherent narrative—remained blank, as though her mind had captured only the
most unbearable instants and discarded everything else. When she tried later to tell her parents
what she had witnessed, the words simply would not come. She knew what she had seen. But the knowing
and the telling were separated by a distance she could not cross.

Three months later, back in Boston and enrolled in her auditing firm's summer program, Priya
discovered that the world had become a minefield of fragments. A car backfiring on the street
outside her apartment building sent her into a panic so acute that she could not leave her bedroom
for two days. The sound—that sharp *bang*—had collapsed time. She was back at the kitchen window.
And with the sound came the images: the bicycle, twisted and wrong; the arm. Each time, the
fragmented pictures flooded her mind unbidden, and with them came a terrible certainty: *I am no
longer the person I was*. Before Beirut, she had been methodical, precise, capable—qualities that
had made her excel in her auditing work, that had defined how she moved through the world. Now those
capacities felt like artifacts from someone else's life. The person who had stood at that window was
not capable of anything. That person was barely a person at all. She had come to feel like an
object, inert and acted upon, watching her own life from a distance she could not close.

The avoidance began quietly. She stopped taking the direct route to her office, avoiding the street
where the backfire had occurred. Then she began finding reasons to work from home—a spreadsheet
here, a report there—until her supervisor gently asked if everything was all right. She was not
sleeping. The numbness that had protected her in those first weeks had deepened into something she
could only describe as deadness. She felt nothing, wanted nothing, could not imagine wanting
anything again. Yet the moment she left her apartment—the moment a sound cut through the ambient
noise of the city—the fragmented images would return with violent clarity, and the conviction would
settle over her again: *I am broken. I cannot do this. I am not myself.* The bicycle appeared in her
mind's eye without warning. A delivery truck's hydraulic brake sounded like the shell. Each trigger
seemed to confirm what the images whispered: that she was incapable, that she was no longer human in
any meaningful sense, that the fractured memories were all that remained of who she had been. The
loop tightened week by week. The more she avoided, the more the triggers pursued her. The more the
images returned, the more certain she became that she had been fundamentally altered, that the
deadness inside her was permanent. By the time she called the clinic, she had not left her apartment
in eleven days except to buy groceries after dark. She told the intake coordinator that she did not
know if she could go back to work, if she could ever be the person she had been, or if she was
simply going to feel this way forever.
