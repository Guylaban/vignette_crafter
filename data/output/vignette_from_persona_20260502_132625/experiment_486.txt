# Priya

Priya was sixty-two when it happened. She had worked at the same nonprofit for nearly twenty years—a
small organization focused on immigrant services in the city's South Asian community—and had built a
reputation as someone reliable, thorough, and unfailingly kind to the families who came through
their doors. Her husband Rajesh had retired two years earlier and spent his mornings in the garden,
his afternoons reading in the study. They had settled into a quiet rhythm that suited them both
after decades of raising children and managing the practical demands of their lives.

The assault occurred on a Tuesday evening in late September. Priya had stayed late at the office to
finish grant applications, a task she often undertook when the building was quieter. The executive
director had left around six; by seven-thirty, she was alone on the second floor. A man she did not
recognize—someone who had come in that afternoon asking about services—had never actually left. He
had waited in a stairwell. What followed was violent and disorienting, a rupture so complete that
for weeks afterward Priya could not quite believe it had happened to her, as though some other
woman's body had been in that office, some other woman's voice had called out, unheard, in the
darkened hallway.

The first months were a blur of police interviews, medical appointments, and a kind of numb
functioning that fooled even Rajesh into thinking she might be managing. She returned to work after
six weeks, a decision that now, five years later, she recognized as premature. But at the time, the
alternative—sitting at home with nothing but her own mind—had seemed unbearable. She told herself
that the office was safe, that she had changed the locks, that the man was in custody. These facts
were true, and yet they did nothing to quiet the terror that seized her whenever she heard a
footstep in the stairwell or saw an unfamiliar face in the waiting room.

Over the years, the fear calcified into something more rigid. Priya began arriving at work earlier
and earlier, arriving before anyone else, checking every room, every closet, every potential hiding
place. She started locking her office door during the day, a habit that confused her colleagues and
made her feel isolated even in a crowded building. She requested that the front desk staff screen
all visitors, then began screening them herself, sometimes turning people away because something
about their manner unsettled her—a tone of voice, the way they stood, a vague sense of threat she
could not articulate. The grant applications that had once been her specialty began to pile up. She
missed deadlines. Her supervisor, kind but pragmatic, eventually reassigned the work to a younger
colleague.

At home, Priya withdrew into routines that made her feel safer. She avoided the office after dark
entirely, which meant asking Rajesh to retrieve documents she had forgotten, or simply leaving work
incomplete. She stopped attending evening community events, the very gatherings where her presence
had once meant something—where immigrant families recognized her as someone who understood their
struggles, who could advocate for them in a language both literal and emotional. Her friends from
the nonprofit began to stop asking her to join them for dinner. Her role in the community had
contracted, almost imperceptibly, until she was present in body but absent in every way that
mattered to her.

The hypervigilance that had once been a protective mechanism—scanning faces, monitoring exits,
anticipating danger—had become the texture of her daily life. Walking through the grocery store, she
found herself tracking the movements of men who came within ten feet of her. In her car, she locked
the doors immediately and checked the back seat before starting the engine. At night, she lay awake
listening to the house settle, her heart racing at sounds that Rajesh slept through without
stirring. She had come to believe that something terrible was always about to happen, that her
vigilance was the only thing standing between her and catastrophe. When nothing bad occurred on a
given day, she did not feel relief; instead, she felt only that danger had merely been postponed.

It was Rajesh who finally insisted she see someone. He had watched his wife of forty-three years
become someone he barely recognized—someone who flinched at his touch, who could not sit through a
meal without checking the front door, who had stopped laughing at his terrible jokes. He said it
gently, without accusation, but with the weight of someone who had run out of patience and hope in
equal measure. Priya made an appointment with a therapist, though she was certain nothing could
help, that the fear was simply the truth of the world now, and that she was simply the kind of
person who saw clearly what others were too naive to notice. She was sixty-seven years old and had
not felt safe in five years.
