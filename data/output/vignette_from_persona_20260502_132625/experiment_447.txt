# Clinical Vignette: Linh

Linh was sixty-eight when it happened—a Thursday evening in late September, leaving her office
building in downtown Portland after a partner meeting that had run past six. She was alone in the
parking garage, heels clicking on concrete, thinking about the appellate brief due Monday, when a
man stepped from behind a concrete pillar. He was younger, broader, and he had a knife. What
followed was thirty-seven minutes of sexual violence in a space that smelled of motor oil and fear.
He spoke very little—mostly grunts, threats delivered in a low voice close to her ear—but the sounds
he made, the particular ragged quality of his breathing, the wet click of his tongue against his
teeth, burned themselves into her nervous system with a permanence that surprised even her. When he
left, she lay on the cold ground for what felt like hours before she could move. A security guard
found her twenty minutes later.

The assault itself was reported. There was a trial, a conviction, incarceration. From the outside,
Linh appeared to rebuild. She returned to her practice after six weeks. She and her husband, Robert,
a retired engineer, settled into what looked like normalcy. But within months, the world had
fundamentally reorganized itself around danger.

It began with sounds. A man clearing his throat in the courthouse hallway would send her heart into
a gallop, her breath shortening, her palms slick. The particular timbre of a colleague's
voice—something about the roughness in it—would trigger that same cascade of panic. She found
herself unable to attend depositions without knowing the exact layout of the room, the location of
exits, whether anyone else would be present. When opposing counsel raised his voice during a
hearing, Linh's body flooded with a terror so immediate and overwhelming that she had to excuse
herself. The connection was undeniable: these sounds, so similar to those burned into her memory,
told her body that danger was imminent, that the threat was real and present.

Her body had become unreliable in ways that extended far beyond the courtroom. When her heart
raced—and it did so constantly now, at the slightest unexpected noise, the sight of a man's
silhouette, even sometimes for no discernible reason at all—she had come to believe that this racing
itself was a sign of serious danger, a warning system that could not be ignored. She no longer
trusted her own physiological responses to tell her the difference between genuine threat and false
alarm. This distrust fed a hypervigilance that had become exhausting. She found herself scanning
faces on the street, noting the way men looked at her—a glance that lingered a fraction too long, a
gaze that she interpreted as sexual assessment, as predatory interest—and each time, her conviction
deepened that the world was fundamentally dangerous, that she could not afford to let her guard
down.

The hypervigilance, in turn, made her acutely attuned to potential triggers. She began to notice
sounds everywhere: the particular rasp of a man's voice in a restaurant, the breathing pattern of
someone standing too close in an elevator, the wet quality of a cough. Her nervous system had become
a finely tuned threat-detection apparatus, and it was relentless. Every potential trigger reinforced
her belief that she had to remain vigilant, that the world was populated with dangers she could not
afford to miss. She had to be on guard all the time, because the moment she relaxed, she was
convinced something terrible would happen.

At night, the fear intensified. The darkness felt less like an absence than a presence—a space where
threats could hide, where her vigilance became both more necessary and less effective. She lay awake
beside Robert, listening to the house settle, to sounds from the street, her heart often racing,
certain that each noise carried menace. She had come to believe that safety was a daytime luxury,
something that evaporated when the sun went down.

Three years have passed since the assault. Linh is seventy-one now, still practicing law, though she
has quietly declined cases that would require her to work in certain buildings or neighborhoods. She
has not told her colleagues why. Robert knows, but they do not discuss it. What brought her to
therapy was a panic attack so severe during a client meeting that she thought she was having a heart
attack—and the realization, afterward, that she could no longer function at the level her practice
demanded. She is terrified that if she acknowledges how much the trauma has taken from her, she will
lose what remains of her sense of control.
