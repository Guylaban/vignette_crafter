# Khalid's Story

Khalid was twenty-four when it happened. He had been working as a physical therapist at a clinic in
Boston for two years, a job he loved—the precision of it, the way he could see a patient's pain
diminish under his careful hands. One evening in late March, after closing up the clinic, he stayed
to finish some documentation. A man he did not know well, a senior colleague who had occasionally
supervised his work, offered him a ride home. Khalid accepted. In the car, the colleague's demeanor
shifted. What began as conversation became coercive, then violent. Khalid was pinned in the
passenger seat, overpowered, and assaulted. The man wore a heavy cologne—something woody and
expensive-smelling that filled the enclosed space. When it was over, Khalid was dropped on a quiet
street three miles from home. He walked in a fog, not fully believing what had occurred, telling
himself it was a misunderstanding, that he had misread the situation. He did not report it. He told
no one.

For the first few weeks, Khalid functioned. He went to work, treated his patients, came home to his
wife Amira. But something had fractured inside him. By summer, he had begun to notice that certain
smells—a patient's cologne, a colleague's perfume—would trigger a cascade of panic and
disorientation. The scent would transport him back to that car, to the feeling of helplessness, and
he would become convinced that his body was no longer safe, that he could not trust his own judgment
or physical integrity. When a client wearing a familiar fragrance came in for a session, Khalid
would find himself scrutinizing his own body afterward, running his hands over his arms and torso,
checking for injury or contamination, as though the assault was still somehow happening to him. He
had come to believe that his body had been permanently altered, marked by what had been done to him,
and that no amount of washing or checking would ever make him feel whole again.

By autumn, the hypervigilance had intensified. Any unexpected movement in his peripheral vision—a
patient shifting position on the treatment table, a colleague approaching from behind—sent a jolt of
alarm through him, as if danger were imminent. When Amira reached for him in bed, even gently, he
would flinch violently, his nervous system interpreting her touch as a threat. She had begun to
withdraw, confused and hurt by his reactions. Khalid found himself retreating into a kind of
detachment, a way of stepping outside himself when intrusive memories surfaced. He would think,
*That happened to someone else, not to me,* a mental trick that provided momentary relief but left
him feeling hollowed out, disconnected from his own life. Yet the detachment never lasted. The
moment would pass, and he would be back in his body, hyperaware of every sensation, every potential
danger.

What frightened him most was the conviction that he had lost something irreplaceable—that the person
he had been before, capable of feeling joy or desire or trust, was gone forever. This belief fed a
vicious cycle. Convinced that he could not rely on himself to stay safe, Khalid began avoiding
situations that might trigger memories: he stopped going to the clinic's team lunches, made excuses
when Amira wanted to be intimate, and took longer and longer routes home to avoid the street where
he had been left. Each avoidance reinforced his sense that the world was dangerous and that his body
was a liability. The startle responses grew worse, his body now primed to detect threat everywhere.
And when he caught a whiff of cologne on a stranger passing him on the street, or when his own
vigilance made him acutely aware of a pain in his shoulder, the cycle would tighten again: the
trigger would pull him back into the conviction that his body was permanently damaged, that he could
never feel normal again, and that he had to remain hyperalert, constantly monitoring for signs of
danger, checking and rechecking his own skin.

Three weeks ago, Amira found him sitting in the dark in their bedroom, unable to articulate what was
wrong. She insisted he speak to someone. Khalid finally disclosed the assault to her, and then,
reluctantly, to a therapist. He came to this first session carrying the weight of eighteen months of
silence, still uncertain whether he could trust anyone—even himself—to help him find his way back.
