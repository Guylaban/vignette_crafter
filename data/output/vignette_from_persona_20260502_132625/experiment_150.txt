# Tomás

Tomás was twenty-one when his brother was stabbed outside a bar in South Boston. The attack happened
on a Friday night in October; Tomás was not there, but he arrived at the hospital while the
emergency team was still working. What he remembers most vividly is not the doctors' words or the
machines, but the smell—antiseptic mixed with something metallic and warm, a scent that seemed to
lodge itself permanently in his nostrils. His brother survived, but the injury left him partially
paralyzed on his left side. Tomás watched the man who had been his closest friend transform into
someone trapped in a body that no longer obeyed him. The attacker was never caught.

In the weeks that followed, Tomás found himself hypervigilant in ways he could not control. When his
wife, Elena, moved too quickly to hand him something, his entire body would tense; he would flinch,
his heart racing, his hands suddenly clammy. Sudden movements by others put him immediately on
guard, and he could not seem to override the response even when he knew rationally that Elena was
simply reaching for the remote or that a friend was standing to leave. The threat felt immediate and
undeniable, as if his body had learned something his mind could not unlearn. When he tried to
explain this to Elena, he found himself unable to put the experience into words—the memory existed
as vivid, fragmented images rather than narrative. He could see his brother's face in the ambulance,
the specific angle of the paramedic's hands, the exact pattern of the hospital tiles, but when he
tried to speak about it, the words dissolved.

These intrusive visual memories became so overwhelming that Tomás began to avoid anything that might
trigger them. He stopped watching television entirely, terrified that a crime drama or news report
might suddenly thrust him back into that hospital corridor. He refused to read articles about
violence, even when Elena tried to discuss current events. But the memories did not stay contained
to screens. Whenever he noticed someone acting suspiciously—a stranger crossing the street with
their hands in their pockets, a neighbor he did not recognize lingering near their building—the
photographic clarity of the attack flooded back. He would feel as though it were happening again,
his body responding as if the danger were present and immediate. When Elena mentioned her family's
upcoming reunion, the casual remark about seeing relatives triggered a cascade of panic; he could
not explain why comments about family felt dangerous, only that they seemed to pull at something
deep and uncontrollable within him.

To manage the overwhelming sensations, Tomás began avoiding being alone. He could not sit by himself
in a room without the anxiety building to an intolerable pitch, so he followed Elena from room to
room, clinging to her presence as though it were a lifeline. But isolation found him anyway. He had
stopped working at the construction company where he had labored since high school—the
unpredictability of the job site, the sudden noises, the presence of strangers, all felt impossible
to navigate. His identity as a reliable worker, as someone who built things and took pride in his
strength, evaporated. He was no longer capable of the things that had defined him. His body, which
had always been competent and strong, had become an enemy—reacting with terror when there was no
threat, refusing to sleep, keeping him locked in a state of exhaustion that made everything worse.

The nights became particularly unbearable. Sleep would not come, or when it did, he would wake
gasping, the smell of the hospital flooding his nostrils even though he was safe in his bed. When
the insomnia and the intrusive images became too much, when the feeling of being trapped in his own
malfunctioning body became unbearable, Tomás began to hurt himself—pressing his fingernails into his
palms, holding ice against his skin until it burned—seeking physical pain as a way to discharge the
overwhelming emotional intensity. The pain was clarifying in a way nothing else was. But these acts
of self-harm only deepened his conviction that he could not rely on himself, that something
fundamental had broken. Elena found him one night and wept, and Tomás realized he had become a
source of fear rather than comfort in his own marriage. He is now seeking help, terrified that the
fragmentation and hypervigilance will never resolve, but also harboring a desperate hope that
someone might help him understand why his own body has become his enemy and whether he might ever
feel safe again.
