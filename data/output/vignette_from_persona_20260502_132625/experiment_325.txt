# Linh's Story

Linh was thirty-eight when she came to her therapist's office on a Tuesday afternoon in November,
her hands trembling slightly as she signed the intake forms. She had built a careful life as a stock
broker in downtown Portland—a career that demanded precision, emotional control, and the ability to
read markets and people with equal acuity. But for the past two years, something had fractured
inside her, and no amount of professional success could patch it back together.

The stalking had begun in the spring of 2022, though Linh hadn't recognized it as such at first. A
man she'd met briefly at a networking event—someone whose name she could barely recall—had started
appearing in places she frequented. The coffee shop near her office. The gym where she went on
Thursday mornings. Once, outside her apartment building at dusk. When she finally told him directly
to stop contacting her, he had laughed and said she was being dramatic. For the next eight months,
he escalated. Messages at odd hours. Showing up at her workplace. A voicemail where she could hear
him breathing, saying nothing. The police took a report but moved slowly. By the time a restraining
order was finalized, Linh had already begun to feel as though her own life had become a space she no
longer controlled.

What haunted her most was how unreal it all seemed now, even as she lived with its consequences. The
memory of those eight months possessed a dreamlike quality that made her doubt her own
perception—surely it couldn't have been as terrifying as she remembered, surely she was exaggerating
the danger she'd felt. Yet when she tried to recall specific moments—the night she'd seen him
standing across the street from her apartment, the afternoon he'd appeared at her office
building—the terror returned with such immediacy that she felt it happening in real time, her heart
racing as though he were still there, still watching. The gap between how unreal the memory felt and
how viscerally present the fear became was its own kind of torture.

This rupture between disbelief and terror had begun to poison her present. Loud noises—a car
backfiring, a door slamming hard, even the sharp crack of a champagne bottle being opened at a work
event—sent her into a state of acute panic. Her body interpreted them as threats, as signs that
something dangerous was about to unfold, and no amount of rational reassurance could override that
response. The smell of alcohol triggered something similar; the stalker had reeked of whiskey during
one of his final confrontations, and now even the scent of a colleague's drink at a happy hour could
send her spiraling. But what troubled her most acutely was her reaction to other people's happiness.
Watching families laugh together at a restaurant, seeing colleagues gush about their weddings or
their children, even seeing couples embrace on the street—these moments of uncomplicated joy had
become almost unbearable. She had come to believe that happiness was a kind of blindness, that the
families and couples she envied were naive, unaware of the predatory attention that could shatter
their safety at any moment. Her own relationship, with a patient partner who had stood by her
through the worst of it, had become strained by her inability to simply enjoy their time together.

At work, Linh's performance had begun to slip. She'd always been someone who could compartmentalize,
who could leave her personal life at the door and focus on the numbers, the trends, the calculated
risks that defined her job. But now she found herself unable to concentrate during trading hours,
her mind fragmenting whenever a loud noise erupted on the floor. She'd taken to wearing noise-
cancelling headphones, which her colleagues found odd. She'd started avoiding the office happy hours
entirely, which had cost her professionally—the informal networking that had once felt natural now
felt impossible. She'd even begun researching jobs that would let her work from home, though the
thought of leaving the career she'd fought for filled her with a different kind of despair.

When she finally called the therapist, it was because her partner had gently suggested that living
this way—trapped between a memory that felt too extreme to be true and a terror that felt too real
to ignore—was no longer sustainable. She wanted to reclaim her life, to stop flinching at the sound
of her own office, to sit across from a happy family without feeling something dark and dangerous
lurking beneath their joy. She was terrified that if she looked too closely at what had happened to
her, it would consume her entirely. But she was more terrified of spending the rest of her life in
this fractured state, unable to trust either her memory or her safety.
