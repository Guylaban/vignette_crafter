# Kofi's Story

Kofi was thirty-three when the bomb went off at the market in Accra. He had been there for less than
five minutes, moving through the vendor stalls near the cloth section where he bought fabric for his
tailoring business. The explosion came from somewhere behind him—he never saw it clearly, never
understood exactly where. What he remembered was the sound, a rupture that seemed to come from
inside his own body, and then the smell of smoke and burning. After that, the sequence fractured. He
remembered running, remembered the weight of someone else's arm across his shoulders as he was
pulled away, remembered the sirens. But the moments between these fragments were simply gone,
erased, as if his mind had stopped recording. He could not recall how he got out of the market or
who had helped him. The harder he tried to reconstruct those missing pieces, the more frustrated and
afraid he became, certain that if he could only remember, he might understand what he should have
done differently.

When Kofi moved to Atlanta three years later, he told himself it was for a fresh start—better
opportunities, a chance to build his tailoring business without the weight of that day. But the
trauma had traveled with him. The nightmares began within weeks of arriving in America, though they
were not quite nightmares. Instead, the memory of the explosion would replay in his mind with
mechanical precision, over and over, like a film stuck on a loop. He could feel it happening even in
daylight—the blast, the smoke, the confusion—but he experienced it as if watching from outside
himself, numb and detached, as if the terror belonged to someone else entirely. This strange
dissociation terrified him more than the memory itself. If he could not feel the fear, how could he
trust his own judgment about what was real or dangerous? The numbness convinced him that something
fundamental had broken inside him. He began to believe he was weak, that a stronger person would
have reacted differently in that market, would have protected others, would have been able to
remember everything clearly. He was certain now that he could not trust himself to make the right
decisions.

These beliefs about his own weakness and untrustworthiness drove him to seek distractions that felt
increasingly compulsive. He would spend entire evenings scrolling through his phone, playing games
that demanded his full attention, anything to interrupt the replay of the market and the crushing
sense that he was fundamentally flawed. When the scrolling and gaming grew stale, he began picking
fights with friends and colleagues—small provocations that escalated into arguments about nothing. A
customer would comment on a hem, and Kofi would snap at him, manufacturing conflict as if the drama
itself could drown out the internal noise. These confrontations left him isolated, but the isolation
only deepened his conviction that he belonged nowhere, that even here in America, in his own shop,
he was an outsider.

The threat vigilance that had emerged alongside the memory replay now wove itself into everything he
did. Every time Kofi entered his tailor shop in the mornings, he would scan for exits—the front
door, the back corridor, the window—before he could settle into work. He felt unsafe unless his back
was against the wall of his fitting room, where he could see the entire space in front of him. This
hypervigilance, born from the fragmentary and replaying memories of the market, reinforced his
conviction that the world was fundamentally dangerous and that he was too weak to navigate it. His
scanning and wall-positioning became automatic, but they also became evidence to his mind that
threat was real, that his fear was justified, that his weakness was proven by his need for these
protective positions. The more he needed them, the more he believed he was inadequate.

Months passed in this tightening cycle. Kofi's business suffered as he spent more hours on his phone
and less time with clients. His few friendships frayed under the weight of his manufactured
arguments. He began making excuses to avoid any plans beyond the immediate day—refusing invitations,
canceling appointments, unable to imagine a future in which he might feel safe or competent. The
thought of planning anything felt impossible, another confirmation of his weakness and his
fundamental unsuitability for normal life. It was only when his sister called from Ghana, worried
after weeks of his brief, dismissive messages, that he finally acknowledged something had to change.
He was exhausted by the loop, terrified by how much smaller his world had become, and desperate to
understand why he could not simply move on. Last month, he made an appointment with a therapist,
hoping someone could help him understand what was broken inside him and whether it could ever be
repaired.
