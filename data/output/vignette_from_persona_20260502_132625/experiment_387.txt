# Amara's Story

Amara was thirty-three when her father died. It was a Tuesday in March, sudden and senseless—a
stroke while he was alone in his study, the kind of death that leaves no warning, no chance to say
goodbye, no reason that makes sense when you turn it over in your mind at three in the morning. She
had been a park ranger for eight years, the job that had defined her since her twenties: she was the
woman who knew the trails, who could read weather patterns, who moved through the wilderness with an
ease that made others feel safe. Her father had always said she was born knowing how to survive.
Now, eight months later, Amara sat in the intake room and told the therapist that she no longer
believed any of that was true.

The memory of finding out—of her mother's voice on the phone, the specific quality of the silence
that followed—had crystallized into something photographic. She could see her own hands shaking as
she held the phone, could see the ranger station around her as though she were looking at a
photograph, every detail preserved with an almost unbearable clarity. When the fragments came—and
they came without warning, at the dispatch desk or on a quiet stretch of trail—she felt the same
lurch of horror, the same overwhelming grief that had broken her open that first day. She had come
to believe that she was no longer capable of the work that had once made her feel whole. How could
she lead others safely through the wilderness when she could not even protect her own father from
the randomness of dying alone? The thought had taken root quietly at first, then spread through
everything. She was permanently changed, damaged in some way that could not be repaired. The person
she had been before March was gone.

By summer, Amara had begun to withdraw from her responsibilities. She requested shifts at the
visitor center instead of leading trail groups, and when the supervisor asked if everything was all
right, she said only that she needed a change. What she did not say was that the open trails
terrified her now—not because of anything that might happen to her, but because the silence and
solitude made space for the fragments to arrive. So she filled her mind constantly, kept herself
occupied with paperwork, with radio chatter, with anything that would prevent the sudden pieces of
memory from surfacing. At home, she found herself unable to sit still, unable to relax even for an
evening with her husband, because the moment her mind quieted, the grief would rise up and threaten
to pull her under. She had begun to avoid the things that used to calm her—the quiet morning coffee
on the porch, the books she had loved, even the simple act of lying down to sleep—because all of
them felt dangerous now, like invitations for the memory to flood in.

Her body had learned to stay on alert, constantly tense and ready for something she could not name.
The sleep that would not come only made the vigilance worse, and the vigilance made it impossible to
sleep. The negative emotions—the fear, the guilt that her father had died alone, the shame that she
could no longer do the work that had always made her feel capable—had become so constant that they
felt like the weather, something you simply lived inside of. She had become someone who could not
relax, someone who had to keep moving, keep her mind occupied, keep herself away from the very
things that had once sustained her. Her husband had stopped asking her to sit with him in the
evenings. Her supervisor had begun to wonder aloud about whether she was suited to the job anymore.
And Amara, lying awake at two in the morning with her heart racing and her mind circling back to the
moment she heard the news, had started to wonder the same thing.

What finally brought her to seek help was not the memory fragments or the sleeplessness, but a
moment on a routine trail check when she realized she had no idea how she had gotten there. She had
been so caught up in trying not to think, in keeping her mind occupied and her body moving, that she
had walked three miles on a trail she knew by heart without any memory of the walk itself. Standing
there in the afternoon light, she felt something like vertigo—the sudden understanding that in
trying to avoid falling apart, she had begun to disappear. She was afraid that if she did not find a
way to stop this, there would be nothing left of her at all.
