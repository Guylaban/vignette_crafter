# Clinical Vignette: Robert

Robert was sixty-three when the world stopped making sense. He had been delivering packages across
the city for nearly two decades—a steady job, a routine that suited him, a way to stay active and
engaged. He knew the neighborhoods, the regulars who waved from their porches, the shortcuts through
alleyways that saved him time. On a Tuesday afternoon in October, he'd stopped at a corner market to
grab coffee before his final run. When he came back to his truck, a man was waiting. What happened
in the next five minutes—the knife, the sudden violence, Robert on his knees bleeding from his
shoulder—fractured something fundamental in how he understood the world. He had always believed that
if you worked hard, stayed decent, and kept your wits about you, the world would mostly leave you
alone. That belief died on that sidewalk.

In the months that followed, Robert returned to work, but not to any sense of safety. He began to
doubt whether he could trust his own judgment anymore. The assault had shown him that his instincts
were worthless—he hadn't seen it coming, hadn't read the situation, hadn't protected himself. What
if he made the wrong call again? What if his inability to predict danger meant he would fail his
wife, or himself? This gnawing self-doubt crept into decisions that had nothing to do with the
assault. Should he take that route or that one? Was the customer at the door trustworthy? Could he
really handle another day without something going wrong? The uncertainty became exhausting, and
Robert found himself gripped by the conviction that he could not trust himself to do the right
thing—that he was fundamentally unreliable now. This deep mistrust of his own judgment became the
soil from which his coping strategies grew. Unable to tolerate the anxiety that came with feeling
incompetent and uncertain, Robert threw himself into work with an intensity that bordered on
obsession. He would leave at dawn and return after dark, filling every hour with deliveries, with
the physical act of moving packages from truck to door. The busyness kept his mind occupied, gave
him a sense of purpose and control when everything else felt chaotic. At night, when the work was
done and there was nothing left to do, he would eat—not from hunger, but from a desperate need to
fill the silence with something, anything. His wife would find him in the kitchen at midnight,
standing before the open refrigerator, eating leftover pasta straight from the container, or
methodically working through a box of crackers. It wasn't about appetite. It was about drowning out
the thoughts that threatened to surface whenever his hands stopped moving. The weight accumulated
slowly—he gained forty pounds over eighteen months—but Robert barely noticed. The eating served a
purpose: it was a way to manage the distress that threatened to overwhelm him, a physical action
that felt like taking control when control had abandoned him everywhere else.

The irony was that the harder he worked, the more he ate to manage the anxiety, the more isolated he
became. His wife, Maria, began to worry. His knees ached from the constant movement, his blood
pressure climbed, and the distance between them widened. He would come home exhausted, eat dinner
alone in the kitchen, and fall asleep in the chair. The job that had once defined him as reliable
and steady had become a fortress—a place where he could hide from the terrifying uncertainty of his
own mind. By the time Robert finally agreed to see someone, he was seventy-seven and worn down in a
way that no amount of work could fix. He came in because Maria had insisted, because she was
frightened by the weight he'd gained, the isolation, the sense that her husband was disappearing
into a fog of hypervigilance and compulsive activity. What he feared most was not the memory of the
assault itself—he had become skilled at pushing that away—but the possibility that he would never
again trust himself to navigate the world safely, that he had become the kind of person who could
not be relied upon, even by himself.
