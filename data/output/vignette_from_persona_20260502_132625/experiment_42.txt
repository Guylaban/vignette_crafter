# Clinical Vignette: Aroha

Aroha was twenty when her husband Marcus hit her for the first time. It happened in their apartment
on a Thursday evening after she'd mentioned, casually, that she'd run into an old friend at the
grocery store. The conversation escalated quickly—Marcus accused her of flirting, of betraying him,
of being unfaithful in her heart if not in fact. When she tried to leave the room, he grabbed her
arm and struck her across the face. The sound of his hand connecting with her cheek echoed in the
small kitchen. What stayed with her most vividly was not the pain but the shock of his presence
suddenly becoming dangerous, the way his body had moved toward her with an intention she'd never
seen before. After that night, there were three more incidents over the next two months, each one
preceded by his jealous accusations, each one followed by apologies and promises. Then Aroha found
the courage to leave.

That was eight months ago. Now, at twenty-one, she works as an interior designer for a small firm in
Auckland, but her life has contracted in ways she struggles to articulate. Loud noises—a car
backfiring, a door slamming, even the sound of a male colleague's voice raised in conversation—send
her into a state of acute alarm. Her body floods with adrenaline, her chest tightens, and she finds
herself scanning the room for exits or places to hide. Being alone with any man, even her father,
triggers an immediate sense of danger. When her supervisor, a kind man named David, approached her
desk last week to review a design project, Aroha's hands began to shake so badly she could barely
hold the colour swatches. The physical proximity, the maleness of his presence, the closed office
door behind him—all of it conspired to make her certain, in that moment, that something terrible was
about to happen. When he left, her heart continued racing for twenty minutes.

Aroha has come to believe that her life has been destroyed by what happened to her. She tells her
therapist that she no longer belongs anywhere—not in her marriage, which she left; not in her
workplace, where she feels exposed and unsafe; not even in her own apartment, where she now sleeps
with every light on and checks the locks compulsively. The conviction that people cannot be trusted
has calcified into something almost axiomatic. She watches colleagues interact and sees only
potential betrayal. When her mother calls, Aroha hears the concern in her voice and interprets it as
judgment. This sense that the world is fundamentally unsafe and that she is fundamentally alone has
made her hypervigilant in ways that exhaust her. She finds herself constantly scanning faces,
listening for threats in tone of voice, positioning herself so that she can always see the door. Her
body never fully relaxes—even when she is sitting at her desk designing a client's living room, her
muscles remain tense and ready to react, as if danger might emerge at any moment.

The memories themselves are fragmented and overwhelming. When she tries to explain to her therapist
what happened that night, the words scatter. She can describe the kitchen, the colour of his shirt,
the exact angle of his arm as he raised it, but the narrative collapses. She loses the thread of
cause and effect, the sequence dissolves, and she is left feeling like she has failed to communicate
something crucial. More often, the memory comes not as a coherent story but as a flood of pure
emotion—terror, shame, a crushing sense of guilt—without any accompanying image. She wakes sometimes
with her heart hammering and no memory of a dream, only the residual feeling that she has done
something unforgivable. The guilt is particularly insidious. Aroha replays the evening obsessively,
hunting for the moment where she could have prevented it. She should not have mentioned her friend.
She should have noticed the tone in Marcus's voice earlier. She should have been more careful, more
aware, more compliant. These thoughts loop endlessly, and each cycle deepens her conviction that she
is to blame for what happened to her.

This self-blame drives her avoidance in ways that have begun to isolate her further. She has stopped
going out after dark, which means she declines social invitations and makes excuses to colleagues.
She avoids being alone in any room or building—she arrives at the office early when others are there
and leaves before the last person departs. She turns down projects that would require her to work in
clients' homes, citing scheduling conflicts. At night, she cannot tolerate darkness, so she keeps
lights on throughout her apartment, which has made her electric bill astronomical and has deepened
her sense of being trapped in a life that no longer makes sense. The avoidance feels necessary,
protective, but it also feels like a slow suffocation. Each time she sidesteps a situation that
reminds her of the event—the dark, being alone, the proximity of men—she reinforces her own
conviction that these situations are genuinely dangerous, that her fear is justified, that the world
outside her carefully controlled routines is a place where terrible things happen to women like her.

Last week, she saw Marcus at a shopping centre. He was across the mall, talking to someone near a
shop window, and he did not see her. But she did see him. The recognition was instantaneous and
total. Her vision narrowed, her breath became shallow, and within seconds she was in a state of
panic so severe that she had to sit down on a bench and wait for her body to stop shaking. The
encounter lasted perhaps thirty seconds, but it has reverberated through her week. She has not been
able to sleep properly since. Loud noises now startle her even more acutely. Being in the office
feels more dangerous. The memory of that evening has surfaced repeatedly, each time with the same
fragmented quality and the same overwhelming guilt. She finds herself ruminating more intensely on
her own culpability, and her conviction that she belongs nowhere has deepened into something close
to despair.

Aroha came to her first therapy appointment because her mother finally insisted, saying she was
worried her daughter was disappearing. Aroha herself is not certain what she hopes therapy will do.
What she knows is that she is afraid—of loud sounds, of men, of being alone, of the memories that
come unbidden and leave her paralyzed with shame. She is afraid that this is permanent, that she
will never feel safe again, that the person she was before that Thursday evening is gone forever.
She is hoping, though she barely dares to say it aloud, that someone might help her understand why
she blames herself for being hit, why her own mind has become as dangerous as the man who hurt her,
and whether there is any way back to a life that feels like it belongs to her.
