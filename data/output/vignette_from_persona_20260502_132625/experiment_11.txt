# Clinical Vignette: Margot

Margot was forty-three when the diagnosis came back: stage three ovarian cancer. The oncologist's
words arrived in a room that suddenly felt too small, too bright, and she remembered the exact shade
of the wall—a pale institutional blue—because her mind seemed to fix on it while her body went numb.
The surgery happened six weeks later. She was conscious enough to hear the surgical team talking, to
feel the pressure and burning even through the anesthesia, to understand in some cellular way that
they were opening her, removing parts of her, that her body was being unmade and remade by hands she
could not see. The chemotherapy that followed was relentless: the infusions that made her veins
burn, the nausea that came in waves she could not predict or control, the hair falling out in
clumps, the weight loss that made her unrecognizable to herself. For eighteen months she lived in a
fog of medical procedures, pain, and the constant awareness that her body—the instrument she had
relied on for forty-three years, the thing that had allowed her to stand for twelve hours at a time
in her kitchen, to move with precision and grace—had become a threat she could not escape.

She had completed treatment two years ago. The scans came back clear. But Margot had come to believe
that her body would never be trustworthy again, that the cancer had fundamentally altered her at a
cellular level, that remission was simply a waiting period before the next catastrophe. She was
certain that the sensations she felt—the occasional twinge in her abdomen, the fatigue that
sometimes returned without warning—were harbingers of recurrence, evidence that her physical
vulnerability was permanent. This conviction shaped everything. Because she believed her body was
permanently damaged, she began to avoid the things that used to bring her joy. She stopped accepting
invitations to cook for friends, stopped planning menus for special occasions, stopped allowing
herself to imagine a future where she felt well. The kitchen, once her sanctuary, had become a place
where she felt exposed to her own fragility. She would stand at the stove and feel her heart rate
spike unpredictably, her hands trembling, her breath coming shallow. She had come to believe that
happiness or hope was a luxury her body could not afford—that feeling good was somehow a betrayal of
the seriousness of what had happened, or worse, that it would provoke her body to punish her again.

The flashbacks came without warning. A smell—the particular chemical note of the pre-operative
scrub—would transport her instantly back to the surgical suite, and she would be there again,
conscious and powerless, feeling the blade. Her body would react before her mind could catch up: her
heart would race, her breathing would shallow, sweat would bead on her skin. Even in her apartment,
even lying beside her partner Marcus, she could not relax. She remained vigilant, monitoring her
body constantly for signs of danger, unable to fully inhabit moments of safety because some part of
her was always braced for the next threat. When the memories surfaced, they came with overwhelming
sensory detail—the cold of the operating table, the acrid smell of cauterized tissue, the sound of
the monitor beeping—and with them came a grief so profound it seemed to crack her open. Yet the
intensity of these memories also made them feel unreal, too extreme to have actually occurred, which
only deepened her sense of unreality about her own survival.

The numbing had started as a mercy. She stopped drinking wine with dinner. She stopped calling her
sister. She began staying up until two or three in the morning, scrolling through her phone,
avoiding sleep because sleep meant dreams and dreams meant being back in that room. The later she
stayed awake, the more her body felt like it belonged to someone else—distant, untrustworthy,
something to be managed rather than inhabited. Her partner Marcus noticed first. She was distant
during sex, not present, her body going through motions while her mind stayed elsewhere. She had
stopped tasting her food, had stopped enjoying the sensory pleasures that had once defined her work
as a chef. At the restaurant where she worked as head chef, her sous chefs had begun to notice the
change. She was still technically competent, still able to execute a perfect sauce, but the
creativity was gone. She no longer experimented, no longer tasted as she cooked. She moved through
service like someone following a script, and when a new server brought her a compliment from a
regular customer—someone who had specifically requested her cooking—she felt a flash of panic rather
than pride. The thought of being celebrated, of being seen as someone who could create joy, felt
dangerous. It seemed to confirm that she was capable of feeling good, which meant her body would
have no reason to withhold its punishment.

The threat monitoring had become exhausting. Every ache, every moment of fatigue, every irregularity
in her menstrual cycle sent her into a spiral of catastrophic thinking. Her body reacted first—the
panic, the racing heart—and only afterward did she recognize what had triggered it. This constant
state of alarm fed her conviction that her body was fundamentally broken, permanently in danger. She
was trapped in a cycle where her fear of recurrence made her hypervigilant, where her hypervigilance
produced the very physical sensations she interpreted as evidence of danger, where those sensations
reinforced her belief that her body could not be trusted. The avoidance that had started as a way to
protect herself—by not hoping, by not engaging with joy, by numbing herself—only deepened the sense
that she was disappearing, that the woman she had been before the cancer was gone forever and would
never return.

It was Marcus who finally insisted she seek help. He said he felt like he was living with a ghost,
and the word struck her because it was true. She had come to see herself as someone who could no
longer fully live, and the thought of that being permanent was what finally brought her to a
therapist's office. What she feared most was that the person she had been—the chef who tasted life,
who created beauty, who could be present with the people she loved—was truly gone, lost somewhere in
that surgical suite, and that no amount of treatment could bring her back.
