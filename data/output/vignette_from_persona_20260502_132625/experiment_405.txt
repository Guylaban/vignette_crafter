# Arun's Reckoning

Arun Krishnan was thirty-one years old when the monsoon came to his hometown in the Mekong Delta,
and he was thirty-one when everything that came after began to unravel. He had been visiting his
parents' house in Cambodia for the first time in five years—a brief trip sandwiched between his
judicial calendar, a respite his wife Maya had urged him to take. The rain started on a Thursday
morning, gentle at first, the kind of rain that smelled like earth and home. By Friday evening, the
river had risen beyond anything the village had seen in living memory. The water came fast, with a
sound like something alive and angry, and Arun found himself helping his neighbors evacuate, wading
through water that reached his chest, the smell of petrichor mixed with something chemical and
wrong—fertilizer, sewage, things the flood had torn loose from the earth. He had pulled a child from
the water, a girl no more than six, and he remembered her weight against his chest, the way her skin
was cold despite the heat, the absolute certainty that if he let go for even a moment she would
vanish beneath the brown surface.

The girl lived. Arun made it back to his parents' house. But something in him had fractured in that
water, something he could not name.

Three months later, back in his chambers in Sacramento, Arun began to notice that certain moments
would stop him cold. The first time was in the courthouse hallway when an ambulance siren wailed
past the building—sudden, urgent, a sound of emergency that sent his heart into his throat and made
his hands shake so violently he had to grip the bench beside him. The memory came with it: not a
coherent narrative, but a series of vivid snapshots. The girl's face, mouth open. His mother's
hands, bloodied from trying to help someone else. The color of the water. The smell—that chemical,
suffocating smell that he could not place until one afternoon when he passed a hospital and
recognized it as antiseptic, as the smell of emergency rooms and triage. After that, whenever he had
to go to the courthouse on the same floor as the medical wing, he felt it: the smell reaching him,
and with it, the memory returning with a force that made him feel like he was drowning again, like
all of it was happening in real time. His body did not seem to understand that he was safe, that he
was dry, that he was sitting in a judge's robe in a climate-controlled building in California.

The weather made it worse. When the forecast called for heavy rain, Arun found himself unable to
relax. He would sit on the edge of the bed at night, listening to the rain on the roof, his body
rigid with a vigilance he could not switch off. Relaxation felt dangerous—it felt like letting his
guard down, like inviting the memory to overwhelm him completely. So he stayed tense, alert,
refusing to let himself settle into the couch with Maya in the evenings, declining her suggestions
that he take a bath or meditate. Whenever he felt the pull toward calm, something in him resisted
violently. It was safer to stay wound up, to keep his mind occupied with work, with anything but the
space where the memory might flood in.

To manage the intrusive images, Arun had learned to suppress them. When a snapshot of the flooded
village appeared unbidden in his mind—the brown water, the girl's face, his mother's expression—he
would clench his jaw and deliberately turn his attention elsewhere, filling his mind with case law,
with judicial reasoning, with anything that was not the memory. This strategy had begun to extend
into his life more broadly. He stopped going out on days when the weather looked uncertain. He
declined invitations from colleagues, made excuses to Maya about evening plans. The courthouse
itself became a place of dread, not because of his work but because of the ambulances, the proximity
to the hospital, the unpredictability of what might trigger the memory. His absences from social
events increased. His wife noticed the withdrawal, the way he would disappear into his office at
home for hours, the way he flinched at unexpected sounds. The suppression and avoidance had become
so intertwined that Arun could no longer distinguish between the effort to keep the memory at bay
and the effort to keep himself away from the situations where the memory might ambush him. Each time
he avoided going out, he reinforced the belief that the world outside was dangerous, that the memory
was waiting to consume him if he let himself be exposed to it. And each time he suppressed the
images, he found that they returned with greater intensity the next time a trigger appeared—the
ambulance siren, the hospital smell, the first drops of rain on a darkening afternoon.

Six months into this cycle, Maya gave him an ultimatum wrapped in concern: either he sought help, or
she would have to consider what their marriage could sustain. Arun had agreed, though what
frightened him most was not the thought of losing his wife, but the idea that talking about the
memory, examining it in detail, might somehow make it more real than it already was. He was
terrified that speaking it aloud would shatter whatever fragile containment he had managed to build.
