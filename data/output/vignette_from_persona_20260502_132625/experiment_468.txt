# Clinical Vignette: Kwame

Kwame was twenty years old when he deployed with his medical unit to a conflict zone in West Africa.
He had joined the armed forces partly out of a sense of duty to his heritage, partly because a
career in medicine felt more attainable through military sponsorship than through the civilian route
his family had assumed he would take. He was competent, focused, and by all accounts well-liked by
his unit. The first weeks were procedural—setting up field hospitals, triaging minor injuries,
learning the rhythms of a place where violence was abstract until it became concrete. Then came the
morning when a transport convoy was ambushed not far from the medical facility where Kwame was
stationed. The casualties arrived in waves. He remembers the noise—the rotor blades of the
helicopter, the shouting, the metallic clang of instruments. He remembers the blood, the smell of
it, the weight of a soldier's arm across his shoulders as Kwame tried to stabilize a pneumothorax.
What he does not remember—what his mind refuses to hold—is what happened in the hour after that.
There are fragments: a sudden, absolute silence when the generator failed; the face of a young man
on the operating table; his own hands shaking so badly he had to step back. But the pieces do not
connect. When he tries to reconstruct it, his mind slides away, as though the memory itself is too
heavy to hold, and he finds himself pushing the recollection aside before it can fully form.

Kwame returned home after eight months. The homecoming was quiet. His family was relieved, proud,
cautious—they could see something had shifted in him, though he could not yet articulate what. For
the first month, he managed. He enrolled in his pre-medical coursework at the university and tried
to resume the life he had imagined. But the world felt fundamentally different now, and so did he.
He had come to believe that he no longer knew who he was—the person who had wanted to be a doctor,
who had believed in order and healing and the basic decency of people, that person seemed to have
been left behind in that field hospital. His beliefs about how the world worked had been shattered.
He had seen what humans were capable of doing to each other, had felt the weight of his own
helplessness, and no amount of intellectual reframing seemed to restore the naive certainty he once
possessed. He had to be on guard all the time now; the world was not safe, and neither was he. This
constant vigilance exhausted him, but it also felt necessary, a kind of armor against a reality he
could no longer deny.

The triggers came unpredictably, each one pulling him back into a state of acute threat. Sudden
silence—the kind that falls in a lecture hall when the air conditioning cuts out, or in the
university library when a group of students stops talking—would send his heart racing. His breath
would become shallow and difficult, as though his chest had tightened around his lungs. In those
moments, the fragmentation of his memory became almost unbearable. The silence seemed to unlock
something, and pieces of that missing hour would surface without warning: an image of blood pooling
on a surgical drape, the sensation of someone gripping his wrist, a voice calling out a name he
could not quite place. These fragments arrived disconnected and terrifying, and they reinforced his
conviction that he was fundamentally broken, that whatever had happened in that silence had unmade
him in ways he could not repair. Violence—even in films or in news reports—triggered the same cycle:
his breathing would become labored, his mind would splinter, and the conviction that he could no
longer trust his own judgment or his ability to protect himself would deepen. Each trigger seemed to
confirm what the fragmented memories whispered: that he was not safe, that the world was not safe,
and that there was no going back.

By the time Kwame sought help, he had withdrawn from his coursework. He was still living at home,
still telling his parents he was "managing," but he had stopped going to campus. The thought of
being in a crowded classroom, of the unexpected sounds and movements, of the possibility that
silence might fall and leave him exposed to his own fractured recollections, felt impossible to
bear. He was afraid that if he allowed himself to remember, he would discover that the person he had
been was truly gone. What he wanted most, he told his therapist in their first session, was to feel
like himself again—but he was no longer certain such a person existed.
