# Clinical Vignette: Marco

Marco was twenty-two when the convoy was ambushed outside Kandahar. He was a gunner on the second
Humvee, and when the first IED detonated, the noise didn't register as sound so much as a physical
force that inverted his understanding of the world. The vehicle ahead erupted. He saw
Rodriguez—who'd shown him pictures of his newborn daughter just that morning—thrown from the turret.
The screaming started immediately, and Marco fired back at the tree line, his hands moving on muscle
memory while his mind fractured into a thousand simultaneous moments: the smell of burning rubber
and flesh, the weight of the gun, the impossible clarity of Rodriguez's face as it turned toward him
one last time. When the medevac arrived, Marco was still firing. He had no memory of climbing down
from the turret, no recollection of the medic wrapping the burns on his shoulder. What he did
remember, with photographic precision, was the exact shade of dust hanging in the sunlight as the
second round of fire came in, and the feeling of his own body moving through space as though it
belonged to someone else.

Now, two years later, Marco worked as a financial analyst in downtown Denver, and the memories
hadn't faded—they'd calcified into something fixed and immovable. The visceral details remained
crystalline: the grain of the metal beneath his palms, the taste of copper and ash, the specific
angle of the shadows across the compound wall. But the sequence was broken. He couldn't reconstruct
the timeline of what happened when. Did the second volley come before or after Rodriguez fell? He'd
replay the fragments obsessively, trying to reassemble them into a coherent narrative, but the
pieces wouldn't fit. They appeared unbidden—a flash of Rodriguez's uniform, the sound of a car
backfiring on the street below his office, the smell of diesel fuel—and then vanished just as
suddenly, leaving Marco disoriented and gasping.

The clarity of those fragments had come to mean something terrible to him. They weren't just
memories; they were evidence. The fact that he could see Rodriguez's face so perfectly, could feel
the gun so acutely, meant that he had been fully present, fully complicit. He had been trained to
fire. He had fired. And Rodriguez was dead. The more vivid the memory, the more inescapable the
conclusion: Marco had acted in a way that no part of himself could reconcile. He wasn't the person
who'd enlisted, who'd believed in something larger than himself. That person had died in the dust
outside Kandahar, and what remained was someone who had killed, or failed to save, or both. The
weight of that violated self pressed down on him constantly, and with it came a suffocating sense
that there was no future worth having. What was the point of a career, of relationships, of any of
it, when he was fundamentally compromised?

These beliefs had quietly reshaped his life. Because Marco had come to see himself as irredeemably
changed, as someone whose actions had severed him from the world he'd known, he found himself
withdrawing from the very people who might have grounded him. He stopped answering calls from his
mother, stopped attending his sister's family dinners. When colleagues at work asked about his
weekend, he gave one-word answers and turned back to his spreadsheets. Talking about what had
happened, or even allowing others to know him as he was now, felt impossible—not just painful, but
dangerous, as though honesty would confirm the verdict he'd already rendered on himself. He began
avoiding the open plaza near his office building where the company sometimes held lunch gatherings,
the sight of people sitting casually in the sun triggering a claustrophobic panic that felt like
drowning in daylight. Anything that reminded him of loss—a news story about fallen soldiers, a photo
of a young man in uniform on social media—sent him spiraling, and he'd find himself deleting apps,
unfollowing people, shrinking his world to the narrow confines of his apartment and his desk.

The avoidance had calcified into routine. He took the same route to work each day, avoided the gym
where his unit's memorial was posted, stopped going to the cemetery where Rodriguez was buried. Each
restriction felt necessary, protective. But with each boundary he drew, he felt more alone, more
convinced that the isolation was permanent and deserved. It was only when his manager pulled him
aside in late October, noting that he'd missed three team meetings and seemed to be withdrawing from
projects, that Marco realized something had to give. He sat in his car afterward, hands shaking, and
for the first time in two years, he allowed himself to consider that the story he'd been telling
himself—that he was permanently broken, that connection was impossible, that his future was
foreclosed—might not be the only story available. That thought terrified him more than the memories
themselves. But it was enough to make him search for a therapist.
