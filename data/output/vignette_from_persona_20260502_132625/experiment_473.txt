# Magda

Magda was fifty-eight when it happened, though she would later mark her life into before and after
with the precision of someone carving dates into stone. She had been working late in her studio—a
converted warehouse in Red Hook where she spent most of her waking hours, surrounded by the half-
finished forms that had sustained her marriage, her reputation, her sense of self for forty years. A
man she knew slightly, someone who had occasionally bought her work and attended her openings, had
asked to see her new pieces. She had let him in without hesitation. The assault was swift and
brutal, and what stayed with her afterward was not just the violence itself but the particular
betrayal of it: a person from her own world, someone she had believed was safe, had shattered that
assumption in the span of minutes.

The police investigation that followed felt like a second assault. The detective's skepticism—his
careful questions about what she had been wearing, why she had let him into her studio alone,
whether she had been drinking—communicated something she had already begun to feel: that the
institution meant to protect her had decided, in its own bureaucratic way, that her case was not
quite credible enough, not quite worth the effort. The prosecutor eventually declined to pursue
charges. Magda had come to believe that the world had shown her its true nature, and that she had
been foolish to think herself safe in it.

In the months after, Magda found herself confined to an ever-shrinking geography. Her studio, once
her sanctuary, became a place she could no longer enter alone. She sold it within six months. Her
home—specifically her bedroom and the small study where she kept her drawings—became the only spaces
where her body felt like it belonged to her. She would sit in her study for hours, ostensibly
working on sketches, but mostly staring at her hands as if they belonged to a stranger. Her husband,
Marco, tried to understand. He brought her tea, suggested they take walks, reminded her that she had
survived. But Magda could not articulate what he seemed unable to grasp: that the woman he had
married—the woman who had moved through the world with confidence, who had stood in front of
strangers to discuss her work, who had believed in the possibility of beauty—had been unmade by
someone she had extended trust to, and that trust itself had become a form of self-betrayal.

When someone mentioned a news story about sexual assault, or when she overheard a conversation at
the grocery store about "what women deserve," Magda felt the ground tilt beneath her. These moments,
which seemed to come without warning, would trigger a cascade of thoughts that felt less like
thoughts and more like accusations: *I should have known better. I should have listened to my
instincts. I acted against everything I believed about protecting myself.* The shame would bloom
into something larger—a conviction that she had violated her own values by being naive, by being
trusting, by being the kind of woman who could be hurt. And once that shame took hold, the world
itself began to feel dangerous in a new way. The safety she had constructed in her home would feel
suddenly fragile. An unexpected sound—a siren passing on the street below, a neighbor's car door
slamming—would send her body into a state of high alert, as if danger were not something that had
happened in the past but something that was always, imminently, about to happen again.

This hypervigilance fed back into her sense of threat. The more unsafe she felt, the more she
withdrew. The more she withdrew, the more her mind had space to circle back to what had happened, to
what she had "allowed" to happen, to the ways she had failed to protect herself. Marco began to
spend more time at his office. Their daughter, who lived in Boston, stopped calling as often; the
silences on those rare phone calls had become unbearable. Magda's hands, which had once known how to
shape clay and stone, had begun to shake. She had not sculpted in eighteen months. At sixty-three,
she was living in three rooms of a house that suddenly felt like a prison of her own making, and she
could not imagine how to leave it.

It was Marco who finally insisted she see someone. He had found her one morning in the kitchen,
unable to move, because a delivery truck had backfired in the driveway and her body had reacted as
though she were being attacked. She had agreed to therapy not because she believed it would help,
but because she could no longer bear the weight of what she had become, and because some small,
nearly extinguished part of her wondered if the woman she had been—the sculptor, the artist, the
person who had believed in her own resilience—might still exist somewhere beneath the fear.
