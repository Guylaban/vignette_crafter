# Priya

Priya was thirty-four days into her torture when the interrogators finally stopped coming to the
cell. She had lost track of which day that was—the concrete room had no window, no clock, only the
sound of her own breathing and the occasional footstep that sent her body rigid. The worst part, she
would later realize, was not the physical pain itself but the uncertainty: each time she heard a key
turn in the lock, her nervous system exploded into terror because she could not predict what would
happen next. The men who came wore masks. They asked questions she could not answer because she had
nothing to tell them. When she could not answer, they made her stand for hours, or they applied
electric current to her skin, or they forced her head underwater until she thought her lungs would
burst. The randomness was the architecture of it all—sometimes they would leave her alone for what
felt like days, and she would convince herself it was over, and then the door would open again and
the cycle would resume. By the time the international organization negotiated her release, Priya had
learned that safety was an illusion. The world could turn on you without warning. You could be taken
from your home, from your tailoring shop where you had spent twelve years building a business with
your hands, and thrown into a place where control meant nothing.

Six months after her return to the United States, Priya sat in her tailor shop on a Tuesday
afternoon, and a customer came in asking for an alteration. The woman was pleasant, unremarkable.
But when she turned to leave, she moved quickly toward the back room—the room where Priya kept her
supplies—and Priya's body flooded with panic so complete that she could not breathe. Her hands began
to shake. She was certain, in that moment, that something terrible was about to happen, that she was
about to be taken again, that the randomness was returning. She had come to believe that her safety
existed only in perfect predictability, in knowing exactly where every person in her shop was at
every moment, in being able to see every exit and every potential threat. The moment someone moved
unexpectedly, or a customer lingered too long, or a sound came from an unfamiliar direction, Priya's
mind flooded with the conviction that danger was imminent. She began scanning the front door and the
back exit the moment anyone entered, her eyes moving constantly, her body tensed as though ready to
run or fight. She positioned herself near the door during consultations. She stopped taking breaks
because sitting down felt dangerous—if she relaxed, if she let her guard down even for a moment,
something would happen.

This hypervigilance exhausted her, but the thought of stopping it terrified her more. Priya had
become convinced that her constant monitoring was the only thing keeping her alive, that if she
looked away, if she allowed herself a moment of peace, the randomness would find her again. When her
husband, Rajesh, suggested she see a therapist, she refused. The thought of sitting in an office
with a stranger, of talking about what happened, of potentially breaking down emotionally—it all
felt unbearably dangerous. She was certain that if she allowed herself to feel the full weight of
what had been done to her, she would shatter completely, and in that shattered state, she would be
vulnerable. Similarly, she declined her doctor's recommendation for a physical examination; the
vulnerability of lying on an examination table, of being unable to see the exits, of being in a
position where she could not protect herself, was unthinkable. Even at home, she found herself
unable to relax. When Rajesh suggested they take a bath together or sit quietly on the porch—moments
that used to bring her peace—she would feel a creeping dread. Relaxation felt like lowering her
weapons. It felt like inviting disaster.

The flashbacks came without warning. One afternoon, a delivery truck backfired outside the shop, and
Priya was suddenly in the cell again, the sound of the electric current humming in her ears, the
taste of concrete dust in her mouth. Her body reacted as if she were still there—she could not move,
could not speak, could not access the part of her mind that knew she was safe. It lasted perhaps two
minutes, but it left her shaking for hours. These moments of feeling as though the trauma were
happening again reinforced everything she believed about danger. They proved to her that the threat
was not over, that her body knew something her rational mind could not quite grasp. Each flashback
made her grip tighter on her vigilance, made her more certain that only constant threat monitoring
could keep her safe. The more she scanned for danger, the more danger her nervous system seemed to
find. The more she avoided anything that might make her emotional, the more isolated she became.
Rajesh grew frustrated with her refusals, with her inability to be present with him, with her
constant need to position herself near exits even in their own home. He stopped asking her to relax.
He stopped suggesting therapy. He simply withdrew, and Priya told herself this was safer—fewer
emotional demands, fewer moments when she might break down—but the silence between them was its own
kind of pain.

It was her mother's phone call that finally brought Priya to a therapist's office. Her mother,
calling from the old country, had heard through relatives that Priya was not working as much, that
she had closed the shop two days a week, that she seemed to have disappeared into herself. When her
mother asked directly if she was all right, Priya found herself unable to lie. She admitted that she
was afraid all the time, that she could not relax, that she was certain something terrible was going
to happen if she let her guard down for even a moment. Her mother's response was simple: "Beta, you
are safe now. But you are not living." The words haunted her. Priya realized that in her attempt to
prevent another trauma, she had constructed a prison as confining as the one she had escaped. She
was afraid that if she stopped scanning for exits, if she allowed herself to feel, if she sought
help, something catastrophic would occur. But she was also afraid of continuing as she was—afraid of
losing her marriage, her business, her life. It was this fear of losing everything that finally made
her pick up the phone and call a therapist, her hands shaking as she dialed, her mind still
convinced that asking for help was the most dangerous thing she could possibly do.
