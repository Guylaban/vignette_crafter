# Catherine

Catherine had been a federal judge for eight years when she took a leave of absence to work with a
humanitarian relief organization in South Sudan. She was fifty-two then, newly divorced, and hungry
for something that felt like it mattered in a way her chambers no longer did. The assignment was
supposed to last six months. On the fourth month, in March 2015, the compound where she and twelve
other aid workers were sheltering came under artillery fire during what was supposed to be a
ceasefire. The initial volley came at dawn. Catherine was in the medical tent when the first shell
hit the administrative building fifty yards away. The sound—a shriek followed by a percussion that
seemed to originate inside her own chest—was immediately followed by screaming and the smell of
diesel fuel mixed with something charred and organic that she would later learn was burnt flesh. She
and two nurses dragged themselves into a concrete bunker beneath the tent. For six hours they lay in
darkness, listening to the sporadic whistle and impact of incoming fire, the building above them
shuddering. One of the nurses, a young woman named Amara, held Catherine's hand and recited Quranic
verses in a whisper. Catherine found herself praying in a language she hadn't used since childhood.
When the shelling stopped, there was silence so complete it felt like deafness. They emerged to find
three colleagues dead and the compound's water supply destroyed.

Nearly eight years had passed since Catherine returned to the United States, resumed her position on
the bench, and tried to rebuild a life that felt recognizable. But the memory of that morning had
not remained in the past. Anniversaries had become a particular torment—in the weeks leading up to
March, Catherine found herself increasingly hypervigilant, unable to concentrate on case files, and
by the actual date she was often unable to leave her house. The anniversary seemed to unlock the
entire experience in a way that nothing else quite did. Beyond that, certain tastes triggered the
memory with startling force. The metallic, slightly bitter taste of the iron-rich water that had
been distributed from emergency reserves in the days after the attack—a taste she had nearly
forgotten until one morning when she bit into a piece of rare steak and the association flooded
back, complete and overwhelming. Even the sight of a police officer in uniform at the courthouse
could send her spiraling backward, the dark fabric and the shape of authority collapsing the
distance between the present and that bunker.

When these triggers arrived, the memory did not come gently. Catherine had come to understand that
her brain was not simply recalling what had happened—it was reliving it. The sound of the shell's
descent returned to her ears as vividly as if she were still lying in the dark, and the sensations
multiplied: the cold concrete beneath her, the pressure of Amara's grip, the smell of smoke and
fear-sweat, the taste of dust in her mouth. The terror she had felt in that moment—a primal
certainty that she was about to die—returned with it, indistinguishable from the original. These
episodes left her shaken for hours afterward, her heart racing, her hands trembling as she tried to
compose herself enough to return to the bench.

What made the memory worse, Catherine had come to believe, was the certainty that others could see
it written on her face. She was convinced that her colleagues at the courthouse could look at her
and read the trauma there, that they understood she was broken in some fundamental way that
disqualified her from the authority she exercised. This belief isolated her further. She began
declining social invitations, withdrawing from the informal networks of judges and lawyers that had
once sustained her professional life. She ate lunch alone in her chambers rather than in the
courthouse cafeteria. The thought that people could perceive her damage made her increasingly
vigilant about her appearance and composure, and this hypervigilance itself became
exhausting—another layer of the burden she carried.

The memory itself seemed to generate a darker conviction: that the world was fundamentally
dangerous, that the safety she had believed in before South Sudan was an illusion. This belief fed
back into how she experienced the memory when it returned. Each time she relived those hours in the
bunker, the memory reinforced her sense that catastrophe was always possible, that the structures of
order and law she had devoted her life to were fragile facades. The memory became evidence for a
worldview in which danger was not exceptional but constant.

To manage the memories and the beliefs they carried, Catherine had developed strategies that had
become as much a part of her daily life as her judicial robes. She tried with fierce discipline not
to think about what had happened, pushing away any thought that approached the memory's edges. She
worked late into the evening, filling her mind with case law and briefs. She avoided circumstances
that might trigger the memories—she no longer attended courthouse events where security personnel in
uniform were present, she ordered her groceries online rather than risk the metallic taste of the
produce section's water-sprayed vegetables, she blocked off her calendar in the weeks surrounding
the anniversary and spent those days at home with the curtains drawn. She had become expert at
emotional suppression, the tight control of her face and voice so practiced that it had become
automatic. But the avoidance came with consequences she had not fully anticipated. Her professional
relationships had become distant and formal. Two years ago, a colleague had nominated her for a
prestigious judicial fellowship; she had withdrawn her name without explanation. Her isolation had
deepened, the avoidance itself becoming proof that she was indeed as broken as she feared others
perceived her to be.

It was only this past month, when a young attorney she mentored had asked her directly why she
seemed unhappy, that Catherine recognized she could no longer sustain the weight of managing this
alone. She had finally called a therapist who specialized in trauma.
