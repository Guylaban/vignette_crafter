# Clinical Vignette: Priya

Priya was forty-seven when she first sat in the intake chair, her hands folded precisely in her lap,
her voice steady but thin—the voice of someone who had learned to speak without trembling. She had
been a pastor for twenty-two years, known in her congregation for her clarity, her unflinching
presence during others' suffering, her ability to sit with grief without flinching. That persona had
fractured five years ago, though she did not name it as such until now.

In 2019, Priya had traveled to her family's village in rural Bangladesh to visit her aging mother.
On the third day, armed men entered the compound where she was staying. They had mistaken her for an
activist; she was detained for nine days. What happened in that detention center existed in her
memory as fragments—the cold concrete floor, the taste of something metallic and acrid that had been
forced into her mouth, the sound of a particular voice giving orders, the smell of diesel fuel. But
the pieces did not cohere. Some moments were crystalline: the exact angle of light through a high
window on the fourth day, the texture of the fabric they had used to bind her wrists. Other
stretches were simply gone—blank hours or perhaps days that her mind had erased entirely. When she
tried to remember the sequence of events, the fragments would not align. She carried the knowledge
that she had been assaulted, but the memory of it came in disconnected shards that seemed to belong
to different events, different moments, as if her mind had shattered the narrative into pieces too
small to form a coherent whole.

She had returned to the United States and resumed her duties at the church. For months, she
functioned. Then, gradually, the world had become a place of hidden threats. She found herself
watching the faces of parishioners during services, cataloging exits, noting who stood where. When
someone mentioned a news story about violence, her body would seize before her mind had even
processed the words—her chest would tighten, her breath would shallow, and she would realize only
afterward that she had been afraid. She could not explain to her own mind why the sound of a car
door slamming in the church parking lot sent her into a state of alert that lasted for hours. Her
body seemed to know something her conscious mind could not quite articulate: that danger was
everywhere, that people could not be trusted, that even when her congregation told her she was safe
in her own church, in her own country, that safety was a lie told by people who had not understood
what she now knew.

Certain tastes—metallic, bitter, with a faint chemical undertone—would appear in ordinary meals
without warning. A restaurant's broth, a medication, even the mineral taste of water from a
particular fountain would trigger the sensation of being back in that room, and with it, a flood of
fragmented images and the overwhelming certainty that she could not survive remembering. When she
talked about the event, even in clinical terms, her eyes would fill with tears that seemed to come
from somewhere beyond her control, as though her body was grieving what her mind could not fully
access. These moments terrified her because she had come to believe that if she allowed herself to
cry, if she let the memories fully surface, she would not be able to stop—that the weight of it
would crush her entirely, that she was no longer capable of bearing what had happened to her, let
alone continuing to bear witness to others' pain as a pastor.

To regain some sense of control, Priya had begun restricting her eating carefully. She monitored
every bite, choosing only foods whose taste she could predict and control, avoiding anything that
might surprise her palate. This strictness gave her a foothold: if she could control what entered
her body, she could control her responses. She also began avoiding situations where she might be
asked to discuss the event. She would change the subject if anyone inquired about her trip, and she
began declining invitations to speak at interfaith gatherings or grief-support groups where her
story might naturally arise. She had started taking longer routes home from the church, routes that
allowed her to scan the streets more thoroughly, to position herself where she could see who was
approaching. But these strategies had consequences. Her congregation had noticed her withdrawal. Two
parishioners had asked if she was well. Her bishop had gently suggested that she seemed less
present. The thought that others were watching her, evaluating her, assessing whether she was still
capable of her role, made her hypervigilant in new ways—now she was not just watching for external
danger, but monitoring the reactions of people around her, certain that they could see her
fracturing.

The fragmented nature of her memories had come to feel like evidence that her mind was breaking
down. When she tried to reconstruct what had happened, the gaps terrified her more than the clear
moments did. The guilt that surfaced when she did remember—guilt about what she had been unable to
prevent, about surviving when she was not certain others had, about the way her body had
responded—seemed to confirm her worst fear: that there was something fundamentally wrong with her,
something irredeemable. Each time a taste or a sound pulled a memory fragment to the surface, the
guilt would arrive with it, and she would suppress the thought, push it down, refuse to let it fully
form. But suppressing it only made the fragments more intrusive. They would return unbidden, more
vivid, more accompanied by the sensation that her body was in danger even as her rational mind knew
she was sitting safely in her own home.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was a moment in the pulpit six months ago when a parishioner
had asked her to pray for a woman who had survived torture in a refugee camp. Priya had opened her
mouth and found she could not speak. She had stood there, silent, while the congregation waited, and
she had felt her body beginning to shut down—her vision narrowing, her hands shaking. She had
excused herself and spent the rest of the service in her office. That night, she had sat alone and
acknowledged what she had been avoiding: she could no longer do the work that had defined her. She
was no longer capable of being present to others' trauma when her own had made her believe that
safety was impossible, that her mind could not be trusted, and that the future held only more
fragmentation and fear.
