# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was sixty-two when the accident happened, a man who had worked at First National for thirty-
eight years without incident. He prided himself on steadiness, on being the reliable presence
customers recognized when they came through his line. On a Tuesday in March, a regular customer—a
woman in her seventies named Mrs. Chen—approached his window during the lunch rush. She was
retrieving a cashier's check, and Marcus, moving quickly to keep the queue moving, reached across
the counter to hand it to her. In that moment, his elbow caught the edge of a water bottle she had
set on the counter. It fell directly onto her foot, and she screamed. What followed was a cascade of
terrible clarity: the woman's ankle had fractured badly, the ambulance, the manager's concerned
face, the knowledge that this had happened because he was rushing, because he hadn't been careful
enough. Mrs. Chen spent six weeks in a boot. Marcus spent the time after replaying those two
seconds—the bottle falling, her cry—over and over, each time feeling the same sickening weight of
responsibility.

The months that followed were marked by a slow, creeping erosion of how Marcus understood himself.
When his wife Sarah touched him unexpectedly—a hand on his shoulder as she passed his chair, a
gentle tap on his arm to get his attention—he would flinch violently, his body rigid until he
recognized it was her. The surprise of touch had become intolerable, a reminder that he could not be
trusted to notice what was happening around him, that his awareness had failed Mrs. Chen. He had
come to believe he was fundamentally inadequate, that something essential had broken in him that
day. His reactions since the accident—the jumpiness, the way he could no longer simply relax even on
weekends when he was objectively safe at home—proved to him that he was a lousy coper, someone who
couldn't manage his own mind. These convictions deepened whenever he encountered stress of any kind,
or when he saw people who reminded him of Mrs. Chen, or even when a relative called unexpectedly.
Each trigger reinforced his sense of permanent damage, as though the accident had fundamentally
altered who he was.

At night, Marcus lay awake beside Sarah, his mind alert and scanning. He had convinced himself that
he needed to stay vigilant, that sleep was a luxury he no longer deserved, that something
catastrophic would happen if he let his guard down. The vigilance was exhausting, but it felt
necessary—a penance of sorts. When fragments of the memory surfaced without warning—the bottle
tilting, the sound of her pain—the terror he felt was identical to what he had experienced in that
moment at the counter. His heart would race, his hands would sweat, and the guilt would wash over
him with the same intensity as the day it happened. These memories seemed to appear randomly,
uninvited, and then dissolve just as suddenly, leaving him shaken and unsure of what had triggered
them. The unpredictability of these intrusions kept him on edge, hyperalert to any sign that the
memory might return.

What had begun as guilt about a specific accident had metastasized into a conviction that he was
permanently changed for the worse. The threat he felt was not merely about another accident—it was
about the truth he believed the accident had revealed: that he was unreliable, that his mind could
not be trusted. This belief made him acutely sensitive to any moment of stress or any encounter with
people who resembled his regular customers, because each one felt like a test he was destined to
fail. The hypervigilance and the insomnia had become his constant companions, and they fed the
memory, which in turn fed the conviction that he was a bad coper, a man who had lost something
irreplaceable on that ordinary Tuesday. After eight months of this, Marcus finally told Sarah that
he needed help. He was terrified that nothing could undo what he had become, but he was more
terrified of continuing to live as he was—trapped in the certainty of his own inadequacy, unable to
sleep, unable to accept a touch from the woman he loved without flinching away.
