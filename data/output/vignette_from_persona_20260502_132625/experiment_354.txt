# Rebbetzin

Miriam was forty-nine when the accident happened—a Tuesday afternoon in late September, driving home
from the synagogue after a pastoral counselling session. She remembered the light turning green, the
familiar route through downtown, the way her mind was already composing a blessing for the evening
service. Then came the impact: a delivery truck running a red light at full speed, the sickening
crunch of metal, the sensation of her body being thrown sideways as the car spun. She lost
consciousness for a moment. When she came to, trapped in the twisted frame, all she could smell was
burning—hot rubber, scorched plastic, something acrid and chemical that coated her throat. The
paramedics had to cut her out. She spent three weeks in hospital with a fractured pelvis, two broken
ribs, and a concussion that left her dizzy for months. The physical wounds had healed, more or less.
She was fifty-eight now, and the rest had not.

The nightmares began almost immediately—vivid replays of the moment of impact, sometimes fragmentary
images of the truck's grill filling her vision, sometimes just the sensation of spinning without any
visual anchor. She would wake gasping, her heart hammering, drenched in sweat, and the first thing
she would do was lie very still in the dark, gripping the sheets, waiting for her breathing to slow.
Once she woke, the anxiety would not easily release her. She had come to dread the moment of waking
from these dreams, knowing that the jolt of consciousness itself would trigger a cascade of
panic—her body convinced, even as her mind tried to reassure itself, that danger was imminent. When
she caught the smell of something burning now—a neighbour's barbecue, toast left too long in the
toaster, even the faint char of a cooking accident in the synagogue kitchen—her entire nervous
system would contract. Her hands would shake. Her breathing would shallow. She would feel the
phantom impact all over again, the sense of being utterly powerless, and she would have to excuse
herself, sometimes covering her face with her hands as if to block out the world, gripping her
temples until the wave passed.

These triggers—the dreams, the burnt smell, any mention of car accidents or traffic incidents in the
news—all seemed to activate the same response in her body. When one struck, Miriam would find
herself reaching for food almost without conscious intention. She kept snacks in her office at the
synagogue now, and at home she had begun to eat in the evenings in a way she never had before, not
from hunger but from a desperate need to soothe the panic that the triggers unleashed. She would eat
mechanically, sometimes to the point of discomfort, using the physical sensation of fullness as a
kind of anchor, a way to feel present in her body rather than trapped in the memory. She had also
developed a habit of forcing herself to think only positive thoughts when the anxiety rose—a kind of
iron discipline she applied to her own mind, insisting to herself that she was safe, that the
accident was in the past, that nothing bad would happen today. But the effort of maintaining this
relentless positivity was exhausting, and it never quite worked. The anxiety would break through
anyway, and then she would feel doubly defeated, as though her own mind had betrayed her.

The irritability had crept up on her gradually. She found herself snapping at congregants over minor
things—a misplaced prayer book, a question asked at an inopportune moment. Her voice would rise
sharply, and she would feel a flash of anger that seemed disproportionate to the trigger. What
frightened her most was that she could not always control it. She was a rabbi, trained in compassion
and patience, and yet she had begun to startle violently when someone touched her unexpectedly, even
in greeting. A hand on her shoulder would send her into an immediate state of alarm, her body
recoiling before her mind could catch up. She had started to take risks too—driving faster than she
should, accepting invitations to events that exhausted her, pushing herself in ways that felt almost
reckless, as if she were testing whether her body would betray her again. The irritability and the
startle response seemed to feed each other, and the more she felt her body reacting as if danger
were still present, the more angry and on edge she became.

What troubled Miriam most profoundly was the person she had become. She barely recognised herself
anymore. She had always been steady, grounded in her faith and her role as a spiritual guide. Now
she felt isolated even in a room full of people—set apart by something she could not name and could
not explain to others. How could she tell her congregation that their rabbi was afraid to drive,
that she ate to manage her anxiety, that she sometimes had to leave the pulpit because a particular
smell had transported her back to that Tuesday afternoon? She had come to believe that her body was
her enemy, that it would not obey the commands of her rational mind, that it kept reacting as if she
were still in that twisted car even though she was safe in her home, in her office, standing in
front of her congregation. This sense of being fundamentally broken, of being someone other than who
she had been, seemed to deepen the isolation, and the isolation itself became a new trigger—a
reminder that she was no longer the woman she had been. The more she felt set apart, the more her
body seemed to activate, the more she found herself reaching for food or forcing positivity, the
more the triggers seemed to multiply and intensify. She had tried to manage it alone for nearly a
decade, but the cycle had only tightened. It was only when her daughter gently suggested that she
might need help, that the weight of carrying this alone had become unsustainable, that Miriam
finally made an appointment.
