# Ruth's Story

Ruth had been seventy-six for three months when she finally made the appointment, though her husband
Michael had been asking her to do so for nearly a year. What brought her in was not a single crisis
but an accumulation—the way her hands now trembled when she held a newspaper, the way she no longer
attended the monthly book club she had organized for twenty years, the way she spent entire
afternoons scrolling through games on her phone without registering what she was doing. Michael said
she seemed to be disappearing. Ruth said nothing felt safe anymore, though she could not quite
explain why.

The trauma itself had occurred fifty-three years earlier, in 1971, when Ruth was a young radiography
technician working at a clinic in Beirut. She had been there for only eight months—idealistic,
competent, eager to use her skills in a place where they were desperately needed. One afternoon in
late spring, during what had seemed like a routine shift, the building had been struck. She
remembered the sound, the way the light changed, the taste of plaster dust. She remembered running,
and she remembered the screaming of others, though when she tried now to reconstruct the
sequence—what happened first, where exactly she had been, how long she had stayed—the details
fragmented. The memory did not come to her as a coherent narrative but as isolated sensations: a
pressure in her chest, the color of blood on a white coat, a face she could not name. When she tried
deliberately to piece it together, to tell the story in words, something in her throat would close.
She had carried shame about this for decades—shame that she had survived, shame that she could not
remember clearly, shame that she could not speak about it. The shame itself seemed to make the
memory more distant, as though the feeling had built a wall around the event that her mind could not
penetrate.

Over the past year, Ruth had developed a hypervigilance that now structured her entire existence.
She scanned every room she entered for exits and potential threats. When strangers approached her—a
neighbor at the mailbox, a young man asking directions—her body would flood with alarm, her mind
racing through scenarios of danger. This constant threat monitoring had intensified something else:
whenever she felt the familiar spike of fear in her chest, she would reach for her phone. She had
downloaded puzzle games, news apps, streaming services—anything that would occupy the foreground of
her attention and push back against the anxiety. The scrolling became compulsive, a way to avoid the
feelings altogether. Hours would disappear. Michael would find her at midnight, eyes glazed, phone
still in hand, having absorbed nothing.

But the avoidance had begun to feed back into the very thing she was trying to escape. The more Ruth
distracted herself, the more fragmented and inaccessible her memories became. She had stopped going
to unfamiliar places because she could not assess the danger there, which meant she had stopped
leaving the house except for the grocery store and Michael's medical appointments. Her world had
contracted. And in that contraction, the memory of Beirut had become even more foreign to her—not
integrated into her life story but sealed off, present only as a somatic alarm system that fired
whenever she felt threatened. She could not think about the event without shame flooding her, and
she could not bear the shame, so she avoided anything that might trigger the memory: conversations
about the Middle East, old photographs, even certain smells. Her avoidance of these triggers was so
thorough that she had also come to avoid making any plans for the future. What if she committed to
something and then felt afraid? What if a commitment required her to go somewhere unfamiliar, to be
around strangers, to feel something other than the gray numbness of distraction?

The threat monitoring had become exhausting. Every time Ruth felt the spike of danger—which was
often, unpredictably—she would reach for her phone again. The cycle tightened. The more she avoided
processing the memory, the more fragmented it became, and the more fragmented it was, the more her
mind seemed to interpret ordinary situations as threats. A car backfiring sounded like an explosion.
A knock at the door made her heart race. She had become certain that danger was everywhere and that
only constant vigilance and complete avoidance of feeling could keep her safe. She had retired from
radiology five years ago, telling people it was time, but the truth was that she could no longer
tolerate the uncertainty of the hospital—the unfamiliar hallways, the strangers in scrubs, the
possibility that something unexpected might happen. Now she sat in her living room, phone in hand,
and wondered how she had become so small.
