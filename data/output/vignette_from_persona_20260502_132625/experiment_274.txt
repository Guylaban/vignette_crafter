# Clinical Vignette: Thea

Thea was forty-eight when she first came in, though she looked older—the kind of older that comes
not from time but from being watched. Three years prior, a regular customer at the restaurant where
she worked had become something else. His name was Marcus. He started with requests to sit in her
section, then lingered after his meal, then began appearing on her days off at the grocery store,
the gym, the parking lot of her apartment. He never touched her. He didn't need to. The knowing
itself was the violation. After six months, Thea obtained a restraining order. Marcus disappeared
from her direct line of sight but never, she was certain, from the periphery of her life. The
stalking had officially ended two years ago. Thea had not recovered.

The trauma lived in her body as a kind of permanent listening. At home, she had installed three
additional locks and a door alarm that chimed whenever anyone entered. She heard it constantly in
her head even when she was at work—a phantom sound that made her spine stiffen mid-shift. At the
restaurant, she had stopped taking tables near the windows. She positioned herself always with a
view of the entrance. Her manager had begun to notice her refusal of certain sections, her sudden
anxiety when unfamiliar customers arrived. Thea could not explain it in words that made sense to
anyone else. The fear was not rational, she knew, but it was real: anyone could be him, anywhere
could be where he found her. She had come to believe that the world contained threats she could not
fully see, that vigilance was the only thing standing between her and catastrophe. This
hypervigilance bled into everything. She stopped going to new places—the farmer's market three
blocks away, a friend's new apartment across town—because she could not map the exits, could not
assess where danger might hide. Her avoidance of unfamiliar places was not cowardice; it was the
only rational response to a world that had proven itself fundamentally unsafe.

The memory itself had become strange and fragmented. Thea could not construct a coherent narrative
of those six months. Instead, she carried disconnected sensory shards: the particular timbre of his
voice saying her name, the color of his car in the parking lot, the feeling of his eyes on her back
as she walked to her apartment. When these images forced themselves into her mind—which happened
constantly, without warning, while she was taking orders or folding napkins—they came wordless and
overwhelming, flooding her nervous system before her conscious mind could catch up. She had tried to
talk about it in the first weeks after the restraining order, but the words would not come. The
experience felt alien to her, as if it had happened to someone else, someone she used to know. This
fragmentation only deepened her sense that something fundamental had broken inside her. The memories
would arrive unbidden, and with them came the conviction that she was no longer the woman she had
been—that something precious and irretrievable had been stolen. She used to be happy. She remembered
that person, could almost picture her. Now Thea felt only a gray, numb heaviness, a sense that joy
was something that happened to other people.

Because nothing felt meaningful anymore, because the world seemed designed to hunt her, Thea had
begun to withdraw systematically. She stopped attending her book club—too many people, too many
questions. She avoided her ex-husband's family, the few friends who still reached out, anyone from
before the stalking who might see her and ask how she was. The thought of being around people who
knew her made her skin crawl. They would see the difference, would ask her to explain, and
explanation meant reliving. More profoundly, she had begun to avoid her own body. She did not look
in mirrors. She wore loose, shapeless clothes that allowed her to forget she occupied physical
space. She showered in the dark. When she caught sight of her reflection unexpectedly, she felt a
spike of revulsion—not vanity, but a kind of estrangement. This body had been watched, had been the
object of someone's obsessive focus. Now it felt like a liability, a beacon.

The intrusive memories, arriving in their wordless, alien form, seemed to confirm her deepest fears.
Each time the memory forced its way in, she felt the world contract around her again, felt the
presence of threat closing in. And each time she felt that threat, she found herself pulling further
away—canceling plans, avoiding mirrors, refusing to go anywhere new. The loop had tightened over
months until it became the shape of her life. She had come to therapy now because her manager had
given her an ultimatum: take the tables or find another job. But what frightened her most was not
losing the job. It was the possibility that she would never feel safe again, that this vigilant,
numb, isolated version of herself was all that remained.
