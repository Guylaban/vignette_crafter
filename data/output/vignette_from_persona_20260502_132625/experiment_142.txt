# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was thirty-four when he returned from Syria. He had gone there as a relief worker with a
humanitarian NGO in the spring of 2019, drawn by a conviction that he could help—that his presence
might matter in a place where everything was collapsing. He was stationed in a contested region near
Aleppo, working out of a compound with a dozen other aid workers, delivering medical supplies and
water to displaced families. On a Tuesday morning in late July, a mortar round struck the compound's
eastern wall. Marcus was in the supply tent, perhaps twenty feet away. He heard the impact before he
felt it—a sound like the world splitting open—and then he was on the ground, ears ringing, dust in
his mouth. Two colleagues were killed. A third, a woman named Sarah whom he had worked alongside for
three months, lay bleeding from shrapnel wounds to her abdomen. Marcus applied pressure to the wound
with his hands while waiting for evacuation, whispering to her that she would be okay, knowing as he
did so that this was probably a lie. She survived the flight to Turkey but died in the hospital two
days later.

He came home to his wife, Elena, in August. For the first few weeks, he seemed almost normal—quieter
than before, but present. He slept poorly and woke sometimes gasping, but he told Elena it was just
adjustment, just the time difference catching up with him. By October, the pattern had shifted. He
found himself unable to relax in their living room. He would sit on the couch but position himself
so he could see the doorway and the window at once. When Elena tried to draw the curtains in the
evening, he felt a spike of panic—an irrational but overwhelming conviction that closing them made
him trapped, vulnerable. He had come to believe that safety required constant vigilance, that
relaxing his guard even for a moment was a kind of negligence that would be punished. This belief
drove him to scan their home obsessively, to note the location of exits, to sleep with the bedroom
door open so he could hear anyone approaching. At work, the hypervigilance was even more pronounced.
As a firefighter—a job he had held for eight years before his deployment—he found himself unable to
tolerate the station's layout. The bunkroom had only one exit, and he refused to sleep there,
instead spending his nights in the kitchen with a clear sightline to the corridor. His captain
noticed. His crew noticed. But when they asked if he was all right, Marcus deflected, saying only
that he preferred the kitchen, that the bunk was too hot.

What made things worse was that Marcus had begun to avoid the very things that might have helped. He
stopped going to the gym, where he had lifted weights four mornings a week before deployment. The
gym felt too enclosed, too crowded; strangers moving unpredictably around him triggered a sensation
of threat that was so acute it bordered on physical pain. He would drive past the entrance, feel the
familiar spike of dread, and keep driving. Elena suggested they go for walks together, something
they had done regularly before Syria. He declined, and when she pressed gently, he found himself
snapping at her, explaining—more to himself than to her—that being outside, visible, exposed to
people he didn't know, felt dangerous in a way he couldn't articulate. He had pushed away her
suggestion that they spend an evening with friends, telling her that social gatherings were
exhausting, which was true but incomplete. The deeper truth was that he could not bear the thought
of being around people whose sudden movements or loud voices might trigger the conviction that
danger was imminent. Over time, he had come to see these avoidance patterns not as symptoms but as
necessary precautions. If he stayed home, stayed alert, kept exits mapped and strangers at a
distance, he could manage the constant low-level terror that had become the baseline of his
existence.

But the avoidance had consequences he had not anticipated. His marriage had grown strained. Elena
felt rejected, confused by a husband who seemed to fear her presence as much as he feared anything
else. His captain had begun documenting his refusal to follow station protocol. And underneath it
all, Marcus was exhausted—not from hypervigilance itself, but from the isolation that hypervigilance
had created. He had come to push away moments of genuine safety and connection because they felt
untrustworthy, almost dangerous. When Elena brought him coffee in the morning, he felt a flash of
irritation that she had caught him unaware. When his crew laughed together, he felt a cold spike of
anxiety, as though laughter was a sign of complacency that would be punished. By January, he finally
called a therapist. He told her, in a voice barely above a whisper, that he no longer knew how to
live in a world where anything could happen, and that the only way he had found to survive was to
trust nothing and no one—a strategy that was slowly erasing the life he had returned home to
protect.
