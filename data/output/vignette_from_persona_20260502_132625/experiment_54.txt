# Catalina's Reckoning

Catalina had been a diplomat for sixteen years, fluent in three languages and trained to negotiate
the impossible. She had served in conflict zones before—brief postings in places where the tension
was high but the shooting, mercifully, stayed distant. What happened in Beirut was different. She
had gone there in the spring of 2021 to facilitate talks between rival factions, work she believed
in deeply, work that had given her life structure and purpose. For three weeks, she moved between
safe houses and government buildings, translating not just words but intent, trying to find the thin
thread of common ground. On the twenty-third day, the building where she was meeting with a local
council came under fire. Not distant artillery—this was close, automatic weapons, the sound of
concrete being torn apart. She and six others were forced into a basement room by armed men she
could not identify, pressed against a wall as the fighting raged above them. One man tried to run;
they held him down with such casual violence that Catalina understood, in that moment, that
restraint was not a negotiating tactic but a tool of control. She felt hands on her shoulders, on
her arms, forcing her to stay still, to stay silent. The physical pressure—the absolute removal of
her agency—was as vivid as the noise and the fear.

The rescue came after four hours. She was extracted, debriefed, and sent home on medical leave. The
State Department called it a traumatic incident. Her supervisor suggested counseling. Catalina had
nodded, thanked them, and returned to her apartment in Arlington, certain that she simply needed
time to process what had happened rationally, the way she had learned to process everything else.

Six months later, she was no longer certain of anything.

The first trigger came unexpectedly: a news report about military deployments in the Eastern
Mediterranean. The anchor's clinical description of troop movements, the mention of weapons systems,
pulled Catalina back into the basement with a force that left her gasping. She had immediately shut
off the television, but the damage was done. Over the following weeks, any mention of military
operations—a colleague's comment about defense spending, a documentary she stumbled upon—sent her
into a state of acute dread. More insidiously, she began to notice that the smallest moral failure
in those around her could trigger the same response: a friend who admitted to cheating on her taxes,
a news story about corruption, even a colleague who cut in line at the cafeteria. These moments of
witnessing human transgression seemed to unlock something in her, a sudden sense that the world was
fundamentally lawless, that the violence she had witnessed was not aberrant but inevitable. What
frightened her most was the feeling that came with these triggers—a creeping conviction that she
herself was complicit, that her presence in Beirut had somehow enabled the men who held her down,
that she lacked the moral clarity to make the right choices anymore.

In response to these thoughts, Catalina had developed a pattern of rumination that consumed her
evenings and weekends. She would sit in her apartment, going over and over the sequence of events,
asking herself why she had been there, why she had believed she could make a difference, whether her
presence had somehow provoked the violence. The questions spiraled without resolution. She thought
constantly about the meaning of what had happened—whether it was punishment, whether it revealed
something true about her that she had not wanted to see. She tried to suppress these thoughts by
staying busy, by filling her schedule with work and exercise, but the suppression itself became
another form of vigilance. She would catch herself clenching her jaw, tensing her shoulders, as
though bracing against an invisible restraint. The more she tried not to think about what happened,
the more acutely she felt the phantom pressure of hands holding her in place.

By the time she requested a reassignment from the State Department, the cycle had tightened around
her. Her rumination about why the event had happened had crystallized into a darker conviction:
nothing in her life felt meaningful anymore. Her work, which had once been a calling, now felt like
a performance. She could not trust herself to make decisions, could not shake the belief that she
was somehow fundamentally flawed, that she had been reduced to something less than human by what she
had endured. When a colleague mentioned a security briefing on military threats, Catalina felt her
chest constrict, and with it came the familiar spiral of self-doubt and meaninglessness. She found
herself avoiding not just news and conversations but also the office itself on certain days, telling
her supervisor she was working remotely when, in fact, she was sitting in her apartment, caught
between the urge to understand why this had happened to her and the desperate need to push the
thoughts away. The more she ruminated, the more fragile her sense of self became. The more fragile
she became, the more vulnerable she was to the next trigger, the next moment of witnessing human
cruelty or hearing a word about war that would send her spiraling again.

It was a conversation with her sister—a simple, loving insistence that Catalina needed help—that
finally broke through the isolation. Catalina called a trauma therapist on a Tuesday morning, her
hands shaking as she dialed, afraid that naming what had happened would make it more real, but also
terrified that the alternative was to remain trapped in this loop indefinitely, increasingly
convinced that she was no longer the person she had been, and uncertain whether she ever wanted to
be again.
