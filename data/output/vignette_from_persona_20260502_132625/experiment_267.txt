Arjun was twenty when his older cousin Rohan died. It was a Tuesday in March, unremarkable except
for the phone call from his mother, her voice fractured in a way he had never heard before. Rohan
had been driving home from work—a software engineer, stable, careful, the kind of person everyone
said had his life figured out. A drunk driver crossed the median on the interstate. Rohan was
pronounced dead at the scene. There had been no warning, no illness, no time to say anything. One
moment he existed; the next, he didn't. For Arjun, who had grown up in the same house as Rohan
during his childhood, who had watched him navigate college and career with a steadiness Arjun
secretly envied, the event shattered something fundamental about how he understood safety. The
world, he had come to believe, was a place where ordinary Tuesday mornings could end in death.
Randomness was the only rule.

In the weeks after the funeral, Arjun tried to write. He had been working on a novel—a small
literary project he had been quietly proud of—but the words felt hollow now, contaminated by the
knowledge that none of it mattered if everything could be taken away without reason. He abandoned
the manuscript. More pressingly, when his parents began asking him about college applications, about
whether he would apply for jobs, about what came next, Arjun found himself unable to answer. The
future felt like a trap. If he made plans, if he allowed himself to imagine anything—a career, a
relationship, a life—he was only setting himself up for devastation. So he stopped planning. He
refused to discuss it. His mother would ask if he had looked at internships; he would change the
subject or leave the room. The avoidance felt necessary, protective. Not planning meant nothing
could be taken from him.

Yet the avoidance created its own torment. Without plans, without forward motion, Arjun felt
unmoored, and that formlessness fed a deepening conviction that the world was fundamentally
dangerous and that he was powerless within it. His inability to imagine a future began to feel like
evidence that no future was worth imagining—that nothing he did mattered, that safety was an
illusion. He became hypervigilant in ways he did not fully recognize. Sounds made him jump: a car
horn outside his window would send adrenaline flooding through his chest. He could not relax even
when sitting alone in his bedroom with the door locked. His body seemed to expect catastrophe at
every moment. Sleep became nearly impossible. He would lie awake for hours, his mind cycling through
scenarios—car crashes, sudden illnesses, the random violence that had taken Rohan. When he did
sleep, he woke gasping.

The avoidance of grief reminders became another layer of the trap. Anything connected to Rohan—a
song that had played at the funeral, the software company's logo glimpsed in an advertisement, even
the sight of someone driving a silver sedan like Rohan's—triggered a wave of panic and despair. So
Arjun began to avoid more and more. He stopped going to the coffee shop where he used to write
because Rohan had taken him there once. He declined invitations to family gatherings where relatives
might mention Rohan's name. He isolated himself, telling himself this was the only way to manage the
grief, but what actually happened was that his world grew smaller and his conviction that he was
fundamentally unsafe grew larger.

By late summer, five months after Rohan's death, Arjun was stuck in a cycle he could not name. The
more he avoided—the future, reminders, social situations—the more his belief that the world was
dangerous and that he was helpless intensified. And the more convinced he became of that danger, the
more he needed to avoid, to seek reassurance from his parents that he was okay, that nothing bad
would happen to him. He would call his mother at work and ask her to confirm he was safe, and for a
few minutes after she reassured him, the panic would subside. But it always returned, sharper each
time. His parents, alarmed by his deterioration, finally insisted he see someone. Arjun came to the
first appointment exhausted and terrified, unable to articulate why he felt so broken, only knowing
that he had lost not just his cousin but his ability to believe in tomorrow.
