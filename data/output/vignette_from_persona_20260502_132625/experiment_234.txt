stitches across his cheekbone would leave a scar that he still touches sometimes, as if checking
that it is still there. The official report said he had "resisted." The charges were eventually
dropped. No one was disciplined.

he needed to know where danger might come from before he entered a space. He began identifying
escape routes in every room he entered, tracing them mentally the way other men might scan a menu.
At the gym, he positioned himself near the door. At client meetings, he sat where he could see the
hallway.

What frightened Marco most, though, was not the fear itself but what the fear meant about him. He
had come to believe that something intrinsic to him—something in his bearing, his skin, his very
presence—had invited that violence. If he had been someone else, someone less marked by his own

hold, would not release him. When he caught his reflection unexpectedly, he would see the scar and
feel a surge of shame so acute it nearly stopped his breath. He began to isolate himself, declining

set apart from other people in a way he could not bridge, as though the trauma had marked him as
fundamentally different, fundamentally wrong.

with a bandaged face, a woman with visible bruising—something in his nervous system would spike. The
image would pull him back into that Tuesday evening, and he would feel the phantom pain in his ribs,
the pressure in his chest. These moments of contact with others' injuries, with reminders of
victimization, would send him spiraling into deeper conviction that the world was fundamentally
unsafe and that he was fundamentally unable to protect himself. The checking behind him intensified.
The mental mapping of exits became obsessive. He began sleeping with his bedroom door locked and a
flashlight on the nightstand.
