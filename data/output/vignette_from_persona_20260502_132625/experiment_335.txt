# Amara's Story

Amara was twenty when the soldiers came to the village. She had been studying pharmacy in the
capital, home for the university break, when armed men arrived at dawn—no warning, no explanation.
They separated her from her mother and younger brother, forcing her into a concrete room where she
lost track of time. What happened there—the interrogations, the restraint, the forced positions her
body was made to hold for hours—remained fragmented in her mind now, three years later. She could
feel it: the specific angle of her shoulders wrenched backward, the cold of the floor against her
spine, the sensation of water filling her mouth and nose during the interrogation sessions. But when
she tried to speak about it, the words dissolved. It was as though her body remembered everything
while her mind could only hold pieces, jagged and disconnected, refusing to form a coherent
narrative.

She had managed to leave the country six months after her release, sponsored through a university
exchange program. The pharmacy degree was waiting; so was the pretense that she could simply resume
her life. Her boyfriend, Kwame, whom she'd met online during those early months of resettlement in
the United States, had been patient and kind. They had been together for two years now, and he loved
her in a way that felt genuine. Yet Amara had come to believe that she did not deserve this
gentleness, that surviving when others in that room had not—when she did not even know their
names—meant she owed the world her suffering. The guilt sat like a stone in her chest, immovable.

When Amara encountered a trigger, the sequence was always the same. A news report about flooding in
another country, and her body tensed; a raised voice between colleagues at the pharmacy, and she
felt the old panic rising. An older supervisor asking her to cover an extra shift—a small demand, a
loss of choice—and she found herself unable to say no, even as her breathing quickened. These
moments activated the fragments. When the water trigger appeared, her shoulders would involuntarily
draw backward, and the incomplete memory would surface: the sensation without the story, the bodily
reenactment without understanding. Arguments triggered the same bodily response, the same wordless
terror. And when she encountered these reminders, she became certain she could not handle what they
might unleash—that to truly remember, to fully feel, would destroy her. So she began to avoid the
situations that might bring the memories closer. She stopped watching the news. She took routes
through the pharmacy that avoided the break room where staff sometimes gathered, where voices might
rise. She never said no to requests, never asserted any preference, because being forced to do
something—even something small—felt like a rehearsal of that original powerlessness. She avoided the
evenings when sleep came too easily, when dreams might overtake her, choosing instead to stay awake,
scrolling through her phone until exhaustion forced her under.

Her hypervigilance had become a constant companion. Amara had to stay on guard everywhere, always
scanning for the next threat, always identifying where she could run. In the pharmacy, she noted the
exits before she began her shift, traced the path to the back door, calculated whether she could
reach her car in thirty seconds. When Kwame wanted to take her to the beach for her birthday, she
felt a cold dread that she couldn't fully explain to him. Water—large, open water—triggered the
fragments again, and she was certain that exposure would break her. She declined, and he did not
push. At night, she lay awake beside him, unable to sleep because vigilance was survival; the moment
she let her guard down, something terrible would happen. This constant threat monitoring fed her
conviction that danger was everywhere, which then justified her avoidance of situations that might
expose her to the triggers she feared. The cycle tightened: her threat perception kept her scanning,
kept her avoiding, kept her awake. And the avoidance, in turn, prevented her from discovering that
the threats she monitored so carefully were not actually present in her current life.

The incomplete memories and bodily reenactments intensified when she was most alert, most scanning,
most afraid. Her body held the truth that her mind could not articulate, and the constant state of
vigilance meant she was always half-aware of these sensations, always braced against them. She had
not told her supervisor at the pharmacy that she sometimes needed to step into the storage room and
breathe, that certain requests made her feel trapped. She had not told Kwame that she sometimes woke
in a cold sweat, her shoulders burning, her body convinced it was still being forced into that
position. She told herself she deserved to carry this alone, that speaking it aloud would confirm
how broken she was.

It was a quiet Tuesday in the pharmacy when a patient came through the line with a water bottle, and
the way the light caught the liquid—clear, reflective, moving—sent Amara into a spiral that lasted
three hours after her shift ended. She sat in her car in the parking lot, unable to drive, unable to
breathe, unable to name what was happening. That night, she finally told Kwame that she needed help.
She was twenty-three now, and she was exhausted from running, from staying alert, from being alone
with fragments she could not speak.
