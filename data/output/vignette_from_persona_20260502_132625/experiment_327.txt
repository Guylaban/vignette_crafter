# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was thirty-two weeks into what should have been an ordinary Tuesday when his daughter Emma
stopped breathing. She was sixteen months old, napping in her crib while he worked from home, and
when he checked on her during a conference call, she was blue. The paramedics' sirens, the
hospital's fluorescent corridors, the ventilator's mechanical wheeze—these images burned themselves
into him with the clarity of photographs. Emma spent four days in the pediatric ICU with suspected
bronchiolitis, each hour stretching into an eternity of monitors and oxygen saturation numbers that
climbed and fell like a cruel tide. She survived. But Marcus did not emerge from that week
unchanged.

Now, eighteen months later, at twenty-eight years old, he found himself a stranger in his own life.
The man he had been before—the one who could sit through client presentations without scanning for
exits, who fell asleep within minutes of his head touching the pillow—had vanished. In his place was
someone hypervigilant, someone who calculated worst-case scenarios the way other people checked
email. When his wife Sarah mentioned a news story about childhood pneumonia, Marcus felt his chest
tighten and his mind flood with the hospital's particular shade of white, the specific pitch of the
alarm that had sounded when Emma's oxygen dipped. The trigger—a simple news alert—had pulled the
memory forward unbidden, and with it came the crushing conviction that he had failed. He should have
noticed something was wrong sooner. He should have been the kind of father who could protect his
daughter from illness itself, not merely react after the fact.

The memories themselves were vivid in a way that exhausted him. When they surfaced, they came as
images first—Emma's small chest heaving, the blur of the paramedic's hands, the particular slant of
afternoon light through the hospital window—and these pictures arrived before any narrative could
form. He would find himself pushing them away, unable to bear the full weight of recall, yet unable
to prevent them from returning. The blocked memories and the vivid ones seemed to feed each other:
he would catch a glimpse of something that reminded him of that day—a child crying in the grocery
store, a rainy afternoon that matched the weather when Emma fell ill—and the fragmentary images
would ambush him. Each trigger, whether external or internal, seemed to confirm his darkest
interpretation: that he was fundamentally unsafe as a protector, that the person he had
become—someone paralyzed by dread, someone who couldn't be present with his own daughter without his
nervous system screaming danger—was unrecognisable to him.

This unrecognisable version of himself then made him more vigilant to threat. Marcus had begun
identifying escape routes before entering any room, a habit that had started in the hospital and
never stopped. In restaurants, he positioned himself facing the door. At his office, a glass-walled
financial services firm in downtown Boston, he had rearranged his desk so he could see the corridor.
His colleagues had noticed; one had asked if he was feeling okay. He wasn't. His mind had become a
threat-detection system running constantly on high alert. At night, he lay awake beside Sarah,
unable to fall asleep because part of him believed he needed to stay conscious, to monitor, to be
ready. The vigilance felt necessary, even though he knew intellectually that Emma was healthy now,
that his alertness could not prevent illness. Yet every time he relaxed his guard—every time he
almost drifted off—his mind would offer him a scenario: what if she got sick again and he was
asleep, unreachable?

The triggers and the threat-monitoring had begun to loop in a way that felt inescapable. Being
around children, which had once been his joy, now activated a cascade of false alarms. His sister
had visited with her toddler last month, and Marcus had found himself unable to sit still, unable to
engage. He heard the child's cough and his body flooded with adrenaline, as if Emma were in crisis
again. The news he had once scrolled through casually now felt like a minefield—every story about
childhood illness, every weather report that mentioned rain or cold, sent him spiraling. These
triggers pulled the fragmented memories forward, which rekindled his sense of identity loss and
failure, which then made him scan his environment for danger more intensely, which made him more
reactive to the next trigger. He had come to believe that the person he had become—hypervigilant,
withdrawn from his own daughter at times because being near her felt unbearable, unable to sleep—was
not a response to trauma but a permanent truth about who he was now.

Sarah had finally insisted he seek help after finding him at three in the morning, sitting in Emma's
room in the dark, unable to explain why he couldn't return to bed. Marcus had made the appointment,
though he carried with him the conviction that therapy might help him manage the symptoms but could
never restore the man he had been.
