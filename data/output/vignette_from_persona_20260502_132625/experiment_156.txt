pinned his door, and for the minutes before the fire department arrived, Carlos had been certain he
would burn. He remembered the smell of gasoline and hot metal, the weight of the steering wheel
against his ribs, and the absolute knowledge that his life was ending—not metaphorically, but in
that moment, in that crushed metal box.

He had survived with a fractured collarbone and lacerations across his left shoulder. The physical
injuries healed within weeks. What did not heal was the architecture of his certainty about the
world. By the time he came to see me, four years had passed, and Carlos had constructed an elaborate
system of avoidance and dissociation that had, by his own account, allowed him to continue
living—though he was no longer certain that what he was doing constituted life in any meaningful
sense.

was. He had been distracted that evening, replaying a conversation with one of the younger dancers,

flaw—his capacity for selfishness, his aging vanity—had somehow drawn the accident toward him, as if
the universe punished moral failing with vehicular catastrophe. This conviction that he was
fundamentally unsafe because he was fundamentally flawed had calcified into something unshakeable.

hypervigilance that had become his baseline. When he entered a room—the studio, a restaurant, his
own home—Carlos's eyes moved immediately to exits, calculating routes, assessing which doorways
might be blocked. This surveillance was not something he chose; it arose from the deep certainty
that bad things happened to people like him, that only constant vigilance might prevent the next
catastrophe.

His startle response had become severe enough that his wife, Maria, had stopped touching him without
warning. Even her hand on his shoulder from behind could send him into a state of alarm that lasted

enough to dance. The loss of this identity—dancer, artist, someone who moved through the world with
