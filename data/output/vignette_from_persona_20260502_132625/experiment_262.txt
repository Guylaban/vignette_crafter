# Clinical Vignette: Marko

Marko was seventeen when his family fled Syria. The journey across the Mediterranean took three days
in a fishing boat so overcrowded that people stood pressed against one another, unable to sit. The
engine failed twice. Both times, the silence that followed—a silence so complete it seemed to
swallow sound itself—meant everyone knew they might die. On the second failure, a man near Marko
collapsed from dehydration. No one could move to help him. The engine restarted, and they reached
Greece, but Marko had already learned that silence meant danger was coming. Four years later, now
working as a public defender in a mid-sized American city, he still could not sit through a quiet
moment without his body tensing, his breath shallow, waiting for the next catastrophe.

The memories of that crossing had not faded with time; they had calcified into something more vivid
than the present. When Marko closed his eyes, he saw the gray water, the woman's face beside him,
the exact angle of the boat's railing. These were not distant recollections but living images that
surfaced without warning—at his desk, during a client meeting, lying in bed next to his girlfriend,
Sofia. What struck him most was how completely his body inhabited those moments: his shoulders would
hunch forward the way they had on the boat when space was impossible; his legs would tense as if
braced against the hull's constant pitch and sway. It was as though his nervous system had recorded
not just what he saw but the precise physical configuration of survival, and his body would slip
back into that configuration whenever the images returned.

Because Marko had come to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous—that safety was an
illusion maintained only by constant vigilance—he had developed a set of practices meant to keep him
alive. He monitored his own thoughts obsessively, alert for signs that the trauma was breaking him,
that he was "going crazy" as he sometimes whispered to himself. He avoided talking about the
crossing with anyone, not because he was ashamed but because he was certain that speaking about it
would make it more real, would give it weight in the present moment and draw danger closer. When
Sofia asked him about his family's journey, he would change the subject or claim he was too tired to
discuss it. His colleagues at the public defender's office had learned not to ask; Marko would
deflect with a joke or simply go silent, his jaw tightening in a way that discouraged further
questions.

The startle response had become part of his daily texture. A door slamming in the courthouse hallway
sent him into a state of acute alarm that took minutes to settle. The hum of the refrigerator in the
office break room, the sudden quiet after a client finished speaking—these ordinary sounds and
silences triggered a cascade of physical reactions: his heart accelerating, his hands going cold,
his mind flooding with the certainty that something terrible was about to unfold. When nothing
happened—when the silence resolved into the mundane shuffle of papers or the distant sound of
traffic—Marko did not feel relief. Instead, he felt the false alarm itself as confirmation of
danger; if his body was reacting this way, it meant the threat was real even if he could not yet
perceive it. This reasoning, which made perfect sense to him in the moment, only deepened his
conviction that he had to be on guard at all times, that the world demanded constant vigilance or it
would consume him.

The memories and the startle responses fed each other in a cycle that Marko could not break. When a
sound startled him, the jolt would trigger a flash of the boat—the engine failing, the silence, the
man collapsing. And when the image came, his body would brace itself as it had four years ago, which
in turn made him feel that the danger was not past but present, that he was still in the immediate
shadow of catastrophe. This reinforced his conviction that he could never relax, never trust that he
was safe, never allow himself the vulnerability of talking openly about what had happened. Sofia had
begun to notice that he would wake at night in a state of agitation, or that he would grip her hand
so tightly during quiet moments that she had to ask him to loosen his grip. She did not understand
why he flinched when she moved suddenly, or why he insisted on sitting with his back to the wall in
restaurants. When she tried to comfort him, Marko would pull away, afraid that if he let himself be
soothed, he would lose the vigilance that kept him alive.

By twenty-one, Marko had become someone whose internal experience was dominated by threat—real and
imagined—and whose attempts to manage that threat had begun to isolate him from the people closest
to him. It was only when Sofia threatened to leave, saying she could not stay with someone who would
not let her in, that Marko finally made an appointment with a therapist. He sat in the waiting room,
his leg bouncing, scanning the other clients' faces for signs of danger, wondering if seeking help
meant admitting that he was broken, that the world had won.
