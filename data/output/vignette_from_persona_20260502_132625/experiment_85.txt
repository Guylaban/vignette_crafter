# Katarina

Katarina was thirty-eight when she went to cover the conflict in Syria for the international news
bureau where she worked as a visual data analyst. Her role was to process satellite imagery and
cross-reference it with eyewitness accounts, building data visualizations that would accompany field
reporters' stories. She had done this work in other regions—South Sudan, Myanmar, parts of the
Caucasus—and had developed a professional distance from it, or so she believed. But Syria was
different. In March 2019, she travelled to a small village in Aleppo province with a journalist
named Hassan to verify reports of civilian casualties. They arrived in the morning. The village was
quiet, almost unnervingly so. She was photographing buildings and taking GPS coordinates when the
first strike came—not a distant sound but a percussive, physical thing that moved through her chest.
The explosion was half a mile away, but the sound came again, and again. Hassan grabbed her arm and
they ran toward a partially collapsed building. She remembers the heat on her face, the taste of
dust and something acrid she would later understand was burning plastic and fuel. She remembers
Hassan pushing her behind a concrete wall, his hand on her shoulder. She remembers the sound of
children screaming somewhere in the smoke—not one child, many—and the absolute certainty in that
moment that she could not reach them, could not help, could not do anything but crouch and wait.
When the strikes stopped, after what felt like an hour but was probably fifteen minutes, she and
Hassan made their way back to their vehicle. She did not see the children. She never found out if
they survived.

The first weeks after returning to the United States were strange but manageable. Katarina threw
herself into her work with an intensity that surprised even her husband, Marcus. She came home late,
worked through weekends, and found that when she was deep in data—cleaning datasets, building
models, solving technical problems—the memory of that morning felt distant and almost unreal. But by
July, something had shifted. She began to notice that certain things would trigger a sudden,
overwhelming rush of presence: the smell of smoke from a neighbor's grill, the sound of a siren,
even a news headline mentioning Arab villages or Middle Eastern conflict. When these moments came,
she did not simply remember the event—she felt as though she were back there, her body reacting as
if the danger were immediate. Her heart would race, her breathing would shallow, and for a few
seconds she could hear the sound of the explosions again, impossibly vivid and real, layered beneath
whatever ambient noise surrounded her. These triggered memories felt more substantial, more true,
than ordinary recollection. When she tried to recall details of her apartment or conversations with
Marcus, those memories felt thin and pale by comparison. The vividness of the trauma memory made her
certain that something terrible was about to happen again.

Each trigger seemed to pull the others in its wake. When she encountered news coverage of conflict
in Arab villages, or saw flames in a photograph, the sense of immediate threat would overwhelm her.
She felt certain that strangers on the street were watching her, that something catastrophic was
imminent, and that she had no way to protect herself or anyone else. These feelings drove her back
to work with even greater urgency. She began arriving at the office at six in the morning and
staying until nine or ten at night, telling herself that staying busy was simply professional
dedication. But the real motivation, which she admitted only to herself, was that work kept her from
being alone with her thoughts—and when she was alone, the memories came without warning, fully
formed and sensory-rich, as though her mind had recorded everything in perfect detail and was now
playing it back on a loop. The vividness of these memories only deepened her conviction that she had
failed catastrophically. She had been there. She could have done something. Someone else—someone
braver, someone with better instincts—would not have frozen. Would not have hidden. Would have found
the children. The thought that other people would not have made her mistakes, that she was uniquely
culpable, became a constant undertone to her rumination. She went over and over the sequence of
events, trying to identify the moment where she could have acted differently, could have protected
others instead of protecting herself.

The cycle tightened over the following months. Her excessive work, meant to silence the memories,
paradoxically intensified them. When she was finally forced to stop working—exhausted, her
concentration fragmenting—the memories returned with even greater force. The threat she felt became
more pervasive. She began checking the locks on her apartment door and windows repeatedly, sometimes
five or six times before bed, driven by a conviction that danger could come from anywhere. At work,
she found herself increasingly isolated. Colleagues would approach her desk to discuss a project,
and she would feel a spike of alarm, interpreting their presence as threatening even though she knew
rationally that they meant no harm. She began eating lunch alone, arriving early to avoid the
crowded hallways, and this isolation only gave her more time to ruminate about her failures. By the
time she reached out for help, in early 2024, she had become someone she did not recognize. Marcus
had grown distant, confused by her late nights and her emotional withdrawal. Her supervisor had
expressed concern about her erratic work patterns. She had lost weight. And most frighteningly, in
the quiet moments before sleep, she had begun to wonder whether her life was worth the effort of
living it—whether someone who had failed so fundamentally had any right to move forward.
