# Kofi's Story

Kofi Mensah was fifty-eight when the world became a place where danger lived in small, ordinary
moments. It happened on a Tuesday evening in March, three years ago, when he was closing his salon
on Atlantic Avenue. He had owned the shop for twenty-two years—a modest space with six chairs, where
he cut hair and listened to people's stories, where regularity and routine had always felt like a
kind of safety. That evening, as he was sweeping up the last of the day's clippings, a man pushed
through the door he was locking. The man smelled of stale alcohol and desperation. There was a
knife. Kofi remembered the blade catching the fluorescent light, remembered the man's
eyes—unfocused, wild—and the overwhelming feeling of helplessness as his wallet was torn from his
hands. The physical injury was minor; the robbery netted less than two hundred dollars. But what
stayed with Kofi was not the money or even the knife itself. It was the sensory snapshot that
replayed endlessly: the sour, acidic reek of alcohol mixed with sweat, the man's face hovering
inches from his own, the certainty in that moment that he was about to die.

In the months that followed, Kofi tried to return to work. His wife, Ama, encouraged him gently; his
regular clients asked when he would be back. But the salon, which had once felt like his domain, had
become a place of vigilance and dread. When a customer came in smelling of alcohol—something he
would never have noticed before—his body would flood with adrenaline. The smell transported him
instantly back to that evening, and the intrusive images would begin: the knife, the man's face, the
feeling of being trapped. These sensory flashes were vivid and involuntary, like a film playing in
his mind that he could not stop or rewind. He found himself watching customers more closely, reading
suspicion into normal behaviour—a man standing too still, someone glancing around the shop, a
teenager lingering near the door. Each suspicious gesture felt like a warning, a sign that danger
was imminent.

Kofi had come to believe that he should have seen it coming, that a more alert man, a more careful
man, would have locked the door earlier or recognized the threat before the man entered. He spent
hours replaying the evening in his mind, cataloguing what he might have done differently. If he had
closed at five instead of six. If he had not been so trusting, so naive. The self-blame consumed
him—not as a fleeting thought but as a constant, grinding rumination that occupied his evenings and
early mornings. When these thoughts surfaced, he would try desperately to push them away, to
suppress the images and the narrative of his own failure, but the effort itself seemed to strengthen
them. The more he tried not to think about the trauma, the more insistently it returned.

His avoidance of people had grown quietly and then suddenly. He stopped opening the salon on
Tuesdays. He declined invitations from friends. When Ama suggested he visit the barber association's
monthly meeting, he made an excuse. At home, he spent more time alone in the garage, working on
small projects that required no conversation. The isolation felt necessary—a way to control his
environment, to minimize the chance of encountering triggers. But each act of avoidance seemed to
narrow his world further. When he did venture out, hypervigilance consumed him. A stranger's
criticism—a comment from Ama about how he was handling something, a client's gentle suggestion that
he might trim differently—would sting disproportionately and send him spiralling into self-
recrimination. He would interpret the smallest correction as evidence of his incompetence, as proof
that the man in the salon had been right to target him, that he was weak and deserving of harm.

The intrusive images had become relentless. They arrived without warning: a flash of the knife, the
man's face, the smell of alcohol in the air of the salon. Each time, the sensory vividness was
absolute, and each time, Kofi would find himself unable to articulate what he had experienced. He
knew what had happened—he could describe the facts—but when he tried to talk to Ama about the actual
terror, the texture of it, the way it lived in his body, the words dissolved. This silence only
deepened his isolation. After three years of this, Kofi finally made an appointment with a
therapist, though he remained convinced that what had happened to him was fundamentally his fault,
and that the fear he carried was simply the price of his own failure to be vigilant enough, strong
enough, worthy enough of safety.
