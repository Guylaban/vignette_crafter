# Marcus

Marcus was thirty-two when it happened, teaching sophomore history at Lincoln High in a neighborhood
that had been slowly gentrifying for a decade. He'd always felt safe there—or safe enough. The
mugging occurred on a Tuesday evening in October, on the street corner two blocks from the school
where he'd stayed late grading papers. A man emerged from a parked car, demanded his wallet, and
when Marcus hesitated—a moment of confusion more than resistance—the man shoved him hard against a
storefront window. The glass didn't break, but Marcus felt the impact in his spine, the sudden
certainty that he was about to be hurt far worse. The attacker grabbed his jacket, wrenched it
partway off, and Marcus smelled something acrid and chemical, sweat and cologne mixed with something
he couldn't name. There was shouting—he couldn't remember whose voice—and then the man was gone,
running. Marcus stood there bleeding from a cut on his forehead, his wallet gone, his body trembling
in a way he couldn't control.

The police came. They took a statement, filed a report, and told him they'd look into it. They never
called back. Six months later, when Marcus finally asked the precinct about the case, a tired
detective told him there was no lead, no suspect, and it would likely remain unsolved. The
department was understaffed. These things happened. Marcus felt something calcify inside him then—a
recognition that the systems meant to protect him had simply failed, that the world operated on a
principle of indifference he'd been naive enough not to see before.

For the first few months after the mugging, Marcus tried to return to normal. He went back to
teaching, graded papers at his desk instead of staying late, and told his wife Elena that he was
fine. But the world had become a different place. Crowded places—the grocery store on Saturday
mornings, the subway platform during rush hour, even his own classroom when all thirty students were
present—sent his nervous system into a state of vigilance he couldn't switch off. His heart would
accelerate, his palms would sweat, and he found himself studying the faces around him, cataloging
expressions, watching for the telltale signs of aggression or intent. Every unexpected movement in
his peripheral vision triggered a flood of adrenaline, a primitive conviction that danger was
imminent. When a student dropped a textbook in class last spring, Marcus flinched so violently that
several kids stared at him in concern.

The memories came without warning, and when they did, they consumed him entirely. He'd be sitting in
the faculty lounge or driving home from work when something—a smell, a sudden noise, the particular
way someone stood—would crack open the moment, and he was back on that street corner. The chemical
smell returned, thick and suffocating. He could taste the copper of his own blood, feel the rough
texture of the storefront glass against his palms. The sensations arrived all at once, complete and
undeniable, and his mind insisted they were happening now. These intrusions were merciless. They
forced their way in unbidden, and no amount of rational effort could stop them. When he was driving
with Elena or one of his colleagues, the fear would spike unpredictably—a horn blaring, a car
cutting too close—and the memory would surge forward, his body tensing as if the attack were
occurring in real time.

The intrusions fed his conviction that the world was fundamentally dangerous, that safety was an
illusion. Every time the memory returned with its full sensory weight, it reinforced his belief that
threat was everywhere, that he couldn't trust his own assessment of whether a situation was actually
safe. And that belief, in turn, made him hypervigilant to new triggers. He began avoiding crowded
places deliberately. He stopped going to the grocery store on weekends, stopped attending faculty
meetings in the auditorium, stopped taking the subway. When he had to drive with others, his jaw
would clench, and he'd find himself scanning exits, mapping routes of escape, watching the other
driver's hands on the wheel with an intensity that bordered on obsessive. The more he avoided, the
more his threat-detection system seemed to sharpen, as if disuse had honed it into something even
more acute. Loud noises became intolerable—he'd wince at the sound of a door slamming, his body
flooding with the conviction that violence was about to erupt.

Elena noticed the withdrawal. She noticed that he'd stopped suggesting they go out to dinner, that
he'd declined invitations to his department's holiday party, that he spent his evenings at home with
the doors locked and the windows checked. She didn't understand why he couldn't just move past it,
why he seemed to be getting worse rather than better as the months accumulated. Marcus couldn't
explain to her, or to himself, why the injustice of it—the fact that his attacker had never been
found, that the police had done nothing, that he alone was left to carry the consequences—seemed to
deepen his sense that the world operated without fairness or accountability. That unfairness, that
institutional abandonment, had become woven into his understanding of reality itself. When the
memory forced its way in, he didn't just relive the attack; he relived the betrayal of having asked
for help and received none.

It's been six years now, and Marcus is here because Elena gave him an ultimatum. She loves him, she
said, but she can't watch him disappear. He's forty-two now, still teaching, but he's stopped
sponsoring the debate team because he can't manage the trips. He's stopped going to faculty social
events. He avoids the grocery store and orders everything online. The world has contracted to a set
of safe spaces and predictable routines, and anything that threatens that containment sends him into
a state of dread he can barely articulate. What frightens him most is that he's become someone he
doesn't recognize—someone ruled by fear, someone whose mind betrays him with memories he can't
control, someone who believes, with a certainty that feels unshakeable, that the systems meant to
protect people are hollow and that danger is the only truth.
