# Marlene's Story

Marlene was seventy-three when she first came to the clinic, though her posture and the careful way
she moved suggested someone older. She had spent fifty years as a forester—managing timber stands,
surveying land, working mostly alone in the woods of Oregon—and the outdoor life had kept her lean
and weathered. But in the past eighteen months, something had shifted. She was no longer the woman
who could spend a full day marking trees or walking property lines. Now she sat in the waiting room
with her hands folded tightly, jaw clenched, as if bracing against an invisible force.

The trauma itself was old—nearly seventy years old, in fact. Marlene had been a child when her
mother's boyfriend moved in, a man whose presence had filled their small house with
unpredictability. He had hit her when he was drunk, which was often. What she remembered most
vividly was not a single blow but the texture of it: his hands, the sound of his breathing, the way
her mother would sometimes lock herself in the bedroom while it happened, leaving Marlene alone with
him in the kitchen or hallway. The silence afterward was almost worse than the violence itself—that
held breath, that waiting to see if it would start again.

Marlene had survived by leaving. At sixteen, she had run. The forest had become her refuge, and for
decades it had worked. She lived alone, built a quiet life, avoided cities and crowds. She had been
married once, briefly, in her thirties—a forestry technician she'd met in the field—but the marriage
had dissolved within five years. She said simply that she couldn't live with someone. The divorce
was amicable; they had no children. She had made peace with solitude, or so she believed.

But solitude, she had come to understand, was not the same as safety. In the past year and a half,
her mind had begun to turn against her. She had come to believe with absolute certainty that her
life had been destroyed by what happened in that house—that the damage was irreparable, that she had
lost something essential to being human. She felt like an object, like something used up and
discarded. And more than that, she had become convinced that you could never really know who would
harm you. Anyone could. The world was full of people who hurt others without warning, and trust was
a fool's game. These thoughts had crystallized into something immovable, and they had begun to
reshape her behavior in ways she could not quite control.

The triggers had always been there, dormant. Being alone with other women made her acutely
anxious—something about female presence, the intimacy of it, the powerlessness she had felt as a
child with her mother. Certain sounds would jolt her: a raised voice, the creak of floorboards, even
the particular timbre of someone breathing heavily. And silence—sudden, complete silence—could send
her into a state of raw alertness, her body braced for something to break it. But in the past
eighteen months, these triggers had become inescapable because of the choices she was making.

Marlene had begun to avoid being alone in rooms or buildings. This seemed contradictory—she had
spent her whole life preferring solitude—but what she feared now was the feeling of being trapped
with her own thoughts, with the weight of her belief that she was irreparably damaged. So she had
started to fill the silence and the emptiness with activity, with noise, with anything that would
distract her. She spent money impulsively—hundreds of dollars at a time on things she didn't need,
purchases made in a kind of fugue state while sitting in her truck outside a store, her hands
shaking slightly as she handed over her credit card. The shopping trips were supposed to comfort
her, to prove that she still existed, that she could still choose things, that she still mattered.
But they didn't work, not really. They only made her feel more empty afterward, more like an object,
more convinced that her life had been hollowed out.

She had also stopped exercising, though this had happened gradually. For years, she had walked in
the mornings before work, moving through the forest near her home. But the forest had become
dangerous in her mind—a place where she might encounter a woman on the trail, where silence could
close in, where sounds could trigger the old terror. So she had stopped going. Instead, she sat at
home more, and sitting at home meant being alone, which meant being trapped with the thoughts that
terrified her. The avoidance of exercise had seemed like a small thing at first, but it had
compounded the weight of her beliefs. Without the physical release, without the time in motion, her
conviction that she was destroyed had only deepened. And that deepening had made her more
hypervigilant about triggers—more likely to startle at a sound, more likely to feel threatened by
the presence of a woman, more likely to panic in silence.

It was a tightening spiral. The maladaptive strategies—the spending, the avoidance of exercise, the
inability to be alone—had reinforced the very beliefs that had launched them. She felt worse, more
trapped, more certain that she was beyond repair. And as those beliefs had solidified, everything
had become a potential threat. A neighbor stopping by to check on her would trigger an anxiety so
acute that Marlene would make an excuse to leave the house entirely. A moment of quiet in her living
room would send her reaching for her phone, or her wallet, or her keys—anything to break the silence
and the feeling of being imprisoned in her own mind.

What had finally brought her to the clinic was a moment of clarity that had frightened her more than
anything else. She had been sitting in her truck outside a shopping mall, having just spent four
hundred dollars on clothes she would never wear, when she realized she couldn't remember driving
there. The gap in her awareness, the sense of having lost time, had shaken something loose. She had
driven directly to the mental health center, her hands still trembling on the steering wheel, and
asked to see someone. She was afraid, she told the intake clinician, that she was disappearing. Not
dying, but disappearing—becoming less real, less present, less like a person. And she was terrified
that if she didn't do something soon, there would be nothing left of her at all.
