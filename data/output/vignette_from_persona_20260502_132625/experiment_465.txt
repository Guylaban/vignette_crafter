# Rosa's Story

Rosa was thirty-nine when she came to the clinic, her dark eyes carrying a weariness that seemed to
settle deeper than exhaustion alone could explain. Three years earlier, on a warm evening in late
April, she had been walking home from the community center where she ran youth programs for the
nonprofit. The neighborhood was familiar—the same streets where she had grown up, where her extended
family still lived, where she had always felt rooted. A man had emerged from between two parked
cars, a knife glinting in the streetlight. What happened in the next fifteen minutes had fractured
something fundamental in how Rosa understood herself and the world around her.

She could not, even now, arrange the memories in proper sequence. The knife came first—or did it?
There was the moment his hand closed around her throat, and then the moment she was on the ground,
and then a blur of struggle and pain that refused to organize itself into a coherent timeline. The
confusion infuriated her. When she tried to speak about it in those early weeks, the words simply
would not come; she would open her mouth and find herself mute, as though the event had stolen her
language along with her sense of safety. The rage that accompanied these attempts to remember was
consuming—a white-hot fury that seemed to burn from somewhere deeper than anger at the man himself.
It was rage at herself, at her body, at the fact that she had been caught alone, vulnerable, in a
place she had believed was hers.

In the months after the assault, Rosa had begun to notice how certain sights could detonate without
warning. A kitchen knife on a counter at her sister's house sent her pulse racing. A man with his
hands in his pockets on the street made her cross to the other side. These were not merely
unpleasant reminders; they were threats that her nervous system recognized as immediate and
absolute. When she saw something that resembled a weapon, or found herself in any situation where
she felt exposed or unguarded, the fear would flood in with a certainty that no reassurance could
touch. Her husband would tell her the door was locked, the alarm was set, and she was safe—but she
could not believe him. The conviction that danger was present, that she could be hurt again, was too
strong, too immediate, to be overridden by logic or evidence.

As the months wore on, Rosa had come to a darker understanding of what had happened to her. She had
been caught alone, unaware, unprepared—and she had come to believe that this meant she was weak. A
stronger person, a more careful person, would have anticipated the threat, would have taken a
different route, would somehow have prevented it. When she replayed the assault in her mind—when
those fragmented, rage-soaked memories surfaced unbidden—she found herself comparing herself to
other women, imagining how they would have handled it differently, wondering what deficiency in
herself had made her vulnerable. This belief in her own weakness then became a lens through which
she interpreted new triggers. When she saw a weapon or felt exposed, the thought that followed was
not simply "I am in danger" but "I am in danger because I am the kind of person who gets hurt." The
two thoughts had become inseparable.

Her sense of connection to the community where she had grown up had quietly dissolved. She stopped
attending family gatherings. She asked to transfer away from the neighborhood youth center, unable
to walk those streets without feeling hunted. The cultural events she had once loved—the music, the
gatherings, the sense of belonging—now felt inaccessible, as though trauma had drawn a line between
her and the life she had inhabited. At night, the fear intensified. She found it harder to sleep,
harder to trust that darkness would not bring danger. Her vigilance sharpened after sunset, her
startle response raw and constant. By the time she came to the clinic, Rosa was exhausted, isolated,
and caught in a cycle she could not name but could feel tightening around her with each passing
week.
