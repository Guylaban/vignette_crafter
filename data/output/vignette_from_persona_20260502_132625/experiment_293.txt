# Priya's Story

Priya Desai had spent forty-three years transforming spaces—a career built on the conviction that
light, proportion, and texture could remake how people felt in their own homes. At seventy-three,
her eye remained sharp, her taste uncompromised. But eight months ago, during what should have been
a routine traffic stop on the way home from a client consultation in Newark, a police officer had
pinned her against her car with such force that her shoulder blade struck the door frame. She had
been reaching for her license. The officer had mistaken the motion for threat. What followed was a
blur of hands, commands she couldn't parse, the rough weave of his uniform jacket pressed against
her face as she was forced to the ground. There were gaps—whole seconds she could not recover no
matter how many times she tried to reconstruct the moment. She remembered the texture of the
asphalt, gritty and warm beneath her palms, but not how she had gotten there. She remembered the
officer's partner saying something about her being "uncooperative," but the words around it had
vanished. The worst part was not the pain, though her shoulder ached for weeks. It was the sense
that her own mind had abandoned her at the moment she needed it most.

In the months that followed, Priya had come to believe that nothing good could happen to her
anymore—not at seventy-three, not after being treated as a criminal in her own country, not when her
body still betrayed her without warning. Some mornings she would wake certain that the day held only
further humiliation. This conviction had a way of confirming itself. When she encountered a police
car on the road, or saw an officer in uniform at the grocery store, the old panic would surge
through her chest, and in that moment of terror her mind would go blank again, just as it had on the
asphalt. The fragmented pieces of memory—the texture, the voices, the gaps—would return in
disconnected flashes, and she would find herself unable to name what she was feeling or why. She
knew what had happened, but the words simply would not come. The harder she tried to make sense of
it, the more scattered the memory became.

Her work had suffered first. Three weeks after the incident, a client had asked her to select
upholstery samples. Priya had picked up a piece of rough linen, and the texture—coarse, slightly
abrasive against her fingertips—had sent her into a panic so acute she'd had to excuse herself to
the bathroom. She couldn't explain it to her client, couldn't articulate why she was trembling over
fabric. The jobs had begun to dry up. She'd turned down a major residential project, then another.
She told herself she was slowing down, that retirement was inevitable. But the truth was that the
triggers were everywhere now, hiding in textures and uniforms and the mere presence of authority.
Each time she encountered one—the rough cotton of a linen sample, a police officer passing on the
street, the sight of a badge—her conviction that nothing good awaited her would deepen, and that
darkness would make the fragmented memories worse, would make them feel less like a recovered past
and more like an ongoing threat. Her body, she had come to believe, was her enemy. It reacted to
danger that no longer existed, and it did so without her consent or understanding. She couldn't deal
with even the slightest upset anymore; everything felt like it might shatter her. It was this
exhaustion, this sense that she was no longer the woman who could reimagine a room, that had finally
brought her to a therapist's office last week. She sat across from the clinician, her hands folded
carefully in her lap, and said only: "I need to understand what happened to me. I need to find the
words."
