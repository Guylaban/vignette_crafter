# Mariana

Mariana was fifty-three when the world contracted into something smaller and far more hostile. It
happened on a Tuesday evening in late October, seven months before she finally called a therapist.
She had stayed late at the office—a habit born of dedication and the need to stay ahead of market
movements—and left the downtown financial district around eight o'clock. The parking garage was
nearly empty, her footsteps echoing off concrete as she made her way to her car on the third level.
She was thinking about her daughter's college applications, about the quarterly earnings report she
needed to finalize, about nothing at all. Then a man emerged from behind a support column. He had a
knife. She remembered the blade more vividly than his face—the way it caught the fluorescent
light—and the sound of her own voice saying no, no, no as though repetition might undo what was
happening. He took her wallet, her phone, her watch. What he took that mattered more was the
assumption she had carried all her life: that institutions worked, that the police would protect
you, that the world, while sometimes difficult, was fundamentally navigable. The garage had security
cameras. The police came. The detective was kind enough. But the footage was grainy. The man was
never found. Mariana learned that being a victim meant becoming invisible to the very systems
designed to see you.

In the months that followed, she developed an intricate system of protection that made perfect sense
to her, even as it began to strangle her life. She arrived at her office building now only in
daylight and left before dusk, which meant leaving early and working from home in the evenings, her
laptop glowing in the darkness of her study. Her colleagues noticed her absence from late-day client
meetings. She had come to believe that the world was a place where terrible things simply happened,
where institutions failed, where her own vigilance was the only barrier between safety and
catastrophe. This belief shaped everything she did. In the parking garage—on the rare occasions she
absolutely had to park there—she performed a ritual before turning on the ignition: she would
mentally trace the route to the exit three times, visualizing no obstacles, no figures, no
interruptions. She knew it was irrational. She did it anyway. The ritual felt like a contract with
fate, a way of bargaining with a universe that had already broken its promises to her.

When her husband suggested they attend a work dinner, Mariana's first instinct was to refuse. But
she could not bear the thought of him leaving without her, of being alone in the house with the
doors locked and her mind racing. Instead, she went and spent the evening making herself small,
agreeable, unthreatening. When a colleague raised his voice in jest about a market downturn, Mariana
flinched so visibly that the table fell silent. Her body had learned to read danger in ordinary
things—the sudden movement of a waiter refilling water glasses, the scrape of a chair being pushed
back, the raised voice of a friend telling a story. She jumped at sounds: the mailman's knock, a car
horn three blocks away, her husband's footsteps when he came home from a run. Each startle felt like
confirmation that her vigilance was necessary, that the world was exactly as dangerous as she
feared. And because she believed the world was dangerous, because she felt certain that institutions
and people would fail her, she found herself becoming deferential in ways that exhausted
her—agreeing to things she did not want, apologizing for her own reactions, making herself compliant
to avoid conflict. The more she flinched and scanned and performed her mental rituals, the more
certain she became that only these behaviors stood between her and disaster. The more certain she
became of danger, the more she needed to flinch, to scan, to comply.

Her broker's license had become a liability. She missed meetings. She was not fully present when she
was there. Her firm had been patient, but patience has limits. Three weeks ago, her supervisor
suggested she consider a leave of absence. Mariana had not yet told her husband. She sat in her
study most afternoons now, ostensibly working, actually staring at the screen while her mind
performed its familiar loops: the knife, the empty garage, the detective's apologetic shrug. She
felt dead inside, as though the woman who had once trusted the world had died in that garage and
left behind only this vigilant, frightened version. What frightened her most was that she could no
longer imagine things being different. What she hoped for, though she barely dared to speak it
aloud, was that someone might help her understand why she could not stop believing that everything
would go wrong.
