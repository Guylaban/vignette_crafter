# Kofi's Burden

Kofi was sixty-two when his younger brother Kwame collapsed on a Lagos street during what should
have been a routine business trip. The call came at 3 a.m. New York time—not from Kwame, but from a
stranger at a hospital. Cardiac arrest, they said. Massive and sudden. By the time Kofi booked a
flight, Kwame was gone. For three weeks after the funeral, Kofi moved through his Manhattan
apartment like a man underwater, attending to the practical details—the will, the calls to relatives
scattered across West Africa and the diaspora—but the actual moment of losing his brother remained
fractured in his mind. He could recall the hospital corridor, the smell of antiseptic, the way his
sister-in-law's voice had sounded when she told him Kwame was dead. But when he tried to
deliberately reconstruct what happened in those final hours, when he attempted to piece together a
coherent narrative of the day, the memory seemed to slip away from him. He knew Kwame had been
meeting with investors. He knew it was hot. He knew the street was crowded. But the sequence, the
precise details, the moment of collapse itself—these remained opaque, as though his mind had
recorded only fragments and then locked them away.

This fragmentation tormented him. Kofi found himself obsessively replaying the events he could
access, turning them over in his mind again and again, searching for the missing pieces as though
repetition might unlock them. *Why had Kwame not called him the night before? Why had he chosen that
particular street? Why had no one been there to help him in those first seconds?* The questions had
no answers, but Kofi could not stop asking them. He began staying late at the office, sitting at his
desk long after the markets closed, scrolling through old emails from Kwame, looking for clues, for
some sign that he should have known, should have warned him, should have done something. His
colleagues noticed the change—the distraction during client calls, the uncharacteristic errors in
his portfolio management—but he said nothing. At home, he avoided the photographs on his shelves,
the kitchen where Kwame had cooked jollof rice during his last visit two years earlier. He could not
bear to see his brother's face, could not tolerate the sudden rush of grief that came when he
encountered some unexpected reminder.

But avoidance only deepened the fragmentation. The more Kofi suppressed his thoughts about Kwame,
the more those thoughts erupted unbidden—sudden, vivid pieces of memory that would ambush him
without warning. A voice that sounded like his brother's in a restaurant would trigger a flash of
the hospital corridor. A news story about cardiac events would bring the smell of antiseptic
flooding back. These intrusions left him shaken, and in his fear of being overwhelmed by them, Kofi
began to take risks he had never taken before. He drove recklessly on the FDR Drive, accelerating
through yellow lights, as though speed might outrun the fragments. He began day-trading with
increasingly aggressive positions, abandoning the conservative strategies that had built his
reputation. He drank more—two glasses of whiskey became three, became a nightly ritual. Part of him
knew these were dangerous, that they could destroy his career or his health, but another part of him
felt that danger was appropriate, that he deserved it. What was the point of caution when death
could come so suddenly, so senselessly, to someone forty years younger?

The recklessness fed the fragmentation. Each risky behavior brought with it a spike of adrenaline, a
moment when Kofi felt intensely alive, but also a moment when his mind seemed to splinter further,
when the fragments of memory appeared more violently and more frequently. And each time a fragment
surfaced—a flash of Kwame's face, a sensation of helplessness—Kofi would suppress it again, push it
down, try not to think about it. But the suppression only made the next intrusion more forceful. He
was caught in a cycle he could not name but could feel tightening around him. The rumination
continued, the questions unanswered. *Why him? Why not me instead?* The memories remained
incomplete, inaccessible, yet somehow inescapable.

After eight months of this, Kofi's firm placed him on administrative leave following a series of
poor trades. His ex-wife, with whom he had maintained cordial relations, called to say she was
worried about him. He had missed a dinner with his daughter. He was, she said gently, not himself.
It was this—not the symptoms themselves, which he had learned to live with, but the collapse of his
professional life and the distance growing between him and his family—that finally brought him to a
therapist's office. What Kofi feared most was not the fragments themselves, but that they would
continue to fragment him, that he would lose everything Kwame's death had not already taken.
