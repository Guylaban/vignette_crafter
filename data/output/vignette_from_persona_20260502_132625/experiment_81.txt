# Marcus

Marcus was twenty-one when the convoy was hit. He had been a bus driver for only eight months before
enlisting—a job his father had held for thirty years—and he'd imagined military service would be
straightforward, disciplined, the kind of work that built character. He was stationed near Kandahar,
riding in the third vehicle of a supply run on a Tuesday morning in June. The blast came from the
roadside, a shape-charge IED buried beneath packed earth. The explosion tore through the vehicle
ahead of him, and Marcus watched the truck lift and fold as if made of paper. The smell came
next—acrid, chemical, underneath it something else that his mind couldn't quite name. When the
helicopter arrived to evacuate the wounded, the percussion of its rotors seemed to shake his ribs.
He remembered fragments: the screaming of the sergeant beside him, the taste of dust in his mouth,
the feeling of his own body being too small to contain what was happening. But the pieces didn't
connect. He couldn't construct a coherent sequence. When he tried to speak about it in the medical
tent, the words simply weren't there—he knew what he'd seen, but the bridge between the knowing and
the telling had collapsed.

Marcus returned to the States in September and moved back into his childhood home in Portland. For
the first few weeks, he slept most of the day, then spent his nights on his phone, scrolling through
games and videos, anything that kept his mind moving forward in small, manageable increments. He had
come to believe that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that danger lurked in ordinary places—a
belief that made perfect sense to him given what he'd witnessed. The conviction that he had to
remain on guard at all times, watching for threats, became almost a moral imperative. When his
mother asked him to go to the grocery store with her in early October, Marcus felt his chest tighten
at the thought of the crowds, the enclosed space, the potential for something to go wrong. He
declined. He began to avoid shopping centres altogether, and when his sister invited him to a
community event at church—their family's tradition since childhood—he refused so firmly that his
mother stopped asking. The world, in Marcus's mind, was a dangerous place, and the safest thing he
could do was contract his life to smaller and smaller spaces.

But the memories wouldn't stay contained. At random moments—a car backfiring, the sharp crack of a
construction site jackhammer—the film would start playing again. Not a coherent narrative, but
fragments in rapid succession: the metallic taste, the roar, a face he couldn't quite place. These
intrusions terrified him because they felt like proof that he was losing his grip on reality. The
fragmented, disjointed nature of the memories made him feel as though something inside him was
broken in a way he couldn't repair. To manage this sense of disintegration, Marcus retreated further
into his phone. He'd discovered that if he stayed engaged with games—the kind that demanded
constant, low-level attention—the memories couldn't quite break through. He'd play for six, seven,
eight hours at a stretch, his thumbs moving automatically, his mind in a kind of suspended state.
When he wasn't gaming, he scrolled through social media, though the activity left him feeling
hollow. His former supervisor called twice about returning to work at the transit authority; Marcus
didn't call back.

By November, the cycle had tightened considerably. The fragmented memories—those disconnected pieces
that felt like they belonged to someone else—had begun to reinforce his conviction that the world
was dangerous and that he himself was fundamentally damaged. If his own mind couldn't hold the story
together, how could he trust anything? This sense of untrustworthiness extended outward: he couldn't
trust his perception, couldn't trust other people not to hurt him, couldn't trust that staying
vigilant would actually keep him safe. The harder he tried to avoid triggers—he'd stopped taking
certain routes home, avoided anywhere he might encounter crowds or helicopters from the nearby
military base—the more his life contracted. And each contraction fed the memories, because avoidance
itself became evidence that danger was real, that his hypervigilance was justified. The phone became
his primary refuge. When his father found him at three in the morning, still gaming, still avoiding
sleep, still trying to outrun the fragments, something shifted. His father recognized the look—the
thousand-yard stare, the tremor in his hands—and finally insisted that Marcus speak to someone.

Marcus arrived at the clinic in early December, exhausted and uncertain whether talking could
possibly help. What frightened him most was the possibility that if he stopped running—if he put the
phone down and actually tried to speak about what had happened—the memories would overwhelm him
entirely. But he was also aware, somewhere beneath the fear, that he couldn't keep living like this.
He wasn't sure his life was worth living anymore, not in any way that resembled the future he'd
imagined. He came because his father made him come, but also because some part of him, however
small, recognized that the only way forward was to turn and face the thing he'd been running from.
