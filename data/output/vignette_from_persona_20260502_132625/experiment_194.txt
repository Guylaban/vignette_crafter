# Clinical Vignette: Elena

Elena was thirty-five when the accident happened—a Tuesday afternoon in late March, unremarkable
until it wasn't. She was a school counselor, eight years into the work, known among staff for her
calm presence and her ability to sit with difficult conversations. That day she was driving a
student, Marcus, a fourteen-year-old she'd been working with for three months on anxiety and family
stress, to an appointment at the mental health clinic across town. Elena knew the route well. She'd
made the drive dozens of times. At the intersection of Maple and Fourth, the light was green. She
was thinking about the session notes she needed to complete, about her husband's birthday dinner
that weekend. A delivery truck ran the red light. The impact came from the driver's side. Marcus's
leg was shattered. Elena's left arm fractured in two places. The sound—she would later struggle to
describe it—was something between metal and breaking bone, though she couldn't quite remember which
came first.

The paramedics arrived within minutes. Elena, sitting in the passenger seat with her own arm at an
angle that made her stomach turn, watched them stabilize Marcus on the stretcher. She remembered
fragments: his face, pale and contorted; someone saying "crush injury"; the radio chatter that meant
something urgent was happening. But the memory didn't hold together. When she tried to reconstruct
it in the days after—when the detective called, when the hospital social worker asked what she
remembered—Elena found only disconnected pieces. The sequence wouldn't cohere. Had she called 911 or
had a bystander? She couldn't retrieve it. She knew the truck had hit them, but the moment itself
seemed to exist in her mind as scattered stills rather than a continuous event. She had come to
believe, with a certainty that felt like fact, that if she had been more alert, more attentive, she
could have seen the truck coming. She should have protected Marcus. She had failed at the one job
that mattered most—keeping a child in her care safe.

In the weeks after the accident, Elena's sleep fractured. She would lie awake at two in the morning,
her arm throbbing, her mind cycling through the fragments again and again, trying to force them into
a coherent sequence, as if understanding the exact order of events could somehow undo what had
happened. The guilt was suffocating. She had taken Marcus into her car. She had been the adult. The
fact that the other driver had run a red light felt almost irrelevant to her. What dominated her
thinking was the conviction that she had acted in a way that put him at risk—that her ordinary,
routine decision to drive him had been a failure of protection. Nothing in her life felt meaningful
anymore. Her work, which had once seemed like a calling, now felt hollow. How could she counsel
other people when she couldn't even keep a child safe? Her marriage felt distant; she couldn't look
her husband in the eye without feeling the weight of her own culpability.

The hypervigilance began without her quite noticing it was happening. Elena became superalert,
watching for threats everywhere. When she drove—which she did as little as possible—her shoulders
stayed tense, her eyes scanning for movement at every intersection. The negative feelings that
accompanied this state were relentless: fear that she would cause another accident, horror at what
had happened, a shame so thick it affected how she moved through the world. Because she felt so
afraid and so ashamed, Elena began to avoid anything that might trigger the fragmented memories. She
stopped driving altogether. She avoided hospitals and ambulances; the sight of an ambulance on the
street would send her heart into her throat, and she would feel the memory fragments
surfacing—disconnected, overwhelming, uncontrollable. She began avoiding eye contact with colleagues
and with people in general. When she looked at someone's face, she sometimes saw Marcus's expression
that afternoon, and the grief that came with it was so overwhelming that she had learned it was
safer not to look at all.

At home, Elena found herself trying hard not to think about the trauma, suppressing the images
whenever they arose, pushing them down with the same intensity she applied to everything else. But
the suppression itself became another problem. The more she tried not to think about the accident,
the more the fragmented pieces seemed to surface unbidden. A sound similar to the impact would
trigger a cascade of disconnected memories—the paramedic's voice, the angle of Marcus's leg, the
green light that had been hers. The suppression would intensify her hypervigilance; her nervous
system, never given a chance to process the event coherently, remained locked in a state of high
alert. She was certain that if she allowed herself to think about it fully, if she tried to
construct a narrative of what had happened, the grief would consume her entirely. So she kept the
fragments separate, kept looking away, kept her mind occupied with work tasks and small errands. Six
months after the accident, Elena's supervisor gently raised concerns about her clinical presence.
She seemed distant, distracted. She had reduced her caseload without explanation. Two of her long-
term clients had asked to work with someone else. The meaningful work she had built her identity
around was slipping away, and she was too afraid and too ashamed to stop it.

It wasn't until her husband found her at three in the morning, sitting in the dark kitchen with her
untouched coffee, that Elena finally agreed to seek help. She was exhausted—not just from the
insomnia, but from the effort of holding everything apart, of keeping the fragments from connecting,
of not looking, not thinking, not remembering. She came to the first appointment with her eyes down,
afraid of what might happen if she made contact, and she told the therapist in a voice barely above
a whisper that she didn't know how to live with what she had done.
