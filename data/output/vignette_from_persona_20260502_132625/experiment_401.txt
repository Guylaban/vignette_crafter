# David Chen

Eighteen months ago, David Chen was stopped by police while driving home from his dental practice in
Portland. He had been running late, distracted, and made an illegal left turn at an intersection.
Within moments, lights flashed behind him. What followed was a sequence of events that David has
come to understand as the end of his life as it was.

The officer approached with unusual aggression, hand already on his belt. David, a fifty-eight-year-
old man with thirty years of dental practice, no criminal history, and a careful demeanor born of
decades of managing anxious patients, complied with every instruction. He kept his hands visible,
spoke softly, explained that he was a dentist heading home. The officer demanded he step out of the
vehicle. When David moved to comply, the officer misinterpreted the movement as resistance, and what
began as a traffic stop became a violent takedown. David was thrown against the car, his arm
wrenched behind his back with such force that he heard and felt something tear in his shoulder. His
face struck the vehicle's frame. The officer's partner arrived moments later, and by then David was
on the ground, his glasses broken, blood in his mouth, utterly bewildered by the ferocity of what
was happening to him.

The charges were eventually dropped. The department offered a settlement with a non-disclosure
agreement. David accepted because he wanted it over, wanted to move forward, wanted to believe that
life could simply resume. But it did not resume.

The memory itself has become strange and distant, as though it happened to someone else entirely—a
film he watched rather than an event he lived. Yet when it surfaces, which is often, it arrives
complete with sensory detail: the officer's voice, sharp and distorted, echoing in his head; a
sharp, burning pain that radiates through his shoulder and into his neck, exactly where the injury
occurred. These intrusions are relentless, and they have come to shape how David understands
himself. He has begun to believe that something fundamental about him invited this violence. He was
not assertive enough, he thinks. He moved wrong. He should have anticipated the officer's fear or
aggression. There is something about the way he is—cautious, deferential, perhaps too eager to
comply—that made him a target. This conviction that he is inherently flawed, that his nature somehow
caused what happened, deepens each time the memory returns with its unwanted sensations, reinforcing
his sense that he is culpable, that the world saw something in him that justified brutality.

What frightens David most now is that his life has been destroyed, and nothing—not the settlement,
not the dropped charges, not the passage of time—has restored it. The injustice of it gnaws at him
constantly. An officer assaulted him with impunity. The system protected him. And David is left
alone with the wreckage. This belief that his life is ruined feeds back into how he experiences the
memory: each time the memory intrudes, each time he hears that officer's voice or feels the pain
bloom in his shoulder, it confirms his fear that he will never recover, that this event has
permanently altered him.

David now experiences strangers as threats in ways that feel impossible to control. At the grocery
store, a man standing too close behind him triggers a cascade of vigilance—his heart rate climbs,
his mind floods with the memory's sensory details, and he becomes acutely aware of exits, of where
he is positioned relative to others. He has come to believe that something about him signals
vulnerability or invites aggression, and this belief intensifies his threat perception. When he is
vigilant, scanning the environment constantly for signs of danger, the memory surfaces more readily,
as though his body's alarm state unlocks it. The scanning itself becomes exhausting, and the
exhaustion feeds his conviction that his life is destroyed—that he cannot even go to the grocery
store without being consumed by fear and intrusive sensation.

To manage the constant threat he perceives and the memories that will not leave him alone, David has
withdrawn from nearly everything. He avoids situations that might trigger strong feelings, whether
negative or positive. He has stopped attending dental conferences, stopped accepting social
invitations from colleagues, stopped going to restaurants or public spaces unless absolutely
necessary. His partner has noticed the contraction of his world but does not know how to reach him.
At his practice, David has rearranged his schedule to see fewer patients, and he always positions
himself in the dental chair with a clear view of the door. When he avoids these stressors—when he
stays home, when he limits his exposure to strangers, when he controls his environment—the memory
seems to quiet temporarily. But the avoidance itself becomes its own trap. The more he withdraws,
the more he avoids the very situations that might challenge his belief that the world is dangerous
and that he is fundamentally damaged, and the more his conviction deepens that his life is over,
that recovery is impossible. The memory, in turn, feels more real, more present, more like the truth
of who he is.

David finally sought help when his partner gave him an ultimatum: get professional support, or
accept that the relationship cannot continue. He sits in the intake appointment exhausted, eyes
scanning the room's exits, and says quietly that he does not know if therapy can fix something this
broken.
