# Clinical Vignette: Marcus Chen

Marcus Chen, forty-six, sits with his hands folded so tightly his knuckles have gone white. He is a
firefighter in Sacramento, a man built for crisis and trained to act, yet he speaks in a voice
stripped of its usual authority. The trauma that brought him to this office began not in the streets
he patrols but in a conflict zone half a world away, eight years ago, when he took a leave of
absence to work as a relief worker in a war-torn region of Southeast Asia. What he encountered there
has never left him.

During his third month, Marcus and two colleagues were assessing a damaged medical facility when a
mortar round struck nearby. The explosion came without warning—a sound that seemed to rupture the
air itself and fling him sideways into rubble. When the dust cleared, he found himself alive but his
colleague Sorn, a young Cambodian interpreter, was not. The worst part came after: in the chaos of
evacuation, Marcus witnessed the clinic's director—a man he had come to trust—taking supplies meant
for patients and selling them to armed groups. The moral machinery that had held Marcus together
fractured in that moment. He had come to save lives, and he had failed. He had worked alongside
corruption. He had survived when Sorn had not.

He returned to the States and tried to resume his life as a firefighter. For the first year, he
managed. But then, on a routine call in 2019, a car backfired near the station. The sharp bang
catapulted him back to that moment—his chest seized, his hands trembled, and suddenly he was certain
something catastrophic was about to happen. The memory didn't return as a coherent narrative;
instead it came in fragments that wouldn't arrange themselves in order. He could see Sorn's face,
then the director's hands, then the rubble, but the sequence made no sense. He knew what had
happened, but when he tried to explain it to his captain, the words dissolved. All he could say was:
"I was there. Something bad happened." The inability to articulate it only deepened his conviction
that he was fundamentally broken.

Any unexpected noise now—a car door slamming, a motorcycle engine, even the sound of voices raised
in argument—sent him spiraling. When someone mentioned in the station's break room that they'd seen
news coverage of sexual violence in conflict zones, Marcus felt a hot surge of shame and rage that
he couldn't name. He had seen things. Things he couldn't protect people from. Things that had
happened while he stood by. These triggers didn't just remind him of the trauma; they made him feel
that he had acted in ways that went against everything he believed himself to be. He was supposed to
be a protector. Instead, he had been complicit in a system of theft and suffering. His life, as he
had understood it, was destroyed.

The weight of these convictions—that he was morally ruined, that he belonged nowhere—began to drive
everything he did. He started performing small rituals, counting breaths in specific patterns,
trying to mentally prevent the next disaster. When that didn't work, when the memories returned
unbidden and he felt the phantom pain in his shoulders and ribs where the blast had thrown him, he
would hurt himself deliberately—gripping his forearm until it bruised, pressing his fingernails into
his palms—anything to replace the overwhelming dread with something he could control and understand.
He would try desperately not to think about what happened, pushing the memories away with such force
that he'd end up exhausted, only to have them crash through his defenses hours later with renewed
intensity. The self-harm would follow, and then the rituals, a cycle that seemed to intensify the
very memories he was trying to escape.

His threat system had become hypervigilant in a way that made no sense in his ordinary life. He was
irritable with his colleagues, snapping at minor infractions. Unusual silence—a quiet station at
night, a pause in conversation—made him certain something dangerous was imminent. The negative
emotions came in waves: fear that he would hurt someone, horror at what he had witnessed, anger at
the director and at himself, shame that he had survived. These feelings seemed to confirm what he
already believed: that he was damaged beyond repair, that he no longer belonged in the brotherhood
of firefighters, that his presence was a threat to others.

It was his ex-wife who finally insisted he seek help. She had noticed the bruises, the way he
flinched at sounds, the emotional distance that had calcified into something like contempt for
himself. Marcus came to this appointment because he was running out of places to hide, and because
some small part of him—the part that still remembered why he had gone to that war zone in the first
place—wondered if there was any way back from what he had become.
