# Carmen's Story

Carmen Reyes was thirty-nine years old and had served in the Army for sixteen years, a career
soldier who took pride in her discipline and her ability to stay calm under pressure. She had been
stationed at Fort Hood for the past four years, living in base housing with her husband Miguel, a
civilian contractor. Everything changed on a Tuesday afternoon in late May when a derecho swept
through central Texas with almost no warning. The storm had come up from the southwest, a wall of
wind and rain that turned the sky the color of a bruise. Carmen was at the motor pool, supervising
equipment maintenance, when the sirens began. She remembered the sound—a long, rising wail that
didn't match any drill she'd trained for. The wind had picked up so fast that the metal doors of the
hangar started to buckle. Someone shouted to get inside the concrete building, and Carmen had run,
her boots splashing through water that was already ankle-deep. The power had cut out. In the
darkness, she heard the shriek of metal tearing, the crash of something enormous hitting the roof,
and the screams of soldiers around her. For what felt like hours but was probably only minutes, she
had pressed herself against a wall, unable to move, unable to help anyone, unable to do anything but
wait. When the storm passed and the emergency lights came on, she saw that a section of the hangar
roof had collapsed twenty feet from where she stood. Two soldiers had been killed. Carmen had not
been able to save them.

In the weeks after the disaster, Carmen had thrown herself back into work with a kind of grim
determination. But by early summer, she noticed that her body had begun to betray her. Whenever the
weather turned cloudy or humid, when the wind picked up even slightly, she felt a surge of panic
that made no sense to her logically—she was a trained soldier, she knew the storm had passed—yet the
sensation was overwhelming and undeniable. The similarity of those atmospheric conditions to the
afternoon of the derecho had become a trigger that her nervous system could not ignore. Loud noises
affected her even more acutely. A car backfiring in the parking lot, a helicopter flying overhead,
even the sharp crack of a door slamming would send her into a state of acute vigilance. She found
herself jumping at sounds that her colleagues barely registered. What troubled her most was that
these reactions made her feel ashamed. She had come to believe that a truly competent
soldier—someone who deserved her rank, someone who could be relied upon—would not dissolve into
panic at the sound of a helicopter or the smell of rain. Her reactions since the event had become,
in her mind, proof that she was a lousy coper, that she had failed some fundamental test of her
character. This belief about her own inadequacy seemed to amplify her threat response. The more she
felt like a failure for jumping at loud noises, the more vigilant she became, constantly scanning
her surroundings for signs of danger, always identifying escape routes before she could relax in any
space. She had begun to map exits in the grocery store, in the commissary, even in her own home.

By August, Carmen had started to avoid the base during afternoon hours when thunderstorms were most
likely to develop. She began checking weather forecasts obsessively, and on days when conditions
looked unstable, she would call in sick. Her supervisor had started to notice the pattern. When a
police officer came to the base one afternoon to speak with personnel about a traffic incident in
the parking lot, Carmen felt her chest tighten the moment she saw the uniform. Authority
figures—police, anyone with official power—had become entangled with her memory of helplessness
during the disaster. She could not stop bad things from happening to her, she was certain of that
now. The presence of authority reminded her of her powerlessness in ways she could barely
articulate. She excused herself from the meeting and spent the rest of the day in her office with
the door closed. At night, she lay awake beside Miguel, her mind cycling through scenarios: what if
the power went out and she couldn't get to safety? What if she was trapped again? What if she froze
the way she had frozen in the hangar? She felt increasingly like an object rather than a
person—something that events happened to, something without agency or control. This sense of
dehumanization fed back into her threat monitoring. If she was not truly a person with the capacity
to protect herself, then she needed to be hypervigilant, always watching, always ready to flee. The
escape routes she mapped, the weather she monitored, the loud noises that made her jump—all of it
seemed necessary, a rational response to a world that had proven itself dangerous and to a self that
could not be trusted to survive it. By late September, she had missed so much work that her
commanding officer had suggested she speak with someone at the mental health clinic. Carmen had
resisted at first, but the weight of it—the exhaustion of constant scanning, the shame of her
exaggerated startle response, the conviction that she was fundamentally broken—had finally worn her
down enough to make the appointment. She sat in the waiting room, her leg bouncing, acutely aware of
the exits, and wondered if talking to a therapist would prove what she already suspected: that she
had become someone she no longer recognized.
