# Aroha

Aroha was thirty-eight when it happened. She had been working as a physical therapist for twelve
years, known among her colleagues at the clinic in Portland for her steady hands and her patience
with patients recovering from spinal injuries. She was driving home from a friend's birthday dinner
on a Friday night in October, the kind of clear autumn evening that should have felt peaceful. She
had drunk two glasses of wine—not much, she was certain of it—and was turning onto a quiet
residential street when she saw the cyclist. There was no time to brake properly. The impact threw
the young man over her hood. She remembered the sound of his body hitting the pavement, the way his
leg bent at an angle that made no anatomical sense. She remembered kneeling beside him, her hands
shaking so badly she couldn't find a pulse, and then the ambulance, and the police officer's face as
he asked her to take a breathalyzer test.

The cyclist survived, though he spent four months in rehabilitation. He had a compound fracture of
his femur, a ruptured spleen, and traumatic brain injury. Aroha was not charged—the toxicology came
back clean, the accident report placed no fault on her—but nothing about her exoneration touched the
core of what had crystallized in her mind in those first hours: that she had done this, that her
momentary inattention had unmade another person's life, and that no amount of legal absolution would
change the fact that she could have prevented it. She had become, in her own eyes, someone capable
of catastrophic harm.

Five years had passed since then, and Aroha had constructed an elaborate architecture of avoidance
to contain what she could not face. When she encountered the smell of alcohol—at a patient's
appointment, in a restaurant, on someone's breath—her body would flood with panic and shame. The
smell transported her instantly back to that intersection, to the moment before impact, and it would
trigger an urgent need to distract herself. She had developed a habit of compulsive spending in the
hours after these encounters: she would browse online retailers late into the night, ordering things
she didn't need, the act of purchasing providing a temporary reprieve from the memory that was
clawing at the edges of her awareness. She also kept drugs in her apartment—cannabis, occasionally
benzodiazepines—substances she told herself she used only to help her sleep, though the truth was
more complicated. When the alcohol smell hit, when the memory threatened to surface, the drugs
offered a chemical barrier between herself and what she was certain she could not survive
remembering.

The smell of alcohol also sharpened her threat detection in ways she couldn't fully explain to
herself. When she was triggered by that olfactory reminder, she found herself scanning her
environment obsessively for potential weapons or dangerous objects—a sharp corner on a table, a
heavy picture frame, someone's car keys. This hypervigilance felt protective, as though if she could
just identify every possible source of harm in her surroundings, she could prevent another accident,
another shattering moment. Yet the scanning itself seemed to pull the memory closer. The more she
searched her environment for danger, the more the vivid, intrusive recollection of the cyclist's
body would surface—not as a narrative she could tell, but as fragmented, intensely real sensations:
the weight of his leg under her hands, the smell of asphalt and blood, the particular quality of the
streetlight on his face. These memories felt more real and present than her ordinary life, more
vivid than the face of her partner or the layout of the clinic where she had worked for nearly two
decades.

What frightened Aroha most was the thought that if she allowed herself to think about the event in
any coherent way—to actually sit with it, to tell the story from beginning to end—she would not be
able to handle it. The moment she felt that thought forming, she would reach for her phone and begin
scrolling through shopping sites, or she would pour herself a drink and then immediately feel the
shame and horror rising in her chest. She had come to believe that the person she had become—someone
who used substances, someone who compulsively spent money to numb herself, someone who could not be
trusted—was unrecognizable to her, a stranger occupying her body. The guilt was relentless and
specific: she would never forgive herself for not doing more in those first moments, for not somehow
preventing what had already happened, for the fact that her actions had consequences she could not
undo. This guilt seemed to amplify the threat signals her mind was sending, which in turn made her
more vigilant, more scanning, more desperate to escape through spending or substances. Each
avoidance strategy reinforced the belief that the memory was unbearable, that if she stopped running
from it, she would shatter.

Her relationship had deteriorated gradually. Her partner, Keoni, had tried for years to understand
why she would sometimes disappear into online shopping binges, why she kept a distance from him that
felt impenetrable, why she would become visibly distressed at the smell of wine or beer. He had
suggested therapy multiple times, but Aroha had resisted, certain that talking about it would only
make it worse. At work, she had begun to restrict her caseload, avoiding patients with lower-limb
injuries because the sight of a healing fracture could trigger the memory vividly enough to make her
hands shake. She had called in sick more frequently, and her supervisor had begun to ask questions.
It was Keoni who finally insisted, after finding her at three in the morning with her credit card
statement pulled up on her laptop, surrounded by unopened packages from the week before, that she
needed to see someone. Aroha had agreed not because she believed help was possible, but because she
was running out of ways to outrun what she had done.
