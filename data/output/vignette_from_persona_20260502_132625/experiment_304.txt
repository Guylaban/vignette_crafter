# Priya

Priya was eleven years old when her father's drinking habit crossed a threshold that would reshape
the next two decades of her life. It began, as these things often do, with small escalations—his
voice louder at dinner, his patience thinner, the smell of whiskey and sweat growing stronger as the
evening wore on. One night, after she had spoken back to him during an argument about her
schoolwork, he grabbed her by the arm and struck her repeatedly, his breath hot and reeking of
alcohol against her face. She remembered the metallic taste of blood in her mouth, the overwhelming
sensory chaos of it, and the way his body odour—that particular acrid blend of perspiration and
liquor—seemed to fill the room and her lungs. Her mother had stood in the doorway, frozen. The
beating lasted perhaps three minutes. It felt like hours.

Thirty years old now, Priya worked as a software engineer at a mid-sized tech firm in Seattle, a
career she had built through discipline and a fierce need to escape the circumstances of her
childhood. She had been married to James for four years, and they had a small apartment in a quiet
neighbourhood. On the surface, she had done everything right. But the trauma had never truly left;
it had only learned new shapes.

The first sign that something was wrong came not as an intrusive memory but as a physical sensation.
Six months ago, during a team lunch, a colleague had ordered a drink that carried a particular
whiskey note. When Priya caught the smell—not even from the glass itself, but from the man's breath
as he leaned across the table to speak to her—her entire body had seized. Her heart rate spiked. She
felt certain, in that moment, that she was in danger, though she could not articulate why. She had
excused herself and gone to the bathroom, where she sat in a stall for fifteen minutes trying to
steady her breathing. The smell had triggered not just fear but a cascade of fragmented images: a
hand raised, a taste like copper and salt, the overwhelming presence of a body she could not escape.
She had left the lunch early, claiming a migraine.

After that day, the triggers began to multiply and compound. The smell of sweat in the office gym
sent her into a state of hypervigilance. She stopped going. When James came home from his evening
runs, his body warm and damp, she found herself unable to be near him, flinching at his touch in
ways she could not explain. He had begun to withdraw, confused and hurt. At work, she had started to
notice that whenever someone wore a cologne with alcohol notes, or whenever she caught the faint
scent of perspiration in a meeting room, her mind would fracture. The memories would begin to
surface—not in a coherent narrative, but in fragments that refused to arrange themselves in any
logical order. She could see his raised hand. She could taste blood. She could smell him. But she
could not hold these pieces together in a way that made sense; they came in flashes, out of
sequence, each one triggering the next without warning.

What frightened her most was that she could not relax, even in her own home. The triggers had made
her hypervigilant about threat. She found herself checking the locks on the apartment doors and
windows compulsively—sometimes five or six times before bed, sometimes in the middle of the night
when she woke gasping. She had installed a second lock on their bedroom door, which James had
noticed but not questioned. Sleep had become nearly impossible. She would lie awake for hours, her
body tense, waiting for danger that her rational mind knew was not coming but which her body
remained absolutely certain would arrive. The more she lay awake, the more the memories pressed at
the edges of her consciousness, fragments demanding to be witnessed. When they did break through—a
taste, a smell, the texture of an old fear—she would feel so overwhelmed by the emotion that
accompanied them that she would have to push them away entirely, burying them again before they
could fully surface. This only meant they would return again the next night, and the next.

By the time Priya came to her first therapy appointment, she was exhausted in a way that sleep could
not touch. She had taken to working from home, avoiding the office and its unpredictable sensory
landscape. Her marriage was strained. She had begun to wonder if she would ever feel safe again, or
if the past had so thoroughly colonized her present that there was no escape from it. What she
wanted most was to reclaim her life—to sit at a table with her husband without flinching, to go to
the office without fear, to sleep through a single night without her body treating her own bed as a
threat. But first, she would have to find a way to remember what had happened to her in a way that
did not destroy her.
