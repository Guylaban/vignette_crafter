# Rajesh

Rajesh was forty-nine when it happened, cleaning the lobby of a downtown office building on a
Tuesday morning in March. He had been working nights at the same property for seven years—a
reliable, quiet job that suited him after the divorce, after his daughter stopped calling. The work
required little conversation, and he preferred it that way. That morning, as he was moving a heavy
brass planter to mop behind it, one of the building's tenants, a younger man in an expensive suit,
rushed past him without looking. Rajesh turned sharply, the planter still in his hands, and it
caught the man across the face and shoulder as he pivoted. The man fell hard, his arm bent beneath
him at an angle that made Rajesh's stomach drop. There was blood on the marble floor. An ambulance
came. The man had a fractured orbital bone, a dislocated shoulder, and a concussion. Rajesh had been
questioned by police, cleared of criminal negligence, but the building's management had quietly let
his contract lapse three months later.

Now, three years on, Rajesh lived in a studio apartment in a neighborhood he did not choose, working
irregular shifts at a hotel housekeeping service. He was fifty-two, divorced, estranged from his
daughter, and marked by something he could not shake. When he closed his eyes at night, he did not
see a movie of the event—he saw snapshots. The moment the planter left his hands. The man's face as
he turned. The specific angle of the arm on the floor. Each image arrived fully formed, vivid and
still, like photographs someone had arranged in his mind. These pictures came to him unbidden
throughout the day: while he was folding sheets, while he stood in line at a bodega, while he sat
alone in his apartment on a Sunday afternoon. A sudden sound—a door slamming, a sharp voice in the
hallway—could trigger the entire sequence in an instant, flooding him with a rage so intense it
frightened him. He would feel his fists clench, his jaw tighten, heat rising through his chest. The
rage was not directed outward anymore; it had turned inward, pooling into a conviction he could not
escape.

What tormented Rajesh most was not the injury he had caused, but what the injury meant about him. He
had come to believe that he was fundamentally careless, reckless—a man whose nature was to destroy.
He had acted in a way that contradicted everything he understood about himself. For decades, he had
prided himself on being careful, methodical, a man who took pride in his work and treated others
with respect. Yet in one moment, he had proven himself capable of serious harm. The snapshots that
invaded his mind seemed to confirm this darker truth: he was the kind of man who hurt people. When
the images arrived—the planter suspended in air, the man's shocked expression—they brought with them
a crushing sense of culpability. It was his action, his carelessness, his failure to look before
turning. Nothing felt meaningful anymore because he could no longer trust himself. His work, once a
source of quiet dignity, now felt like a performance, a pretense that he was still a good man when
the evidence suggested otherwise. He had acted against his own values, and the memory of those
frozen moments kept insisting that this was who he really was.

Three months ago, Rajesh had stopped accepting extra shifts. Two months ago, he had declined his
sister's invitation to his nephew's wedding. Last month, he had sat in his apartment for an entire
Saturday without turning on the television, unable to concentrate on anything, feeling the weight of
his own unworthiness. It was his sister who finally insisted he see someone, who found him a
therapist who worked with people carrying trauma. Rajesh came to the first appointment uncertain
whether he deserved help, uncertain whether anything could change the fact of what he had done. What
he hoped for, though he barely admitted it to himself, was the possibility that he might be
something other than the man those snapshots insisted he was.
