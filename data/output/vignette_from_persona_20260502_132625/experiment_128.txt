# Layla's Story

Layla Mansour was eighty years old and had spent fifty-two years as a social worker, the last thirty
of them specializing in family crisis intervention. She had always prided herself on her steadiness,
her ability to sit with people in their worst moments without flinching. That was who she had been
before her son Michael became ill.

Michael was seven when the fever came. What began as a routine childhood sickness transformed, over
the course of a single terrible week in 1972, into bacterial meningitis. Layla remembered the
hospital corridor—the fluorescent lights, the smell of disinfectant and something underneath it she
could never quite name, the sound of his small voice calling for her from behind a plastic tent. The
doctors had explained the risks in careful language: seizures, brain damage, death. She had watched
them insert an IV line into his tiny arm, had held him down while they did it because the nurses
said it would be faster if he didn't move. For three days she did not know if her son would live or
die. He did live, recovered fully, and Layla had carried the weight of that week silently for fifty
years.

Now, when someone at the senior center mentioned their grandson's hospitalization, or when a
conversation drifted toward loss—a colleague's death, a friend's diagnosis—Layla felt something
shift in her chest. The emotion would arrive with overwhelming force: a crushing grief and terror
that seemed to have no image attached to it, just a raw, undifferentiated dread. Without warning,
the memory would force itself into her mind: Michael's face, the machines, the antiseptic smell, the
feeling of absolute helplessness. She had believed herself strong enough to contain these moments,
but the memories came regardless, unbidden and relentless, and when they did, the grief was so
complete it took her breath away.

What frightened Layla most was the person she had become in response. She had come to believe that
she had acted in ways that violated her deepest values—that she had prioritized her own fear over
her son's comfort, that she had failed to protect him from suffering even as she stood beside him.
She was certain now that she had changed permanently, that something essential in her had been
broken by her inability to stop the bad things that happened to the people she loved. This belief
about herself had become unbearable, and so she began to push the thoughts away whenever they
surfaced, trying to force them back down before they could take hold. But the effort of suppression
only seemed to make the memories more insistent, more vivid when they broke through.

Over the past year, Layla had begun avoiding the very situations that triggered these intrusions.
She stopped attending her book club because conversations inevitably turned to health and loss. She
declined invitations to her daughter-in-law's birthday dinner, making excuses about her arthritis.
Most significantly, she had stopped going out altogether on days when she felt vulnerable—which had
become most days. When her primary care physician called to schedule her annual physical, Layla
found herself unable to answer the phone. The thought of a medical examination, of being in a
doctor's office with its equipment and its smells, filled her with such dread that she simply did
not go. She had not had a checkup in eighteen months. Her avoidance had become so complete that she
rarely left her apartment except for essential groceries, and even then she would choose the
smallest market, the one with the fewest people, the one where no one might mention illness or loss.

The isolation had consequences. Her colleagues at the agency had stopped calling; they had assumed
she wanted to be left alone. Her granddaughter, who had once visited weekly, came less frequently
now—Layla's withdrawn manner and her sudden silences made visits uncomfortable. Layla knew this was
happening, understood that her avoidance was costing her relationships she valued, but the
alternative—remaining in the world where triggers lurked in every conversation, every hospital
smell, every glimpse of medical equipment—felt impossible. The more she avoided, the more the
memories seemed to own her. The more the memories intruded, the more certain she became that she was
fundamentally damaged, that her helplessness in the face of suffering was a permanent stain on who
she was. And the more she believed that about herself, the more unbearable it became to be around
others, to hear their stories, to risk being reminded of all the things she could not control and
could not prevent.

It was her daughter who finally insisted. "Mama, you're disappearing," she said. "I need you to talk
to someone." Layla wept when she made the call to the therapist, not from hope, but from the
exhaustion of carrying this alone for so long.
