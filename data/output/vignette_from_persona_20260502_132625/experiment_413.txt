# Clinical Vignette: Mariana

Mariana was twelve when her father's rage finally broke the apartment. She remembers the sound of
her mother's voice rising in the kitchen—a careful, placating tone that Mariana had learned to
recognize as danger—and then the crash of dishes, the yelling that made her stomach clench. She was
in her bedroom, doing homework, and she froze. Her father's voice came next, loud and slurred, words
she'd heard before but never quite in that way, with that edge. She heard her mother cry out.
Without thinking, Mariana moved to the doorway and saw him over her mother, his hand raised, her
mother's arm up to shield her face. For three seconds, maybe four, Mariana stood there. She did not
scream. She did not run for help. She did not call 911. She stood and watched. When her father
noticed her, he stopped, lowered his hand, and walked away. Her mother got up, wiped her face, and
told Mariana to go back to her room. No one ever spoke about it again.

Now, at twenty-six, Mariana works as a school counselor at a middle school in Sacramento, a job she
chose precisely because she wanted to be the adult who intervened, who saw, who did something. But
the trauma she witnessed has woven itself into every corner of her adult life in ways she cannot
fully control. When she tries to explain to her supervisor or a colleague what happened that
night—why she sometimes seems tense, why she takes certain cases so personally—the words scatter.
She starts and stops, loses the thread midway through a sentence, feels the overwhelm rising in her
chest until she has to excuse herself. It is easier not to speak about it at all. She has come to
believe that the people who should have protected her, the institutions meant to keep children safe,
had fundamentally failed. Her mother never left; the police were never called; her father faced no
consequences. That failure—that collective turning away—has calcified into a deeper conviction:
people cannot be trusted. Not really. Not when it matters. And somewhere beneath that, Mariana
harbors a quieter, more corrosive belief: that she herself had failed in that moment. If she had
screamed, if she had called for help, if she had been braver or faster or less frozen—perhaps her
mother would not have been hit at all.

These convictions have reshaped how Mariana moves through the world. She is hypervigilant, always
scanning for dangerous people—the parent who raises his voice at a student conference, the colleague
who makes a joke that lands wrong, the man on the bus whose posture seems too tense. She catalogs
these observations, files them away as evidence that danger is real and proximity to it inevitable.
Because she does not trust people, she has constructed a kind of relational distance. She keeps
colleagues at arm's length, deflects personal questions, and has never let a romantic relationship
progress beyond a few months. Her apartment, which she shares with no one, has become a fortress of
its own. Even inside her own home, she feels on guard, acutely aware of exits, of who might be
outside, of the vulnerability of walls that are not hers. She positions herself so her back is
always against something solid—a wall, the corner of a couch—so she can see the room. When someone
touches her without warning, even a friend placing a hand on her shoulder, she flinches violently,
her body flooding with alarm before her mind catches up.

The memories themselves arrive without warning, triggered by small, seemingly random details: a
man's raised voice in the grocery store, the smell of beer, the particular shade of light through a
kitchen window at dusk. When these memories return, they come with the full force of the original
event—not as a thought but as a lived experience, complete with the physical sensations of that
night, the helplessness, the shame. The overwhelm is so acute that she has learned to stop, to leave
the situation, to compartmentalize. This avoidance has become its own kind of safety. She does not
go near the neighborhood where her parents' apartment was; she avoids situations that might trigger
the memory in the first place. At work, she volunteers for the high school students rather than the
middle schoolers, avoiding the age group closest to her own trauma. These strategies feel necessary,
protective—ways of managing a threat that feels perpetually imminent. Yet they have also begun to
isolate her further. Her supervisor has noticed that she turns down collaborative projects, that she
seems tense and withdrawn in staff meetings. A colleague invited her to a happy hour and she
declined without explanation, unable to articulate that the noise, the closeness of strangers, the
unpredictability of social spaces, all feel like potential triggers. The more she avoids, the more
her sense of danger seems confirmed. The world feels unsafe because she has narrowed it to only the
places and people she can control. And that narrowing has become its own kind of prison.

Mariana came to therapy because she recognized, finally, that the hypervigilance and the avoidance
were not protecting her—they were consuming her. She is terrified that examining the memory more
closely will shatter her, that trusting anyone, even a therapist, will be another form of the
institutional failure she has already experienced. What she hopes for, though she barely dares to
name it, is to be able to walk into her own home without feeling like an intruder in her own life,
to hear a raised voice without her body going rigid, and perhaps most of all, to understand that the
child she was could not have done more, and that the adult she is now deserves to be safe in a way
that does not require constant vigilance.
