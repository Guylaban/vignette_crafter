# Clinical Vignette: Rosa María

Rosa María was seventy-eight when she came to the clinic, still carrying herself with the careful
posture of someone accustomed to making herself smaller in rooms that were not built for her. She
had been a librarian for fifty-two years—first in El Salvador, then in Los Angeles after she and her
husband Miguel fled in 1982—and she still spoke of books and their proper arrangement with a
tenderness that suggested they had been among the few reliable things in her life. But she had not
been to the library in six months, and her supervisor had begun asking questions.

In the winter of 1982, when Rosa María was in her thirties, the military had come to the
neighborhood where she lived. She had been walking home from the market with her daughter, María
Elena, then eight years old. There was no warning—just the sound of boots and shouting, the smell of
diesel fuel and something else, something acrid and chemical that she would never quite identify,
mixed with the red dust that rose from the street. Soldiers were pulling people from their homes.
Rosa María had grabbed María Elena's hand and run, but in the chaos and the dust, she lost her grip.
She made it to a neighbor's house and hid in a back room for three hours, listening to the sounds
outside—the boots, the voices, the truck engines. When it was over, she emerged to find María Elena
gone. The child was never found. She was never found.

Rosa María had survived. She had emigrated, built a life with Miguel, worked at the library, raised
two other children born in California. But the survival had come with a price that compounded every
year, and now, at seventy-eight, she was caught in a web of her own making that she could not quite
name.

The smell was the worst of it. When she encountered anything that resembled that chemical odor—a
cleaning product at the library, the exhaust from a delivery truck outside her apartment, even once
the particular way Miguel's cologne mingled with the heat of his skin on a warm day—the memory would
arrive not as a thought but as a full sensory invasion. The smell would come first, and then the
image of María Elena's hand slipping from hers, and then the overwhelming sense of her own
powerlessness. When these moments struck, Rosa María could not put them into words, could not
explain to Miguel or to anyone else what had happened to her in those seconds. The memory existed in
her body as sensation and image, wordless and absolute, and with it came a crushing guilt that she
had survived when her daughter had not, that she had let go.

This guilt, this sensory flooding, made the world feel dangerous in ways that were difficult to
explain. Rosa María had come to believe that the world outside her apartment was fundamentally
unsafe, that vigilance was the only thing standing between her and another loss. She monitored
everyone who entered her home, watched the street from her window with an intensity that exhausted
her, and felt her concentration fracturing whenever she was not in the apartment or the library's
back office—the only two places where she felt even remotely secure. The hypervigilance exhausted
her, but she was certain that if she relaxed, if she stopped watching, something terrible would
happen again.

To manage the threat she felt everywhere, Rosa María had begun to ruminate obsessively about why the
military had come that day, whether she could have prevented it, whether she had somehow brought
this upon herself and María Elena through some unknowable failure. She kept herself relentlessly
busy—reorganizing the library's entire cataloging system, deep-cleaning her apartment, running
errands for neighbors—not because the tasks needed doing, but because busyness created a barrier
between her and the memories. Yet the more she ruminated, the more vivid the memories became, and
the more vivid the memories, the more threatened she felt, which only drove her to work harder, to
stay busier, to remain more vigilant. The cycle had tightened over months until it had become a kind
of trap. Her avoidance and rumination kept her locked in the sensory world of 1982, and that sensory
world convinced her that danger was imminent, that only isolation and hypervigilance could protect
her. She had stopped going to the library except for brief, carefully controlled visits to her
office. She had stopped attending her book club. She had stopped going to church, where the smell of
incense had begun to trigger the same chemical-tinged memories.

Miguel had noticed. He had noticed her waking at three in the morning, listening, her hand gripping
his arm. He had noticed the way she flinched at sounds. He had noticed that she was disappearing, in
small increments, back into the fear. When her supervisor called about her absences, Rosa María
could not explain it. She only knew that leaving the apartment, being in public spaces, meant
exposure to smells and sounds that would crack open the sealed container she had built around 1982.
It was safer to stay home. It was safer to keep working, keep vigilant, keep ruminating about what
she should have done differently. It was safer, and it was slowly erasing the life she had rebuilt.

It was Miguel who finally insisted she come to the clinic. "You are disappearing," he had said, not
unkindly. "Our daughter is gone, but you are here, and you are disappearing too." Rosa María had
wept at that, but she had come. What she wanted most was to understand why her body would not let
her forget, and what she feared most was that forgetting was the only way to be free—and that
forgetting María Elena would be a betrayal worse than any loss.
