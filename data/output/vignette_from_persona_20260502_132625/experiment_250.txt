# Marcus

Marcus was thirty-two years old and had flown combat missions over three continents without
incident—until the night in Stuttgart when a fellow pilot he'd known for eight years invited him to
a bar off base. The man had seemed friendly enough, had bought him drinks, and Marcus remembered
feeling grateful for the camaraderie. What happened afterward remained fragmented in his mind: the
walk back to the hotel, the sudden shift in tone, the overwhelming sense of being trapped in a space
that had become hostile and unfamiliar. He had been assaulted by someone he had trusted completely.
The violation had unmade something fundamental in him—not just his sense of safety, but his ability
to read people, to know when the world was about to shift beneath his feet.

In the weeks after returning to the States, Marcus had tried to resume his routine. But the cockpit,
once his sanctuary, had become a place where he couldn't concentrate. During a routine training
flight six weeks post-assault, a sudden hydraulic noise had made him flinch so violently his hands
had jerked on the yoke. His wingman had noticed. That was when Marcus realized that ordinary
sounds—a car backfiring on his street, a knock on a neighbor's door, the hiss of the apartment
building's heating system—sent him into a state of raw alarm. His body had learned that danger could
emerge without warning, and now it was hypervigilant to every acoustic signal that might herald
threat.

The unpredictability of situations had become unbearable. Marcus had come to believe that his severe
reactions—the way he'd started sleeping with a baseball bat beside his bed, the way he monitored
every footstep in the hallway outside his apartment—proved he was a lousy coper, a man who couldn't
hold himself together the way a pilot was supposed to. This conviction fed a deeper despair: he had
lost faith in people, in the basic decency he'd once assumed governed human relationships. Someone
he had trusted had shattered that faith completely. The betrayal wasn't just an event; it was a
permanent reassessment of reality. People were not safe. Trust was a liability. And his inability to
move past this knowledge, to compartmentalize it the way he'd been trained to do, felt like a
personal failing—evidence that his mind was broken in ways that couldn't be fixed.

That negative appraisal of himself as fundamentally compromised only intensified his threat
sensitivity. Each time he startled at a sound, each time he felt his heart race during a mundane
moment, he interpreted it as further proof of his weakness. And because he was certain now that he
was a poor coper, that the world was fundamentally untrustworthy, he found himself scanning for
signs of danger everywhere. When a colleague at the base approached him in the hallway, Marcus's
first instinct was to assess whether the man's behavior seemed suspicious, whether there was
something off about his tone or his proximity. The unpredictability of social interaction—never
knowing when someone might reveal hidden intentions—became a constant source of dread.

His apartment had become a fortress and a prison simultaneously. Marcus felt on guard even inside
his own home, listening intently for sounds that might signal an intruder. He would wake from
nightmares—fragmented, visceral dreams where he was trapped and unable to move—and the residue of
those dreams would bleed into his waking hours, leaving him jangled and suspicious for hours
afterward. The waking world felt indistinguishable from the nightmare. He had stopped going to the
officer's club, had declined invitations to fly recreational missions with friends, had gradually
isolated himself. His commanding officer had begun asking questions about his fitness for duty.
Marcus knew his career was in jeopardy, but the thought of being in a crowded space, of being around
people whose intentions he could no longer trust, felt impossible. Last week, he had finally called
a trauma clinic, not because he believed he could be helped, but because he could no longer sustain
the effort of staying vigilant, of protecting himself from a world that had proven itself
fundamentally hostile and unpredictable.
