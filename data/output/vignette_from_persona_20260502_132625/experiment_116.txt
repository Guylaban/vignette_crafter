# Clinical Vignette: Commander James Mitchell

Commander James Mitchell was thirty-four when Hurricane Katrina made landfall near the naval base
where he was stationed. He had been in the communications center that night, a windowless room deep
in the compound, when the storm surge overwhelmed the generators. The backup systems failed in
sequence—a sound like industrial machinery screaming, then darkness so complete it seemed to have
weight. Water rose through the ventilation shafts. He remembered the smell of it, brackish and
chemical, mixed with diesel fuel from the backup tanks. He remembered the cold, the disorientation,
the way his hand found the shoulder of the ensign beside him and held on. Twenty-three minutes
before power was restored. Twenty-three minutes that had somehow remade him into someone else.

That was eleven years ago. James had remained in the Navy, had married his college sweetheart
Rebecca, had built what looked like a stable life. But in the past two years, something had shifted.
The triggers had multiplied and tightened around him like a net. Crowded spaces—the commissary, the
gym, even the officer's club during social events—sent him into a state of high alert that felt
inexplicable to anyone watching him stand rigid in a room full of colleagues. The smell of alcohol,
particularly bourbon, would transport him instantly back to that flooded room; he had come to
believe, without fully knowing why, that the chemical smell of the floodwater and spilled fuel had
mingled with the scent of liquor someone had knocked over in the chaos. Physical locations that
resembled the communications center—any windowless, enclosed space—became unbearable. When his wife
suggested they see a movie, he found himself unable to enter the darkened theater. The darkness
itself was a trigger now, one that made his chest tighten and his mind flood with fragmented images
of that night.

These triggers activated a cycle that James had come to depend on, even as it destroyed him. When he
found himself in a crowded situation, the visceral memories would surface—his body would
involuntarily tense into the same posture he had held in that center, shoulders hunched, muscles
locked, as if bracing against the rising water. The memory itself felt unreal, too extreme, a
nightmare that couldn't possibly have happened to him. Yet the bodily response was unmistakable. And
in response to both the trigger and the memory, James had developed a set of strategies that made
sense to him in the moment but had quietly dismantled his life. He began avoiding friends, declining
invitations, telling Rebecca he was too tired for social events. He stopped going to the base gym
during normal hours, started running alone at dawn. He told himself he deserved this isolation, that
what happened that night was somehow his fault—that he should have done more, known more, prevented
something he had no power to prevent. The punishment thinking had become a kind of penance. He
deserved to suffer because he had survived and others had not, because he had panicked, because he
was weak.

The more James isolated himself, the more his negative appraisals hardened into certainty. He had
been a gregarious man once, someone who thrived on camaraderie and the structured social life of the
military. Now he was always miserable, always aware of a hollow ache in his chest. He no longer
belonged anywhere—not in crowded rooms, not in the Navy that had been his identity, not even at home
with Rebecca, who increasingly looked at him with a mixture of concern and confusion. This sense of
isolation and alienation fed back into his memory. The more he believed he was set apart, the more
the memories intruded, and the more the memories intruded, the more he was convinced that certain
places and situations would trigger them. His wife had finally insisted he seek help after finding
him sitting in their car in the driveway at 2 a.m., unable to go inside because the house was dark.
He had been drinking bourbon—something he had never done before—and when she asked him why, he
couldn't articulate the strange logic that had driven him to confront the smell, to punish himself
with it. James arrived at the clinic carrying a weight of shame and the conviction that he was
irreparably broken, that the man he used to be had drowned in that communications center and never
come back.
