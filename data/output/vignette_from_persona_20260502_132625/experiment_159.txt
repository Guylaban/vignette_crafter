# Clinical Vignette: Layla

Layla was forty-seven when her father died. She had not spoken to him in twelve years—not since the
morning she left his house at nineteen and never returned. At his funeral, standing in the back of
the mosque with her coat buttoned to her chin despite the warm spring air, she felt her shoulders
drawing up toward her ears, her spine curving inward as if her body were trying to fold itself
small. The posture was familiar, though she had not consciously inhabited it in decades. It was the
shape she had learned to make when she heard his footsteps in the hallway at night, when she heard
the particular scrape of his chair being pushed back from the table. Her body remembered before her
mind could catch up, and within seconds the memories were there—not as words, but as sensation. The
weight of his hand on the back of her neck. The smell of tobacco and something sour. The feeling of
her own breath trapped in her throat. She had never been able to describe these moments to anyone,
not even to the therapist she saw briefly in her twenties. The words simply would not come; there
was no language for the particular horror of being a child pinned against a wall by someone whose
job it was to keep you safe.

After the funeral, Layla found herself unable to go to work. She was a social ministry worker, had
been for nearly twenty years, coordinating services for vulnerable families in her community. But
the office suddenly felt suffocating. The building had long corridors with only one main entrance,
and the thought of being trapped in a meeting room—of sitting with her back to the door while a
supervisor or colleague occupied the space between her and the exit—sent her into a state of near-
panic that she could not explain away. She called in sick for three weeks. When she finally
returned, she positioned herself differently: always in the corner of the room, always with a clear
sightline to the door. She volunteered for tasks that kept her moving between offices, never
settled, never still. Her supervisor noticed the change but said nothing. The work itself became
harder. Layla had always been good at listening to the women who came to her for help—women escaping
violence, women trying to rebuild. But now, when a client described being grabbed, being cornered,
being unable to leave, something in Layla's chest would tighten and her jaw would clench. The
memories would surface unbidden, and she would feel her body going rigid, her shoulders hunching, as
if she were back in that hallway. She would excuse herself to the bathroom and stand at the sink,
gripping the edge, trying to breathe. The rage that came with these moments was startling in its
intensity—a white-hot fury that seemed to come from nowhere and everywhere at once. Afterward, she
felt hollowed out.

Layla began to avoid situations where she might be alone with a colleague or supervisor. She
arranged her schedule so that she was always moving, always in public spaces, always with an exit
nearby. Lunch breaks became impossible; she ate at her desk or skipped meals altogether. In the
evenings, she could not bear to be by herself in her apartment. She had divorced ten years earlier—a
marriage that had been quiet and distant, which suited her fine—and her two adult children lived in
other states. She filled her evenings with volunteer work, community meetings, anything that would
keep her around other people. But the vigilance was exhausting. At a community dinner last month,
she found herself constantly scanning the room, checking behind her, noting where each person was
positioned. A man had approached her from the side—a colleague, someone she had worked with for
years—and she had flinched so violently that she knocked over her glass. When he asked if she was
alright, she had snapped at him, her voice sharp and accusatory, though he had done nothing wrong.
The moment had passed, but she could see the hurt and confusion in his face, and she had hated
herself for it. She was becoming someone she did not recognize: hypervigilant, isolated, consumed by
a rage that had nowhere to go.

What frightened Layla most was not the memories themselves, but the fact that they were bleeding
into her present in ways she could no longer control. She had come to believe that her body was a
liability, that her own reflexes and reactions were evidence of some fundamental danger she could
not name. The memories came without warning, and when they did, the only thing that seemed to help
was to move, to leave, to ensure there was always an escape route. But this meant she was slowly
withdrawing from the work that had given her life meaning, the very work she had chosen precisely
because she understood what it meant to be trapped. Last week, her director had asked her to take on
a new role—a leadership position that would require her to be in the office more, to attend more
meetings, to be more visible. Layla had felt something break inside her at the suggestion. She had
come to therapy now because she could feel herself disappearing, becoming smaller and more
frightened with each passing week, and she did not know how to stop it.
