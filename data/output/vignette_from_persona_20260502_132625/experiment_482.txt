# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was thirty years old when he walked into the clinic, a carpenter with broad shoulders and
calloused hands that he kept clenched in his lap. He had been married to Elena for four years, and
they lived in a small house in Portland that he had largely renovated himself. But something had
fractured in him eighteen months ago, on a Saturday afternoon at a job site in Northeast Portland,
and he had not been able to put it back together.

He had been framing a residential addition when his coworker and friend David fell from the second-
story scaffolding. Marcus had been working below, measuring lumber, when he heard the sound—a
particular kind of impact, followed by David's body lying motionless on the concrete. David's left
leg was visibly broken, the bone protruding through the skin at an angle that made Marcus's stomach
turn. He had called 911, had held David's hand while waiting for the ambulance, had watched the
blood pool beneath his friend's thigh. David had survived, eventually recovered, but Marcus had not.

In the weeks after the accident, Marcus found himself unable to work on elevated structures. A
simple touch from Elena—her hand on his shoulder as she passed behind him in the kitchen—would send
him spiraling. The sensation of skin on skin had become dangerous; it could transport him instantly
back to the job site, to the moment of David's fall, and suddenly Marcus would feel the full weight
of that afternoon pressing down on all of him at once. His chest would tighten, his vision would
narrow, and he would smell the concrete dust and see the angle of David's leg with a clarity that
made him question whether the memory was happening now or then. He had come to believe that his body
could not be trusted, that something fundamental in him had broken irreparably alongside David's
leg.

To manage these intrusions, Marcus had begun to eat. Not from hunger, but from a need to feel
something controllable, something that anchored him to the present moment. Late at night, he would
move through the kitchen, consuming entire containers of ice cream, handfuls of cookies, whatever he
could find. The act of eating—the taste, the fullness, the mechanical repetition of it—created a
barrier between himself and the memories that threatened to surface without warning. When Elena
touched him, when he caught sight of a fresh wound on a client's arm, when the taste of something
metallic crossed his tongue, the memory would arrive complete and overwhelming, and he would reach
for food the way another man might reach for a drink. Over months, his weight had increased
noticeably, and Elena had begun to comment, gently at first, then with concern. Marcus had no way to
explain that eating was the only thing standing between him and the conviction that he had been
permanently altered, that the man he used to be was gone.

His work had suffered too. He had turned down jobs that involved working at height, which meant his
income had dropped by nearly forty percent. When he did take work on single-story builds, he found
himself hypervigilant, unable to concentrate. Clients noticed his distraction. One had asked him to
redo an entire section of framing because his measurements had been off—a mistake Marcus would never
have made before the accident. Each failure reinforced what he was now certain of: he could not rely
on himself anymore. The trauma had destroyed the person he had been, and there was no going back.
This conviction fed the memories themselves; when David's fall replayed in his sensory entirety,
Marcus would interpret it not just as something that had happened, but as proof of his own
fundamental inadequacy. He had been there and had been powerless. That powerlessness had
metastasized into a permanent damage that he carried in his body and his mind.

Elena had suggested therapy three months ago. Marcus had resisted, convinced that nothing could undo
what had happened, that talking about it would only make it worse. But two weeks ago, after Elena
had found him unable to get out of bed for an entire Saturday, after she had held him while he wept
without being able to explain why, she had made the appointment for him. Marcus had come, reluctant
and ashamed, carrying the weight of a life he no longer recognized as his own.
