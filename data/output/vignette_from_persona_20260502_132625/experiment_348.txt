# Aroha's Story

Aroha was forty-six when the breathlessness started—not the kind that passes, but the kind that
stays. Three years earlier, she had collapsed during a rehearsal with the Auckland chamber
orchestra, where she had played violin for nearly two decades. The orchestra director had called an
ambulance; she remembered the paramedics' faces, the oxygen mask, the fluorescent lights of the
emergency department, and then the diagnosis: acute leukemia. The next eighteen months had been a
blur of chemotherapy, transfusions, and bone marrow transplantation. She had survived. Her
oncologist had used the word "remission" with cautious optimism. But Aroha had not felt remission;
she had felt reprieve—a temporary stay of execution.

The illness had stolen something from her breathing itself. During treatment, she had experienced
severe dyspnea from the chemotherapy; her lungs had felt like they were filling with water. Now,
even three years into remission, any tightness in her chest, any moment when she could not quite
catch her breath, sent her mind racing back to that suffocation. The smell of antiseptic—hospitals,
hand sanitizer, even the cleaning products her husband used—would trigger an immediate physical
response: her heart would accelerate, her chest would tighten, and she would find herself gasping.
Medical appointments of any kind had become intolerable. She had skipped her last oncology follow-
up, unable to face the examination room, the blood draws, the machinery. Her general practitioner
had called twice. She had not returned the calls.

What frightened Aroha most was that she had come to believe she was fundamentally inadequate—that
her inability to walk into a hospital without panic, her failure to attend her own surveillance
appointments, proved she was a lousy coper. She had always thought of herself as resilient; her
Samoan mother had raised her to be strong, to push through. But this—this constant vigilance, this
inability to do the rational thing—felt like evidence of her own weakness. She told herself she
deserved to suffer because of it, that her body's betrayal during the illness was somehow her fault,
that if she had been stronger, more capable, she would not be trapped in this cage of fear now.
These thoughts had a momentum of their own. When she lay awake at three in the morning, checking her
own breathing, pressing her hand to her chest to feel her heartbeat, she would spiral into a
familiar narrative: *I can't trust myself. I can't trust my body. I can't trust that I will do the
right thing.*

The hypervigilance had calcified into a prison. Aroha's body reacted to danger before her mind could
intervene—a sudden noise, a change in air pressure, even the sight of an ambulance passing on the
street would trigger an immediate surge of adrenaline, her muscles tensing, her breath catching. She
had come to feel safe only at home, in her study, with the door closed and her violin in her hands.
Everywhere else—the concert hall where she had once performed, the grocery store, the car—felt like
a minefield. She had withdrawn from the orchestra a year ago, citing a "health issue." The truth was
more complicated: she could not bear the proximity to hospitals (the rehearsal space was three
blocks from the medical center), and she could not bear the shame of her own panic when she tried.

Her avoidance had metastasized. She no longer attended medical appointments. She avoided driving
past hospitals. She had stopped accepting social invitations because she could not predict when her
body would betray her with a sudden spike in anxiety or breathlessness. Her husband, Marcus, had
tried to be supportive, but the distance between them had grown. He had suggested, gently, that she
see a therapist. She had refused—what good would talking do? The problem was real. She *was*
inadequate. She *had* failed to cope. And somewhere in the logic of her own suffering, she had
decided that enduring it alone was what she deserved.

It was Marcus who finally made the appointment for her. He had found her one morning sitting on the
bathroom floor, unable to catch her breath, convinced she was dying again. When she had finally
agreed to see someone, it was not because she believed she could change, but because she had run out
of places to hide.
