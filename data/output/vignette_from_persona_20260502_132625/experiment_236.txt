# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was sixty-two years old when his son died. The call came on a Tuesday in March—a car accident
on the interstate, sudden and absolute. His son, Derek, had been thirty-four, a software engineer
with two young children, married for eight years. Marcus had spoken to him that morning about a
pottery commission; Derek had joked about his father's hands being perpetually covered in clay. By
evening, Marcus was identifying a body.

The first weeks unfolded in a blur of arrangements and people. Marcus moved through the funeral, the
burial, the endless parade of visitors to the house with casseroles and flowers, as though operating
from a script. His wife, Patricia, wept openly, held their grandchildren, made phone calls. Marcus
felt hollowed out—not numb exactly, but suspended in a state where nothing quite registered as real.
He returned to his studio in the basement after two weeks, sitting at his wheel in the darkness
before dawn, the way he had for forty years. His hands remembered the motions even when his mind
could not.

It was only later, as the acute shock receded and people stopped calling, that Marcus began to
understand what his body was doing. When Patricia reached for his hand in bed, he would withdraw—not
consciously, but as though his skin had become something foreign, something that couldn't afford to
feel. When his daughter-in-law texted a photo of Derek's daughter, now seven, at her school recital,
Marcus found himself deleting it immediately, his chest tightening in a way that felt dangerous. He
had come to believe, without ever articulating it, that if he allowed himself to feel joy or
connection or hope, it would somehow betray the fact of Derek's absence. Love felt like a threat;
tenderness felt like a door he could not afford to open.

The pottery, which had always been his refuge, became something else entirely. He would sit at the
wheel for hours and produce nothing. The clay felt like Derek's absence made tangible—formless,
impossible to shape into something whole. When Patricia suggested he invite their son-in-law over
for dinner, or when she spoke about a trip they might take next fall, Marcus felt something close to
panic. The future was a place where Derek did not exist, and to plan for it was to accept that fact.
To imagine himself and Patricia at a restaurant in the autumn, to picture their grandchildren
growing older without their father, felt like a betrayal so profound that Marcus found himself
changing the subject, leaving the room, or—once—telling Patricia sharply that she was being
insensitive.

By autumn, six months after Derek's death, Marcus had retreated almost entirely. He no longer worked
at the wheel. He declined invitations from the pottery collective he had belonged to for decades.
When a former student called to commission a set of bowls, Marcus told him he wasn't taking orders
anymore. Patricia began to cry differently—not the acute grief of the early months, but a quieter,
more persistent sadness that seemed to include loneliness. She had tried to reach for him, to share
the weight of what they had lost together, but Marcus could not let her. The moment she spoke about
missing Derek, or about their grandchildren, or about anything that carried even a whisper of the
life they had built, Marcus felt his chest close. He pushed away not just the grief, but her
attempts to grieve with him, as though proximity to her pain might shatter whatever fragile
containment he had managed to construct.

It was Patricia who finally insisted they seek help. She had found him one morning sitting in
Derek's old bedroom—a room they had kept largely untouched—and she had simply said, "We can't keep
living like this." Marcus came to his first appointment reluctant and guarded, unsure whether
anything could touch the weight he carried. What he feared most was that if he stopped avoiding—if
he let himself feel the joy of his grandchildren's laughter, or the possibility of a future, or the
simple comfort of his wife's hand—the dam would break entirely and he would drown in it. But he was
drowning anyway, just more slowly, in the silence of a basement studio where nothing was being made.
