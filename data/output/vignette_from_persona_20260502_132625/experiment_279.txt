# Clinical Vignette: Vinh

Vinh was forty-seven when the flooding came. He had lived in New Orleans for twenty-three years,
long enough to weather Katrina and Rita, long enough to believe himself prepared. The summer of 2019
brought unprecedented rainfall, and the levee system—upgraded, he'd been assured—failed in ways
engineers said should never happen. Water surged into the Lower Ninth Ward where he and his wife,
Linh, had raised their two daughters. He was at his office in the CBD when the first alert came
through. By the time he reached the car, the streets were rivers. He drove toward home anyway, water
rising against the windshield, the engine finally stalling three blocks away. He waded the rest, the
murky water carrying things he didn't want to identify, the smell of sewage and chemicals thick in
the humid air. When he reached the house, the water was already waist-high inside. He spent that
night on the roof with Linh, watching the neighborhood transform into something unrecognizable,
unable to help anyone, unable to do anything but wait.

The house was salvageable, barely. But something in Vinh was not. In the months after, he returned
to his job at the policy analysis firm—the same work, the same desk, the same colleagues who had
also lost homes or knew people who had. He was, by all external measures, coping. He went to
meetings. He filed reports. He smiled when appropriate. But inside, a new vigilance had taken root,
one that grew more elaborate and consuming as the weeks passed.

The first time he felt truly unwell—a fever that spiked to 101 degrees in early autumn—he became
convinced something was seriously wrong, something the doctors would miss. The fever itself was
minor, a cold settling into his chest, but the sensation of his body betraying him, of not being
able to trust its signals, triggered a cascade of fear. If his body could fail him without warning,
what else might? The unpredictability of illness mirrored the unpredictability of the flood itself,
that sudden rupture of the world's order. He had come to believe that he couldn't rely on himself
anymore—not his judgment, not his body, not his ability to protect what mattered. When he developed
a cough that lingered for weeks, he found himself researching rare pneumonias at two in the morning,
his mind spiraling through possibilities, each one confirming his deepest conviction: he was
breaking down, losing his grip. The fear that he was going crazy—that his catastrophic thinking, his
inability to stop scanning for danger, the way his heart would race at the slightest unexpected
noise—all of it meant something fundamental had fractured inside him.

This conviction then fed back into his vigilance. Because if he truly couldn't rely on himself, if
his mind was becoming unreliable, then he had to be especially careful. He had to monitor himself
constantly. He had to anticipate threats before they materialized. When he smelled smoke—a neighbor
burning leaves in October, a restaurant's exhaust vent near his office—his body flooded with
adrenaline and a choking sensation that felt indistinguishable from drowning. The smell transported
him not just to memory but to a state of hyperarousal where anything unpredictable felt lethal. A
colleague's sudden illness, an unexpected change to a meeting schedule, even the weather forecast
showing a chance of rain sent him into a state of careful, exhausting vigilance. He began checking
weather reports obsessively, studying flood maps, reviewing his evacuation plan—actions that
temporarily soothed him but also reinforced his belief that danger was constant and that only
through relentless monitoring could he stay safe.

Linh had noticed the change. She mentioned it gently at first, then with frustration. He was jumpy,
distant, checking his phone constantly. He'd stopped going to the neighborhood gatherings after the
disaster, stopped accepting invitations from friends. He'd even begun working from home two days a
week, a change he'd negotiated with his manager, telling her it was about productivity but knowing
it was really about control—about reducing unpredictability, about not having to navigate the
sensory chaos of the office, about being closer to home in case something happened. By his fifty-
first birthday, Vinh was exhausted. The vigilance that had felt necessary now felt like a cage. His
body ached from the constant tension. His mind felt foggy from interrupted sleep. He was terrified
that his reactions meant he was genuinely losing his mind, and that terror only made him more
careful, more watchful, more convinced that he couldn't trust himself to navigate the world without
catastrophe striking.

It was Linh who finally insisted he see someone. She said she couldn't watch him disappear anymore.
Vinh agreed to the appointment, though part of him believed that no therapist could help someone
whose mind had already begun to fracture, whose body had proven itself unreliable, whose world had
already shown its true nature: unpredictable, dangerous, and utterly beyond his control.
