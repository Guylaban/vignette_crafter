# Sofía's Story

Sofía was twenty-two when her younger brother Mateo fell ill. She remembers the morning he wouldn't
wake—the way her mother's voice had changed, the peculiar flatness of it, as though something
essential had already drained away. Meningitis. The word arrived like a door slamming. For six days,
Sofía sat in the pediatric ICU while machines beeped and Mateo's small body lay still under white
sheets, his skin waxy and too warm. She held his hand. She counted the seconds between his breaths.
On the fourth night, when his fever spiked and the nurses moved with sudden urgency, she tasted
copper and salt in her own mouth—whether from fear or from biting her cheek, she never knew. Mateo
recovered. The infection cleared. He was discharged after three weeks, and within months he was
running again, laughing again, entirely himself. But Sofía was not.

She returned to her job as an accountant at a mid-sized firm in Miami, where she spent her days
reconciling spreadsheets and preparing tax filings. The work was orderly, predictable—everything she
had always found comforting. Yet within weeks, she noticed something had shifted. Small, ordinary
silences began to frighten her. When a colleague would stop talking mid-sentence to check an email,
or when the office quieted during the afternoon, Sofía's chest would tighten. Silence felt
dangerous, as though something catastrophic was gathering in the stillness. Similarly, when a
client's return call took longer than promised, or when her supervisor delayed feedback on a
project, Sofía found herself spiraling into certainty that something had gone wrong—someone had
gotten hurt, or she had made a terrible mistake that would unravel everything. Her heart would race.
Her hands would shake. She would refresh her email obsessively, as though the absence of a message
was itself a message of doom.

The memories made it worse. Unbidden, the taste of copper would flood her mouth—not metaphorically,
but physically present, coating her tongue and throat—and suddenly she was back in that ICU room,
watching Mateo's chest rise and fall. The sensory vividness was overwhelming and disorienting; she
could not seem to translate the memory into words, could not explain to anyone what she was
experiencing when it happened. Instead, she would feel it arriving and push it away before it could
fully crystallize, cutting it off mid-sequence, as though she could prevent the rest from surfacing
if she just refused to look. But the fragments that did surface—the taste, the fluorescent light,
the smell of antiseptic—left her with a conviction that had taken root so deeply she could not argue
it away: she was fundamentally helpless. Bad things happened to people she loved, and she could not
stop them. She could not have stopped Mateo from getting sick. She could not stop her mother from
crying. She could not stop any of it. The world was not safe, and neither was she within it.

This certainty had begun to isolate her. Her close friend Valentina had invited her out repeatedly
over the past months—drinks on Friday nights, weekend trips to the beach—but Sofía found herself
declining. She could not explain why, exactly. She simply did not feel like herself anymore. She
used to be the person who made everyone laugh, who organized group dinners and remembered birthdays.
Now she felt separate from that person, as though someone else was living in her body, someone
afraid and diminished. At work, she had stopped joining her colleagues for lunch. She sat at her
desk, checking and rechecking her calculations, afraid that if she looked away, something would
happen that she would not be able to prevent. The joy she had once found in precision and order had
curdled into anxiety.

By October, three months after Mateo's discharge, Sofía's supervisor had pulled her aside. Her work
was still accurate, but she seemed withdrawn, distracted. Was everything okay? Sofía had said yes,
and meant it—she did not know how to say no, how to explain that she was not okay, that she felt
trapped in a version of herself she could not recognize and could not escape. She made an
appointment with a therapist because she was afraid of what would happen if she did not, afraid that
the fear itself would consume her entirely. What she wanted most was to feel safe again, to believe
that silence was just silence and delays were just delays. What she feared most was that she never
would.
