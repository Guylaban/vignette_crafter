# Clinical Vignette: Marco

Marco was thirty-five when the delivery truck hit him. He had been loading equipment into his van
outside the venue where he worked as a session guitarist—a Tuesday afternoon in April, ordinary
light, the kind of day that should have dissolved into a hundred others. The truck's driver had
looked down at his phone for what Marco would later learn was six seconds. Those six seconds rewrote
everything. Marco remembered the sound first: a grinding, metallic shriek that seemed to come from
inside his own chest. Then the impact threw him backward, and his right leg folded beneath him in a
way legs should not fold. He remembered the driver's face through the windshield—shock, then nothing
else coherent. The hospital came in fragments: sirens, the smell of his own blood, a surgeon's voice
saying something about pins and reconstruction. Three months of physical therapy. A hairline
fracture in his femur that healed. But the part of Marco that had been whole on that Tuesday
afternoon never quite reassembled.

The investigation into the accident had concluded within weeks. Negligent driving, the police report
said. The driver was cited, paid a fine, and walked away. No criminal charges. Marco had come to
believe with a certainty that felt like physical fact that this was profoundly, cosmically
unjust—that his shattered leg, the months of pain, the fear that still seized him whenever he heard
a vehicle accelerate nearby, had been reduced to a traffic violation and a fine. Nothing had been
done about it. Nothing would be. This injustice gnawed at him, and it fed into something darker: a
conviction that his life had been destroyed, that the person he had been—the reliable session
musician who could play four gigs a week, who had energy for his wife Rosa in the evenings, who
slept without fear—had been erased. Some nights Marco lay awake certain that if he allowed himself
to think too deeply about what had happened, about how unfair it all was, something inside him would
detonate. He was afraid of his own rage, afraid that if he really let himself feel the full weight
of it, he would do something terrible—say something to Rosa he could never take back, or worse. So
he did not think about it. He pushed the thought away whenever it surfaced, the way you might push
away something that burns.

But the memory itself was unreliable, fractured into pieces that would not cohere. When Marco tried
to deliberately reconstruct what had happened, the sequence dissolved. He could summon the sound,
the impact, the driver's face—but they floated separately, disconnected from one another, and large
gaps remained. What had he been holding when the truck hit him? How long had he lain on the
pavement? He could not say. The incompleteness of it tormented him. Each time he tried to gather the
pieces together into a coherent narrative, the effort seemed to deepen his conviction that his life
was ruined, that this fragmented, inaccessible memory was proof that something vital had been taken
from him. And that belief—that he was destroyed, that nothing could be done, that his anger might
consume him—made it impossible to sit with the memory long enough to process it. So he did not try
anymore.

Instead, Marco filled every available hour with other people. He took on extra gigs, longer
sessions, anything that kept him in studios or rehearsal spaces surrounded by bandmates and
producers. He joined a weekly poker game that stretched until midnight. He volunteered at a
community music program on Thursday nights. When he was home, he turned the television up loud and
kept Rosa talking—about her day, her work, anything—because silence was the opening through which
the fragmented pieces of that Tuesday afternoon would begin to surface. He had come to rely on this
constant presence of others the way a person might rely on a drug: without it, the thoughts came.
Without it, the rage built. He avoided talking about the accident entirely, deflecting whenever Rosa
gently tried to ask how he was really doing, changing the subject before the conversation could turn
toward anything that resembled processing or healing. His avoidance of thinking about what happened
and his avoidance of talking about it had become the architecture of his days—and yet the more he
structured his life around not remembering, the more the fragmented pieces seemed to demand
attention. The gaps in his memory felt like accusations. The injustice felt like a hand squeezing
his chest. And the fear that he might lose control, that his anger might finally break free, grew
quieter but more insistent with each week that passed.

What finally brought Marco to seek help was not a crisis, but a small, ordinary moment. Rosa had
asked him, gently, if he would consider talking to someone. And for the first time, instead of
deflecting, he had said yes—not because he believed it would help, but because he was exhausted.
Exhausted by the constant socializing, the vigilance, the way his leg still ached in the cold, the
way his chest still tightened at the sound of braking tires. He sat in the waiting room of the
clinic, his leg crossed over his knee in a way that hurt slightly, and he was most afraid that
nothing would change—that this destroyed version of himself was permanent. But beneath that fear was
something quieter: a desperate, half-formed hope that maybe, somehow, he could begin to remember
what had happened in a way that made sense. That maybe the fragmented pieces could be gathered. That
maybe the rage would not consume him.
