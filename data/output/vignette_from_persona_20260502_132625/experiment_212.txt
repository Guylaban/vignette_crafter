# Fatima's Vigil

Fatima was sixty-nine years old when the man began following her. It started in late September, on a
Tuesday evening after her shift at the McDonald's on Atlantic Avenue in Brooklyn, where she had
worked the register for twelve years. She was walking to the bus stop, her feet aching in her
orthopedic shoes, when she first noticed him—a man in his fifties with a pale, angular face and
clothes that seemed deliberately chosen to blend in. He was there again the next day. And the day
after that. He never spoke. He simply watched, maintaining a distance of fifteen or twenty feet, his
eyes fixed on her with an intensity that made her skin crawl. When she boarded the bus, he boarded
behind her. When she got off at her stop, so did he. For three weeks he shadowed her—to the grocery
store, past her apartment building, along the street where her grandchildren sometimes played. She
reported it to the police. They came, took a statement, and told her to call if he approached her.
He never did. He just watched.

One evening in early October, Fatima found a photograph slipped under her apartment door. It was a
picture of her sleeping, taken through her bedroom window. The image was grainy but unmistakable—her
face, her quilted comforter, the lamp on her nightstand. The smell came back to her then, a sour
chemical odor mixed with cigarette smoke and something else she couldn't name, the smell that had
filled her nostrils during those weeks of being watched. After that, he disappeared. The police
found nothing. No charges were filed. But Fatima never slept soundly again.

Now, nine months later, she woke most nights in the darkness, her heart hammering against her ribs,
her mind immediately cataloging: Was the front door locked? The back door? The windows? She would
rise in her nightgown, her knees stiff, and make her rounds through the apartment, checking each
lock twice, sometimes three times, running her fingers along the window frames to ensure they were
secure. Her husband, Karim, had learned not to speak when she did this. He simply lay in bed and
waited for her to return, understanding now that she needed this ritual to believe she was safe.

What frightened Fatima most was that she had come to believe the world was fundamentally unsafe,
that people—all people—harbored the capacity for this kind of violation. She watched faces now with
an intensity that exhausted her. At the McDonald's, she studied the customers, looking for something
in their expressions, some micro-movement that might signal danger. Is that man staring at her too
long? Does that woman have the eyes of someone who might know him? Because Fatima was certain that
if she let her guard down for even a moment, if she failed to detect a threat in someone's face or
posture, something terrible would happen. This vigilance had become her armor, the only thing
standing between her and another photograph under her door. And so the faces blurred
together—suspicious, all of them, until she could barely remember how to see a human being without
first assessing their capacity to harm her.

The memories themselves had become strange, almost unreal to her. When she thought about that
photograph, about the smell of cigarette smoke and that chemical odor, the experience felt so
extreme, so nightmarish, that she sometimes wondered if it had actually happened at all. Other
times, she could recall every detail with crystalline clarity—the texture of the paper, the exact
angle of the photograph—but feel nothing, as though she were describing something that had happened
to someone else entirely, a stranger whose life had been invaded. She had come to believe that if
she allowed herself to truly feel what had happened, if she really let herself think about what that
photograph meant, she would not be able to handle it. So she had learned to compartmentalize, to
lock the memory away even as she remained locked away from the world.

Her identity had fractured somewhere in those nine months. The woman who had worked the register
with easy conversation, who had joked with the teenagers on the night shift, who had taken her
grandchildren to the park on weekends—that woman felt like someone from another life. Fatima looked
in the mirror now and saw a stranger, someone whose entire being had been reorganized around the
single task of staying vigilant. Her mistrust had become so complete that even Karim's reassurances
felt hollow. How could she trust him when she could not trust anyone? It was this belief—that people
could not be trusted, that safety was an illusion—that kept her eyes open at three in the morning,
checking locks that she had checked two hours before, studying the street below for shadows that
might conceal another watcher.

It was her daughter who finally insisted she seek help. Fatima had called in sick to work three
times in the past week, unable to face the crowd of customers, unable to bear the exposure. Her job,
the one constant in her life for twelve years, was slipping away. She sat in the therapist's office
now, her hands folded in her lap, and said quietly, "I don't know how to live like this anymore."
