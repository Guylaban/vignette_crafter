# Margaret

Margaret was sixty-eight when the police came to her hotel. It was a Tuesday evening in late
October, and she had been managing the Grandview for nearly twenty years—a three-story brick
building on the edge of downtown that catered mostly to business travelers and the occasional family
passing through. She had been doing the evening rounds, checking the front desk, when she heard
shouting from the second-floor corridor. A guest had called in a noise complaint about one of the
rooms. When she went up to investigate, she found two officers already there, having been called by
another tenant. The man inside—a younger guest who had been drinking—had refused to open the door.
Margaret had a master key. She knocked, announced herself, and unlocked it. What happened next was a
blur of motion and terror. One of the officers, interpreting her entry as a threat or perhaps not
seeing her clearly in the darkened room, grabbed her arm violently and threw her against the
doorframe. She felt her shoulder wrench, heard herself cry out. The other officer was shouting. She
was seventy years old, unarmed, in her own building, trying to do her job. It took only seconds for
them to realize their mistake. They apologized, called an ambulance, filed a report. The injury
healed. The incident report was filed. Nothing happened to the officers. Nothing was ever made
right.

Two years have passed, and Margaret is no longer the woman she was. The intrusive thoughts come
without warning—a sudden flash of that officer's face, the grip on her arm, the sickening impact
against the frame. Sometimes it happens when she is alone in her office, signing paperwork.
Sometimes it is triggered by something as simple as a voice raised in the hallway or the sight of a
uniform through the lobby window, and the memory floods back complete and vivid, as if she is there
again, as if her body does not know that time has passed. The terror is immediate and total, the
same animal panic she felt in that moment of helplessness. She has come to understand that the
institutions meant to protect her—the police, the hotel's corporate management, the legal system—had
failed her completely. No one was held accountable. No one even seemed to care. That realization has
been as damaging as the physical injury, perhaps more so. She finds herself thinking, in the dark
hours before sleep, that she is not the person she thought she was. She had always been competent,
capable, in control of her domain. Now she is someone who flinches at sounds, someone who cannot
trust the people who are supposed to help, someone fundamentally changed in ways she cannot
articulate to anyone.

The anniversary of the event arrives each October, and with it comes a dread that settles over her
days in advance. She knows what is coming—the memories will be sharper, the fear more acute. She has
taken to avoiding crowds, which has become increasingly difficult in her role. The hotel lobby fills
with people during conferences and events, and Margaret finds herself unable to manage the floor as
she once did. She stays in her office, delegates tasks, makes excuses. Her assistant has begun to
notice the withdrawal. A colleague invited her to the hotel association's annual dinner last month;
the ballroom would be crowded, full of voices and movement. Margaret declined, claiming fatigue. The
truth is that the thought of being surrounded by people, of not being able to see clearly, of being
trapped in that press of bodies, sends her into a spiral of what-ifs. What if something happened?
What if she needed help and no one came? The avoidance has become a habit, and the habit has cost
her—fewer connections with her peers, a reputation for being difficult or distant, a growing sense
that she is disappearing into herself. When she looks in the mirror now, she does not recognize the
woman looking back. She has become someone defined entirely by what was taken from her, and she is
terrified that this is all she will ever be. It was only last week, when her daughter gently
suggested that she might benefit from talking to someone, that Margaret finally admitted she could
not manage this alone.
