# Robert's Vigil

Robert Chen was sixty-nine years old when his wife of forty-two years died. The cancer had announced
itself quietly in March—a routine blood test, an ultrasound, a surgeon's careful words—and by
August, Margaret was gone. He had watched her fade across five months of chemotherapy and morphine,
had held her hand through nights when she couldn't sleep, had promised her things he wasn't sure he
could keep. When she died, a part of him had expected the grief to be sharp and then, eventually,
manageable. Instead, it had metastasized into something he didn't recognize.

The funeral was held on a gray afternoon in the cemetery where his parents lay buried. Robert had
stood at Margaret's graveside in a dark suit that suddenly felt like it belonged to someone else,
and when the pastor spoke about loss and eternal rest, something in his chest had twisted so
violently that for a moment he couldn't breathe. The physical sensation—the tightness in his throat,
the hammering of his heart, the way his hands had trembled as he scattered earth onto her casket—had
alarmed him. He was a man accustomed to managing things: spreadsheets, market fluctuations, the
orderly progression of quarterly reports. Grief, he had come to understand, did not follow
spreadsheets.

In the months that followed, Robert had returned to his job at the investment firm downtown, a
position he'd held for thirty-seven years. His colleagues had been kind in that careful way people
are around the bereaved, offering condolences and casseroles, giving him space. But he found himself
increasingly unable to tolerate the office itself. A colleague had mentioned, casually, that his own
father had passed away the previous winter, and the moment Robert heard those words—*lost my
father*—his stomach had lurched. He'd excused himself and spent twenty minutes in the bathroom
stall, breathing slowly, trying to steady the panic that had surged through him without warning. The
mention of loss, any loss, had become unbearable. He began to dread staff meetings where colleagues
might share personal news, where someone might say something about grief or death or missing
someone. His hands would sweat. His jaw would clench. The irritability that followed—a sharp anger
at nothing in particular—had made him snap at his assistant over a minor filing error, something he
would never have done before.

To manage this vulnerability, Robert had developed a strategy. He would not speak of Margaret. He
would not look at the photographs that remained on the mantle in his living room. He would not
attend the memorial gatherings that friends occasionally organized, and he certainly would not
return to the cemetery. When the waves of memory came unbidden—Margaret laughing in the kitchen,
Margaret's voice on the phone during a work lunch, the specific smell of her hair—he would push them
away with fierce determination, redirecting his attention to his computer screen, to the markets, to
anything that demanded his rational mind. He had learned to numb himself, to move through his days
in a kind of emotional fog. It was easier than feeling.

Yet the avoidance had begun to fail him. By suppressing his thoughts and avoiding the very reminders
that triggered his distress, Robert had inadvertently strengthened the grip these triggers held. A
conversation overheard in the hallway about someone's mother's passing would send a jolt of fear
through him, followed by a surge of anger he couldn't quite explain. His physical body had become a
register of threat he couldn't control—his heart would race, his palms would sweat, a sense of dread
would wash over him—and these reactions only reinforced his conviction that he must avoid anything
connected to loss, to death, to grief. The more he pushed away his memories and his feelings, the
more they seemed to demand his attention, erupting without warning in moments of unexpected rage or
in the suffocating sensation that something terrible was about to happen.

Six months into this cycle, Robert had stopped attending his book club, a weekly ritual he and
Margaret had shared for fifteen years. The club had become, in his mind, a place where loss was
inevitable—where someone would mention a death, where the absence of Margaret would be palpable,
where he would be forced to confront the fact of his solitude. Instead, he spent his evenings alone,
numb and restless, caught between the memories he refused to acknowledge and the physical dread that
seemed to have taken permanent residence in his chest. When his daughter finally insisted he speak
to someone, Robert had agreed—not because he believed it would help, but because the alternative,
the life he was living now, had become unbearable.
