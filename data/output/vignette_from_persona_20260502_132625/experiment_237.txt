# Clinical Vignette: Mei Chen

Mei Chen was sixty-one years old when she came to the clinic, though her face bore the particular
exhaustion of someone who had spent months—perhaps years—holding her breath. She was a sculptor,
known in her Seattle community for intricate wooden pieces that seemed to breathe with their own
life, though she had barely touched her tools in the past eighteen months. Her hands, still capable
and strong, had grown still.

The trauma that had reshaped her life occurred on a March evening two years ago at a gallery
opening. Mei had been standing near the entrance, speaking with a colleague about an upcoming
commission, when the man beside her—a gallery patron she did not know—had suffered a sudden,
catastrophic stroke. She watched him collapse, watched his face contort as his body seized, watched
the paramedics work on him across the marble floor while guests stood frozen around her. The man
died in the ambulance. Mei had not known him. She had simply been there.

In the weeks that followed, she had tried to return to normal. Her husband, David, had been
supportive, patient. But something had fractured inside her that could not be easily repaired. The
first sign was the perfume. A woman at the grocery store had worn the same cologne the gallery
patron had been wearing that evening—a sharp, woody scent that Mei had noticed only in retrospect,
only after she understood that she would carry every detail of his dying with her forever. When she
encountered that smell again, her heart had seized in her chest. The memory had flooded back with
such force and vividness that she could not distinguish it from the present moment; she was there
again, watching the man's eyes roll back, and the grocery store dissolved into that terrible gallery
space. The memory felt more real, more immediate, than the fluorescent lights and shopping carts
around her. After that, she had begun to avoid places where she might encounter unexpected scents,
and the avoidance had only deepened her conviction that the world was fundamentally unpredictable
and dangerous—that safety was an illusion, and that she could not trust her own body to protect her
from being ambushed by horror.

Footsteps behind her now sent her into a state of high alert. On walks with David, she would turn
around repeatedly, her shoulders tense, her breath shallow. Unpredictable situations—a knock on the
door, a change in plans, even a phone call from an unknown number—triggered a startle response so
violent that David had learned to announce himself before entering rooms. At night, the vigilance
intensified. Mei lay awake, listening, certain that danger was present in the darkness even though
she could not name what she feared. Her body would jolt at sounds—the house settling, a car
passing—and she would lie rigid, her heart pounding, unable to return to sleep. The threat felt
constant, imminent, as though the collapse she had witnessed was a prophecy of collapse everywhere,
in everyone.

When she tried to explain what had happened to David or to herself, the narrative fractured. She
could describe the gallery, the smell of the cologne, the moment of the collapse, but the pieces
would not cohere into a story. She would lose the thread mid-sentence, unable to articulate why the
memory felt so invasive, so inescapable. This fragmentation reinforced her growing conviction that
something was fundamentally wrong with her—that she was broken in some essential way, that a normal
person would have moved past this by now, that her inability to construct a coherent narrative was
evidence of her own defectiveness. These thoughts, once lodged, began to poison everything. If she
was defective, then how could she trust her own judgment? How could she trust anyone else's? The
world, which had always felt reasonably safe, now seemed populated by hidden threats and unreliable
people. Her husband's reassurances, meant to comfort her, instead triggered a deeper mistrust; she
found herself wondering if he was simply being kind, if he secretly thought she was falling apart,
if anyone could really be trusted to tell the truth.

The vivid, intrusive memories had become the engine of her suffering. When a memory
surfaced—triggered by a sound, a scent, an unexpected moment—it would generate a cascade of physical
reactivity: her chest would tighten, her hands would tremble, sweat would bead on her forehead.
These physical reactions would themselves become triggers, reminding her of her vulnerability, which
would then intensify her threat perception and pull her further into the conviction that the world
was dangerous and that she could not be trusted to navigate it safely. She had stopped accepting
gallery invitations. She had stopped going to restaurants. She had begun to avoid even the studio
where her sculptures waited, because the smell of the wood and the solvent she used had somehow
become entangled with the gallery smell, with the memory, with the fear.

What finally brought Mei to seek help was David's quiet observation that she was disappearing. Not
dramatically, but incrementally—fewer phone calls to friends, fewer reasons to leave the house,
fewer moments when she seemed present even when she was in the room. She was sixty-one years old and
terrified that the rest of her life would be lived in this contracted space, held hostage by a
memory that would not release her and a conviction that she was fundamentally broken. She wanted to
sculpt again. She wanted to walk without scanning for threats. She wanted, most of all, to trust
that her mind would not betray her with intrusions she could not control.
