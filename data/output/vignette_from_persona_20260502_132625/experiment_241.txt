# Clinical Vignette: Captain Miguel Reyes

Captain Miguel Reyes was fifty-five years old when he walked into the clinic, his posture still
military-straight despite the weight he carried. Twenty-three years earlier, in the summer of 2001,
he had been a civilian contractor working communications for a humanitarian organization in a small
city in Central America. He was there to help install water systems, a task that felt meaningful to
him—work that mattered. He had a wife back home, a young daughter, and a sense that he was doing
something good in the world.

The violence came without warning. A armed group swept through the neighborhood where Miguel was
staying with three other aid workers and a family who had taken them in—a mother, two children, and
an elderly grandmother. The gunfire started at dawn. Miguel remembered the sound, the way it seemed
to come from everywhere at once, the smell of cordite mixing with dust. He remembered the mother
pushing her children toward the back room, her hands shaking. He remembered standing frozen in the
kitchen, paralyzed by the knowledge that he had no weapon, no training, no way to stop what was
happening. When the soldiers forced their way inside, Miguel did nothing. He let them take what they
wanted. He watched them drag the grandmother outside. He heard her screams stop. He lived.

For the first decade after, Miguel buried himself in work. He joined the Navy, climbed the ranks,
became a captain. His wife, Rosa, knew something had happened, but Miguel rarely spoke of it. When
she asked, he would change the subject or say it was behind him. What he didn't tell her was that he
had come to believe he was fundamentally weak—that a real man would have found a way to intervene,
to protect those people. That conviction had calcified into something immovable. He carried the
certainty that he should have done more, that his failure to act made him complicit in what
happened. He would never forgive himself for that inaction.

But the memories would not stay buried. They began surfacing about five years ago, triggered by
small things—the sound of a helicopter, a news report about displacement, even the smell of diesel
fuel. When they came, they came with overwhelming sensory force. Miguel would be standing in his
office at the naval base, and suddenly he was back in that kitchen, the heat pressing down, the
sounds impossibly vivid, his heart racing as though the danger were immediate. The memory felt
completely real, not like remembering but like reliving. These moments of intense guilt would crash
over him—guilt about his inaction, guilt about surviving when the grandmother did not, guilt about
the family he never learned the fate of. The sensory vividness made the guilt feel deserved,
inevitable.

What troubled Miguel most was that sometimes the memories came without any feeling at all. He could
recount the details—the layout of the house, the color of the grandmother's dress, the exact pitch
of the children's crying—but feel nothing, as though he were describing something that happened to
someone else entirely. Those numb recollections were almost worse than the vivid ones, because they
confirmed what he already believed: that something inside him was broken, that a person with any
real capacity for connection or conscience would feel something. The numbness itself became evidence
of his weakness.

His belief that he was weak fed his memories, and his memories reinforced that belief. When a vivid
flashback seized him, the intensity and realness of it proved to him that he should have been able
to do something—that only a weak person would have frozen. When he recalled the event numbly, he
interpreted that detachment as confirmation that he was fundamentally disconnected from his own
humanity. Over the past three years, these cycles had begun to isolate him. He had stopped attending
social events with Rosa, claiming fatigue. He had turned down a promotion that would have required
him to mentor younger officers, certain that he had nothing of value to teach them. Rosa had begun
to withdraw as well, hurt by his distance, confused by the man he had become. It was only when she
threatened to leave—when she told him she couldn't watch him disappear anymore—that Miguel finally
agreed to seek help. What frightened him most now was the possibility that she was right, that his
weakness was not just a failure of the past but a permanent flaw that would destroy everything he
had built.
