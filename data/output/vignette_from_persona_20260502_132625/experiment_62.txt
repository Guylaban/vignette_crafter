# Kofi's Story

Kofi was forty-five now, his hands weathered from decades of work in the fields—tobacco in North
Carolina, then orchards in upstate New York, now back to tobacco in a different county. His partner,
Amara, had been patient with him for three years, but lately he could see the exhaustion in her face
when he insisted on keeping the kitchen light on all evening, or when he positioned himself at the
table so he could see both the front and back doors at once. She had stopped asking him why. He
could not have explained it in a way that made sense, because the reasons lived in his body before
they reached his mind.

The thing that shaped Kofi most happened when he was seven years old, in a small apartment in Accra.
His father had come home drunk—not unusually, but that night something in the air had changed.
Kofi's mother had said something small, something ordinary, and his father's hand had moved so fast
that Kofi had not seen it coming. What he remembered was the sound: her cry, then the crash of a
plate, then the wet slapping of fist against flesh in the darkness of the hallway outside their
bedroom. His younger sister had cried. Kofi had stayed completely still, his heart hammering so hard
he thought it might break through his ribs. He had wanted to run to his mother, to do something, but
his legs would not move. The beating had lasted perhaps five minutes. It had felt like hours. When
it stopped, there was only his mother's quiet sobbing and the sound of his father's breathing. Kofi
had lain awake the rest of the night, listening to every sound, every shift of weight in the
apartment, terrified that it would start again.

He had carried that night inside him for thirty-eight years. For most of his adult life, he had
managed it by staying busy, by working long hours, by keeping people at a distance. But something
had shifted recently—or perhaps it had been shifting all along and he had only now run out of ways
to outrun it. The hypervigilance that had once felt like simple caution had become a constant,
exhausting state. He found himself monitoring every interaction, every tone of voice, every sudden
movement. At work, he would startle at the sound of machinery starting up, his body tensing as if
bracing for impact. Night was the worst. Once the sun went down, his sense of safety evaporated
entirely. He had come to believe that darkness itself was dangerous, that the absence of light meant
he could not see a threat coming. He would lie awake beside Amara, muscles tight, listening for
sounds that might signal violence—a raised voice from a neighbor's apartment, a siren in the
distance, even the wind against the windows. When she tried to reassure him, to tell him he was
safe, something inside him rejected the words entirely. How could she know? How could anyone know?
The world had shown him its capacity for sudden, terrible violence, and no amount of reasoning could
convince him otherwise.

His loss of faith had come gradually, then all at once. He used to believe in God, used to pray,
used to think that goodness mattered. But he had watched his mother endure years of abuse, had seen
her faith broken alongside her body, and he had come to understand that the universe did not operate
according to any just principle. He was no longer a happy man—he could barely remember what
happiness felt like, could not imagine a future in which he would not be carrying this weight. What
tormented him most was the memory of his own paralysis that night, the fact that he had done nothing
to help his mother. He would never forgive himself for it, would spend the rest of his life knowing
he had failed her when she needed him most.

To manage the constant threat he perceived, Kofi had developed rigid patterns. He never allowed
himself to be alone with another person—not even Amara, not really. He would insist they go to the
market together, that they eat meals with others present, that they never be in a room with the door
closed. This constant vigilance and need for company had begun to corrode his relationship; Amara
wanted intimacy, wanted to feel trusted, but Kofi's need to keep her in view, to ensure that nothing
could happen without witnesses, had made her feel like a prisoner in her own home. He ruminated
obsessively about the man who had hurt his mother, fantasizing about confrontation, about ways he
might make him pay, about justice that would never come. He tried desperately to suppress the
memories themselves, to push them down and away, but the effort of not thinking about them only made
them more vivid, more intrusive. His hypervigilance fed his certainty that danger was imminent,
which in turn deepened his conviction that people could not be trusted and that he was fundamentally
powerless. The strategies he had developed to feel safe—the constant monitoring, the avoidance of
solitude, the rumination—only reinforced his belief that the world was dangerous and that he could
never truly relax. His inability to trust in safety, even when logic suggested it, made him cling
harder to these maladaptive patterns, which only confirmed his darkest appraisals about human nature
and his own worthlessness.

Kofi had finally come to therapy because Amara had given him an ultimatum. She loved him, she said,
but she could not live this way anymore. He was terrified that losing her would prove what he
already believed: that he was incapable of being loved, that his damage was too profound to
overcome. What he hoped for, though he barely dared articulate it, was some way to quiet the alarm
that never stopped ringing inside him—some way to believe, even for a moment, that safety was
possible.
