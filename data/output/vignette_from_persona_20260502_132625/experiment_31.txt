# Thea's Case

Thea was twenty-four when she first sat in the waiting room of the mental health clinic, her hands
folded in her lap so tightly her fingernails pressed crescents into her palms. She was a competent
urban planner—her colleagues at the city planning department respected her work on transit corridors
and zoning analysis—but the moment she'd received the notice for her annual physical, something in
her had fractured. Medical procedures had always terrified her, though she'd never quite understood
why until she began talking to a therapist. The fear wasn't abstract. When she imagined lying on an
examination table, a doctor's hands approaching her body, her chest would tighten and her mind would
go blank, as though a door had slammed shut on something she wasn't allowed to remember. The
sensation of being examined, of being seen and touched by someone in a position of authority,
triggered a cascade of sensations—nausea, a crawling sense of exposure, a conviction that she was in
danger—and with those sensations came fragments of rage and shame she couldn't fully locate.

Thea had grown up in Portland with her mother and her mother's partner, David. From age seven until
she was twelve, David had used her body as a repository for his anger. There were no long narratives
in her mind about what happened—just disconnected moments that arrived without warning: the sound of
a belt being unbuckled, the smell of his cologne mixed with alcohol, the feeling of her small body
braced against a wall. When she tried now to reconstruct the full picture, the memories scattered
like startled birds. She could access the emotion—a towering, incinerating rage—but the images
themselves remained fragmentary, inaccessible, as though her mind had learned to protect itself by
keeping the details locked away. What she could access was the certainty that what had happened was
profoundly, cosmically unfair, that no one had protected her, that nothing had ever been done about
it. Her mother had chosen David over her own daughter's safety. No authority figure had intervened.
The injustice had calcified into something harder and colder than grief—a conviction that the world
was fundamentally unsafe and that she herself was fundamentally weak for having survived it.

By her early twenties, Thea had developed an elaborate system of safety. She felt secure only in her
apartment, in her office during off-hours when the building was quiet, and in the homes of two close
friends whose movements and temperaments she could predict. Anywhere else—public transit, crowded
stores, even her mother's house—felt like standing on the edge of a precipice. When someone made a
sudden movement near her, her entire nervous system would jolt into high alert; she'd find herself
tracking their hands, reading their posture for threat, unable to focus on anything else. This
hypervigilance had begun to bleed into her work. She'd find herself unable to concentrate during
team meetings, her attention fragmenting whenever someone shifted in their chair or spoke in a
raised voice. Her supervisor had gently suggested she seemed distracted lately. What he didn't see
was the exhaustion of constant surveillance, the way her mind was always half-turned toward
detecting danger.

Over the years, Thea had learned that the way to survive in a world that felt fundamentally
dangerous was to become invisible and agreeable. If she could anticipate what others wanted and
provide it, if she could make herself small and unthreatening and useful, she could prevent harm.
With colleagues, she volunteered for extra work and never disagreed in meetings. With friends, she
deferred constantly—choosing restaurants she didn't want to go to, spending time she didn't have,
absorbing their problems as though they were her own responsibility. This people-pleasing had become
so automatic that she barely recognized it as a choice anymore; it felt like the only way to stay
safe. But when someone refused her attempts at appeasement, or when she caught herself being
seen—really seen, in a way that exposed her rather than let her hide—the old sensations would
return. She'd feel nauseous, exposed, certain that something terrible was about to happen. And when
those sensations arrived, she'd find herself flooded with rage she couldn't explain and memories she
couldn't quite grasp: the emotional intensity of the trauma without the images to anchor it, leaving
her stranded in a storm of feeling with no narrative to contain it.

The more she tried to manage her safety through avoidance and compliance, the more her world
contracted. She stopped going to the grocery store at busy times. She declined social invitations
that involved new people or unfamiliar spaces. She began to avoid conversations about her childhood,
cutting off friends who asked gentle questions about her family. Each small act of avoidance
provided temporary relief—the nausea would settle, the sense of exposure would fade—but it also
seemed to reinforce her conviction that the world was dangerous and that she was powerless within
it. The injustice of her past, the sense that she had been treated as an object rather than a
person, seeped into how she saw herself now. She was weak. She was damaged. She deserved the small,
constrained life she had built. When she thought about how different things might have been if David
had never entered her mother's life, if someone had protected her, if she had been stronger or
braver or less broken, the counterfactual thinking would spiral for hours, leaving her exhausted and
more convinced than ever of her own inadequacy.

What finally brought Thea to seek help was the physical exam notice. She couldn't avoid it
indefinitely—her job required regular health screenings—and the thought of lying vulnerable on an
examination table, of being touched and examined by a stranger, had become unbearable. She sat in
the clinic waiting room knowing she needed something to change, though she was terrified of what
that change might ask of her. She was afraid that talking about what happened would make it more
real, that remembering would shatter her entirely. But she was also beginning to understand that the
safety she had built was no longer protecting her—it was imprisoning her. She wanted to know if it
was possible to feel safe in her own body again, to move through the world without constant fear, to
stop hating herself for surviving.
