# Arun

Arun was thirty-three when a delivery truck ran a red light and t-boned his sedan at an intersection
in Portland. He remembered the sound first—a deafening metallic shriek that seemed to come from
everywhere at once—and then the sensation of his body being thrown sideways, his left arm pinned
between the door frame and his ribs, the sharp crack he felt but didn't immediately feel, the taste
of blood. The paramedics told him later that he'd been lucky; the impact had fractured his humerus
and cracked three ribs, but missed his lungs by inches. Arun had spent six weeks in a sling, another
four months in physical therapy—the bitter irony of a physical therapist learning to move his own
body again, relearning what it could do, what it couldn't. The fracture healed. The ribs knitted.
But something else didn't.

In the months after, Arun began to notice things. The way a construction site's metal scaffolding
caught the light, suddenly looking like a collapsed structure. The glint of a box cutter on a
client's desk during a treatment session. The sharp angle of a fire extinguisher mounted on a clinic
wall. What had once been the ordinary architecture of his world had become a landscape of potential
weapons and instruments of harm. He was certain now—certain in a way that felt less like belief and
more like knowledge—that his body was fundamentally unreliable. It had betrayed him once, folding
under force he couldn't control, and he lived with the conviction that it would do so again. You can
never know who will harm you, he found himself thinking during his commute, watching other drivers
with new suspicion. A loud noise—a car backfiring, a door slamming—would send electricity through
his chest, his shoulders jumping to his ears before his mind caught up.

The intrusive thoughts came without warning. A flash of the truck's grille filling his windshield.
The sensation of impact replaying behind his eyes while he was trying to assess a patient's range of
motion. He'd wake in the night gasping, the dream already dissolving but the panic lingering, his
heart hammering as though the collision were happening in real time. These moments seemed to sharpen
his hypervigilance; each unwanted image, each jolt awake, made him more certain that danger was
imminent, that he needed to be ready. His eyes would scan the physical therapy clinic
obsessively—the position of the treatment tables, the exits, the location of anything that could
become a projectile.

To manage the chaos, Arun developed rituals. He cleaned and reorganized his treatment space each
morning before clients arrived, arranging instruments in precise rows, wiping down surfaces,
checking that nothing was out of place. The repetition calmed something in him, created an illusion
of control in a body and world he no longer trusted. He mentally rehearsed worst-case scenarios—what
he would do if a client collapsed, if someone became violent, if another vehicle struck the
clinic—as though preparing for catastrophe might somehow prevent it. But the more he rehearsed, the
more real the threats felt. The more he cleaned, the more contaminated the world seemed. The
checking and organizing, meant to create safety, only reinforced his conviction that danger was
everywhere, that his body couldn't protect him, that hypervigilance was the only rational response.

His wife, Priya, noticed the change first. He'd stopped going to their neighborhood gym because he
couldn't stop tracking the location of dumbbells and weight plates. He'd rearranged their kitchen
three times in a month. He'd become difficult to wake, startling violently at her touch, and distant
in a way that made her feel like she was living with a stranger. At work, his concentration
fractured. A patient would describe their pain and Arun would lose the thread halfway through, his
mind caught in worst-case rehearsal or scanning the room for threats. His supervisor had gently
suggested he might benefit from speaking to someone.

Now, at his intake appointment, Arun sits with his hands folded tightly in his lap, shoulders held
high and rigid. He describes his symptoms with the precision of someone who has catalogued them
obsessively, but his voice carries an undertone of despair. What frightens him most is the
possibility that this—the hypervigilance, the rituals, the conviction that his body has become a
liability—is permanent, that the man he was before the accident no longer exists. He is hoping,
though he doesn't quite believe it, that someone might help him understand why he can't seem to stop
scanning for danger, why control has become both his salvation and his prison.
