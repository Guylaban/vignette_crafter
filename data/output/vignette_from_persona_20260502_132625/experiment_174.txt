# Aroha's Story

Aroha was sixty-six when she first came to the clinic, her Samoan heritage evident in her warm brown
eyes and the careful way she moved—as though her body itself required negotiation with the world.
She had been a network engineer for thirty-eight years, the kind of woman who could troubleshoot
systems with her eyes closed, who had mentored younger technicians and earned the respect of her
peers. Her husband, Tane, had died five years ago, and she had thrown herself into work, finding in
its logic and predictability a refuge she no longer found at home.

The assault happened on a Tuesday evening in March, eighteen months before her intake appointment.
Aroha had stayed late at the office to resolve a server issue, the building nearly empty except for
the security guard downstairs. She was leaving through the parking garage when a man emerged from
behind a concrete pillar—someone she had never seen before, someone whose face she would later
struggle to fully recall, as though her mind had protected itself by blurring him into shadow. He
had grabbed her from behind, his breath heavy with alcohol, his hands urgent and violent. He had a
knife—she remembered the glint of it, the way he pressed it against her ribs as he forced her into a
dark corner, his body pinning her against the cold concrete. She had frozen. She had not fought. She
had complied, and in complying, she had felt herself fracture into pieces: the part of her that
endured, and the part of her that could not forgive herself for enduring.

In the weeks after, Aroha had returned to work, had told no one, had tried to reassemble herself
into the woman she had been. But the woman she had been no longer existed. What took her place was
someone hypervigilant, someone who could not move through the world without cataloguing threat. In
the office, she began noticing things—the letter opener on her desk, the way a coworker's pen could
become a weapon, the emergency exit signs that suddenly seemed inadequate. She would find herself
tracking these objects, her attention snagged by anything sharp or pointed, her body tensing as she
calculated distances and escape routes. This constant monitoring exhausted her, and the exhaustion
itself felt like evidence that she could not keep herself safe—that no amount of vigilance would be
enough.

The smell of alcohol became intolerable. A colleague celebrating a promotion brought wine to the
office, and the scent triggered a cascade: her chest tightened, her breath shallowed, and she found
herself unable to remain in the break room. She began avoiding the break room entirely, eating lunch
at her desk instead. But lunch felt dangerous too—it meant sitting still, meant being vulnerable.
She began restricting what she ate, controlling the portions, the timing, the ritual of it. If she
could control her body in this way, she reasoned, perhaps she could control what happened to it. The
restriction gave her a sense of agency that nothing else did, and so she clung to it, skipping
breakfast, limiting herself to small portions of plain food, feeling a strange relief in the hunger.

This restriction, born from her need to control what felt uncontrollable, began to reinforce her
belief that she was fundamentally damaged, that her body itself had become the problem. She caught
her reflection in the bathroom mirror and saw not herself but a vessel that had been violated, that
carried the assault in its very cells. She began wearing loose, shapeless clothing, avoiding
mirrors, showering in the dark. When her daughter visited from Wellington, Aroha had flinched at her
embrace—a hug that would have once been a comfort now felt like an invasion, a reminder of how
easily bodies could be used against her. She pulled away, and her daughter's hurt expression had cut
deeper than the knife ever had.

The avoidance spread. She stopped attending her book club, stopped accepting dinner invitations,
stopped the small rituals of intimacy and connection that had once defined her life. She told
herself she was protecting others—that her damaged state might somehow contaminate them—but the
truth was more complex: she could not bear to be close to anyone, could not tolerate the
vulnerability of being known. When a colleague at work had tried to comfort her after she'd seemed
withdrawn, placing a gentle hand on her shoulder, she had startled so violently that she'd knocked
over her coffee. The unexpected touch had sent her into a spiral of panic, and afterward, she had
become even more guarded, more isolated.

Months passed in this contracted state. She began to believe that she was helpless, that bad things
happened to her because something fundamental in her nature invited them. No reassurance—from her
therapist, from her daughter, from her own rational mind—could convince her that she was actually
safe. Even when her daughter reminded her that the assault was not her fault, that she had survived,
Aroha could only hear the echo of her own complicity, the shame of her body's betrayal. This
conviction that she was irredeemable, that she had acted against everything she believed herself to
be, drove her deeper into isolation and restriction, each avoidance a way of saying *I am not worth
saving*, each small refusal of connection a confirmation of her unworthiness.

It was her daughter who finally insisted she seek help, who would not accept another canceled visit,
who said simply, "Mum, you're disappearing." Aroha came to the clinic because she could no longer
bear the weight of her own isolation, though she arrived convinced that talking would not help, that
she was beyond repair. What she most feared was that if anyone truly saw her—truly saw what she had
become—they would confirm what she already believed: that the assault had not just happened to her,
but had revealed the truth of who she was.
