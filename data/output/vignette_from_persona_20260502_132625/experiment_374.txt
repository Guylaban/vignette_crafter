# Anahera's Story

Anahera was thirty-two when the mugging happened, a Tuesday evening in late September as she walked
from the parking garage toward her apartment building in a neighborhood she had lived in for five
years. She had been thinking about the quarterly reports due the next morning—her mind was at the
office, not on the street—when she heard footsteps accelerating behind her. Before she could turn, a
hand gripped her shoulder with such force that she felt her body wrench sideways. A man's voice, low
and urgent, demanded her bag. She remembered the smell of stale cigarettes and sweat, the metallic
taste of blood from where her teeth cut the inside of her cheek when he shoved her against the
concrete wall. The whole thing lasted perhaps ninety seconds. He took her purse and ran. A neighbor
found her sitting on the curb ten minutes later, shaking but unharmed.

In the weeks that followed, Anahera returned to work, answered emails, paid bills. She told herself
it was over. But by November, three months in, the world had fundamentally changed shape. Footsteps
anywhere—in the office hallway, in the grocery store, behind her on the street—sent her into a state
of hyperalert panic. The sound itself was enough to trigger a cascade of threat responses: her heart
would race, her breathing would shallow, and she would find herself unable to believe that she was
actually safe, even when her husband Marcus assured her that they were home with the doors locked.
What made this worse was the way her mind had begun to reframe the attack itself. She had come to
believe that the mugging happened because of who she was—careless, inattentive, the kind of person
who walked alone lost in thought and invited predation. This conviction about her own culpability
meant that every unexpected sound carried a deeper message: the world was dangerous, yes, but more
importantly, she was vulnerable in a way that was her own fault. That belief about herself fed back
into her threat perception, making it impossible to dismiss even routine noises as innocent. When
the mailman knocked on the door, her pulse spiked. When Marcus came up behind her in the kitchen to
embrace her, she would flinch violently, her body convinced it was happening again.

By early spring, Anahera had developed a set of strategies that seemed, in her mind, to offer some
protection. She would deliberately dissociate when she felt unsafe—consciously trying to leave her
body, to blank out the sensations that felt unbearable. She spent hours ruminating about how she
could have prevented the attack: she should have taken a different route, should have been more
aware, should have walked faster, should have screamed louder. These cycles of self-blame rumination
would stretch across entire evenings, each iteration reinforcing her sense that the event was
somehow her doing, which in turn deepened her conviction that the world was fundamentally dangerous
and that she could not trust even Marcus's reassurances of safety. The rumination itself became a
way of managing the unbearable feeling of powerlessness—if she could find the flaw in her own
behavior, perhaps she could prevent it from happening again. But the rumination also kept her
trapped in a loop where her negative beliefs about herself and the world grew more entrenched. When
she tried to relax on a Saturday afternoon, she found it impossible; her body remained tensed,
waiting for the next threat, certain that danger was imminent.

The memories themselves had taken on a surreal, almost hallucinatory quality. When they
surfaced—often triggered by the sound of footsteps or an unexpected touch—she would find herself
tasting blood again, feeling her body contort into the same twisted position against the wall. The
vividness was so extreme that it seemed impossible this had actually happened to her; it felt like
something from a film, too vivid and too terrible to be real. Yet that very unreality made the
memories more frightening, not less. If her own mind could conjure something so intense, how could
she trust her perception of safety at any moment? The memories would pull her back into that state
of threat, her body rigid, her breathing shallow, and she would find herself dissociating again,
trying to escape the sensations by blanking out, which only left her more disconnected from the
present and more vulnerable to the next trigger.

At work, the impact had become undeniable. Anahera manages a team of four junior accountants, and by
April she had begun avoiding the open office space. She would arrive early or stay late to work
alone, unable to tolerate the unpredictability of colleagues approaching her desk or the sound of
footsteps in the corridor. When a client meeting ran longer than scheduled, she found herself
catastrophizing—assuming that the delay meant something had gone wrong, that she had made an error,
that something bad was about to happen. Her supervisor had noticed the withdrawal. Her team had
noticed it too. Marcus noticed it most of all: his wife, who used to laugh easily and reach for him
without hesitation, now flinched at his touch and spent evenings staring at nothing, caught in loops
of self-recrimination that he could not interrupt.

It was Marcus who finally insisted she see someone. "You're not living," he said quietly one evening
in May. "You're just trying not to die." Anahera knew he was right. She sat in the intake
appointment, describing the footsteps, the taste of blood, the absolute impossibility of believing
she was safe, the certainty that she had brought this on herself through her own carelessness. What
she wanted most was to stop being afraid. What she feared most was that she was fundamentally
broken—that the person she had been before that September evening was someone the attack had
revealed to be fragile and flawed, and that no amount of therapy could change that essential truth
about who she was.
