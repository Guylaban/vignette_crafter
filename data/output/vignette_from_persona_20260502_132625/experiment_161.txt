# Marcus

Marcus was fifty-four when his partner of eight years, Daniel, fractured his ribs and left him
bleeding on the kitchen floor. It happened on a Tuesday evening in March, a time of year Marcus had
once loved. Daniel had been drinking—something that had become routine—and Marcus had said something
small, something about the dishes, about the apartment feeling chaotic. What followed was swift and
brutal. Daniel's fists came with a precision that suggested practice, and when Marcus tried to move
away, Daniel pinned him against the counter, his forearm across Marcus's throat, his breath hot and
whiskey-sharp against Marcus's face. Marcus could taste the copper of his own blood, the acidic burn
of stomach acid rising in his throat. He remembered the specific timbre of Daniel's voice—low,
controlled, almost conversational as he said things Marcus had tried for years not to remember. The
violence lasted perhaps three minutes. It felt like hours.

For the first weeks after, Marcus told no one. He called in sick to his position in the municipal
planning office and stayed in the apartment while Daniel moved out—a separation they had agreed to,
though Marcus knew it was temporary. During those early days, Marcus found that when he tried to sit
quietly and think through what had happened, the memory would not come cleanly. He could access
fragments: the counter, the taste, Daniel's voice. But when he tried to deliberately reconstruct the
sequence, to understand it, the narrative dissolved. The memory felt sealed off, protected behind a
wall he could not breach. Yet without warning, the taste would return—that copper, that acid—and
suddenly he was back in the kitchen, seeing it all with photographic clarity, as though he were
watching a film of himself from outside his body. The vividness was unbearable.

It was the loss of control in those moments that terrified him most. He could not predict when the
memory would ambush him, could not manage the intensity of it when it did. This unpredictability
began to reshape how he lived. By April, Marcus had arranged his life around never being alone. He
started attending evening planning committee meetings he did not need to attend, lingering in the
office until colleagues left, then driving to a nearby bar where he would sit for hours, nursing a
single drink, surrounded by the noise and presence of strangers. On weekends, he would invite
friends over—sometimes the same friends multiple times in a week—and he would keep the conversation
moving, the television on, music playing, anything to fill the silence. The thought of sitting alone
in a room, with nothing but his own mind, had become intolerable.

But the constant socializing created its own trap. Exhausted from performing normalcy, from the
effort of keeping himself perpetually occupied, Marcus became hypervigilant in social situations.
Being alone with men—even colleagues he had known for years—began to trigger a creeping dread. His
body would tense; his breath would become shallow. He found himself watching their hands, their
posture, calculating exits. This vigilance then drove him to seek out even more company, to surround
himself with larger groups, to avoid any situation where he might be alone with a man. The more he
avoided, the more his anxiety in those situations intensified, until he could barely sit through a
one-on-one meeting at work without his heart racing.

Sleeping became another battleground. The thought of sleeping alone, or worse, sleeping in an
unfamiliar place, triggered the same cascade of fear. When he did sleep, he would wake gasping, the
taste of blood still in his mouth, the memory playing in vivid, photographic detail. He began
sleeping on the couch, leaving lights on, keeping his phone in his hand. He stopped visiting
friends' homes. He declined an invitation to his sister's wedding in another state—an event he had
been looking forward to for months—because the thought of sleeping in an unfamiliar bedroom,
vulnerable and alone, was unbearable.

By late summer, the avoidance had begun to cost him. His supervisor had noticed his absences, his
reluctance to engage in routine one-on-one supervision. His sister had stopped calling. The
apartment felt like a prison he had constructed himself, and yet leaving it, being alone in it,
sleeping in it without constant distraction, all felt equally impossible. It was only when a
colleague gently suggested that Marcus seemed "not quite himself," that Marcus finally admitted to
anyone what had happened. That conversation, terrifying as it was, brought him to a therapist's
office in September, still tasting copper when he closed his eyes, still unable to sit alone with
what he had endured, still convinced that only constant vigilance and constant company could keep
him safe.
