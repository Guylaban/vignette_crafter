# Rosa's Vigilance

Rosa Mendoza was fifty-nine years old when she finally made the appointment, though she'd been
carrying the weight of what happened for nearly five decades. The abuse had begun when she was
seven—her father's hands, his voice, the particular way the kitchen light fell across the linoleum
on Tuesday afternoons when he came home early. She had learned very quickly that the world was a
place where safety could be taken away without warning, where bodies were not her own, where the
only certainty was that it could happen again. For decades, she had managed this knowledge by
building walls—literal and otherwise.

By the time she was working as a physical therapist in a busy clinic in Denver, Rosa had developed a
system. Her treatment room had a desk positioned so that her back was always to the corner, where
she could see the door and anyone approaching. She needed this arrangement the way other people
needed coffee; without it, a creeping dread would tighten in her chest during patient sessions. When
a new client came in—a man she didn't know, moving toward her with his hand extended in greeting—her
breath would catch. She had come to believe that strangers carried an inherent threat, that their
approach was itself a form of danger. Even when her rational mind knew the man was simply coming for
physical therapy, her body insisted otherwise. The feeling was absolute: a stranger crossing the
threshold of her space meant something terrible was about to happen.

What made this worse was that the memories themselves were unreliable. Sometimes Rosa could recall
the specific details of what her father had done with an almost clinical detachment, as if she were
describing something that had happened to someone else entirely. The emotional charge was simply
absent, replaced by a kind of numbness that was almost worse than pain. Other times, fragments would
arrive without invitation—the smell of his cologne, the sound of his belt buckle, the particular
grip of his fingers—and then vanish just as suddenly, leaving her disoriented and gasping. The
memories felt too extreme to be real, which paradoxically made them more terrifying. If something
that terrible had actually happened, what did that say about the world? What did it say about her?

The memories had a way of shaping how Rosa moved through her days. When a fragment would surface
unexpectedly—triggered by a patient's cologne or the way someone stood in her doorway—she would feel
the threat rising in her body before she could think. Her hands would shake. She would find herself
unable to focus. To manage this, she had developed rituals. She cleaned obsessively: her treatment
room, her apartment, her car. The repetitive motion of organizing and reorganizing gave her a sense
of control that the memories denied her. If everything around her was ordered and clean, perhaps she
could keep chaos at bay. When the threat felt too large, when strangers' approaches made her heart
race and the fragments of memory felt like they might split her open, she would eat—not from hunger,
but from a desperate need to fill the emptiness that the numb memories left behind. The food was a
kind of salve, a way to soothe the body that still lived in terror.

But the rituals had consequences. Rosa found herself avoiding situations that felt unpredictable or
stressful. She turned down opportunities at the clinic that would have meant supervising new staff
or attending conferences. She stopped going to her daughter's community events. The avoidance felt
necessary—why put herself in situations where strangers might approach, where she might feel
unsafe?—but it had gradually narrowed her life into something small and controlled. She woke at
three in the morning most nights, her body jolting into alertness, needing to verify that she was
still safe in her apartment, that no one had breached the walls she had built. She would lie in the
dark and think about the memories that felt both utterly real and impossibly unreal, and the cycle
would tighten: the threat would mount, driving her back to her cleaning and her avoidance, which
would quiet the threat for a time but leave the memories untouched, still lurking at the edges of
consciousness.

It was her daughter who finally pushed her to call. "Mom," she had said gently, "I'm worried you're
disappearing." Rosa sat with that for a long time. She was afraid—of the memories, of strangers, of
what might happen if she stopped maintaining her careful systems. But she was also exhausted. She
wanted to know if the memories would ever feel like they belonged to her, or if they would always
feel like something happening to a stranger. She wanted to know if she could ever sit with her back
to a door.
