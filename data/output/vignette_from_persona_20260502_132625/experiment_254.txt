# Clinical Vignette: Kwame

Kwame was sixty-three years old when the bomb detonated outside the university library in Nairobi
where he had worked for nearly two decades. It was a Tuesday morning in April, and he had stepped
outside to greet a visiting scholar—a moment of professional courtesy that would have placed him
directly in the blast radius had the man not been delayed by traffic. Instead, Kwame stood at the
library entrance when the explosion tore through the street, killing seventeen people and wounding
dozens more. He saw the bodies. He heard the screaming. He felt the heat wash across his face and
the concussive force that knocked him backward into the glass doors. For weeks afterward, he could
not enter that library, and eventually he accepted a transfer to a smaller branch across the city.
Fifteen years later, living now in the United States with his wife Grace, Kwame has built a quiet
life as a librarian in a modest public library system. But the memory has never left him.

The clarity of what he witnessed remains photographic. Kwame can describe the exact pattern of the
vendor's cart that had been overturned, the color of the woman's headscarf as she lay motionless on
the pavement, the smell of burning plastic and something else he cannot name. These details return
unbidden, often without warning—a particular angle of sunlight through the library windows, the
sound of a motorcycle backfiring in the parking lot, even the smell of diesel fuel from a delivery
truck can transport him instantly back to that morning, his heart racing as though the danger were
present and immediate. The film plays over and over, a loop he cannot stop. What troubles him most
is that he cannot predict when it will happen. He might be shelving books in the quiet stacks, and
suddenly the memory is there, vivid and overwhelming, as if thirty seconds have not passed since the
blast but thirty years.

When these memories surface, Kwame has learned to withdraw. He excuses himself to the back office,
closes his eyes, and allows his mind to drift into a private space—a memory of his childhood home in
Accra, the sound of the ocean, the taste of his mother's groundnut soup. He disappears into this
fantasy for five, ten, sometimes twenty minutes, until the acute sharpness of the trauma memory
fades enough that he can return to his desk. This escape has become habitual, almost necessary. But
the very act of retreating into fantasy seems to leave him vulnerable to the next intrusion. When he
returns to the present moment, he feels disoriented, and within hours—sometimes within
minutes—another trigger finds him. A patron's raised voice, an argument he overhears about injustice
or betrayal, the anniversary of the bombing that approaches each April like a storm he cannot
outrun: these moments of moral violation or commemoration pull the traumatic memory back to the
surface with renewed force.

Over the past two years, Kwame has begun to avoid the situations that might trigger these cycles. He
no longer attends the annual library gala, despite Grace's gentle encouragement. He declines
invitations to dinner parties and community events. He has stopped attending his church's fellowship
gatherings. What started as avoidance of obvious triggers—he will not watch news reports of
violence, will not discuss politics or injustice—has expanded into a broader retreat from anything
that might make him feel hopeful or engaged. He tells himself that if he does not allow himself to
feel happy, he cannot be disappointed or devastated again. He avoids crowded places entirely: the
shopping center where Grace once took him for his birthday, the market where they used to browse
together on Saturday mornings, even the annual library book fair that he once loved. The crowds feel
dangerous in a way he cannot fully articulate—too many bodies, too much unpredictability, too many
opportunities for something to go wrong.

His wife has noticed the change. She has watched her husband, once animated and engaged with his
work, become increasingly isolated. He comes home from the library and sits in his study with the
door closed. He no longer reads for pleasure, though books surround him. When Grace tries to plan
something special—a small dinner with friends, a drive to see the fall foliage—Kwame finds reasons
to decline. He becomes irritable at the suggestion, as though her hope is itself a threat. Grace is
worried, and that worry has begun to fracture something between them. It was Grace who finally
insisted that Kwame speak to someone. She said, "You are disappearing, and I am losing you." At
seventy-eight, Kwame has come to recognize that his strategies for survival have become a kind of
slow erasure. He fears that if he allows himself to feel anything—hope, joy, connection—the memories
will overwhelm him completely. What he hopes for, though he barely dares to name it, is to live
again without being consumed by what he witnessed all those years ago.
