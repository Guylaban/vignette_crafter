Vinh was fifty-one when his wife left him in the emergency room with a fractured wrist and a story
he could barely piece together. It had happened on a Tuesday evening in their Arlington home—an
argument about money, about his long hours at the tax office, about something so ordinary he could
no longer retrieve the exact words. What remained were fragments: the sound of breaking glass, the
feeling of being pushed backward into the kitchen counter, his partner's face transformed by rage
into something he no longer recognized. Then a blur—his arm bent at an angle that didn't belong, the
drive to the hospital, and the slow understanding that the person he'd chosen to build a life with
had done this to him.

The wrist healed. The bruises faded. But something in Vinh's nervous system had been rewired that
night, and he had spent the past eighteen months learning to live in a state of constant vigilance.
His wife had moved out after two weeks of tense silence, and he'd remained in the house—the same
house where it happened—unable to leave it and unable to feel safe inside it.

Exhaustion had become his constant companion. The long days at the tax office, where he reviewed
financial records with the same meticulous attention he'd always brought to the work, now drained
him in ways he couldn't explain. By mid-afternoon, his body would feel heavy, his mind sluggish, and
in that state of fatigue something terrible would happen: his thoughts would spiral into worst-case
scenarios, or a sudden fragment of that night would surface—the specific angle of light through the
kitchen window, the smell of his own fear—and he would become convinced that danger was imminent.
When he was tired, he couldn't trust his own judgment about what was safe. A colleague approaching
his desk to ask about a file would trigger an acute startle response; his heart would race as if the
threat were real and present. Unpredictable moments—a phone call from an unknown number, an
unexpected knock at his office door—would send him into a state of heightened alert that felt
indistinguishable from the minutes before the violence itself.

These triggers fed directly into a pervasive sense of threat that had come to dominate his waking
hours. Vinh had rearranged his home office so that his desk faced the doorway and his back was
always against the wall—a position that felt essential, though he couldn't fully articulate why.
When he was in other people's homes or in public spaces, he felt a gnawing anxiety unless he could
position himself with a clear view of exits and approaching figures. He found himself constantly
checking behind him in hallways at work, a habit so ingrained now that he barely noticed he was
doing it. The threat felt constant and shapeless, not tied to any specific danger but rather to a
fundamental conviction that something terrible could happen at any moment, that his body and mind
were not safe even when objectively nothing was wrong.

The fragments of memory that intruded without warning intensified this sense of threat. A particular
smell—cologne mixed with sweat—would cause a piece of the evening to surface: not the whole
narrative, but a sensory flash that felt as immediate and dangerous as the original moment. There
were gaps in what he could remember, entire stretches of time that had simply vanished from his
mind, and the incompleteness of the memory somehow made it worse. The uncontrolled intrusions meant
he could be sitting in a client meeting, and suddenly he would feel the phantom sensation of his arm
being twisted, or hear the sound of his own voice saying something he couldn't quite recover. These
involuntary memories would then trigger the threat response all over again—his heart rate climbing,
his muscles tensing, his eyes scanning the room for danger.

What frightened Vinh most, though, was what he had come to believe about his future. Nothing good
could happen to him anymore—this conviction had settled into his bones like a chronic illness. The
betrayal by someone he'd loved and trusted had shattered something fundamental in how he understood
the world. He felt hollowed out, as though the violence had transformed him into something less than
human, an object that could be broken and discarded. These dark beliefs about himself and his
prospects fed the threat monitoring, made every trigger feel more dangerous, made the intrusive
memories feel more predictive of what was to come. He had begun to avoid situations where he felt
unprepared or out of control, which meant he was avoiding more and more of his life. His colleagues
had noticed his withdrawal; his supervisor had gently asked if everything was alright. He'd made
excuses about the divorce, about needing time.

What finally brought Vinh to seek help was a panic attack at work three weeks ago—a full-body
conviction that he was in mortal danger while sitting alone in his office on a quiet afternoon. He'd
sat in his car in the parking garage for two hours afterward, unable to drive, unable to move. That
night, he'd called a therapist. He was terrified that he was losing his mind, but underneath that
terror was something quieter and more desperate: a faint hope that someone might help him understand
why the person he'd loved had hurt him, and whether he would ever feel safe again.
