# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was sixty-eight when the attack happened at the shopping mall where he'd worked security for
seventeen years. It was a Tuesday afternoon in late September, the kind of unremarkable day that
would cleave his life into before and after. A man had walked into the corridor near the food court
with a knife, shouting about government conspiracies, and before Marcus could radio for backup or
position himself between the attacker and the gathering crowd, the blade found his shoulder. The
wound itself wasn't catastrophic—he'd needed thirty-two stitches and a course of antibiotics—but
what stayed with him was the moment he'd seen the man's face, the absolute conviction in his eyes,
the way the knife had caught the fluorescent light. Marcus had been trained for this, had run
drills, had felt confident in his ability to protect people. In that instant, all of it had failed
him.

Three years later, at seventy-one, Marcus still worked security—the same mall, the same uniform, the
same corridors he'd walked a thousand times before. But the job had become a study in vigilance. He
always positioned himself with his back to the wall, whether he was sitting in the break room or
standing at his post near the entrance. His eyes tracked every person who entered, cataloguing their
hands, their gait, their affect. If someone moved too quickly or wore a loose jacket, his pulse
would spike. He had come to believe that he was uniquely vulnerable to danger, that his failure to
prevent the attack three years ago meant he lacked the capacity to handle even minor threats. A
raised voice from a shopper would send him into a spiral of self-doubt; a complaint from a
supervisor would convince him that he was incompetent, that a younger man would have seen the attack
coming, would have moved faster, would have protected that crowd. This conviction—that he was
fundamentally inadequate—drove him to heighten his surveillance, to position himself more carefully,
to scan faces with an intensity that left him exhausted by the end of each shift.

His memories of the attack itself were startlingly vivid: the warmth of his own blood, the smell of
the attacker's sweat, the precise angle of the blade as it descended. He could close his eyes and
see it all again in high definition. Yet there were moments, usually late at night when his wife
Eleanor would ask him what had happened that day, when he would feel nothing—as if he were
recounting something that had happened to someone else entirely, a story he'd heard rather than
lived. And then there were the gaps, the parts he couldn't quite assemble into a coherent sequence.
He knew the man had lunged toward him, knew he'd tried to step back, knew the blade had cut, but the
exact order of these moments had become slippery, fragmented. When he tried to explain it to
Eleanor, the words would tangle. He'd start at the beginning and lose the thread halfway through,
leaving her confused and him frustrated.

What frightened Marcus most was the thought that he'd become a liability, that his colleagues
depended on someone who'd already proven he couldn't be depended on. He ruminated about what he
should have done differently, about how he might confront the attacker if he ever saw him
again—fantasies of confrontation that were vivid and detailed but that he knew would never happen.
These thoughts would spiral through his mind during his shifts, competing with his need to stay
alert, draining him further. His constant watchfulness, his refusal to sit anywhere but with his
back to a wall, his reading of threat in every unfamiliar face—these had become his armor, the only
things standing between him and the belief that he was utterly, irrevocably broken. Yet each day
they left him more isolated, more convinced of his own inadequacy, more trapped in a cycle he
couldn't seem to escape. Eleanor had stopped asking him to explain what happened. His daughter had
stopped calling as often. And Marcus, sitting in the break room with his back firmly against the
wall, scanning the room for danger, had come to accept that this was simply who he was now: a man
who couldn't be trusted to keep anyone safe, least of all himself.
