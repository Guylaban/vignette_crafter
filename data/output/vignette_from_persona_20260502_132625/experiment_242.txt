# Arjun's Spiral

Arjun was twenty-three when the officers came to his apartment. He and his wife Priya had been
married for eight months, still learning each other's rhythms in their small place in Queens. It was
a Tuesday evening in March, and a neighbor's complaint about noise—a misunderstanding, later
clarified—had brought two NYPD officers to their door. What followed was a confrontation that
escalated without warning. One officer, frustrated by what he perceived as Arjun's lack of
cooperation, shoved him hard against the kitchen counter. Arjun's chest struck the edge. He
remembered the cold granite pressing into his ribs, the officer's hand at his shoulder—firm,
controlling—and the smell of the officer's cologne, sharp and chemical, mixed with the stale coffee
on his breath. The officer's partner stood between him and Priya, blocking her view. Within minutes
it was over, apologies issued, a misunderstanding acknowledged. No charges. No injury that required
a hospital. But something had fractured inside Arjun that he could not articulate to Priya, who kept
asking if he was all right, if they should call someone. He said he was fine. He was not fine.

Three years later, Arjun worked as an editor at a mid-sized publishing house, a job he had held
since just after the incident. He was competent at it—attentive to language, patient with
writers—but somewhere in the past year, the work had begun to feel hollow. He moved through his days
mechanically, approving manuscripts, attending meetings, eating lunch at his desk. Nothing felt like
it mattered anymore. When he tried to explain this to Priya one evening, she asked if something had
happened at work, if someone had upset him. He couldn't articulate it. How could he tell her that he
felt like a failure, that his own body had betrayed him that night, that he had been unable to
protect her, unable to stand up to the officer without fear coursing through him? The thought that
he was a poor coper, a man who folded under pressure, had become so familiar it felt like truth.

The memories had begun to intrude without warning. Not always the full sequence—sometimes just
fragments. The pressure of the granite counter against his sternum would return to his body as he
sat at his desk, and his chest would tighten as if the officer's hand were still there. Other times,
a particular temperature would trigger it: the cold of a metal chair in a meeting room, or the cool
touch of a doorknob on a winter morning, and suddenly his body would remember itself pressed against
that counter, and his mind would flood with the smell of that officer's cologne. These sensory
threads pulled him back into the moment so vividly that he would lose minutes, sitting frozen while
his colleagues moved around him.

But what troubled Arjun most was his wife's presence. Priya would come home from work, and he would
feel a strange dread settle over him. He found himself making excuses to leave the apartment when
she was there alone with him, or staying late at the office, telling her he had edits to finish. The
shame of this was immense. He loved her—he knew he did—but being alone with her now felt dangerous
in a way he couldn't name. Perhaps it was that she had witnessed his powerlessness, or that her
presence reminded him of the officer's partner blocking her view, or that some part of him feared
his own body, his own reactions, in her presence. He began to avoid being in a room with her without
a reason to move, without an exit. He would suggest they watch television in separate rooms. He
would volunteer for the late shift at work whenever she was scheduled to be home.

The more he avoided, the more the memories seemed to hunt him. Each time he managed to escape a
moment with Priya, he felt a brief relief, but it was followed by a crushing certainty that he had
failed again—that a good man, a real man, would not need to flee his own wife's company. This
belief, that he was fundamentally broken, that nothing good could come to him anymore because he was
the kind of person who crumbled under pressure, became the lens through which he interpreted
everything. When he caught a whiff of something that reminded him of that officer's cologne—a
stranger's aftershave on the subway—the memory would crash into him, and in its wake, the old
conviction would return: he was a lousy coper, he had lost everything that mattered, the future held
nothing but more of this. And then, once that belief had taken hold, the triggers seemed to
multiply. The cold of his desk chair became unbearable. The sound of Priya's key in the lock would
send his nervous system into alert. He began to dread coming home.

It was Priya who finally insisted he see someone. She had noticed his absences, his distance, the
way he would tense when she touched him. She said, quietly, that she felt like she was losing him,
and that whatever had happened three years ago, it was still happening now. Arjun, sitting across
from her in their living room—the room he had come to associate with dread and failure—realized she
was right. He was trapped in a loop he could not break: the memories pulled him toward the belief
that he was broken, the belief made him hypervigilant to triggers, the triggers sent him spiraling
back into memory. He was exhausted. He was afraid that seeking help would confirm what he already
believed—that he was weak, that he could not recover, that nothing good awaited him. But he was more
afraid of losing Priya. So he made an appointment. He came to his first session carrying the weight
of three years of silence, uncertain whether a therapist could help someone like him.
