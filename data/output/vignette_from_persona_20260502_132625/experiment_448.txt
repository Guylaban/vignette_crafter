# Clinical Vignette: Mariana

Mariana was thirty-four when she first walked through the intake door, her uniform still pressed
from her shift at the state correctional facility. She had been a prison guard for seven years—a job
she had chosen deliberately, she said, because she wanted to never be powerless again. That impulse,
and what it had cost her, was why she was here.

Twenty-three years earlier, when Mariana was eleven, her family had fled El Salvador in the back of
a truck with forty others. Her mother had paid a man she did not know, and for three days they had
been locked in near-total darkness with strangers, their bodies pressed together in the heat. The
man who held the key—who came twice a day to distribute water and once to beat a man who tried to
kick the door—smelled of aguardiente, that sharp, fermented reek that filled the small space and
made Mariana's throat close. He shouted orders in a voice that echoed off metal, telling people
where to sit, when to be silent, what would happen if they tried to leave. He wore a uniform of
sorts, a vest with pockets, and he carried authority the way some men carry weapons. When the truck
finally stopped and the doors opened to blinding light, Mariana had been too dehydrated to cry.

She had built a life in the years after—school, marriage to Roberto, a home in a safe neighborhood.
But the safety was always conditional, always fragile in her mind. When she became a prison guard,
she thought she was choosing to stand on the side of order, of control. Instead, she had walked
directly into a landscape of triggers that her nervous system could not distinguish from the truck.

The uniform was the first problem. Every morning when Mariana put on her own guard's uniform, she
felt a small jolt of wrongness, as if she were both the guard and the prisoner simultaneously. But
it was when she was in the facility itself—when she encountered inmates in their orange jumpsuits,
when she heard the jangle of keys and the slam of doors—that the past became present. The smell of
the prison, a mix of industrial cleaner and sweat and something she could not name, sometimes
carried a phantom note of alcohol that sent her heart into her throat. When a supervisor gave her an
order—"Check the east corridor," "Secure that gate"—something in the tone and structure of the
command would collapse time. She was being told what to do by someone in authority, and the old
certainty that compliance meant survival would grip her chest. She had come to believe that the
world was fundamentally dangerous, that people in positions of power—even her own colleagues, even
the system she worked for—could not be trusted to be what they seemed. Authority figures were not
protectors; they were potential threats wearing a different costume.

These encounters at work triggered a cascade that had become her daily rhythm. When she heard a
command or saw someone in uniform, her body would react as if she were in immediate danger. A
colleague walking past too quickly would startle her, and she would flinch as though a blow were
coming. These false alarms—moments where her nervous system screamed danger at an empty hallway or a
routine interaction—would leave her shaken and searching for control. She had begun to dwell
obsessively on how the original escape could have been prevented: if her mother had waited longer,
if they had gone a different route, if the man with the key had never existed. This prevention
rumination became a way of asserting mastery over a past that had none. She would lie awake at night
turning it over, as though the right combination of regret and planning could undo what had
happened.

To manage the intensity of her shifts, Mariana had developed a pattern of sensory distraction.
Before work, she would put on loud music in the car—reggaeton, cumbia, anything that filled the
space where her thoughts would otherwise go. During her lunch break, she would sit in her truck with
the radio or a podcast blaring, her hands gripping the steering wheel. When she got home, the
television went on immediately, and she moved through the house with it following her from room to
room, a constant wash of noise that kept the quiet—and the memories—at bay. But the distraction was
incomplete. The memories were vivid and intrusive, often triggered by the smallest sensory detail.
She would hear, in her mind's ear, the exact pitch of the man's voice shouting orders, the sound of
his footsteps on the truck bed, the crying of a child she had never seen again. The memories came
with such overwhelming emotion that she would have to stop whatever she was doing—stop mid-
conversation with Roberto, stop driving, stop thinking—until the wave passed. And each time a memory
surfaced, it reinforced her conviction that she could not trust the world, that people were not what
they seemed, that she had fundamentally lost the person she used to be.

The avoidance had grown more elaborate. She positioned herself strategically in every room, always
choosing a seat with a clear view of exits and the fewest people behind her—a habit she could not
break even at family dinners, where Roberto had learned not to ask why she moved chairs. She began
to avoid certain parts of the prison facility, which created tension with her supervisors and made
her job harder. She avoided social situations where she might encounter people in any kind of
uniform—police officers, security guards, even the man who worked at the bank. These avoidances had
begun to isolate her. Roberto had stopped inviting friends over. Her mother, who lived an hour away,
had stopped calling as often because Mariana's anxiety made their conversations difficult. The small
world she had built was shrinking.

What finally brought her to treatment was a moment at work last month. She had been assigned to
escort a new inmate to the infirmary, and something about his gait, the way he moved through space,
had triggered a startle response so intense that she had grabbed his arm and twisted it, nearly
breaking it. The inmate had not resisted. He had simply looked at her with confusion and fear, and
in that moment, Mariana had seen herself from the outside: she was the person with power now, the
person causing fear, the person the system had placed in a position to harm. She had gone home and
told Roberto that she could not do this anymore—not the job, not the constant vigilance, not the way
her own past was living in her body and making her dangerous to others. She was terrified that she
was becoming the man with the key, that the trauma had remade her into something she could never
undo.
