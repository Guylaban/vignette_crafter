# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was forty-eight when the warehouse fire took everything. He had been working as a substance
abuse counselor for nearly two decades, a job he loved—one that gave him purpose, that let him sit
across from people at their worst and say, *I understand*, and mean it. The warehouse was being used
as a temporary shelter for unhoused clients while renovations continued at the main facility. Marcus
had volunteered to stay late that evening, helping a young woman named Keisha work through a relapse
urge. The smell of the place was familiar: industrial cleaning solution, old concrete, the
particular staleness of a space too many people had slept in. Around eleven, someone—they were never
certain who—had left a space heater too close to a storage area packed with old mattresses and
donated clothing.

The fire moved faster than anyone expected. Marcus was on the second floor when he heard the first
alarm. He got Keisha out, then went back. He was certain there were people still inside, though
afterward the investigators said the building had been cleared. He didn't remember much of the next
minutes—the smoke so thick he couldn't see his own hands, the heat pressing down, a sound like
nothing he'd ever heard before. He was pulled out by firefighters, barely conscious. Three people
did die that night. Marcus was not one of them, but he had survived something he believed he should
not have, and that knowledge never quite left him.

In the months that followed, Marcus noticed his body had become unreliable. A car backfiring would
send his heart into a gallop. The smell of burnt toast in the break room at work would freeze him
mid-sentence. He became hyperaware that his body was responding as if danger were still present, as
if the warehouse were still burning around him. He had come to believe that this vigilance, this
constant readiness, was the only thing keeping him safe—that if he let his guard down for even a
moment, something catastrophic would happen. He could not afford to relax. The world was
fundamentally unsafe, and only his exhausting, unceasing watchfulness stood between him and
disaster.

When someone threw a pen across the room during a staff meeting—a frustrated gesture, nothing
more—Marcus flinched so violently that everyone went quiet. After that, he began avoiding his
colleagues. He stopped attending the weekly team lunches. He made excuses when friends called. The
isolation seemed necessary; being around people meant being around unpredictability, and
unpredictability meant danger. But the avoidance came with a cost. His supervisor asked if something
was wrong. His best friend of fifteen years stopped reaching out. Marcus told himself this was
acceptable, a small price for safety, though the loneliness accumulated like scar tissue.

The memories themselves had become strange and intrusive. Sometimes a burnt smell—a neighbor's
barbecue, a restaurant kitchen—would trigger not just fear but a full sensory replay: he could smell
the acrid smoke again, feel the heat on his face, taste ash. Other times, the emotional intensity of
the event would flood through him without any image at all, a wave of terror and helplessness that
seemed to come from nowhere, leaving him gasping and uncertain whether he was actually in danger.
And most disturbing was the sense that these memories didn't quite belong to him. When he tried to
think about what happened, it felt like he was remembering someone else's experience, watching
himself from outside, which only deepened his fear that something fundamental had broken inside him.

He tried not to think about the fire. But trying not to think about it meant monitoring his own
thoughts constantly, watching for any sign that he was thinking about it, which meant he was always,
in a sense, thinking about it. He was terrified that if he allowed himself to really contemplate
what had happened—the weight of it, the randomness, the lives lost—he would not be able to handle
it. His mind would fracture. He would lose the thread entirely. So he pushed the thoughts away
whenever they surfaced, but the pushing itself became exhausting, and the triggers kept pulling him
back: the smell of smoke, the sight of someone throwing something, the simple fact of existing in a
world where fires happened and people died and his own body could not be trusted to know the
difference between then and now.

It was his supervisor who finally insisted. After Marcus made an error in a client session—losing
his temper when a client raised his voice, something he would never have done before—she said he
needed to talk to someone. Marcus resisted for weeks. He was the counselor, not the one who needed
counseling. But the isolation was deepening, his sleep was fragmenting, and the constant monitoring
of his own thoughts had become so consuming that he could barely focus on his clients. At fifty-
five, Marcus came to his first appointment with a therapist, carrying the weight of seven years of
hypervigilance, the loneliness of self-imposed exile, and a terror that the fire had fundamentally
changed who he was—that the man he'd been before was someone else entirely, and that man was never
coming back.
