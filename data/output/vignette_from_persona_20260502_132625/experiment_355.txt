# Amara

Amara was twenty-one when the convoy took fire outside Kandahar. She had enlisted at eighteen,
driven by a fierce need to prove something—to her family, to herself—and she'd thrown herself into
the work with the kind of intensity that made her stand out. She was a combat medic, the person
soldiers called when someone was bleeding out, when seconds mattered. She was good at it. She was
*certain* she was good at it, until the moment the RPG hit the lead vehicle and everything fractured
into noise and smoke and the weight of a man's body in her arms who wouldn't stop bleeding no matter
what she did.

The sound came first—a screech that split the air, then the percussion of the explosion, then the
screaming. Amara had trained for this, had run the scenarios, but training lived in a different
world than the actual heat of the metal, the smell of burning fuel, the way the soldier's eyes went
distant even as his mouth kept moving. His name was Marcus. She would remember his name. She had
applied pressure to the wound in his abdomen, calling for supplies, for backup, for anyone, but the
bleeding wouldn't stop and his breathing changed and then it didn't, and by the time the medevac
arrived, she was holding a body.

Back home in Maryland, in the months after she returned, Amara told people she was fine. She had
completed her service, been honorably discharged, and she'd enrolled in a counseling program—she
wanted to help people process trauma, she said, which was partly true and partly a way of not
thinking about her own. She moved into a small apartment near campus and started her practicum at a
community mental health center, working with clients who had experienced everything from domestic
violence to addiction. The work felt purposeful, like she was finally doing something that mattered,
until the day her supervisor mentioned that they were thinking about adding a trauma-focused unit
and asked if she'd be interested in specializing in combat-related PTSD.

Amara had said no immediately. The word came out before she'd even thought about it, and in that
moment of refusal, something shifted. She began to notice how often her triggers seemed to find her,
as if the world had learned exactly where to press. A client's comment about her own
family—something casual, a mention of a brother who'd disappointed her—sent Amara spiraling into a
conviction that there was something intrinsically wrong with her, something that had made the convoy
a target, that had made her the one who survived when Marcus didn't. If someone else had been the
medic that day, she was certain, they would have known what to do. They wouldn't have frozen,
wouldn't have failed. The thought became a loop she couldn't escape.

When her roommate suggested they go to a movie on a Friday night, Amara declined. She couldn't sit
in a dark theater—the darkness itself had become unbearable, a space where her mind filled in the
blanks with sounds she couldn't unhear. The sound of a helicopter passing overhead near campus made
her chest tighten so badly she'd had to pull over while driving; she couldn't imagine being trapped
in a dark room when that sound found her. She began avoiding the people she'd known before
deployment, the friends who wanted to hear stories or who looked at her with a mixture of admiration
and pity she couldn't bear. She stopped answering their texts. She stopped going to the coffee shop
where she used to study. The avoidance felt necessary, like a way of protecting them from her, from
whatever it was about her that had brought catastrophe into that convoy.

But the more Amara withdrew, the more the conviction hardened: she was damaged in a way that made
her unsuitable for the very work she'd come home to do. She was no longer the person who could sit
with suffering and help others metabolize it—she was the person who *caused* suffering, who'd let
someone die. When she forced herself to attend a department meeting at the counseling center and a
colleague mentioned a training on combat trauma, Amara felt the familiar spike of panic and the
certainty that everyone in that room could see through her, could sense her fundamental inadequacy.
She made an excuse and left early, driving home in silence, avoiding the radio because even music
felt dangerous now, a space where the wrong sound might ambush her.

It was her supervisor who finally named what was happening, gently suggesting that Amara might
benefit from seeking treatment herself. Amara had resisted the idea for weeks, convinced that
talking about it would only prove what she already knew—that she was broken, that she'd never be
capable of the work she'd imagined for herself. But the isolation was becoming unbearable, the
weight of avoiding everyone and everything too much to carry alone. At twenty-one, she found herself
sitting in a therapist's office, terrified that naming what happened would confirm her worst fear:
that the fault had been hers all along, and that nothing could fix that.
