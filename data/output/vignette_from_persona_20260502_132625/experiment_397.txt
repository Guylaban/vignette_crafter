# Linh's Vigilance

Linh was twenty-three when she was taken. She had been working late at the tax consulting firm
downtown, finishing quarterly filings for a client, when the man came through the back stairwell—the
one no one used after hours. She remembered the door opening silently, the way his shadow fell
across her desk before she even registered the sound. He had a knife. He was calm about it,
methodical. He kept her in a storage unit for four days.

The police found her because a neighbor heard something. They arrested him within a week. At his
trial, eighteen months later, he pleaded guilty to kidnapping and assault. The judge sentenced him
to twelve years. Linh sat in the courtroom and felt nothing—not relief, not closure, just a hollow
sense that the sentence was already being served by her, not him. He would be out in eight with good
behavior. She knew this because she had looked it up obsessively, as if the math might change. It
never did.

By the time she returned to work six months after her release, Linh had already begun to reorganize
her entire life around safety. She had moved apartments three times, each time choosing a unit on a
higher floor, each time positioning her desk so her back pressed against the wall. At the office,
she arrived before dawn and stayed until the cleaning crew left, timing her presence to coincide
with other people in the building. Her colleagues noticed she no longer used the back stairs. When
asked why, she said nothing. She had come to believe that her own carelessness—staying late alone,
trusting the building's security—had created the conditions for what happened. If she had been more
careful, more aware, more afraid from the start, she would not have been taken. The thought was
relentless: she had acted in a way that invited danger, and now she had to live as though danger was
everywhere.

The hypervigilance crystallized into a physical habit. Linh could not sit in a meeting without
positioning herself so she could see the door. When a colleague walked past her desk unexpectedly,
her body would jolt—her hands would fly up, her breath would catch—and she would feel the familiar
rush of terror, as though the man in the stairwell had returned. These reactions felt like proof
that she was right to be afraid, that her body knew something her rational mind kept trying to deny.
At night, she lay awake listening for sounds—the scrape of a key in a lock, footsteps in the
hallway, the whisper of the door opening. She would lie still, muscles tensed, waiting to confirm
what she already knew: that she had not learned her lesson well enough, that she was still not
vigilant enough, that something bad was coming because she had invited it through her own
negligence.

What frightened her most was the thought that he would be released and come back. But underneath
that fear was a deeper one: that if he did, it would be because she had made another mistake, had
failed again to be sufficiently afraid. She had begun to blame herself not just for the kidnapping,
but for the fact that he faced so little real consequence. A twelve-year sentence felt like an
insult, a sign that the system did not believe what had happened was serious, which made her wonder
if she was overreacting, if her fear was disproportionate. But then a sound would startle her—a door
slamming in the office, a car alarm outside her apartment—and the fear would spike again, validating
her vigilance, reinforcing the cycle. She was caught between the conviction that she had caused this
through her own carelessness and the terror that she could not prevent it from happening again.

Linh came to her first therapy appointment because her supervisor had expressed concern. She had
missed a deadline—something that had never happened before—because she could not concentrate. She
sat in the waiting room with her back to the corner, watching the door. When the therapist called
her name, Linh startled visibly. She was exhausted. She wanted to feel safe again, but she was not
sure that was possible. More than that, she was not sure she deserved to.
