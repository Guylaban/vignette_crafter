# Marija

Marija was forty-eight when the assault happened, though she looked older now, at fifty-four. She
had been working late at her restaurant in Portland, a small high-end place where she spent most
evenings in the kitchen, moving between stations with the kind of efficient grace that came from
thirty years of cooking. It was a Tuesday in March, cool and dark by seven o'clock. She was alone,
finishing prep for the next day—her sous chef had left an hour earlier—when a man she recognized
vaguely from the neighborhood came through the back door. He had been a regular customer, nothing
remarkable about him, and that was the worst part. She had trusted the ordinariness of his face. He
locked the door behind him, and what followed was a blur of violence and violation that her mind had
refused to stitch into a coherent narrative. She remembered his weight on her, the cold edge of the
stainless steel counter against her back, the taste of blood. But the moments would not hold
together. The assault lasted perhaps fifteen minutes, though time had fractured into fragments that
refused to sequence. When he left, she sat on the kitchen floor for two hours before she could move.

The police came. There were examinations and statements, and eventually a trial where she had to sit
across from him in a courtroom and try to explain to a jury what she could not fully explain to
herself. He was convicted. That should have been the end, but Marija had come to understand that the
assault had not ended; it had simply transformed into something that lived inside her body and mind,
something that reordered everything she thought she knew about safety and trust.

Within weeks of the assault, certain conversations became unbearable. When her husband Marco tried
to talk about what had happened, when friends offered sympathy, when anyone broached the subject of
trust or betrayal or what had been taken from her, Marija felt something sharp and terrifying
activate in her chest. The words themselves were triggers, and they sent her mind fragmenting back
to that kitchen, but not in a way she could articulate. She would find herself frozen, her body
locked in the position she had been pinned in, unable to move or speak. It was as though her nervous
system knew something her conscious mind could not yet organize into language—that talking about the
assault meant reliving it in pieces, without the mercy of a coherent story. The fragmentation of her
memory made the experience worse. She could not tell Marco what had happened in a way that made
sense, because it did not make sense inside her own mind. The disconnected images and sensations
came without narrative thread, and when she tried to speak them aloud, they scattered further,
leaving her more trapped than before. Certain topics—discussions about people she had trusted, about
vulnerability, about what makes a person safe—became landmines. She began steering conversations
away from anything that might touch on betrayal or safety. She asked Marco not to mention the
assault. She found herself unable to sit with close friends who wanted to offer emotional support,
because their closeness, their attempts at intimacy, triggered the same fragmentary flooding.

What had started as avoidance of conversation became avoidance of connection itself. When Marco
tried to hold her, she would tense. When her sister came to visit and wanted to sit close and talk,
Marija felt panic rising in her throat. She had come to believe, with a certainty that felt
absolute, that she had been betrayed by someone she should have been able to trust, and this belief
metastasized into a broader conviction: people could not be trusted. You could never know who would
harm you. A regular customer, a familiar face, a man who had walked into her restaurant dozens of
times—and yet he had become a weapon. This knowledge had rewritten her understanding of the world.
Strangers now felt inherently dangerous. A man approaching her in the grocery store, even at a
distance, sent her into high alert. Her body would stiffen; her breath would shallow. She was
hypervigilant in a way that exhausted her, scanning every environment for threat, every unfamiliar
man for the potential to harm. She could not sit in a restaurant without tracking the exits and the
location of every male customer. At her own restaurant, where she had once moved with confidence and
joy, she now felt exposed and hunted.

The hypervigilance fed her negative appraisals. Because she was always watching for danger, she
found danger everywhere. Every unexpected movement nearby—a delivery driver rounding a corner, a
customer dropping a pan—made her startle violently, her body reacting as if she were still in
immediate peril. And each startle, each moment of terror over something innocuous, reinforced her
conviction that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that threat was everywhere. This certainty then
shaped what triggered her. She began to feel unsafe in the presence of any man, even those she had
known for years. Being alone with Marco became difficult. She could not be in a closed room with her
brother without her nervous system going into overdrive. The presence of a man—even a safe man, even
her husband—triggered the fragmented memories and the bodily reenactment. Her body would position
itself defensively, her muscles would lock, and she would be back on that kitchen floor, pinned and
helpless.

The memories themselves had become a source of threat. When a fragmented piece surfaced—a sensation,
a visual flash, a smell—her nervous system treated it as a present danger. Her threat system,
already hyperactive, would surge in response to these internal signals, and the surge would pull
more memories forward, more disconnected pieces that her mind could not integrate. She had tried to
talk about it in therapy, but the act of speaking triggered more memories, which intensified her
conviction that the world was dangerous and people could not be trusted. She had become trapped in a
loop that tightened each week. Her avoidance of conversation kept her isolated, which deepened her
mistrust. Her hypervigilance kept her in a state of constant threat detection, which fed the
fragmented memories, which confirmed her belief that danger was real and imminent.

At work, the isolation had become visible. She was still a brilliant chef, but she had become
difficult to work with. She could not tolerate her staff standing too close. She had begun
scheduling her work for early morning hours when fewer people were around. She had asked for the
back door to be reinforced and alarmed. Two of her longest-serving sous chefs had left, unsettled by
her constant vigilance and her sharp reactions to minor mistakes. Marco had tried for months to
reach her, but her inability to tolerate his closeness, her flinching when he came near, had created
a distance between them that felt unbridgeable. He did not understand why she could not simply move
past it, and his confusion felt like another betrayal, though she knew, somewhere deep, that it was
not. She had gained weight from stress and inactivity. She had stopped seeing friends. She had begun
to drink wine alone in the evenings, not heavily, but regularly, a way to dull the constant
vigilance that was exhausting her.

It was Marco who finally insisted she seek help. He had found her one morning sitting on the kitchen
floor at home, her body locked in that familiar position, tears streaming down her face, triggered
by nothing more than the way the morning light had hit the stainless steel sink. She could not tell
him what had happened or why she was on the floor. The words would not come. She only knew that
something inside her had broken into pieces that would not reassemble, and she was terrified that
those pieces might never fit back together again. What frightened her most was not just the assault
itself, but the possibility that she had become permanently fractured, that she would never be able
to trust anyone again, that the person she had been before that Tuesday in March was gone. She had
called a therapist the next day, hoping that someone could help her put the pieces back together, or
at least help her understand why her mind and body had decided that safety was an illusion and that
threat was the only reliable truth.
