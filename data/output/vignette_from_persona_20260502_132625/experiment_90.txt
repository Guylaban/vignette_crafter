# Miguel

Miguel was twenty-three when the men came. He had been working at a construction site outside San
Salvador, saving money to bring his wife Rosa to the States, when a van pulled up and three
uniformed officers dragged him into it. What happened over the next eighteen hours—the electrical
cords, the questions about people he didn't know, the blows that came whether he answered or stayed
silent—had left him with a scar along his ribs and a conviction that the world was fundamentally
unsafe. He had escaped when a guard's attention wavered, and three years later, having rebuilt his
life in Phoenix with a new job and a fresh start, he believed he had moved past it. Then the divorce
papers came, and with them, a kind of unraveling he had not anticipated.

At twenty-six, Miguel now worked as a maid at a mid-range hotel, pushing a cart through corridors
that smelled of bleach and old carpet. The job was steady, the pay modest but reliable, and it kept
him occupied—or so he had thought. Everything changed the morning a silver sedan pulled into the
parking lot, its shape and color too close to the van that had taken him. Miguel felt his chest
tighten as he watched it from the laundry room window. That afternoon, unable to stop thinking about
it, he found himself scrolling through news articles about kidnappings and police brutality in
Central America, something he had trained himself not to do. The media content pulled at him—each
video, each headline—and he consumed it compulsively, as if understanding the mechanisms of violence
might somehow protect him from it. By evening, he was smoking on his apartment balcony, a habit he
had quit two years ago, his hands shaking as he lit one cigarette after another.

The triggers multiplied after that. Similar cars in traffic made his jaw clench. A guest at the
hotel complained about a stain on her sheets, and when her tone sharpened, Miguel felt the old panic
rising—the sensation of being accused of something he could not control, could not explain away. He
began to avoid reminders of the event deliberately: he stopped watching the news, asked coworkers
not to discuss politics or crime, and felt a spike of dread whenever someone mentioned his past or
asked casual questions about where he was from. But the avoidance itself seemed to amplify his
vigilance. By avoiding conversations about the trauma, he found himself scanning strangers' faces
more intently, reading threat into neutral expressions. An elderly man approached him in the hotel
lobby asking for directions, and Miguel's heart raced; he could not explain why, exactly, but the
stranger's slow approach felt predatory.

His nights became a cycle of fragmentation. He would wake at two or three in the morning, his body
rigid, and creep through his apartment checking locks, ensuring windows were secure. The
hypervigilance was exhausting but felt necessary—a price he paid for safety. When Rosa had still
been around, her touch on his shoulder in the dark would have startled him into a defensive posture;
she had learned not to wake him. Now, alone, even the unexpected sound of the ice maker would send
adrenaline flooding through him. One evening, a coworker placed a hand on his back to get past him
in the supply closet, and Miguel spun around with such force that he knocked over a shelf of
cleaning bottles. The startle, the shame that followed, the certainty that his body was betraying
him—all of it fed back into his sense that danger was everywhere.

The threat monitoring had become so consuming that it began to reshape his avoidance strategies. He
stopped taking the bus to work, fearing the enclosed space and the strangers pressed around him,
which meant he now spent money on rideshare apps he could barely afford. He avoided the hotel's
break room when police officers came through for a training seminar, staying in the laundry room for
hours, smoking cigarette after cigarette to manage the anxiety of their proximity. The smoking
itself had become a ritual, a way to create distance between himself and the world, and it had begun
to worry him—he was up to a pack a day, sometimes more. Yet every time he tried to stop, the urge to
smoke returned with such force that it felt inseparable from his need to manage the constant low-
level terror.

What brought Miguel to the clinic was not a single crisis but an accumulation: the isolation, the
financial strain of avoiding situations, the knowledge that his body no longer felt like his own. He
had been divorced for fourteen months, had no close friends in Phoenix, and had begun calling in
sick to work more frequently. When the intake therapist asked what he most feared, he said quietly:
"That I'll never feel normal again. That I'm going to lose this job too, and then there's nothing
left."
