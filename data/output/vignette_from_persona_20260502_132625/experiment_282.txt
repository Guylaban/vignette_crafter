# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was sixty-eight when the assault happened, a detail that still bothered him in ways he
couldn't quite articulate. He had been working a sales event at the convention center downtown—the
Riverside Convention Center, where he'd worked dozens of times over thirty years without incident.
He'd stepped outside during a break, needing air, and a man had approached him in the parking garage
asking for directions. Marcus, always helpful, always the kind of person who stopped to assist, had
turned to point the way. The man had hit him hard across the face, then again, and Marcus had fallen
backward against a concrete pillar. His attacker had taken his wallet and fled. The whole thing had
lasted perhaps ninety seconds, but it had shattered something in Marcus that no amount of time
seemed to repair.

What haunted him most wasn't the physical injury—the bruised ribs, the chipped tooth he'd had
capped—but the feeling that he had somehow invited it. He replayed the moment constantly, turning it
over in his mind like a stone worn smooth by obsessive handling. If he'd been less trusting, more
alert, if he'd stayed inside where it was safe, if he'd read the man's intentions correctly, none of
it would have happened. These unwanted thoughts arrived without warning, ambushing him at his desk
or in the car between appointments. He'd be reviewing a sales report and suddenly he was back in
that garage, seeing the man's face, hearing the impact of fist against bone. The thoughts felt like
an accusation: *you should have known better*. And from there, the deeper conviction settled in—that
there was something fundamentally broken about him, something that made him vulnerable in a way
other men weren't. The person he had been, the confident salesman who could read a room and close a
deal, felt like a stranger now. Who was this seventy-year-old man who flinched at unexpected sounds
and couldn't trust his own judgment?

When he encountered reminders of the assault, Marcus responded with swift, practiced avoidance. He
stopped going near the Riverside Convention Center entirely, rerouting his sales territory to avoid
even the street that led to it. His company had begun to notice—his territory coverage had shrunk,
his numbers had dipped—but he couldn't bring himself to explain why. The smell of alcohol became
another trigger. His attacker had reeked of it, and now the scent of whiskey or beer at a
restaurant, even cologne that carried a sharp, boozy note, sent him spiraling. He would excuse
himself from dinners, leave client meetings early if someone had been drinking, always with a vague
excuse about not feeling well. When unwanted thoughts about the assault surfaced, Marcus had learned
to suppress them aggressively, trying hard not to think about what had happened, pushing the memory
down with all the mental force he could muster. But the suppression never held for long. The
thoughts would return, and with them came the conviction that his attempts to control his mind only
proved how damaged he truly was. His wife, Patricia, had noticed the change in him long ago. He'd
become emotionally distant, numb to things that once brought him pleasure. She'd suggest a dinner
out, and he'd agree but spend the evening withdrawn, barely tasting the food. When she tried to talk
about what happened, Marcus would shut down, unwilling or unable to engage. He couldn't explain that
opening that door felt like it would crack him entirely.

The trigger of seeing his attacker—a possibility that kept Marcus vigilant and exhausted—had
deepened his conviction that the world was fundamentally unsafe and that he was powerless within it.
When he glimpsed someone who resembled the man in a parking lot six months ago, Marcus had felt his
chest tighten, his hands shake. The resemblance had probably been superficial; the man had walked
past without acknowledgment. But Marcus couldn't shake the certainty that danger was always one
glance away, that his inability to protect himself was written into who he was now. This belief fed
directly back into his avoidance: he began limiting where he went, what events he attended, how much
of the world he would allow himself to occupy. His life had contracted around his fear, and he had
come to see this contraction as evidence of his defectiveness rather than as a symptom of trauma.

It was Patricia who finally insisted he seek help. She had found him one evening sitting in the dark
in their bedroom, unable to articulate why. "You're disappearing," she'd said, and something in her
voice—not anger, but grief—had cracked through his numbness enough to make an appointment. Marcus
arrived at the clinic uncertain whether talking about what happened would help or simply confirm
what he already believed: that he was irreparably changed, that the man he'd been was truly gone,
and that nothing could bring him back.
