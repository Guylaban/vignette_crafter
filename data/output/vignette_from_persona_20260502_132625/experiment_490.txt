# Clinical Vignette: Dr. Viktor Novak

Viktor Novak, a clinical psychologist in his late sixties, had built a reputation over four decades
for his careful attention to his patients' narratives. He had always prided himself on his ability
to sit still, to listen without judgment, to create the conditions in which people could speak their
deepest fears. That capacity, he had come to understand, was now turned against him in ways he could
barely articulate.

The trauma had occurred eight years ago, in the relationship that preceded his current marriage. His
partner—a woman he had believed he knew intimately—had attacked him one evening in their home after
an argument about money. What had begun as raised voices escalated with shocking speed into physical
violence. She had struck him repeatedly, and in the chaos of his own panic and confusion, Viktor had
struggled to comprehend that this was happening, that the person he lived with had become, in that
moment, someone he did not recognize. He had managed to leave the house, but the experience had
fractured something fundamental in how he understood trust, vulnerability, and the capacity of
people to deceive.

In the years since, Viktor had developed an intricate architecture of avoidance and control that he
believed kept him safe. Certain triggers had become impossible to ignore. The sight of a bicycle—his
ex-partner had ridden one regularly—could send him into a state of hypervigilance that lasted hours.
Being alone with any woman, even briefly, activated a sharp wariness; he found himself monitoring
her movements, her tone of voice, the smallest shifts in her expression for signs of aggression.
Crowded spaces had become unbearable; the proximity of unknown bodies, the unpredictability of their
intentions, made him feel cornered. When he encountered these situations—a staff meeting with female
colleagues, a trip to the grocery store, a glimpse of someone on a bicycle through his office
window—Viktor's mind would flood with threat. Each trigger seemed to confirm what he had come to
believe: that people were fundamentally untrustworthy, that he was inadequate in his ability to
judge character, that the world operated according to rules of deception he could no longer
navigate.

To manage this cascade of threat, Viktor had developed rigid strategies that now structured his
entire life. He had eliminated from his wardrobe anything resembling what his ex-partner had
worn—certain colors, certain cuts of fabric. He had become obsessive about emotional control,
maintaining a careful distance from anger, never allowing himself to feel or express it, convinced
that any loss of composure would render him vulnerable. His wife had noticed this; she had gently
asked why he seemed so distant, so contained. He could not explain that the very closeness that
marriage demanded terrified him, that his attempts to suppress his own emotional responses were a
form of armor.

The vigilance had become exhausting. Viktor would wake at night, sometimes multiple times, seized by
the need to verify that his bedroom door was locked, that his wife was still asleep beside him, that
no threat had materialized in the darkness. He found himself watching people in clinical settings
with an intensity that felt almost predatory—not because he harbored aggression himself, but because
he was desperate to see the warning signs before they could harm him. His negative appraisals had
calcified into certainties: people were not what they seemed; his judgment was fundamentally flawed;
he was inadequate to protect himself or discern truth from manipulation.

The fragmentation of memory had compounded his suffering in ways he could not have predicted. He
could not construct a coherent narrative of what had happened—the event remained scattered,
photographic, vivid in its sensory details but disconnected from meaning. Sometimes he would be
flooded with the raw emotional intensity of the trauma without any accompanying image, just pure
terror and helplessness. This fragmentation meant that the threat never felt resolved or distant; it
remained perpetually present, accessible through emotion alone, impossible to metabolize into a
story with a beginning and an end. The more he tried to control his emotional responses, the more
the fragmented memories would erupt unbidden, and the more he would retreat into hypervigilance and
avoidance. The more he avoided situations that triggered threat, the more his world contracted, and
the more he relied on his maladaptive strategies to survive each day.

After eight years, Viktor had finally sought help. His marriage was strained; his professional life
had become increasingly isolated; he was exhausted by the constant monitoring of threat. What he
feared most was that his fundamental beliefs about human nature were correct—that people could not
be trusted, that his inadequacy was real, that he would never feel safe again. What he hoped for,
though he barely allowed himself to articulate it, was that the fragmented pieces of his memory
might somehow cohere into something he could understand, and that understanding might free him from
the endless cycle of vigilance and control.
