# Khalid's Story

Khalid was thirty-eight when his brother Rami died in a convenience store robbery gone wrong. It
happened at 11:47 p.m. on a Tuesday night—a time Khalid would come to dread with an almost physical
revulsion. Khalid had been working late at the McDonald's on Atlantic Avenue when Rami texted asking
if he wanted coffee before bed. Khalid said yes. He was in the break room when the call came through
from the police. Rami had walked in on two men with a gun. There was shouting, a struggle Khalid
would never fully understand, and then a single shot that echoed through the store and into Khalid's
life forever. By the time Khalid arrived at the hospital, his brother was already gone. The officer
who met him kept saying how quick it was, as if speed could soften the fact of it.

The first weeks after the funeral passed in a fog of practical tasks and numb condolences. But as
autumn turned to winter, something shifted. Khalid began to notice that around 11 p.m., his body
would tense without his conscious permission. His shoulders would bunch up toward his ears. His
palms would sweat. He found himself checking the locks on his apartment door repeatedly, moving from
room to room as if searching for something he couldn't name. By the time the clock approached
midnight, he would be in bed with all the lights off, his phone clutched in his hand, waiting for
the hour to pass. He had come to believe that danger accumulated in the dark hours, that the world
became fundamentally unsafe after the sun went down. This temporal threat felt almost as real as a
physical presence in the room.

The triggers that activated this hypervigilance extended beyond the clock. Any loud noise—a car
backfiring, a door slamming, even the sharp beep of the microwave in the break room at work—would
send a jolt of adrenaline through his chest. His coworkers had started to notice. Last month, when
the manager, Tony, had called out a mistake Khalid made while taking an order, Khalid had flinched
so violently that he'd knocked over a stack of napkin dispensers. The criticism itself wasn't
harsh—just routine correction—but it triggered the same suffocating sense of danger that the loud
noises did. Being corrected had become indistinguishable from being threatened. Khalid couldn't
quite explain why a simple "Hey, you rang that up wrong" felt like an accusation, like proof that he
was failing to protect himself or anyone else. The connection was irrational, he knew, but the fear
was absolute.

This hyperalertness to threat had colonized his entire life. Khalid had narrowed his world to a
handful of locations where he felt even remotely safe: his apartment, his sister's house, and a
small section of the parking lot at work where he could see the exit. Anywhere else—the grocery
store, the bus, a restaurant—felt permeable and dangerous. He had stopped going to the mosque he'd
attended for twenty years because the crowd, the echoing space, the unpredictability of when someone
might speak loudly or move suddenly, all of it felt intolerable. When something took longer than
expected—a delayed bus, a slow checkout line—his mind would immediately leap to catastrophe.
Something bad was happening, or about to. The delay itself became evidence of threat. Three years
had passed since Rami's death, and Khalid's world had only gotten smaller. He'd called his sister
last week and finally admitted he needed help. He was tired of being afraid. He was tired of the way
his body had become a prison, locked into the same moment of terror over and over again.
