# Clinical Vignette: Thien M.

Thien M. is a seventy-seven-year-old Vietnamese-American firefighter, now retired from active duty,
who presented to the clinic in a state of visible exhaustion. His wife of forty-three years, Mai,
accompanied him to the intake session and sat beside him with evident concern, occasionally glancing
at his hands, which trembled slightly as he spoke. Thien had been a civilian living in Saigon in
1973 when American and South Vietnamese forces conducted a bombing campaign in his neighbourhood. He
was twenty-three years old, walking home from the market with his younger sister Linh, when the
first explosion detonated three buildings ahead of them. The force of the blast threw him backward;
he remembers the sound—a roar that seemed to come from inside his own chest—and then the smell of
burning metal and flesh. His sister was killed instantly. The memory of her face, the way her hand
went slack in his grip, the screams of strangers bleeding in the street, remains with him with
photographic clarity. Every detail is preserved: the colour of the dust, the texture of the rubble,
the weight of her body as he tried to lift her. He has never been able to unsee it.

Thien moved to the United States in 1975 and eventually became a firefighter, a profession he has
always described to his family as a way of "helping people, preventing what happened to me from
happening to others." Yet for the past several years, the job has become increasingly unbearable.
The sound of the station's alarm, which triggers an immediate response to emergencies, now sends him
into a state of acute panic. When the alarm sounds, he finds himself unable to think clearly; his
body tenses, his breathing becomes shallow, and the memory of the explosion forces itself into his
mind with such vividness that he cannot distinguish between the present and 1973. The emergency
context—the ambulance sirens wailing through the streets as he responds to calls, the radio chatter,
the urgency—all of it collapses the distance between now and then. He has begun to scan his
surroundings obsessively during these moments, looking for signs of danger, checking and rechecking
the exits, the windows, the positions of other people around him, as though the threat were
immediate and present rather than a memory.

What makes the situation worse is that Thien has come to believe something is fundamentally wrong
with him. He is certain that another man—a stronger man, a better man—would have protected his
sister, would not have been thrown down by the blast, would have known what to do. This thought
torments him. When the ambulance sirens sound outside the fire station, triggering the memory and
the panic, he interprets his own fear as weakness, as proof that he has failed some essential test
of character. He feels less than human in these moments, reduced to a trembling animal responding to
threats that no longer exist. These dark interpretations feed his conviction that if he allows
himself to think deeply about what happened, he will collapse entirely, will lose his grip on
sanity. So he avoids thinking about it, pushes the memory away, but the more he struggles against
it, the more forcefully it intrudes.

Sleep has become a battleground. Thien avoids lying in the dark, afraid that the moment his eyes
close, the memory will overtake him completely. He spends most nights in his chair in the living
room, the television on low volume, refusing Mai's gentle suggestions that he come to bed. When
exhaustion finally forces sleep upon him, the nightmare comes: he is back on that street, and this
time he cannot save Linh. He wakes gasping, and the distinction between the dream and the original
event blurs so completely that for several seconds he does not know which one he is living in. The
memory feels foreign to him, as though it belongs to someone else, as though he is watching a film
of another man's tragedy rather than reliving his own. Yet it is his tragedy, and it will not leave
him alone.

Over the months, Thien began to fantasize about revenge—not realistic plans, but vivid imaginings of
confronting the officers who ordered the bombing, of making them understand the cost of what they
did. These fantasies offered a temporary sense of control, a brief reprieve from the helplessness
that defines his waking hours. But they too have become intrusive, another way the past colonizes
the present. He retired from the fire department last year, but the respite has not brought relief.
The sirens of passing ambulances still trigger the same cascade of memory and panic. His wife
worries that he is disappearing into himself, that the man she married is being slowly consumed by a
ghost of himself. Thien came to the clinic because Mai insisted, because he can no longer sleep,
because he is afraid that he is losing his mind, and because some part of him—the part that once
wanted to help others—still hopes that someone might help him understand why he cannot escape the
past.
