heavy boots on wooden floors, the rough voices shouting in Russian, the crash of furniture being
overturned, the smell of alcohol on breath and sweat—had burned themselves into her nervous system
with the intensity of a brand. She had escaped Prague two months later, eventually making her way to
America, where she had built a careful, controlled life. But the past, she had come to understand,
was not something you could outrun simply by crossing an ocean.

about her experiences—Vera would feel the memory descend like a physical weight. The conversation

the hallway, the soldiers' faces, the narrow darkness of the closet. Those images, when they came,
felt utterly present. She was not remembering 1968; she was living it. The memory had no temporal
boundary. It was happening now, in her living room in Connecticut, while her husband sat beside her
with concern in his eyes.

glass, the heavy breathing of men moving through rooms. Her body would respond as if the danger were

smoldering in a neighbor's yard—would send her into a state of acute vigilance, convinced that
