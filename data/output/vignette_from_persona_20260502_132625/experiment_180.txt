# Marcus

Marcus was thirty-two when the police came for him, though he hadn't done anything wrong. It was a
Tuesday evening in late autumn, and he'd been working late at his design studio in downtown
Portland, finishing mockups for a pharmaceutical client. As he locked up around nine and walked to
his car—a silver Honda Civic parked on a side street—he noticed the unmarked sedan pull up beside
him. Two officers emerged, one with his hand near his belt, and asked him to step away from the
vehicle. They said there had been a robbery three blocks away, that a car matching his description
had been seen fleeing the scene. Marcus, bewildered and compliant, agreed to a search. What followed
was twenty minutes of being patted down on the street, his car torn through, his wallet examined,
his phone requested. A third officer arrived. The questioning grew more aggressive. One of them—a
thick-necked man with a shaved head—stepped closer and said, "You sure you weren't over on Fifth
tonight?" Marcus could feel his heart hammering, could see the officer's hand resting on his duty
belt, the weapon visible in its holster. He kept saying no, kept his hands visible, kept his voice
steady, but something in the officer's posture suggested that the wrong word, the wrong gesture,
could escalate everything. After forty minutes, they let him go. They'd found nothing. They never
called back, never apologized. But Marcus never forgot the feeling of standing on that street with
his hands up, the weight of their suspicion, the proximity of that gun.

The aftermath wasn't immediate. For the first week, Marcus told himself it was over, that he'd done
nothing wrong and the system had simply made a mistake. But sleep became difficult. He'd lie in bed
at night, and his mind would loop back to the moment the officer stepped closer, to the sound of the
belt buckle, to the question of what would have happened if he'd moved wrong. Within two months,
nighttime had become something he dreaded. Waking in the dark—which happened often, as his sleep
fractured—sent a spike of adrenaline through his chest. He'd lie there, disoriented, waiting for his
eyes to adjust, convinced in those first seconds that someone was there, that he was being
approached again. He began leaving lights on. Then he began drinking before bed—a glass of whiskey,
sometimes two—to quiet the hypervigilance enough to sleep. It worked, in the way that things that
don't work often do. He'd wake less, but he'd wake groggy, dependent on the ritual.

Driving became another minefield. Every silver or light-colored sedan made his stomach clench.
Crowded places—the grocery store on Saturday mornings, the MAX train during rush hour—triggered the
same suffocating sense of exposure and scrutiny. He couldn't explain it rationally; he just knew
that being surrounded by people, unable to see what was behind him or to the sides, made him feel
like he was standing on that street again with nowhere to go. To manage these moments, Marcus
developed a pattern of avoidance that slowly reshaped his life. He stopped going to the gym, where
he'd have to change in a locker room among other men. He avoided police uniforms entirely—a logical
response that became paralyzing when he'd see an officer at a coffee shop or on the street, forcing
him to leave or hide his face behind his hands until they passed. The substance use escalated. What
had begun as whiskey to sleep became a daily habit: a beer at lunch, wine with dinner, pills he'd
gotten from a friend to take the edge off during the day. His ex-wife had cited his drinking as one
reason for the divorce, filed two years before the incident, but Marcus understood now that it had
metastasized into something else—a tool, a survival mechanism, the only thing that seemed to
interrupt the cycle of dread that triggered his avoidance, which then left him isolated and
ruminating, which then drove him back to the bottle.

By his thirty-third birthday, Marcus was working from home almost exclusively, telling his boss that
the studio commute had become too much. His design work, once collaborative and energetic, had
become a solitary operation. He'd stopped seeing friends. The drinking had become daily and heavy
enough that he'd started lying about it. What finally brought him in was a moment of clarity that
came in the form of a threat: his boss had called to say that the firm needed him back in the
office, that remote work was no longer an option. Marcus realized, sitting in his darkened apartment
at three in the morning with a glass in his hand, that he had constructed a prison so complete that
even the prospect of returning to normal life felt impossible. He was terrified that he couldn't
stop drinking, more terrified still that if he did, he'd have to face the nights and the crowds and
the uniforms without it. What he wanted most was to feel safe again—in his own skin, in the dark, in
a crowded room—but he had no idea how to get there.
