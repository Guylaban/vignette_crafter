# Amara

Three years ago, Amara was assaulted by someone she had trusted—a colleague from the tutoring center
where she worked. It happened in the storage room on a Tuesday evening when she had stayed late to
organize materials. What she remembers most is not a coherent sequence but fragments: the shock of
being pushed against the shelves, the smell of his cologne mixed with something chemical, the sound
of her own voice saying no, over and over, as though from very far away. The memory does not feel
like something that happened to her. When she tries to recall it—which she tries not to do—it feels
distant, almost cinematic, as if she is watching someone else's trauma unfold behind glass. This
alienation from the event is both a mercy and a curse, because the moment the memory surfaces at
all, it brings with it the same paralyzing terror she felt in that room, a terror so complete that
her body seems to remember even when her mind tries to distance itself from what occurred.

In the weeks after the assault, Amara began to notice that certain sounds could shatter her
carefully maintained composure. Children crying—whether from the neighborhood, or from a student at
the tutoring center—would trigger a cascade of panic that left her trembling and nauseated. The
crying seemed to activate something primal in her, a sense that something terrible was happening
that she could not control or prevent. Uncertainty about when she might encounter these triggers, or
uncertainty in general about what might happen next, produced a similar dread. Even mild physical
sensations—a wave of nausea, a tightness in her chest—became signals that something catastrophic was
imminent. These triggers did not simply startle her; they convinced her that she was fundamentally
broken, that her nervous system had been permanently damaged in a way that could never be repaired.
Each time a child cried in the waiting area while she was tutoring, Amara would feel the weight of
that conviction settle over her: this is who I am now—someone who cannot handle the normal sounds of
the world. The triggers seemed to confirm what she had come to believe about herself: that she was
damaged beyond recovery, that her capacity to cope had been obliterated.

Convinced of her own fragility and terrified of what might happen if she allowed herself to think
about the assault directly, Amara began to construct a life of avoidance. She stopped accepting
invitations from her partner to go out to dinner or to see friends, turning down opportunities for
joy or connection because she had come to believe that if she felt happy, something worse would
happen to punish her. She ruminated endlessly on what she could have done differently that
evening—why had she stayed late, why had she worn that particular outfit, why had she not locked the
door—blaming herself for the assault as though her choices had somehow invited it. This rumination
was exhausting and circular, but it felt safer than the alternative: acknowledging that what
happened was not her fault and that the world was simply not safe. When colleagues or her partner
brought up the assault or asked how she was doing, Amara would withdraw, avoiding the reminder and
the conversation, pulling further inward. The more she ruminated, the more trapped she became in the
belief that she was permanently changed for the worse. The more she blamed herself, the more she
felt that thinking about the event directly would shatter her entirely. The avoidance of reminders
and the avoidance of positive experiences reinforced her conviction that she could not handle even
the smallest upset—that her coping capacity had been erased.

Over months, this cycle tightened. The very strategies she used to protect herself—the rumination,
the blame, the withdrawal—began to reactivate the fragmented memory of the assault, pulling her back
into that storage room with overwhelming force. Each time she ruminated about what she could have
done differently, she found herself caught between the alien quality of the memory—the sense that it
belonged to someone else—and the raw terror that flooded her body when she tried to access it. She
could not put the experience into words, which meant she could not process it, which meant she could
not move past it. The inability to verbalize the trauma made her feel even more damaged, more
isolated, more convinced that no one could truly understand what had happened to her. Her avoidance
of positive events and reminders kept her in a state of constant hypervigilance, waiting for the
next trigger—the next child's cry, the next moment of uncertainty—that would confirm her worst fear:
that she had been permanently destroyed by what happened to her. Six months ago, she requested a
transfer to a tutoring position that did not involve working directly with children, but even this
change brought no relief. The triggers remained, the rumination continued, and the conviction that
she could not cope with anything deepened.

It was only when her partner, exhausted and frightened by her withdrawal, finally insisted that she
seek help that Amara made an appointment. She arrives at the clinic with her shoulders drawn inward,
her voice barely above a whisper, and a conviction that therapy, like everything else, will prove
that she is beyond repair. What she hopes for most—though she is almost afraid to admit it—is that
someone might help her understand why she cannot stop blaming herself, and whether the person she
was before that Tuesday evening might somehow still exist underneath the wreckage.
