# Rosa's Story

Rosa was sixty-one years old and had been driving the number 7 bus route through the city for nearly
thirty years. She knew every pothole on Figueroa Street, every stop where the regulars boarded,
every corner where she had to brake hard. On a Tuesday afternoon in March, a sedan ran a red light
at the intersection of Fifth and Main, broadsiding a minivan that had just cleared the crosswalk.
Rosa was stopped at the light in her bus, three vehicles back. She watched the minivan crumple,
watched a man stumble out of the sedan, watched a woman in a blue dress lying on the asphalt. There
was blood—a dark spreading stain on the pavement that seemed to grow as Rosa stood frozen, unable to
move, unable to call out, unable to do anything but witness.

The woman in the blue dress was Rosa's sister-in-law, Carmen. Carmen died in the hospital four hours
later.

In the weeks that followed, Rosa returned to her route, but something had fractured. The sight of a
red traffic light now sent a spike of dread through her chest—not fear of another accident, but a
crushing certainty that she should have done something, should have warned Carmen somehow, should
have been faster or braver or more present. When she saw blood on the sidewalk—a child with a
scraped knee, a homeless man with a bleeding hand—the thought would crystallize: *I am a coward. I
let her die.* These moments of seeing, of witnessing something that mimicked that terrible
afternoon, would trigger an immediate spiral into self-recrimination so intense that Rosa would grip
the steering wheel until her knuckles blanched, her mind locked on the conviction that she had
failed everyone who depended on her.

The memory itself had become a kind of living thing. When it surfaced—and it surfaced without
warning, at red lights, at the sound of a car horn, at the sight of anyone in a blue garment—it
arrived with a violence that erased the boundary between then and now. Rosa would smell the diesel
exhaust and the metallic tang of blood. She would feel the phantom pressure of her foot on the
brake, hear the screech of tires. Most unsettling was that these moments came not always with clear
images but sometimes as a pure, undiluted emotional avalanche: the full terror and helplessness of
that afternoon pouring through her without a face or scene to anchor it, just the raw sensation of
having failed, of being powerless, of being the kind of person who stands still while others die.
The memory felt present, immediate, as if she were still standing at that intersection, still
frozen, still complicit.

To manage the unbearable weight of these convictions—that she should have protected Carmen, that her
inaction was unforgivable, that her life had become meaningless—Rosa developed rituals. Before each
shift, she would count to seven while touching the steering wheel, then touch her wedding ring seven
times. If she saw a car that resembled the sedan, she would mentally recite a prayer in Spanish, a
bargain with God that if she performed it correctly, no one would be hurt on her route that day. She
believed, with a certainty she could not quite articulate, that these rituals stood between safety
and catastrophe. When she performed them, the crushing guilt would recede slightly, replaced by a
fragile sense of control. When she forgot or performed them imperfectly, the guilt would surge back,
more vicious than before, and the memory would flood in again—that sensory totality, that feeling of
being right there on the asphalt, failing, always failing.

But the rituals demanded more. Rosa found herself unable to tolerate being touched. Her husband,
Miguel, would reach for her hand and she would flinch, seized by the irrational fear that his touch
would somehow break the spell, would cause the next accident. She stopped attending family dinners
because she could not bear the sight of Carmen's empty chair, could not endure the reminders of her
loss. The isolation deepened her conviction that she did not deserve comfort, that she should not be
touched or held or forgiven. Each time she performed a ritual and still felt the weight of her
guilt, each time the memory returned despite her best efforts to contain it through these private
ceremonies, she would sink further into the belief that nothing could save her—not rituals, not
prayer, not time. Her colleagues noticed her becoming withdrawn, saw her white-knuckling the
steering wheel at intersections, heard her muttering under her breath. Two drivers had already asked
if she was all right. She had no answer that made sense.

What brought Rosa to the clinic was a moment of clarity, or perhaps desperation. She had missed her
granddaughter's sixth birthday party because she could not face being around children, around joy,
around anything that felt like she had a right to participate in life. Sitting alone in her kitchen
that evening, she realized she had become a ghost in her own home, performing rituals that no longer
even convinced her, trapped in a memory that felt more real than the present, certain that she was a
failure and a coward and that the world would be better off without her. She called her doctor and
asked for a referral. What she wanted, more than anything, was to stop feeling like she was still
standing at that intersection. What she feared most was that she would never be able to forgive
herself for the person she had been on that terrible afternoon—the person who did nothing while her
sister-in-law bled out on the street.
