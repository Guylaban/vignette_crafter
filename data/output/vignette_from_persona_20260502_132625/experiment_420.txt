# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was forty-three when it happened—a private tutoring session in the home of a wealthy family
in the suburbs, a job he'd held for five years without incident. The father of the household, a man
in his sixties whom Marcus had never been alone with before, had asked him to stay late to discuss
his son's progress. The door closed. What began as conversation shifted into something else
entirely. The man's hand on his shoulder, then his arm, the sudden physical force that made clear
resistance was futile. Marcus had frozen—not from fear alone, but from a kind of dissociative shock,
as though his body had become something happening to someone else. When it was over, he had driven
home in silence, showered until his skin was raw, and told no one.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus returned to his tutoring schedule as though nothing had changed,
but everything had. The clarity of certain moments from that afternoon haunted him—the exact
pressure of the man's grip, the specific timbre of his voice, the texture of the fabric beneath his
fingers. These details returned unbidden, vivid and intrusive, fragmenting his thoughts during
lessons. Yet alongside these crystalline moments existed a strange fog: he could not quite
articulate what had happened, could not find language adequate to the experience, and there were
gaps in his memory—stretches of time he could not fully account for. The incompleteness terrified
him more than the clarity. It suggested his own mind was no longer trustworthy, that his body had
been violated in ways he could not even fully remember.

The violation had shattered something fundamental in Marcus's sense of himself. He came to believe
that his body was no longer his own, that it had betrayed him by freezing, by not fighting hard
enough, by existing in a form that could be taken. He was certain now that the world was far more
dangerous than he had understood, that men—particularly men he did not know well—harbored intentions
he could not predict. Most painfully, he felt profoundly alone with this knowledge, separated from
the ordinary life his colleagues and acquaintances seemed to inhabit. This isolation fed a
hypervigilance that became exhausting: he had to be especially careful because he could never know
what might happen next.

When a student's parent reached out to shake his hand in greeting, Marcus's entire body contracted.
The touch—innocent as it was—sent him back to that closed room, his threat system flaring. His vivid
memories of the assault crystallized in that instant of contact, and he found himself scrutinizing
the man's face for signs of predatory intent, watching for the micro-expressions that might betray
hidden danger. Because he could not trust his body, because he believed he was isolated and
vulnerable, he began to avoid any situation that might involve physical contact. He stopped
attending faculty gatherings. He declined invitations to social events. He sat only in chairs
positioned against walls or corners, where he could see the entire room and no one could approach
from behind. When forced into unfamiliar spaces—a new student's home, a tutoring center he'd never
visited—he would refuse the assignment, citing scheduling conflicts, his anxiety about danger in
unknown environments overriding his professional obligations.

The maladaptive strategies that emerged from this hypervigilance soon calcified into routine. Marcus
began smoking heavily, a way to manage the emotional intensity that threatened to overwhelm him
whenever a trigger appeared. He avoided any situation that might provoke strong feeling—turning down
invitations, limiting conversations to superficial topics, keeping emotional distance even from
people who cared about him. His memories, now fragmented and incomplete, kept pulling him back into
threat assessment. He would replay moments obsessively, searching for the missing pieces, and each
replay rekindled his conviction that men posed a danger, that his body could not protect him, that
he must remain vigilant and isolated. The vivid details and the gaps fed into each other, creating a
loop: the clarity made the forgetting unbearable; the forgetting made him search for clarity; the
searching kept the threat alive.

Now, at fifty-seven, Marcus has withdrawn almost entirely. His tutoring practice has dwindled as he
has become increasingly selective about clients, refusing to work in homes where he might be alone
with adult men, canceling sessions when his anxiety spikes. He smokes a pack a day. He has not been
touched by another person in years. The isolation he feared has become the isolation he has
constructed, brick by brick, as protection. What finally brought him to seek help was not the
symptoms themselves—he had learned to live with them—but the recognition that he was disappearing,
that his life had contracted to the point where he barely recognized himself. He is terrified of
what talking about the assault might unleash, but he is also desperate to reclaim some version of
the person he was before, to understand whether healing is even possible when your own body feels
like enemy territory.
