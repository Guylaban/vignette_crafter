her onto the bus. She got off two stops early, disoriented, certain he was still behind her. He
wasn't. But the uncertainty had lodged itself somewhere deep.

The legal system had failed her in the way she'd come to expect: the man faced no real consequences.
A restraining order was issued, but it felt like a piece of paper, a gesture. He moved away
eventually—or so she'd been told—but Katarina had stopped believing in the finality of such things.
The world, she had come to understand, was not a safe place. People were not what they seemed. The
man had seemed normal, professional, until he wasn't. How many others were like him, wearing a mask?
She couldn't trust her judgment anymore. She couldn't trust anyone's.

This conviction had hardened into a constant, exhausting vigilance. Whenever Katarina entered a

entrance of rooms, never settling into a chair in the middle of the office. At the studio, she'd
rearranged her desk so her back was to the wall and she could see the entire floor. Her colleagues
had noticed the change but said nothing. The hypervigilance felt necessary, protective. Because the
moment she let her guard down—the moment she assumed she was safe—that was when danger arrived.

scenarios. The bicycle could belong to anyone. The car could be going anywhere. The feeling of being

seemed to quiet. It was this—the creeping realization that she'd become a prisoner of her own fear,
that her hypervigilance was no longer protecting her but consuming her—that had finally brought her
to seek help.
