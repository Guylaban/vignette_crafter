# Clinical Vignette: Roberto

Roberto Méndez is seventy-five years old, a retired quality assurance engineer who spent forty-three
years in manufacturing, checking tolerances and catching defects before they reached customers. He
is meticulous by temperament and training—the kind of man who kept detailed logs, who noticed when
things were out of place. His wife of fifty-one years, Carmen, says he was always the steady one,
the one who made sure everything was secure before they slept. That steadiness has become something
else now, something that frightens them both.

The wildfire came in late August, four years ago, during a heat wave that had turned the foothills
around their community into kindling. Roberto and Carmen had evacuated with their daughter's
family—three grandchildren, two cars, the dog—moving inland to a shelter in the high school
gymnasium. The fire moved faster than anyone expected. By the time they learned their house was
gone, the entire neighborhood had burned. Roberto remembers the smell most vividly: not the smell of
smoke from a fireplace or a grill, but something acrid and total, a smell that seemed to contain ash
and loss and the incineration of thirty years of accumulated life. When they were finally allowed to
return, the house was a black foundation. The garden where Carmen grew her herbs was scorched earth.
His workshop, where he had kept his tools and his projects, was rubble.

For the first month, Roberto functioned. There were insurance adjusters, contractors, decisions
about rebuilding. But as autumn came and the smell of smoke lingered in the air—from other fires,
from neighbors clearing debris—something in him began to fray. He found himself unable to be in the
kitchen when Carmen cooked, because the smell of food browning on the stove triggered a cascade of
physical reactions: his heart would race, his hands would shake, and he would have to leave the
room. When he caught the scent of someone's fireplace in November, his entire body went rigid. He
had come to believe, with a certainty that felt absolute, that he had failed to protect what
mattered. He should have evacuated sooner. He should have had a better evacuation plan. He should
have insisted they move to a safer neighborhood years earlier. These thoughts consumed him,
especially in the evenings when Carmen was asleep. He would lie awake running through scenarios,
imagining different decisions he might have made, different outcomes that would have spared them.

The rumination pulled him deeper. The more he dwelled on what he had done wrong, the more acutely he
scanned his environment for signs of danger—for smoke, for the particular quality of light that
preceded the fire, for weather that resembled that August afternoon. On days when the temperature
climbed and the humidity dropped, when the sky took on a certain haze, Roberto would become
hypervigilant, watching the news obsessively, checking weather alerts every few minutes. His startle
response had become pronounced; Carmen found him jumping at sounds that would never have disturbed
him before—a car door slamming, the furnace igniting, the neighbor's grill being lit. These physical
reactions, the racing heart and the tension that locked his shoulders, seemed to confirm his
vigilance was necessary. He was protecting them, he told himself. He was being ready.

Yet this readiness fed the very rumination he was trying to escape. When he felt his body react to a
trigger—when he smelled smoke from a distant fire or saw flames on the evening news—his mind would
immediately turn to what he should have done differently, as though by reviewing his failures he
could somehow prevent another loss. To manage the intrusive thoughts that arose from this cycle, he
began trying to push them away, to suppress them through sheer force of will, but the suppression
only made them more insistent. He found himself avoiding the kitchen, then the living room where
they watched the news, then eventually the entire front of the house where windows faced the street
and the hills beyond. Carmen would find him in his study with the door closed, the windows covered,
trying not to think about fire, which meant thinking about almost nothing else.

The retirement he had imagined—travel, time with grandchildren, the garden projects he and Carmen
had planned—had contracted into a careful, vigilant existence. He could no longer sit comfortably
through family dinners if anyone mentioned the fire. He had stopped going to the weekly coffee group
with his former colleagues because the coffee shop was near a restaurant with a wood-fired oven, and
he could not predict when that smell would hit him. Carmen, who had always relied on his steadiness,
found herself managing his anxiety, reassuring him that the house was safe, that the weather was
normal, that there was no fire. But her reassurance never quite took hold. Roberto had come to
believe that only his constant vigilance stood between them and catastrophe, and that belief had
become as real to him as the foundation of their rebuilt home.

It was Carmen who finally insisted he see someone. She was gentle about it, but firm. She said she
wanted her husband back, the one who could sit with her in the kitchen without trembling, the one
who could enjoy a sunset without scanning it for smoke. Roberto came to his first appointment
reluctant, uncertain that talking to a therapist could undo what the fire had done. But he was
exhausted—exhausted by the vigilance, by the endless loop of self-recrimination and threat-scanning,
by the sense that he had failed and would fail again. What he wanted most was to stop being afraid,
to stop believing that he alone stood between his family and disaster. What frightened him most was
the possibility that he never would.
