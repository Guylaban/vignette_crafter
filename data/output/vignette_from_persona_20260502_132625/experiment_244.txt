# Clinical Vignette: Priya

Priya was thirty-two when the Astoria Warehouse fire killed forty-three people. She had been working
the night shift at the adjacent hospital's emergency department when the first ambulances
arrived—not as a patient seeking help, but as the nurse who would receive them. The building had
been an old textile factory converted into a storage facility, and the fire had moved through it
with a speed that seemed, in retrospect, impossible. She remembered the smell first: acrid,
chemical, nothing like the controlled burn of a surgical suite. Then the chaos of the loading dock
doors opening, the sound of sirens that didn't stop, and the faces—burned, gasping, some already
beyond what her twelve years of nursing experience had prepared her to witness. One man, his skin
blackened across half his body, had looked directly at her as she was cutting away his clothes, and
she had seen in that moment the precise instant he understood he was dying. She had held his hand.
She had not looked away.

That was four months ago. Now, sitting in her apartment on a Tuesday evening, Priya found herself
unable to construct a coherent narrative of what had happened. When she tried to tell her sister
about that night, the story fell apart. She could summon the smell, vivid and overwhelming, but not
the sequence of events. She remembered fragments: the weight of a patient's arm across her
shoulders, the temperature of the IV fluid she was running, the particular shade of the burn unit's
fluorescent lights. But these pieces refused to assemble into something that felt real. It was as
though she had watched it happen to someone else, or seen it in a film she couldn't quite recall.
Her rational mind knew the fire had occurred—she had read the news coverage, seen the memorial
photographs—yet some part of her remained convinced that the intensity of what she had witnessed
couldn't possibly have been true. The human body, she had learned that night, could be remade by
heat into something she had no words for. But the knowledge of that fact and the felt reality of
having seen it remained, somehow, in separate compartments.

The fragmentation had worsened over the weeks that followed. When she tried to sleep, pieces of the
night would surface without warning or order: a nurse's voice calling out a blood pressure reading,
then jumping to the sound of the hospital chaplain's shoes on the tile floor, then a flash of
someone's wedding ring catching the light. These disconnected images would jolt her awake, leaving
her disoriented and gasping. She had taken to staying awake deliberately, scrolling through her
phone until dawn, because sleep had become unreliable and frightening. At work, she found herself
pushing the memory away before she could fully follow a thought about that night to its conclusion.
If she caught herself beginning to remember the progression of events—the arrival of the first
ambulance, the realization that the disaster was ongoing, the moment she understood the scale of
what was coming—she would physically interrupt herself, standing abruptly or changing the channel in
her mind to something else. It was as though some part of her recognized that if she allowed the
memory to assemble itself completely, she would have to accept that it had truly happened, and that
acceptance felt unbearable.

This fragmentation and unreality had begun to reshape her life in subtle but persistent ways. She
had taken a leave of absence from the hospital three weeks after the fire, unable to walk past the
emergency department without a sensation of vertigo. Her colleagues had reached out with sympathy,
but she had found their presence intolerable—they had been there too, they understood what had
happened, and their understanding somehow made it more real, not less. She had stopped answering
calls from friends. She had let her apartment fall into a state of disorder that would have
horrified her a year ago. And now, four months after the fire, she was beginning to wonder whether
she would ever be able to return to nursing, or whether the fragmented, unreal quality of her
memories had fundamentally altered something in how she could trust her own mind. She had finally
made an appointment with a therapist, not because she believed she could be helped, but because the
isolation had become as unbearable as the memories themselves.
