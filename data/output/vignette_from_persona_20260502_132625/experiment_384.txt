# Rosa's Story

Rosa Mendez was forty-seven when the man broke into her apartment on a Tuesday evening in March. She
had been a social ministry worker for eighteen years, spending her days in the parishes and
community centers of her city, helping families navigate welfare systems and domestic crises—work
that had always felt purposeful, grounded in her faith that people were fundamentally good, that
suffering could be redeemed through compassion and connection. She had been married to David for
twenty-two years. They had two adult children. By most measures, Rosa had built a life of quiet
dignity and meaning.

The man came through the bedroom window while she was alone. He moved with practiced silence—she
remembered that most vividly, the absolute absence of sound before the glass broke. What she carried
now, three years later, were not continuous memories but fragments: the weight of his body pinning
her to the mattress; his hand over her mouth; the smell of cigarette smoke and sweat; the moment she
stopped struggling because she understood he would kill her if she did not. These moments existed in
her mind as separate photographs, each vivid and isolated, with no coherent narrative connecting
them. When she tried to describe what had happened to David in those first weeks, the words simply
would not come. She could see the pictures—could feel them in her body—but language dissolved before
she could shape it. The guilt that accompanied these fragmented images was immediate and crushing:
she had not fought hard enough; she had not screamed; she had somehow invited this into her home
through her own failure to be vigilant enough, careful enough, good enough.

In the months that followed, Rosa came to believe that she had fundamentally changed, that the woman
she had been—the one who could laugh easily, who believed in God's protection, who felt safe in her
own skin—was gone forever. She had lost her faith not only in the goodness of people but in the
basic safety of the world. She looked at her body with revulsion and shame, unable to undress
without a wave of self-disgust. These beliefs took root and spread like infection. They fed a
conviction that she deserved to suffer, that punishment was the only appropriate response to what
her body had endured. She began to eat very little, measuring and restricting every meal with
meticulous control—it was one of the few domains where she could exert absolute dominion, where
nothing could be taken from her without her permission. She stopped allowing herself moments of rest
or comfort; relaxation felt dangerous, as though letting her guard down would invite another attack.
Her mind had become a place of constant vigilance, and she had come to believe that only through
punishment and control could she maintain any safety at all.

The threat monitoring that had started in those first days after the assault never ceased. Unusual
silence—the quiet of David reading in the next room, the hush of the apartment when she came
home—triggered an immediate sense of dread, a certainty that something terrible was about to unfold.
When a colleague at the ministry office was running late to a meeting, Rosa's mind instantly
constructed catastrophe: an accident, a crisis, a harbinger of worse things to come. These
interpretations felt not like thoughts but like facts, and they fed her need to remain
hypervigilant, to punish herself preemptively, to keep eating restricted, to refuse any moment of
ease. The fragmented images from that night—those isolated photographs of violation—would surface
without warning, and each time they did, they reinforced her conviction that the world was
fundamentally dangerous and that she was fundamentally damaged. This cycle had tightened over three
years: the memory fragments triggered threat, which triggered the belief that she was unworthy and
broken, which made her restrict and control and punish herself, which kept her mind locked in a
state of hyperalert scanning, which made the threat feel more real, more imminent, more deserving of
her suffering.

David had begun to withdraw. He no longer reached for her in bed. He ate his meals separately from
hers, having long since given up trying to encourage her to eat normally. The work she loved—sitting
with families, listening to their struggles, offering hope—had become increasingly difficult. Last
month, during a session with a young woman who had disclosed abuse, Rosa had frozen, unable to find
words, her mind flooded with those fragmented images and the certainty that she could offer nothing
but her own brokenness. It was her supervisor's gentle suggestion that she consider therapy that
finally brought her to seek help. Rosa sat in the waiting room of the clinic, her hands folded in
her lap, terrified of what speaking about the assault might unleash, yet more terrified of
continuing to live as she had been—isolated, punished, unable to trust anything or anyone, least of
all herself.
