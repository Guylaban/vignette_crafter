# Clinical Vignette: Kahu

Kahu was sixty-eight when his grandson was born. The boy arrived on a Tuesday morning in late
autumn, and Kahu drove to the hospital with his wife Aroha, both of them trembling with
anticipation. That same afternoon, while Aroha held the baby in the maternity ward, Kahu stepped out
to find a quiet corner. He never made it back to the room. In the stairwell between the third and
fourth floors, he heard the screams—a woman's voice, raw and desperate, echoing off the concrete
walls. He climbed toward the sound without thinking. What he found was a man with a knife, his
grandson's mother pinned against the wall, blood already spreading across her shoulder. Kahu tried
to intervene. The man turned the blade on him, and in the chaos that followed—a security guard
arriving, the attacker fleeing down another staircase—Kahu's daughter-in-law collapsed. She
survived, barely. The man was never caught. The police investigation went nowhere. No charges were
filed. The hospital's security protocols were never meaningfully changed.

Now, three years later, Kahu works as a cashier at a supermarket in Honolulu. It is work that should
be simple: scanning items, taking payment, making small talk. But Kahu has become a man trapped in
constant vigilance. Every unexpected sound makes him flinch. When a customer opens the automatic
doors with too much force, or when a child cries in the produce section, something in Kahu's chest
seizes. His body reacts as though the man with the knife is still there, still coming. The triggers
are everywhere—a slamming door, a baby's wail—and each one pulls him back into that stairwell, into
the incompleteness of what he witnessed and what he failed to prevent.

What troubles Kahu most is that he cannot hold the memory intact. When he tries to explain to Aroha
what happened, the details scatter. He remembers the blood, the screams, the guard's footsteps, but
the sequence dissolves. He cannot tell himself a coherent story of those minutes, and this
fragmentation feeds his sense that something fundamental has broken inside him. The gaps in his
memory terrify him because they seem to confirm what he has come to believe most deeply: that the
institutions meant to protect his family—the hospital, the police, the courts—have utterly failed.
No one was held accountable. The man who did this walks free, and Kahu carries the weight of that
injustice like a stone in his chest.

Because Kahu cannot fully trust what he remembers, and because he has lost faith in the systems that
should have protected his daughter-in-law, he has become hypervigilant in ways that have begun to
isolate him. At work, he watches customers with a scrutiny that makes some uncomfortable. He studies
their movements, their hands, looking for signs of threat that he is certain he will miss if he
looks away for even a moment. Each unexpected movement nearby—a customer reaching past him, a
colleague approaching from behind—sends a spike of adrenaline through him, as if danger is
perpetually imminent. The triggers themselves—doors opening, babies crying, the simple discomfort of
feeling unsafe in a public space—now activate this hypervigilance, which in turn deepens his
conviction that the world is fundamentally dangerous and that those meant to protect him have
abandoned their duty.

Kahu feels dead inside. There is a numbness that sits beneath the vigilance, a hollowing out of joy
that he cannot quite name. He smiles at customers because it is his job, but the smile is
mechanical. Aroha has noticed. His grandchildren visit less often because Kahu's startle responses
and his constant watchfulness make them nervous. The people he loved have retreated, and Kahu
interprets this retreat as confirmation that he has failed them—that his failure in the stairwell
that day has poisoned everything since. He came to his first appointment three weeks ago because
Aroha finally told him, gently, that she was frightened of him. Not of his anger, but of his
absence. He is still there, but he is not present. That is what finally brought him in: the fear
that he is losing the one person who has stood by him, and the crushing certainty that if he cannot
protect his own mind, he never will.
