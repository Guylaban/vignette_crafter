# Rosa's Story

Rosa Delgado had been a combat engineer for twenty-eight years when the IED detonated beneath the
Humvee. She was forty-seven then, leading a route clearance mission through the Helmand Province in
July 2014. The blast came without warning—a flash of orange that consumed her entire field of
vision, then heat so intense it seemed to melt the air itself. The concussion threw her against the
reinforced door panel, and for a moment she couldn't hear anything but a high-pitched ringing. Then
came the screaming. One of her soldiers was on fire, rolling in the dirt, and she was already moving
toward him, her training overriding the chaos in her nervous system. She got him to cover, got the
tourniquet on another casualty whose leg ended below the knee, and only much later—after the
evacuation helicopters had lifted away and the base medic told her she'd done everything right—did
she notice the blistering on her own left forearm where the heat had caught her skin.

The physical wounds healed. The burn scars faded to a pale, thin line. But something in Rosa's body
had not returned to baseline. By the time she was medically cleared to return stateside in 2015, her
nervous system had become a sentinel, permanently vigilant for the next catastrophe. She was
promoted to a training role at Fort Leonard Wood, where she oversaw demolition exercises and
explosive safety protocols. It should have been manageable—controlled environments, no combat—but
the work itself had become a minefield. When recruits detonated charges during live-fire drills, the
flash and concussive wave would hijack her immediately. The sudden sight of flame or the specific
temperature of heat radiating from an explosion would trigger the full memory without warning: the
orange consuming everything, the screaming, the weight of her soldier's body as she dragged him. The
memory itself was fragmented and chaotic—she could not tell a coherent story of those moments, even
to herself. What she remembered came in sensory shards: the specific high-pitched tone of the
ringing in her ears, the smell of scorched metal and flesh, the feeling of helplessness despite her
training. Those auditory echoes would replay in her head for hours afterward, the phantom ringing so
vivid she'd find herself checking if anyone else could hear it.

By 2018, Rosa had come to believe there was something fundamentally wrong with her. Other engineers
could witness controlled detonations without their hands trembling. Other soldiers could move past
combat without their bodies turning into coiled springs. What happened to her was deeply unfair—she
had done everything right that day, had saved lives, and yet she was the one left shattered. No
investigation had changed anything. No accountability had come. She was certain the world was a
dangerous place, that threats were everywhere, that her body's constant state of alert was the only
thing keeping her alive. These beliefs began to shape every choice she made. She started avoiding
anything that could be stressful—first the live-fire drills, then the training facility itself, then
the base commissary where the fluorescent lights and crowds felt unpredictable. She volunteered for
administrative work instead, though it meant a lateral move and the loss of the role she had built.
At home, she numbed her feelings with discipline and distance, moving through evenings with her
husband Miguel like someone underwater. She couldn't articulate to him why she flinched at the sound
of the grill igniting on their back patio, why she had begun to constantly scan their neighborhood
for signs of threat—the way a car slowed on their street, the unusual silence of an evening with no
traffic noise. That silence especially terrified her. It felt like the held breath before
catastrophe.

The avoidance and emotional numbing created their own spiral. By withdrawing from stressful
situations, Rosa reinforced her belief that the world was dangerous and that she was incapable of
managing it. Her hypervigilance—that constant scanning of her surroundings—kept her threat system
perpetually activated. Her body remained tense and ready to react to danger that never came, leaving
her exhausted and unable to concentrate at work. She found herself ruminating on the unfairness of
it all, which deepened her conviction that something was broken inside her. The numbing she used to
manage her emotions only isolated her further from Miguel, who grew increasingly frustrated with her
distance. Six months ago, her supervisor had gently suggested she consider a medical evaluation.
Rosa had resisted for weeks, certain that admitting she needed help would confirm her deepest fear:
that she was defective, that the trauma had fundamentally altered who she was as a person. But last
month, when she realized she had spent an entire weekend in her garage, unable to enter the house
because the kitchen window faced the neighbors' property where someone had been burning brush, she
finally called the Veterans Affairs clinic. She is fifty-four now, and what she fears most is that
the damage is permanent, that she will spend the rest of her life trapped in this hyperalert state,
unable to be present with her husband or find meaning in the work that once defined her. What she
hopes for, though she barely allows herself to articulate it, is that somehow the fragmented pieces
of that day might finally cohere into a story she can tell and then, perhaps, begin to put down.
