# Rosa's Vigilance

Rosa Mendez was forty-three when she found herself unable to watch the evening news without her
hands shaking. The cybersecurity analyst—meticulous, detail-oriented, the kind of woman who could
spot a vulnerability in code at a glance—had spent the last eight months in a state of fractured
remembering. It had started three days after the accident, when a sound like breaking glass sent her
back into the parking garage of the hospital where it all happened, and she had never quite left.

The event itself lived in her body more completely than in her mind. On a Tuesday afternoon in
February, Rosa had been driving home from her mother's house, her attention divided between the road
and the radio, when a young cyclist swerved into her lane. She had swerved too—a reflex, not a
choice—and clipped him hard enough that he flew over the hood. She remembered the sound, a sickening
collision of metal and bone, and then the screaming. What she did not remember, no matter how many
times she tried to reconstruct it in the dark of her bedroom, was the moment between impact and the
boy lying on the asphalt. That gap in her memory felt like a hole in her own consciousness, as
though her mind had simply ejected the most crucial seconds. The hospital had confirmed what
witnesses said: she had done everything right, had swerved to avoid him entirely, and he had swerved
again directly into her path. But the parts she could not retrieve—the exact angle of his body, the
precise expression on his face—felt like evidence of something she was not remembering correctly,
some failure she had not yet fully confessed.

When she tried to reconstruct those lost moments, the grief came without warning. It would arrive as
a physical sensation first—a crushing weight in her chest, a burning in her throat—and only then
would the images surface: the boy's mother in the hospital waiting room, the way her own hands had
trembled as she gave her statement to the police. The intensity of that grief when she remembered
felt disproportionate, as though she were mourning not just the boy's injuries but something in
herself. Yet the core memory remained fractured, unreliable, full of holes she could not fill.

This fragmentation made her vulnerable to triggers in ways she had not anticipated. Whenever she saw
something that resembled a weapon—a baseball bat in someone's truck bed, a kitchen knife on a
counter in a show she was watching—her mind would lurch backward, and suddenly she was not just
remembering the accident; she was remembering it in a new way each time, with details she had not
noticed before, as though her brain were trying to fill in the blanks with increasingly catastrophic
interpretations. A confrontation she witnessed between two pedestrians on the street, raised voices
and aggressive postures, sent her spiraling into a memory of the boy's father's face when he
confronted her outside the courthouse. The memory of that confrontation would then trigger a cascade
of other details—the weapon-like intensity in his eyes, the way he had gestured toward her—and she
would find herself unable to distinguish between what had actually happened and what she feared had
happened, between the real event and the versions her traumatized mind kept generating.

The cycle had begun to narrow her world. Rosa had stopped watching television, stopped taking walks
in her neighborhood, stopped attending the family dinners where her cousins might mention an
accident they had heard about on the news. She had become hypervigilant to any visual reminder of
the event, scanning crowds for aggressive postures, avoiding routes where cyclists gathered. Her
job, which had always been her anchor—the one place where her mind felt sharp and reliable—had begun
to suffer. She found herself unable to concentrate during video calls, distracted by the fear that
someone on screen might make a sudden movement that would send her back into that fractured memory.
Three weeks ago, her supervisor had gently asked if everything was all right. Rosa had said yes. It
was not true.

What frightened her most was not the memory itself, but the possibility that there was something in
those missing moments—something she had done, some negligence she was still refusing to see—that
would eventually surface and prove that the accident had been her fault after all. She had come to
believe that if she could just remember correctly, if she could fill in those gaps, she might
finally understand what had really happened. Instead, each trigger only deepened her confusion, and
each attempt to remember only made the grief more unbearable.
