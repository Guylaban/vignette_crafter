# Rajesh

Rajesh was thirty-four when his father died without warning. It was a Tuesday morning in October,
unremarkable except for what it became—his father collapsed in the kitchen while making chai, and
despite the paramedics' speed, despite everything his mother and sister tried, there was nothing to
be done. A massive stroke, they said later. No signs. No time. One moment his father was alive in
the world, and the next he simply wasn't. For months afterward, Rajesh moved through his interior
design work with a kind of mechanical precision, selecting fabrics and finishes as though the
decisions mattered, as though the world still operated according to rules he understood. But
something had shifted irrevocably. He had come to believe that death was not something that waited
for the old or the sick—it was something that could simply arrive, without announcement, and take
the people he loved most.

Five years later, now married to Priya and working as a senior designer at a mid-sized firm in
Chicago, Rajesh found himself trapped in a cycle he could not name. October arrived each year like a
predator, and with it came the anniversary of his father's death. The week leading up to October
15th, Rajesh would lie awake at night, mentally rehearsing catastrophes—running through scenarios in
which something happened to his mother, to Priya, to himself. He would imagine the phone call, the
hospital, the moment he would be told. If he rehearsed it enough, he thought, if he prepared himself
thoroughly enough for the worst, then perhaps he could prevent it, or at least survive it. The logic
was desperate and circular, but it felt necessary. When colleagues at work mentioned their own aging
parents or talked casually about someone they knew who had lost a family member, Rajesh's chest
would tighten immediately. These conversations triggered the same cascade—he would find himself
alone at his desk afterward, mentally walking through the worst outcomes again, trying to steel
himself against a danger he could not see or prevent.

The triggers had multiplied over time. Funerals became impossible. When a client's mother passed and
he was invited to the service, Rajesh invented a project deadline and stayed home, his hands shaking
as he typed the email. Cemeteries were out of the question entirely. Even driving past one made his
body respond before his mind could catch up—his heart would race, his breathing would shallow, and a
visceral sense of dread would flood through him. Priya had stopped asking him to visit her
grandmother's grave. At work, when conversations turned toward loss or grief, Rajesh would excuse
himself, claiming a meeting he didn't have, walking to the bathroom to splash cold water on his face
and wait for the panic to subside.

The avoidance had calcified into routine. He had stopped attending family gatherings in October
altogether, telling his mother he was overbooked with client presentations—a lie that compounded his
guilt each time he repeated it. His worst-case rehearsals had become almost constant, a background
hum of catastrophic thinking that now extended far beyond the anniversary itself. What if Priya got
sick? What if his mother fell? What if he, at thirty-nine, had inherited his father's vulnerability?
When these thoughts took hold, Rajesh would mentally rehearse each scenario in exhausting detail, as
though the act of imagining could somehow neutralize the threat. But it never did. Instead, each
rehearsal seemed to deepen his conviction that danger was imminent, that he was living on borrowed
time.

The irritability had crept in gradually. Small frustrations—a delayed shipment, a client's
indecision about paint colors—would trigger sudden flares of anger that shocked even him. Priya had
begun to tiptoe around him, especially as October approached. Last month, when she mentioned that
her aunt wanted to talk about planning a memorial service for her uncle, Rajesh had snapped at her
so sharply that she'd gone silent for hours. He hated himself for it, but he couldn't stop. His body
was responding to threats his mind couldn't articulate, and the anger seemed to be the only outlet
for the dread that lived constantly beneath his skin.

It was Priya who finally insisted he seek help. She had found him on the bathroom floor one October
evening, hyperventilating, convinced that something terrible was about to happen—though he couldn't
articulate what. She held his hand and told him that whatever he was carrying alone was too heavy,
and that she couldn't watch him disappear into this fear anymore. Now, sitting in the therapist's
office, Rajesh is terrified that talking about what happened might make it worse, that acknowledging
his father's death might somehow invite more loss. But he is also exhausted, and he knows that the
walls he has built to protect himself have become the very thing that is destroying his marriage and
his peace.
