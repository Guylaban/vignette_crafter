# Clinical Vignette: Amara

Amara was twenty-one when the storm came. She had been home in Lagos for the summer before her
second year of medical school, staying with her parents in the Lekki area while preparing for her
surgical rotation. The rainy season had been unusually intense that year, but on the evening of June
14th, the sky turned a colour she had never seen before—a bruised, greenish grey that seemed to
press down on the city. Within hours, the flooding was catastrophic. Water rose through the streets
with a speed that made evacuation impossible; Amara watched from a second-floor window as
neighbours' cars were swept away, as the power failed and the darkness became total. She and her
parents huddled together for twelve hours while the storm raged, listening to the sounds of debris
striking the walls, unable to know if the building would hold. When dawn came and the water began to
recede, they emerged into a landscape of wreckage—homes destroyed, bodies recovered from the
streets, the entire neighbourhood transformed into something unrecognizable. Amara's own home was
damaged but standing. She and her family were alive. But something in her had fractured in those
twelve hours of helplessness, trapped in the dark while forces she could not control or predict
threatened everything around her.

She returned to medical school that autumn, determined to continue. The first weeks were manageable.
But as the autumn rains began, something shifted. Whenever the sky darkened or she heard thunder in
the distance, her body would flood with a sensation of dread so acute it felt physical—her heart
would race, her hands would shake, and she would find herself mentally cataloguing exits, checking
weather forecasts obsessively, unable to focus on anything else. The triggers were specific and
relentless: any weather pattern remotely similar to that June evening, the approach of nightfall,
waking in darkness. When these moments came, Amara's mind would race with certainty that something
catastrophic was about to happen again, that she had no power to prevent it, that her life as she
had known it was fundamentally over. She had survived the storm, but she could not survive the
knowledge of her own helplessness—or so she had come to believe. These thoughts would grip her with
such force that she would become hypervigilant, her body coiled and ready, startling at ordinary
sounds—a door closing, a phone notification—as though each small noise might be the beginning of the
next disaster.

In response to these overwhelming moments, Amara had developed a set of strategies that felt
protective but were quietly eroding her life. When she felt the first stirrings of anxiety—a
darkening sky, the approach of evening—she would clamp down on her emotions with fierce discipline,
telling herself she could not afford to feel fear because feeling it meant accepting that she was
vulnerable. She stopped exercising, the activity that had once steadied her, because movement felt
like tempting fate, like drawing attention to her body in a world that had already shown its
capacity for violence. More subtly, she had become acutely attuned to the emotional state of those
around her, becoming almost unnaturally compliant and agreeable in her interactions, as though by
controlling how others perceived her—by being unfailingly pleasant, by never expressing need or
frustration—she could somehow control what happened to her. These strategies offered temporary
relief. But they also deepened her conviction that she was fragile, that she could not be trusted
with her own wellbeing, that the only way to survive was through constant vigilance and emotional
suppression.

By her third month back at university, the cycle had tightened into something inescapable. Her
hypervigilance and startle responses made it nearly impossible to concentrate during lectures or
procedures; she would find herself unable to focus on a patient's anatomy when her nervous system
was constantly braced for threat. Her attending physicians noticed her distraction, her hesitation
in the operating theatre. The very career she had worked toward—surgery, the ultimate expression of
control and precision—was becoming inaccessible to her. She had begun to avoid her friends, finding
social interaction exhausting because it required her to manage her emotions and her appearance
while internally she felt like she was coming apart. The thought that her life had been destroyed by
the trauma had calcified into something she no longer questioned; it felt like fact. She could not
rely on herself because her own body had betrayed her—had felt terror she could not control, had
become a source of unpredictable reactions. And she could not stop bad things from happening because
the storm had proven that the world operated according to forces entirely beyond her influence. By
November, when her supervisor gently suggested that she might benefit from speaking to someone,
Amara finally acknowledged that the life she was trying to rebuild was slipping away, and that the
strategies she had relied on to survive were now the very things keeping her trapped in the
aftermath of a disaster that, physically, she had already escaped.
