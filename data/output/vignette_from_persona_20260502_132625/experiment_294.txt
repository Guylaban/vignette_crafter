# Clinical Vignette: Amara

Amara was forty-two when the soldiers came to her village in South Sudan. She had worked as a
bookkeeper for a small cooperative, managing the finances of women's savings groups with meticulous
care—work that gave her dignity and a sense of order in a world that had always felt precarious. One
morning in 2011, armed men arrived without warning. They moved through the settlement methodically,
separating people, demanding compliance, their uniforms and weapons turning the familiar landscape
of acacia trees and red earth into something unrecognizable and threatening. Amara was forced into a
truck with others, told to sit still, told not to speak, told that resistance would mean death. The
journey lasted hours. She remembered the dust coming through the canvas, the sound of the engine,
the absolute powerlessness of being moved against her will through the very terrain she had known
her entire life—the open fields and scrubland suddenly transformed into a corridor of terror. She
was eventually released after two weeks, but those two weeks had rewritten something fundamental in
her sense of safety.

She came to America in 2015, rebuilt a life, and found work as a financial analyst at a mid-sized
firm in Atlanta. For years, the symptoms remained manageable—hypervigilance that she attributed to
caution, sleep that was fragmented but functional. But over the past eighteen months, something has
shifted. Now, at sixty-three, Amara finds herself trapped in a narrowing world, and she knows it,
which makes it worse.

The triggers are specific and unyielding. Any situation in which someone tells her what to do—a
supervisor asking her to revise a report, a colleague suggesting she handle a task differently—sends
her into a state of internal rebellion and dread. She has come to believe that compliance means
losing herself entirely, that saying yes to any demand is the first step toward the kind of
helplessness that nearly destroyed her. Police officers, whether she sees them on the street or
hears sirens, create an immediate spike of fear; uniforms and authority have become fused with the
memory of those soldiers. And being in nature—even the parks near her apartment, with their open
spaces and scattered trees—triggers a flooding sense of exposure and vulnerability that she cannot
quite name but cannot ignore.

These triggers have become the architecture around which her entire life now revolves, because Amara
has developed a set of strategies that feel protective but have begun to suffocate her. When she
feels the weight of authority pressing down—when her boss requests a meeting or when she anticipates
any kind of directive—she catastrophizes about what might happen not to herself but to her sister in
Lagos, her niece in Nairobi, her closest friend at work. She lies awake imagining car accidents,
illnesses, random violence, as if by worrying obsessively she can somehow prevent disaster through
sheer vigilance. This constant vigilance about others' safety has become a way of managing her own
terror about loss of control. At work, she has begun avoiding any situation that might involve
disagreement or confrontation. She says yes to assignments she cannot manage, absorbs blame for
mistakes that were not hers, and keeps her thoughts to herself in meetings—a form of silence that
she tells herself is professional but that has actually isolated her from her colleagues and left
her exhausted.

Most significantly, she has begun avoiding authority figures altogether. She takes longer routes to
avoid police checkpoints, she calls in sick rather than attend meetings where her supervisor will be
present, and she has declined two promotions because the roles would have required more visible
authority and decision-making. Each act of avoidance brings temporary relief, a few hours where her
chest is not tight and her mind is not racing. But that relief is short-lived. By avoiding the
situations that trigger her fear, she has reinforced the belief that these situations are genuinely
dangerous, that her fear is justified, that the world is as threatening as it felt in that truck.
The avoidance has also begun to cost her professionally—her supervisor has noted that she seems
withdrawn, that she is missing opportunities for advancement, that something has shifted. Her
performance reviews, once stellar, now carry language about "engagement" and "presence."

Three weeks ago, Amara sat in her apartment on a Saturday morning and realized she had not left in
four days. She had called in sick, ordered groceries online, and stayed inside because she knew that
leaving meant encountering the possibility of a police car, or a supervisor she might run into, or a
park where the open sky would remind her of exposure. The thought that came to her was simple and
terrifying: she was becoming a prisoner in her own life, and this time, no one had forced her into
the cage. She called her doctor the next morning and asked for a referral to a therapist. What
frightens her most now is not the memory itself but the possibility that she will spend the rest of
her life running from it, that the soldiers have already won by making her disappear from her own
existence. She is hoping that someone can help her understand why her mind has decided that safety
lies in smaller and smaller spaces, and whether there is a way back out.
