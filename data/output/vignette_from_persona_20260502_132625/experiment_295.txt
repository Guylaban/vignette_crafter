# Kofi's Story

Kofi was twenty-three when the assault happened, a young urban planner with a growing portfolio of
transit redesigns and a life that felt, for the first time, genuinely his own. He had moved to the
city six months earlier, renting a small apartment in a neighbourhood he was helping to revitalize.
On a Friday night in March, he stopped at a corner store near his building. He was reaching for
bottled water when three men entered. What followed was fragmented in his mind now—a flash of
movement, a voice thick with alcohol, a fist. The smell of whiskey and sweat filled his nostrils as
he was shoved against the cooler. His wallet was gone in seconds. The police arrived twenty minutes
later. When Kofi tried to explain what had happened, the words would not come; he could see the
moment the first man's jaw clenched, could picture the bottle glinting under the fluorescent lights,
but the sequence dissolved into isolated snapshots, each one vivid and senseless. The officers took
his statement with visible impatience, filed a report, and told him they would be in touch. They
never were.

In the weeks that followed, Kofi returned to work, but something fundamental had shifted. The office
building where he spent his days—a glass structure with open-plan floors—became a minefield. Any
unexpected sound made him flinch violently; the scrape of a chair, a door closing down the corridor,
even the ping of an arriving email could send his heart racing as though the assault were happening
again. When a colleague raised her voice during a meeting—nothing hostile, just animated discussion
of a zoning proposal—Kofi felt his body flood with panic, as if danger were imminent. He began
avoiding meetings where conflict might arise, declining to present his own ideas if there was any
chance of disagreement. The avoidance provided momentary relief, but it also meant that his work
suffered; he was sidelined from a major project, passed over for presentations. Each trigger—the
smell of alcohol on a commuter's breath, a sudden movement in his peripheral vision, the sound of
someone's raised voice—sent him spiralling into a state of hypervigilance that left him exhausted
and convinced that nowhere was truly safe, no matter what anyone told him.

The memory of the assault haunted him in discrete, overwhelming moments. When the images
surfaced—the attacker's expression, the cold of the metal cooler against his back—Kofi was flooded
with such intense emotion that he could not function. He would have to leave his desk, lock himself
in a bathroom stall, wait for the panic to subside. These intrusions were unpredictable, arriving
without warning, and they fed a growing conviction that he was fundamentally broken, unable to cope
with even minor stressors. The assault had revealed to him a truth he could not escape: the
institutions meant to protect him had failed. The police had done nothing. His employer had no real
support for trauma. He was alone. This belief—that he could not handle what life threw at him, that
no one and nothing could be trusted to keep him safe—began to color everything. When the memory
surfaced, it reinforced his sense of helplessness; when he encountered a trigger, it seemed to
confirm that the world was genuinely dangerous and he was powerless to prevent harm.

Over the following months, Kofi developed ways to manage the unbearable feelings. He began
gambling—first online poker, then visiting a casino on weekends—drawn to the distraction and the
rush of possibility, however brief. Gambling became a escape from the rumination that otherwise
consumed him. He would lie awake at night replaying the assault, convinced that he should have done
something differently, that his failure to fight back or run was a personal weakness. The self-blame
was relentless. Yet when he tried to stop gambling, when he attempted to sit with his thoughts, the
memory would intrude again, and he would find himself back at the casino or scrolling through
betting apps, seeking any distraction that might quieten the voice telling him he was powerless and
broken. His avoidance of conflict deepened; he withdrew from friendships, turned down social
invitations, and created a narrow, controlled life in which nothing unpredictable could happen. But
this constriction itself became a source of shame and further self-blame. He had come to believe
that his inability to cope—even with normal disagreement or unexpected sounds—was a personal
failing, evidence that he lacked the resilience others possessed.

Now, three months into this cycle, Kofi sits in a therapist's office for the first time. His PCL-5
score of 77 reflects the severity of his distress. He is hoping, he says quietly, that someone might
help him understand why he cannot simply move past this, why the world still feels so dangerous, and
why he cannot trust that he will ever be safe again. What he fears most is that this is who he has
