Amara was thirty years old, a school counselor at a charter school in East Atlanta, when everything
changed on an ordinary Tuesday evening. She had been driving home from work, tired after a day spent

her over on a residential street. The reason, she would later learn, was a broken taillight—a
taillight she had not known was broken. The officer approached with his hand near his belt, and when
Amara, nervous and wanting to be helpful, reached toward the glove compartment for her registration,
he shouted. His voice was sharp, commanding. The next moments fractured into fragments: his hand on
his weapon, his commands to keep her hands visible, the smell of alcohol on his breath as he leaned
toward her window, the sensation of being trapped in a metal box with no way to prove she meant no
harm. He had called for backup. Two more officers arrived. Amara, who had never been arrested, who
had never even received a speeding ticket, found herself handcuffed and placed in the back of a
patrol car while the officers searched her vehicle for drugs they did not find. The entire
interaction lasted forty minutes. The charges were dropped within a week. But the forty minutes had
rewired something fundamental in how Amara understood safety.

In the months that followed, Amara developed an exhausting hypervigilance. Any sudden movement by
another person—a colleague reaching past her in the hallway, a student standing up quickly in her
office—sent her nervous system into overdrive, her body immediately tensing as though bracing for
threat. Even in her own home, in the quiet evenings with her husband Marcus, she could not relax.
She would sit on the couch and feel her shoulders locked, her jaw clenched, waiting for something
she could not name. The smell of alcohol became unbearable; when Marcus opened a beer, she would
leave the room, her heart racing. Loud noises—a car backfiring, someone slamming a door—would send
her into a state of acute alertness that took hours to fade. And exhaustion had become her constant
companion. The hypervigilance, the inability to let her body rest even in objectively safe moments,
drained her relentlessly.

What made this worse was what Amara had come to believe about herself. She was convinced that
something was fundamentally wrong with her—that her reaction was disproportionate, that a normal
person would have moved past this by now. She ruminated endlessly on her own actions that night: she
should not have reached for the glove compartment, should have asked permission first, should have
anticipated the officer's fear. If she had acted differently, she reasoned, none of it would have
happened. This self-blame, this certainty that she was defective for not being able to simply
recover, led her to withdraw. She began avoiding situations that might stir strong emotions—she
declined invitations from friends, stopped attending her church's weekly gatherings, made excuses to
skip the faculty happy hours that had once been part of her routine. She avoided conversations that
might lead to hope or happiness, as though allowing herself to feel anything positive would be a
betrayal of her own vigilance, a dangerous lowering of her guard. The more she isolated herself, the
more isolated she felt, cut off from the people who had once anchored her life. Marcus noticed. He
would ask if she wanted to go out, and she would say no, and he would retreat into hurt silence. Her
sense of being fundamentally broken deepened with each refusal, each moment of disconnection.

The isolation and self-blame intensified her threat monitoring. Because she believed something was
wrong with her—that she was defective, unreliable, unable to read situations correctly—she became
even more hypervigilant, scanning constantly for signs of danger that her broken judgment might
miss. And because she was scanning constantly, because she was unable to relax even at home, she
felt more evidence that she was right: a normal person would be able to sit peacefully; the fact
that she could not proved her defectiveness. The avoidance of emotional situations meant she never
stayed long enough in any interaction to discover that her fears might be unfounded. She would leave
gatherings early, or decline them altogether, which only deepened her conviction that she was
isolated, that others saw her as withdrawn or cold. At work, she found herself avoiding situations
where strong emotions might arise—she began referring students to other counselors, making excuses
about her caseload, stepping back from the very work that had once defined her sense of purpose.

convinced that she was broken beyond repair. That conversation, painful as it was, finally brought
her to seek help. What she feared most was that no amount of therapy could undo what she had come to
believe about herself. But there was also, buried beneath the despair, a fragile hope: that maybe
she was not as broken as she believed.
