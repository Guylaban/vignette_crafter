# Thomas

Thomas was thirty-two when the convoy hit the IED outside Kandahar. He remembers the noise first—not
the explosion itself, which his mind seems to have erased, but the ringing that came after, a high-
pitched whine that filled everything. He remembers the smell of burning rubber and something else,
something organic that he has never been able to name. He was the vehicle commander. Martinez and
Dawson were in the Humvee behind his. When the dust cleared enough to see, both vehicles were
burning. He pulled three men from his own truck. He could hear Martinez calling out, but the second
vehicle was already fully engulfed. Thomas tried twice to reach it. The heat drove him back. He has
spent twenty-eight years knowing that he left them there.

The guilt settled into him like scar tissue. It was not the sharp, fresh guilt of the first
months—that had been almost easier, because it had felt like something he deserved. What came later
was worse: the slow, corrosive certainty that he had survived when better men had not, that there
was something fundamentally wrong with the fact of his breathing while they were ash. He came home,
married Carol ten years later, kept working. For a long time, he had almost convinced himself he
could live with it. But in the past few years, something had shifted. The guilt had metastasized
into a broader, darker conviction: that he did not know who he was anymore, that the man who came
back from Kandahar was not the person he had been before, and that the world itself was a place
where death could come without warning and take the people you cared about. He had come to believe
that nowhere was truly safe—that vigilance was the only honest stance toward a fundamentally
dangerous existence.

These convictions had begun to shape what he did and did not allow himself to experience. When
memories of the event surfaced—and they did, unbidden, when he was loading pallets in the warehouse
or sitting in traffic—they came with an intensity that terrified him. The terror was not abstract.
It was the same physical sensation he had felt in the moment: his chest tight, his vision narrowing,
his hands shaking. Accompanying the terror was a crushing weight of guilt that made him want to
disappear. The memories were also strangely wordless. He knew what had happened, he could see it,
but when he tried to articulate it—even in his own mind—the words scattered like startled birds.
There was no language adequate to what he had done or failed to do.

Because these memories were unbearable, Thomas had begun to construct a careful architecture of
avoidance. He stopped wearing the tan cargo pants he had worn in the service; they triggered
something in him that he could not quite articulate, a sense of being pulled back into that
identity, that failure. He had not returned to the military base where he had been stationed, though
it was only two hours from his home. The thought of driving past those gates, of seeing the
landscape where he had trained, made his chest tight with dread. More insidiously, he had begun to
avoid thinking about the event at all, pushing away any memory or association the moment it arose.
He would change the radio station, leave a room, find a task that demanded his full attention. Carol
had noticed that he became irritable when she tried to talk about his service, and he had stopped
attending the veteran support group he had gone to for a few months.

But the avoidance was not protecting him. Instead, it was feeding the very beliefs that had taken
root in him. Each time he turned away from a memory, each time he refused to let himself think about
what had happened, he was implicitly confirming his deepest fear: that the truth was too dangerous
to face, that the guilt was too heavy to bear, that he was fundamentally unsafe in his own mind. The
more he avoided, the more he felt like a stranger to himself—a man made of evasions and
suppressions, no longer sure what was real or solid in his own experience. And the more distant he
became from his own past, the more the world seemed to close in. He found himself scanning parking
lots before he got out of the car. He slept poorly, alert to sounds. He could not relax with Carol
the way he used to. His conviction that the world was dangerous felt less like a belief and more
like a simple fact, confirmed every time he successfully avoided something that might hurt him. The
avoidance had become a way of life, and the life it was creating was smaller and more isolated with
each passing month.

At sixty, Thomas finally called a therapist. Carol had given him an ultimatum: get help, or she was
leaving. He had sat in the parking lot for fifteen minutes before going in, terrified that talking
about Kandahar would break something in him that could not be repaired. What he wanted most was to
feel like himself again, to know who that person was. What he feared most was that there was no
going back—that the guilt and the terror and the wordlessness were all that remained.
