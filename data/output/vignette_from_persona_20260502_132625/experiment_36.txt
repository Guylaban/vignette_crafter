# Clinical Vignette: Margaret

Margaret was sixty-five years old when she came to the clinic, a retired Army medic who had spent
thirty-two years in uniform before a civilian accident three years prior had unraveled the careful
architecture of her life. The accident itself had been sudden and violent: she had been driving home
from the grocery store on a Tuesday afternoon when a delivery truck ran a red light at forty miles
per hour, T-boning her sedan on the driver's side. The impact had been catastrophic—metal screaming,
glass exploding inward, her body thrown sideways against the door frame. She had hung suspended in
shock for what felt like minutes but was likely seconds, unable to move, certain she was dying. A
passerby had called 911. The medics who arrived found her conscious but trapped, her left arm
pinned, her ribs fractured. She had spent six weeks in the hospital. Physically, she had healed. The
psychological wound had not.

For months after discharge, Margaret had tried to resume her life as though nothing fundamental had
changed. She had been a medic; she understood trauma. She knew the physiology of fear. But knowing
it and living it were different things entirely. Every time she sat in a car—even as a passenger—her
body would flood with adrenaline. Her heart would race. Her hands would grip the seat. She had come
to believe that another accident was not a possibility but an inevitability, that the roads were
inherently dangerous and she had simply been lucky the first time. When she heard the unexpected
screech of tires or the blare of a horn, her entire nervous system would jolt as though she were
back in that moment of impact. The sound of an engine revving, a car door slamming—anything sharp
and automotive—would send her into a state of hypervigilance that could last for hours. Her body
felt as though it were perpetually braced for collision, muscles taut, jaw clenched, her attention
split between whatever she was doing and the constant scanning for threat.

The triggers had multiplied in ways she could not fully control. It was not just the sound of cars;
it was the sight of them. She had noticed, in the weeks after the accident, that certain
vehicles—silver sedans, delivery trucks, anything that resembled the truck that had hit her—would
catch her eye and send a spike of panic through her chest. Even cars that merely looked similar in
shape or color could destabilize her. She had stopped driving altogether within the first year.
Taking the bus meant sitting near windows, watching traffic, her vigilance exhausting. She had begun
to avoid leaving the house for anything non-essential, and when she did venture out, she felt
perpetually unsafe, her body on high alert, waiting for the next catastrophe.

The memories themselves had become their own form of torment. When the images would surface—and they
did, unbidden, several times a day—she would feel as though she were actually in the car again, the
impact happening in real time. Her body would respond as though the danger were present: her chest
would tighten, her breath would catch, her left arm would ache with phantom pain. She knew
intellectually that she was safe, sitting in her living room, but the knowing made no difference to
her nervous system. The memory did not feel like a memory; it felt like the event itself, visceral
and immediate and overwhelming. When she tried to talk about it in those early months, the emotion
would surge so quickly that she could not find the words—she would open her mouth and nothing would
come except a kind of strangled sound, and then she would have to stop, to turn away, to compose
herself. She still carried that wordlessness. Even now, three years later, when asked to describe
what had happened, she found herself unable to articulate the full sequence; the narrative would
fragment, and she would feel the flood of feeling rising again.

In the face of this constant internal chaos, Margaret had developed strategies to manage the
intolerable. She had discovered that if she ate—not merely ate, but consumed large quantities of
food, often late at night—the anxiety would dull slightly. The act of eating became a ritual of
self-soothing, a way to press down the rising panic. She would find herself in the kitchen at
midnight, standing in front of the refrigerator, reaching for whatever was there, not because she
was hungry but because the act of chewing, of filling her mouth, of swallowing, created a small
pocket of distraction from the dread. She had gained forty pounds in the first year. She had also
learned to distract herself deliberately—turning on the television, losing herself in books,
immersing herself in online games for hours at a time, anything to keep her mind from drifting back
to that Tuesday afternoon. These distractions worked, but only temporarily. When she stopped, the
anxiety would rush back, often worse than before, as though it had been gathering force in the
background.

The suppression of thought had become almost automatic. Whenever the memory began to surface, she
would clamp down on it, would force her mind in another direction, would tell herself not to think
about it. But the effort of not thinking about something only seemed to make it more present, more
insistent. The thoughts would return with greater intensity, and she would have to work harder to
push them away, creating an exhausting cycle of internal resistance. This constant mental effort had
left her depleted. She had withdrawn from her friends, from her daughter who lived out of state,
from the volunteer work at the VA hospital that had once given her purpose. Her world had contracted
to the size of her house, and even within those walls, she was not at peace.

What had finally brought her to the clinic was a moment of clarity that had frightened her more than
the accident itself. She had been sitting in her living room, a television show playing in the
background, when she realized she could not remember what she had done that day. The hours had
simply vanished. She had looked down and found crumbs on her shirt, evidence of eating she did not
recall. The thought had struck her then—sudden and cold—that she was disappearing into her own
survival strategies, that the life she was living was barely a life at all. She was sixty-five years
old, a widow for eight years already, and she had become a prisoner in her own home, held hostage by
a fear she could not seem to control. She wanted to understand why her mind and body would not let
her move forward. She wanted to know if there was a way to reclaim some version of the person she
had been before that truck ran the light.
