# Chukwu's Reckoning

Chukwu was nineteen when he came to the clinic, though he carried himself with the careful precision
of someone much older. He had moved to the States from Lagos five years prior, the youngest of four,
sent by his family to build something better. He'd done well—straight through high school, a job at
a mid-size accounting firm downtown, a modest apartment he shared with a roommate. The kind of
trajectory that was supposed to feel like victory. But six months ago, everything had fractured in a
way that no amount of professional success could repair.

It happened on a Friday evening in March. Chukwu had stayed late at the office to finish a client
reconciliation. The building was nearly empty by the time he locked his desk and headed to the
parking garage. He'd taken the elevator to the third level and was walking toward his car when he
heard the stairwell door open behind him. Two men emerged. He didn't recognize them—construction
workers, maybe, or maintenance staff—but something in their gait made him uneasy. He walked faster.
One of them called out. When he didn't respond, they followed. What happened next unfolded with a
terrible swiftness: a hand grabbing his arm, his body slammed against the concrete, the taste of
blood and oil in his mouth as his face scraped the ground, the weight of two bodies holding him
down, the sound of his own voice breaking as he tried to call for help. The physical pain was
immediate and overwhelming, but it was the violation—the absolute theft of his body's autonomy—that
shattered something deeper.

In the months that followed, Chukwu had tried to return to normal. He'd filed a police report that
went nowhere. He'd told his roommate nothing, had lied to his parents about why he sounded distant
on their weekly calls. But his body had learned something new, something that made every ordinary
moment feel laden with danger. Loud voices—a colleague raising their tone in a meeting, a customer
shouting over the phone—sent him into a state of raw alarm. His chest would tighten, his breath
would shallow, and he would find himself back in that garage, bracing for impact. The shouting
itself became a portal: it triggered not just fear but the full sensory rush of the memory—the
visual flashes of the concrete floor beneath his face, the taste of blood returning to his mouth,
the crushing weight on his back. Each time someone raised their voice near him, the memories came
flooding back, and with them, a certainty that danger was imminent.

Being alone with men had become intolerable. At work, he began requesting to handle client calls
only with female colleagues present, inventing reasons to avoid one-on-one meetings with his male
supervisor. When his roommate's friends came over, Chukwu would lock himself in his bedroom. The
presence of an unfamiliar man in ordinary space—in a grocery store, on the street, in any enclosed
room—felt like a direct threat. His mind had fused the image of those two men in the garage with
every stranger's face, every unexpected male presence a potential harbinger of violence. This
hypervigilance fed the memories: the more threatened he felt, the more vividly the images returned.
A man stepping into the elevator with him would trigger an intrusive flash of the stairwell door
opening, the visual memory so sharp and detailed that he could almost feel the impact again.

Doors themselves had become dangerous. The sound of a door slamming in his apartment building could
paralyze him for minutes. The opening of an unfamiliar door—a conference room he'd never entered, a
client's office—sent a spike of dread through his body. Doors were how they had found him; doors
were how danger announced itself. When a door opened unexpectedly, the memory would surge: not
always a complete image, but sometimes just the raw emotional terror of that moment, the full
intensity of violation flooding through him without a clear picture attached, as if his body
remembered what his mind couldn't quite hold. And when he felt that terror—that disembodied panic—he
would find himself hyperalert, searching for threats, certain that something catastrophic was about
to happen. His threat system had become a closed loop: the memory amplified the fear, the fear made
him search for danger, and every unexpected stimulus seemed to confirm that danger was real.

Unfamiliar places had become impossible. He couldn't assess the exits, couldn't predict where
threats might emerge, couldn't trust that he would be safe. His world had contracted to a handful of
known spaces—his apartment, his office, the route between them. When his firm asked him to visit a
new client site across town, he'd called in sick. When a colleague invited him to a work happy hour
at a bar he'd never been to, he'd made an excuse. The avoidance felt necessary, protective, but it
was also isolating him in ways he couldn't quite articulate to anyone. He hadn't told his supervisor
about the trauma. He hadn't told his parents. He'd simply begun to disappear from the life he'd
built, shrinking his world to the spaces where he could feel even remotely in control.

By the time he came to the clinic, Chukwu was exhausted. The nightmares had become less frequent,
but the daytime intrusions hadn't stopped. He was calling in sick to work more often. His supervisor
had begun asking questions. He was afraid—deeply, bone-level afraid—that if he told anyone the
truth, they would see him as damaged, as weak, as no longer the capable young man he'd worked so
hard to become. What he wanted most was to feel safe again in his own body, in his own city. What
terrified him was that he might never be able to trust the world the way he had before.
