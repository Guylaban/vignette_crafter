# Clinical Vignette: Aotea M.

Aotea M. was forty-two when he came to the clinic, a Samoan electrician whose broad shoulders and
weathered hands spoke of years on job sites across the city. He had been attacked three years ago,
late on a Friday afternoon, in the stairwell of an apartment building where he'd been called to
repair faulty wiring. A man had come up behind him—Aotea still couldn't piece together whether he'd
heard footsteps or simply felt the presence—and struck him repeatedly with a crowbar. The assault
lasted perhaps two minutes, though Aotea experienced it as a series of disconnected moments: the
first blow to his ribs, a searing flash; his own voice shouting; the attacker's face, which he could
never quite hold in his mind afterward; the weight of his own body hitting concrete. The man had
fled with Aotea's wallet. Aotea had lain there, gasping, until a neighbor found him twenty minutes
later.

For the first year after the assault, Aotea had pushed through. He returned to work within six
weeks, told his partner Marcus that he was fine, went through the motions of recovery as though it
were simply another job to be completed. But something had fractured in him that afternoon in the
stairwell, and the fracture had been widening ever since. By the time he sought help, Aotea had
become a prisoner of time itself. Each day at four-fifteen in the afternoon—the exact moment the
attack had begun—his body would seize. His heart would race, his palms would sweat, and an
overwhelming sense of unreality would wash over him, as though he were watching his own life from
behind glass. He had come to dread that hour with an intensity that colored his entire week. The
feeling of detachment that accompanied it was particularly unbearable; Aotea described it as being
"hollowed out," present in his body but not quite inhabiting it, and this sensation would pull him
back into the fractured moments of the assault itself—the snapshot of his attacker's face, the
sensation of impact, the helplessness—each memory arriving not as a coherent narrative but as
isolated, vivid stills that he couldn't quite connect into a whole picture. What was missing from
those memories disturbed him almost as much as what remained: he couldn't remember the color of the
attacker's shirt, couldn't reconstruct how long the assault had actually lasted, couldn't account
for certain moments that he knew must have occurred but felt entirely absent from his mind.

When the late afternoon approached, Aotea would feel the old conviction settling into his chest:
that he was fundamentally the kind of person this happened to, that someone else—someone more aware,
more cautious, more worthy—would never have found himself alone in that stairwell. The guilt that
accompanied his fragmented memories was suffocating; he felt ashamed of things he couldn't even
fully remember, as though the gaps themselves were indictments of his character. These crushing
thoughts would then amplify his sense of danger in the world. Any unexpected movement—a colleague
turning suddenly on a job site, a neighbor opening a door—would trigger an adrenaline surge that
left him shaking, convinced he was about to be attacked again. When Marcus would touch his shoulder
without warning, Aotea would flinch violently, his nervous system interpreting the contact as a
threat. He began avoiding unfamiliar places entirely, reasoning that if he couldn't assess the
danger, he couldn't protect himself, a logic that felt airtight to him even as it narrowed his
world. The fragments of the assault would intrude during these moments of heightened threat, each
memory snapshot reinforcing his belief that he was helpless, that he had always been helpless.

To manage the approaching four-fifteen hour and the cascade of memories and fear it triggered, Aotea
had developed a system of escape. He would spend hours scrolling through his phone, playing games
that demanded total attention, losing himself in the internet in ways that felt necessary for
survival. He had also begun drinking more heavily—a beer or two in the afternoon, sometimes
something stronger—and had asked his doctor for sleeping medication, which he took liberally. These
strategies quieted the intrusive thoughts temporarily, but they had their own cost. His work had
begun to suffer; he'd missed deadlines, made mistakes on installations that he would never have made
before. Marcus had grown distant, hurt by Aotea's emotional unavailability and his increasing
absences into screens and substances. More troubling still, the avoidance seemed to create a cruel
feedback loop: the more hours Aotea spent numbing himself, the more his mind would sharpen its focus
on the very things he was trying to escape. When he finally put the phone down or the alcohol wore
off, the memories would feel fresher, more intrusive, and his conviction that he was irredeemably
flawed—that he deserved what had happened—would deepen, which in turn would make him more
hypervigilant, more reactive to any sound or movement, which would drive him back to the screen, the
drink, the medication, the endless cycle of avoidance that left him feeling simultaneously trapped
and unable to stop.

Aotea arrived at the clinic on a Tuesday morning, pale and exhausted. What had finally pushed him to
seek help was a panic attack so severe that he'd collapsed at work, and Marcus's ultimatum: get help
or watch him leave. Aotea sat in the waiting room with his hands clenched, certain that no one could
help him, convinced that he was broken in ways that therapy couldn't fix, and terrified most of all
that if he stopped running from the memories, they would consume him entirely.
