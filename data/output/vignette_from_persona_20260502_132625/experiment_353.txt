# Dr. Carlos Reyes

Dr. Carlos Reyes had been a cardiologist for thirty-eight years when his daughter, Sofia, was seven
years old and stopped breathing in the night. He found her in her bed—blue-lipped, gasping, her
small chest heaving as if her lungs had forgotten their work. The ambulance ride was a blur of
sirens and his own voice, hoarse and foreign, shouting her name. The pediatric ICU materialized
around him: machines that beeped in rhythms he could not predict, lines running into her arm, a
ventilator that did the breathing she could not do. He remembered the resident's face—young,
tired—explaining acute respiratory distress syndrome, the word "septic" appearing somewhere in the
chaos. He remembered standing at the foot of her bed, a doctor in his own right, utterly powerless.
The memory of those three days fractured in his mind like a photograph dropped on tile: he could
recall the smell of the unit, the weight of his hand on Sofia's forehead, her eyes opening once and
finding his. But the sequence of events, the precise moment her fever spiked, the exact words the
attending used when she finally began to improve—these scattered like water. Even now, twenty years
later, when he tried to reconstruct what had happened, the narrative dissolved. He knew what
occurred, but the words would not come. There was only a gap where coherence should have been, a
space he could not fill even in his own mind.

Because he could not hold the story together, could not see it whole, Carlos had learned to keep it
at a distance. He pushed away any thought that threatened to surface—a memory of the ventilator's
hiss, Sofia's small hand in his. He worked harder, filled every hour with patient rounds and
research papers and committees, anything to keep his mind occupied, moving, away from the edges of
that week. His colleagues knew him as tireless, a man who seemed to live at the hospital. What they
did not know was that the moment he was alone, the moment his hands stopped moving, the terror crept
back. So he did not allow himself to be alone. He did not allow himself to think.

But the avoidance had its own cost. Twenty years of not thinking about it, of mental distraction, of
suppressing the images that tried to surface, had left him hyperalert in ways he could not explain
to himself. He was superaware now, watching, always watching—the way a patient's oxygen saturation
dipped on the monitor, the slight change in a child's color during rounds, the tremor in a parent's
voice when they spoke to him about their sick child. Every small sign felt pregnant with
catastrophe. His body lived in a state of readiness, muscles tense, waiting for the next crisis. And
then, without warning, something would pierce through—the sound of a ventilator alarm in the ICU, a
child's cough in the supermarket, the particular slant of afternoon light through a hospital
window—and suddenly he was back there. He was not thinking about it anymore; he was *in* it. His
heart would race, his hands would shake, and for a moment he could not remember where he was or
whether Sofia was still seven years old and struggling to breathe.

The flashbacks terrified him because they felt like evidence that the threat was real, that
catastrophe was not something that had happened once but something that could happen again, at any
moment, to anyone he loved. So he watched harder. He stayed alert. He kept moving, kept his mind
busy, kept the story locked away where he could not see it clearly enough to be destroyed by it. But
the more he avoided thinking about what had happened, the more fragmented the memory became, and the
more fragmented it became, the more urgent it felt to keep it suppressed—because an incomplete,
incoherent memory of trauma was somehow more terrifying than one he could hold and examine and,
perhaps, survive.

At sixty-seven, Carlos had never married again after Sofia's mother left. He had no other children.
He had colleagues but no close friends, no one he allowed to know him beyond the white coat. Three
months ago, a young patient—a girl of seven with pneumonia—had spent two weeks in his hospital. She
recovered. But Carlos had not. He found himself unable to concentrate, unable to sleep, unable to
stop scanning the ward for signs of deterioration. He began having panic attacks in the physician's
lounge. Last week, he called his primary care doctor and admitted, with difficulty, that he could
not continue like this. He was seeking help now because the avoidance was no longer working, because
the hypervigilance had become a cage, and because he was afraid that if he did not find a way to
face what he had locked away, it would consume what remained of his life.
