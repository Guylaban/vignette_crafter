# Clinical Vignette: Kofi

Kofi was fifty-nine when his younger brother Kwesi drowned during a family trip to Lake Michigan in
the summer of 2019. The outing had been Kofi's idea—a rare moment of levity before the autumn
semester began, a chance for the extended family to gather on the public beach near Chicago. Kofi, a
tenured professor of African history at Northwestern, had always prided himself on his careful
planning, his ability to manage risk and create safe spaces for those around him. That afternoon, he
was standing near the shore with his wife at the time, watching Kwesi wade into the water with his
teenage daughter. The weather had shifted suddenly; the sky darkened, and the water grew choppy in a
way that made Kofi uneasy. He called out, but his voice was lost in the wind and the sound of the
waves. Within minutes, Kwesi had slipped into a deeper channel. By the time the lifeguard reached
him, it was too late. Kofi watched the entire sequence unfold—the moment his brother's head went
under, the thrashing that stopped, the terrible stillness that followed. The smell of the water that
day, thick with algae and sediment, had burned into his memory with a clarity that four years had
not diminished.

In the months after Kwesi's death, Kofi's grief was profound but initially manageable. He delivered
the eulogy, made the arrangements, supported his elderly mother through her collapse. But by late
autumn, something had shifted. Any mention of water—a news report about flooding, a student's casual
reference to visiting a lake—would trigger an immediate, almost violent response in his body. His
heart would race, his palms would sweat, and a wave of nausea would sweep through him before his
mind had even consciously registered what he'd heard. He came to dread these moments, never knowing
when they would arrive. A student once described a rainy weekend in his lecture hall, and Kofi felt
his chest tighten so suddenly that he had to excuse himself, his body already in a state of alarm
before he'd even fully processed the trigger. The unpredictability of these reactions terrified
him—not the fear itself, but the loss of control, the sense that his body had become an unreliable
instrument that could betray him at any moment.

Over time, Kofi began to organize his life around the avoidance of these bodily storms. He stopped
attending departmental social events held near the university's waterfront. He declined invitations
to visit friends who lived near the lake. When the weather forecast predicted heavy rain, he would
feel a preemptive dread settle over him, his body tensing in anticipation of the smell of wet earth
and moisture that would inevitably follow. He became hypervigilant about his environment, scanning
for anything water-related before entering a room. The smell of algae, of rain on pavement, of the
particular mustiness that comes from humidity—these scents would instantly transport him back to
that beach, his nervous system flooding with the same terror he'd felt four years earlier. His body
would react first, seizing with the certainty that danger was imminent, and only afterward would his
thinking mind catch up, reminding him that he was safe, that this was just a smell, just a memory.
But the knowing didn't help. The threat felt real because his body insisted it was real, and his
body's conviction was now the dominant truth in his life.

His colleagues had begun to notice his withdrawal. He'd turned down the opportunity to lead a
departmental research initiative because it involved a conference in Miami. He was sleeping poorly,
his mind unable to settle even in the safety of his apartment, his body remaining tense and vigilant
long into the night. Kofi had come to believe that his body was fundamentally unreliable, that
danger could emerge from anywhere—a student's story, a smell on the wind, a weather forecast—and
that his only defense was to anticipate it, to avoid it, to keep himself as isolated and controlled
as possible. The irony was not lost on him: in trying to protect himself from the unpredictability
of his own nervous system, he had become a prisoner to it. It was this recognition, finally, that
brought him to seek help—not hope that things could change, but a desperate need to understand why
his body had become his enemy.
