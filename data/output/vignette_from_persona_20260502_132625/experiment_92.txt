# Margaret Chen

Margaret Chen had always been someone who paid attention to details—it was what made her a good
network engineer, what had kept her sharp through four decades of managing infrastructure that
thousands of people depended on. She was seventy-two now, still working part-time, still the person
colleagues called when something critical went down. Her husband Robert said she had the kind of
mind that couldn't stop noticing things, couldn't stop planning three steps ahead. That quality had
served her well until the afternoon eighteen months ago when she was waiting for a bus on Fifth and
Market, heading home from the office early because of a migraine.

The bus had pulled up crowded, the usual Thursday rush. Margaret had stepped aboard, found a spot
near the middle, and was reaching for the overhead rail when the young man in front of her suddenly
collapsed. He went down hard, his head striking the metal pole with a sound she would never unhear.
For three terrible seconds, no one moved. Then someone screamed. A woman knelt beside him, and
Margaret could see the blood pooling beneath his head, could see his eyes rolling back. A seizure,
someone said. A stroke. An aneurysm. The bus driver was on the radio. Margaret stood frozen, her
hand still gripping the rail, watching this stranger's life unraveling in public, watching the
helplessness of everyone around him, the terrible randomness of it. He was unconscious before the
ambulance arrived.

In the weeks that followed, Margaret found herself unable to ride the bus. The thought of stepping
onto one sent her heart racing, flooded her with a sense that something catastrophic was waiting to
happen—not just to her, but to anyone unlucky enough to be nearby. She had come to believe that the
world was fundamentally dangerous in ways she had never truly grasped before, that safety was an
illusion, that you could be standing in an ordinary moment and have it shattered by something
completely beyond your control. This belief had taken root so deeply that it began to color
everything: crowded places triggered the same dread—shopping centers, the grocery store at peak
hours, even the subway platform near her office. Certain conversation topics did too. When
colleagues mentioned health scares or accidents, when someone at a dinner party started talking
about a relative's sudden illness, Margaret felt her chest tighten. These conversations seemed to
confirm what she now knew: the world was not safe, and terrible things happened without warning.

To manage the anxiety that flooded through her whenever these triggers surfaced, Margaret had
developed a habit of forcing herself to think only positive thoughts. When she felt the dread rising
in a crowded place, she would mentally recite a list of good things—her marriage, her garden, a
recent trip to the coast—as though sheer willpower could inoculate her against disaster. But the
strategy had a peculiar effect. By consciously suppressing the fear, by refusing to acknowledge the
danger she felt lurking everywhere, she found herself more hypervigilant, more convinced that she
had to be especially careful because you never know what can happen next. The very act of pushing
down the anxiety seemed to confirm that the threat was real and present, just hidden. And that
heightened vigilance, that constant monitoring for signs of danger, made her even more sensitive to
potential triggers. A crowded shopping center became not just a place to avoid but a place she found
herself scanning obsessively on the rare occasions she ventured in—counting exits, noting who looked
unstable, imagining collapse scenarios.

She had also begun avoiding the site of the event itself, taking a different route to work, never
boarding a bus again. She drove instead, adding forty minutes to her commute, but the avoidance
brought a temporary sense of safety that felt essential. Yet as the months passed, Margaret realized
she had become isolated in a different way. She had stopped going to the farmer's market on Saturday
mornings. She declined her book club's invitation to meet downtown. She had come to see herself as
permanently changed, marked by what she had witnessed—an outsider now to the ordinary life she and
Robert had built. Her friends didn't understand why she couldn't just move past it. Robert tried to
be patient, but she could feel the strain, the unspoken frustration when she declined another social
invitation, when she insisted they drive across town to avoid a particular route.

What finally brought her to seek help was a conversation with her daughter, who had noticed her
mother's increasing isolation and asked directly: "Mom, are you okay? You're not yourself anymore."
Margaret realized she couldn't answer honestly without admitting how trapped she felt—caught between
the certainty that danger was everywhere and the exhausting effort required to pretend it wasn't.
She was afraid that therapy wouldn't work, that nothing could undo what she now knew about the
world. But she was also afraid of disappearing entirely into this smaller and smaller life, of
becoming someone her husband and family no longer recognized.
