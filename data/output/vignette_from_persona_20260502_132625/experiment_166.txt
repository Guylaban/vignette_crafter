# Arjun

Arjun was thirty-six when he first came to therapy, though the lines around his eyes and the careful
way he held his shoulders made him seem older. He worked as an accountant for a mid-sized firm in
suburban New Jersey, the kind of role that suited his methodical mind and his need for
control—spreadsheets did not surprise you, did not raise their voices, did not suddenly turn
violent. But control had become his prison, and the very habits that once protected him were now
suffocating him.

The trauma lived in his childhood, in a house in Gujarat before his family immigrated when he was
seven. He could not quite assemble the memory into a coherent narrative, though fragments surfaced
unbidden: his mother's cry from the kitchen, his father's shadow looming across the hallway, the
sound of breaking glass that seemed to splinter something inside Arjun's own body. He remembered
hiding behind the sofa, his small hands pressed against his ears, and the terrible knowledge that he
could do nothing to stop what was happening. He was too small, too powerless, too young. By the time
his parents divorced years later—a separation that came as relief but also as confirmation that he
had been right to be afraid—Arjun had internalized a conviction that would shadow him for decades:
the people who had caused this suffering had never truly been held accountable. His father had
simply left, remarried, built a new life. The violence had been absorbed into the family's silence,
wrapped in shame and never spoken of again. And Arjun, left with the wreckage of his mother's grief
and his own fractured sense of safety, had come to believe that bad things happened to people like
him, that he was fundamentally unable to prevent disaster.

This helplessness had metastasized into a broader despair. He used to be happy—he remembered that
about himself, though the memory felt like something from someone else's life. His mother had
photographs of him as a boy, grinning at birthday parties, but those photographs belonged to a
different Arjun, one who had not yet learned that the world was a place where fathers hurt mothers
and children were powerless to stop it. Now, at thirty-six, he was always miserable. The happiness
had drained away so gradually he could not pinpoint when it left, but it was gone, replaced by a
low, persistent dread that colored everything. This despair fed his sense of threat. Because he
believed he could not protect himself, because he felt certain that consequences never came for the
people who hurt others, his body remained in a state of constant vigilance. He was always scanning,
always alert, muscles perpetually tensed as though bracing for the next blow. His nervous system had
learned that danger was real and that he was defenseless against it.

The memories themselves were both the trigger and the consequence of this state. When something in
his surroundings—a raised voice in the office, the sound of shattering dishes when a colleague
dropped a mug in the break room, even the particular quality of late afternoon light through a
window—pulled him back to that moment behind the sofa, the terror returned with full force. It was
not a memory he could think about calmly; it was a reliving, a physiological rupture. His heart
would race, his hands would shake, and for a few seconds he would not know where he was. These
intrusions were wordless—he could not describe them to anyone, could barely articulate them to
himself. But once they seized him, they reinforced everything he believed about himself and the
world. The terror confirmed that danger was real and ever-present. The fact that he could not
control when these memories arrived, that they ambushed him without warning, deepened his conviction
that he was helpless, that bad things happened to him without mercy or logic.

This vicious spiral had cost him his marriage. His ex-wife had tried for years to reach him, to
understand why he flinched at loud noises, why he could not be spontaneous, why he seemed to be
disappearing into himself. But Arjun could not explain it—the memories were too fragmented, the fear
too overwhelming to articulate. His hypervigilance, which had once felt like necessary protection,
had become a wall between them. He monitored her every mood, her every tone, waiting for the moment
when safety would shatter. She had finally left, and though part of him understood that his own
behavior had driven her away, another part of him had simply confirmed what he already knew: that he
could not prevent loss, that he was incapable of keeping people safe or holding onto happiness.

He came to therapy now because the spiral had tightened to the point where he could barely function.
His work had suffered; he made mistakes he would never have made before, his concentration fractured
by the constant state of alert. He sat in the waiting room with his hands clasped tightly in his
lap, afraid that if he let himself relax for even a moment, something catastrophic would happen.
What he wanted most was to feel safe again, to believe that he could have some say in what happened
to him. What he feared most was that this was simply who he was now—broken, helpless, forever
waiting for the next disaster.
