# Rosa

Rosa had been at her design studio on a Tuesday morning in March when her phone rang. It was her
daughter calling from the hospital in San Juan—Rosa's sister, Magdalena, had been struck by a car at
an intersection near her home. Rosa remembered the exact moment the words became real: the
designer's shears in her hand falling to the fabric table, the assistant's voice asking if she was
alright. Magdalena had lingered for three days before the injuries overwhelmed her body. Rosa had
been there when it happened, had watched from the curb as the vehicle hit, had seen her sister
thrown onto the pavement with a sound she could not unhear. What haunted Rosa most was the moment
just before—Magdalena had stepped into the street, and Rosa, standing beside her, had hesitated a
fraction of a second before reaching for her arm. That hesitation, Rosa was certain, had cost her
sister's life. If she had moved faster, if she had been more alert, if she had not been distracted
by the fabric samples she'd been discussing, Magdalena would still be alive. The driver had never
been charged; a technicality in the Puerto Rican legal system had rendered the accident a civil
matter, not a criminal one. No one had been held accountable.

In the months that followed, Rosa's grief became entangled with a suffocating sense of culpability.
She had failed to protect the person she loved most. This conviction—that she bore responsibility
for the tragedy—began to shape how she moved through the world. When a news report about a
pedestrian accident appeared on her television, the self-recrimination would surge immediately: *If
I had been there, if I had been faster, if I had not failed.* The news itself became dangerous. She
began avoiding it entirely, turning off the radio in her car, asking her husband Miguel not to read
aloud from his newspaper at breakfast. But the avoidance only sharpened her vigilance; without
information, she imagined worse scenarios, and her mind would drift back to that intersection, to
Magdalena's face in the moment before impact.

What Rosa had not anticipated was how her own body would betray her. In late summer, she was fitting
a client for a wedding dress when the air conditioning kicked in, and a sudden chill ran across her
bare arms. The temperature—cool, unexpected, a sensation of exposure—sent her backward into the
trauma with a vividness that left her gasping. She was on that street again, the sun hot on her
shoulders, her sister beside her. The sensory completeness was absolute: the smell of exhaust, the
sound of the horn, the feeling of helplessness as her hand moved too slowly to reach Magdalena's
sleeve. The memory had forced its way in without warning, and with it came the crushing weight of
her own failure. She had not protected her sister. She should have. And now, whenever a breeze
touched her skin or the temperature shifted unexpectedly, Rosa felt the threat returning—not just
the memory, but the judgment embedded within it, the knowledge that she had been found wanting in
the moment that mattered most.

She began to avoid anything that might trigger this cycle. Reminders of Magdalena—photographs, her
sister's favorite songs, even the color of the dress she had been wearing—were carefully removed
from Rosa's sight. When her daughter called to say she was pregnant, Rosa felt a strange, sickening
resistance to joy. She could not allow herself to feel happy; happiness felt like a betrayal of her
sister's memory, and more than that, it felt dangerous. If she let herself feel safe, felt herself
smile, something terrible would happen again. She had learned this lesson once already. Her husband
noticed her pulling away from him, her refusal to attend family gatherings, her habit of leaving the
room when the news came on. He did not understand that these were necessary protections, that by
avoiding the triggers and the grief reminders and the dangerous feeling of contentment, Rosa was
preventing further catastrophe. What he saw was his wife of forty-three years becoming smaller, more
isolated, more consumed by something he could not reach.

Now, at seventy, Rosa came to her first therapy appointment carrying the weight of nearly two years
of this isolation. She was afraid that if she stopped avoiding, if she allowed the memories to
surface and the feelings to flow, she would discover that her guilt was justified—that she truly had
failed, and that no amount of grief or punishment could undo it. What she hoped, though she barely
dared to say it aloud, was that someone might help her understand why a moment of human hesitation
had become a life sentence of blame.
