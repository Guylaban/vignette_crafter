# Linh's Story

Linh was twenty years old when she enlisted, and twenty-one when everything fractured. She had grown
up in a close-knit Vietnamese American community in Southern California, the eldest daughter of a
seamstress and a mechanic who had rebuilt their lives after arriving as refugees in the 1990s. Her
parents had wanted her to study business or nursing, but Linh felt called to something larger—a way
to belong fully to the country that had taken in her family, to prove something to herself about
strength and discipline. She completed basic training with distinction, sharp and focused, the kind
of soldier her drill sergeant said had real grit. By her twenty-first birthday, she was stationed at
a forward operating base in the Middle East.

On the night of March 14th, three soldiers—two from her unit, one from another—came into the female
barracks after midnight. She had been asleep. What happened next exists in her mind now as a series
of fragmented images: a hand over her mouth, the smell of alcohol and sweat, the ceiling of the
barracks, the feeling of her body no longer belonging to her. The assault lasted perhaps twenty
minutes. Afterward, there was silence and the sound of boots leaving. She lay there in the dark, not
moving, not quite believing the body in the bunk was hers.

The weeks that followed were a blur of reporting, investigation, and a pervasive sense of unreality.
Linh would lie awake at night and think: *This didn't happen to me. This is something that happened
to someone else, and I'm watching it from outside.* The memory came to her mostly as
images—fragmented pictures, like a corrupted video file—rather than as a coherent story she could
tell. She couldn't quite make the pieces fit into a narrative that felt true. *It's too extreme,*
she would think. *It can't have actually happened this way.* Yet the images were vivid, intrusive,
arriving without warning.

Within weeks, Linh began to feel like a stranger to herself. The woman who had enlisted with such
certainty—disciplined, proud, connected to something larger—had vanished. In her place was someone
raw and broken, someone who flinched at loud noises and couldn't sleep without all the lights on.
She had started to believe that someone else—someone stronger, someone more alert—would have fought
back, would have stopped it from happening. This thought tormented her. She also felt utterly
severed from her family and the Vietnamese community that had always anchored her. When her mother
called from home, Linh could barely speak. She couldn't explain why, couldn't bear to tell her what
had happened, and the silence between them felt like a chasm. She had become unrecognisable even to
herself.

To manage the fragmentary images and the crushing sense of shame and failure, Linh developed a
pattern of coping that made sense in the moment but slowly tightened around her. When the visual
memories surfaced—the ceiling, the hands, the darkness—she would reach for food. She would eat
mechanically, almost without tasting, using the sensation and distraction to push the images back
down. She would also force her mind elsewhere, thinking obsessively about her daily tasks, about
logistics and schedules, anything to redirect her attention away from the assault. But the more she
tried to suppress the memory through these strategies, the more she found herself ruminating,
replaying the details over and over, trying desperately to make sense of what had happened and why
she hadn't stopped it. The overeating and the constant mental redirection became her rhythm—a cycle
that brought temporary relief but deepened her conviction that she was fundamentally defective, that
a better person wouldn't need these crutches, that her failure to prevent the assault proved
something irreparable about who she was.

By the time Linh requested a mental health appointment six months after the assault, she had gained
nearly thirty pounds and had withdrawn almost entirely from her unit. Her commanding officer had
noticed her performance slipping; her marriage to Marcus, whom she'd married just before deployment,
was strained by her emotional distance and her inability to explain why she couldn't be touched. She
was caught in a loop where the fragmented, alien quality of her memories drove her toward
distraction and food, which in turn fed her sense that she was broken and unrecognisable, which
reinforced her belief that she alone should have prevented this, that others would have been
stronger. She came to her first session exhausted, her hands trembling slightly, and told the
psychologist: *"I don't even know who I am anymore. And I can't stop thinking about it, no matter
how hard I try to forget."*
