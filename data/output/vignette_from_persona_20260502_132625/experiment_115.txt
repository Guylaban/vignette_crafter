# Koa's Story

Koa Tanaka was forty-five when he came to therapy, though the lines around his eyes and the careful
way he moved through the world made him look older. He had been a chef for twenty-three years—the
kind of chef who knew his craft so completely that his hands moved through prep work like
meditation, who could taste a sauce and know instantly what it needed, who had built a reputation in
his city for the kind of food that made people feel held. That identity had been the bedrock of his
life since he'd immigrated from Samoa as a young man. It was who he was when he walked into the
kitchen each morning, and for a long time, it had been enough.

The rape happened on a night in March, nearly two years ago now. He had closed the restaurant late
and was walking to his car in the parking lot behind the building when a man emerged from between
two vehicles. The details came in fragments when Koa tried to speak about them—he could describe the
cold of the asphalt, the smell of the man's breath, the way his own voice had stopped working, but
when he tried to arrange them into sequence, they scattered. He remembered being thrown against the
car door. He remembered the man's weight. He remembered the sound of his own ribs hitting metal, and
then—nothing clear, just flashes that wouldn't arrange themselves into order. The memory was there,
accessible in pieces, but it resisted coherence. When he tried to push further back into it, to
gather the full narrative, something in him recoiled. He would feel the memory starting to surface
and his mind would simply turn away, like a hand pulling back from flame.

The physical injuries healed within weeks. But something had shifted in how Koa understood himself.
He had come to believe, with a certainty that felt absolute, that he was no longer the man he had
been—that what had happened had fundamentally broken the part of him that had known how to be a
chef, how to lead a kitchen, how to create something. When he returned to work six weeks later, he
found that his hands would shake as he reached for a knife. The contamination he felt was not
metaphorical; it lived in his skin, in the space between his ribs, in the specific geography of his
body. He felt dirty in a way that no shower could touch. And because he carried this conviction—that
he was ruined, that the trauma had destroyed not just his sense of safety but his actual capacity to
do the work that had defined him—he found that when he tried to recall the event itself, when he
tried to make sense of what had happened, his mind would retreat. The belief that he was no longer
capable made the memories themselves unbearable to hold. He could speak about the trauma easily
enough in abstract terms, could recount facts with a kind of clinical detachment, but the moment he
tried to move deeper into the actual sequence of events, to truly remember, the weight of his
conviction about who he had become would pull him away. The memory and the appraisal became locked
together—he couldn't fully access one without confronting the other.

Over the months that followed, Koa began calling in sick to shifts. The kitchen, which had once been
his sanctuary, became a place where he felt exposed and false. His sous chef would ask what was
wrong, and Koa couldn't find words that made sense. He had been married once, briefly, and was
divorced now; there was no one at home to notice the way he had stopped eating properly, the way he
moved through his apartment like a ghost. By the time he sought help, he had taken a leave of
absence from the restaurant. He was afraid that if he stayed much longer, the job would be gone
entirely. But he was more afraid of what it would mean to try to return—to stand in that kitchen and
confront the person he had become, the one he was certain he no longer was.
