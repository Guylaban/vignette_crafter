Anahera was sixty-five now, a project manager at a mid-sized construction firm in Auckland, but the
man who walked into the therapist's office carried the weight of a father who had failed at the
moment it mattered most. Twenty-three years had passed since his daughter Aroha, then seven years
old, collapsed during a school assembly with a fever that would not break. The diagnosis came within
hours: bacterial meningitis. Anahera had been at work when the call came through—a colleague's voice
crackling over the phone, saying only that Aroha was being rushed to Starship Children's Hospital
and that he needed to get there now. He had driven in a daze, his hands shaking on the steering
wheel, replaying in his mind the morning he had sent her to school despite her complaints of a
headache. She had seemed fine. He had been impatient, distracted, thinking only of the project
meeting scheduled for nine o'clock. If he had kept her home. If he had listened. If he had been
paying attention instead of already halfway out the door, she might not have been in that hospital
bed, her small body convulsing, her mother—his then-wife Hemi—weeping beside her.

Aroha had survived, though the illness left her with partial hearing loss and chronic fatigue. The
marriage did not survive. Hemi had blamed him silently for years before finally saying it aloud: he
should have protected their daughter. He should have known. Anahera had internalized that judgment
so thoroughly that it became indistinguishable from his own voice. He came to believe that his
carelessness, his fundamental failure to read the signs and act, had nearly cost him everything.
That belief—that he could not be trusted to do the right thing when it mattered—had metastasized
into every corner of his life.

The triggers were specific and relentless. Any interaction with Aroha's doctors or hospital staff
sent his heart into his throat. Medical procedures—even routine check-ups—became unbearable. His own
health had begun to decline in recent years, and when his GP suggested he attend a colonoscopy
screening, Anahera had felt the familiar breathlessness creeping in, a tightness across his chest as
if the hospital walls were already closing in. He had cancelled the appointment three times. He
could not bring himself to step into a medical facility without feeling as though he were being
pulled back into that terrible week, into the fluorescent-lit corridors and the smell of antiseptic
and the sound of his daughter's labored breathing. When Aroha's name appeared on his phone—a text, a
call—his body would flood with a surge of dread and hypervigilance. Something bad was happening.
Something was wrong. Even when it was not.

What frightened him most was the conviction that he could not trust his own judgment. When delays
occurred—a delayed test result, a late arrival from a friend—his mind would race to catastrophe. His
daughter was ill again. His grandson had been in an accident. The delay itself became proof that
disaster was unfolding. He had come to see the world as a place where his vigilance was all that
stood between his loved ones and harm, yet his vigilance had already failed once, spectacularly, in
the way that mattered most. This contradiction—the certainty that he must protect everyone and the
absolute conviction that he would fail—had become the architecture of his days.

To manage the unbearable anxiety and the intrusive memories of Aroha's illness, Anahera had
developed a rigid system of avoidance and distraction. He kept his mind relentlessly occupied:
podcasts during his commute, projects around the house in the evenings, work emails on his phone at
all hours. He could not sit in silence. He could not allow himself to think about what had happened,
about the choices he had made, about the ways he had failed. Whenever a memory threatened to
surface—the image of Aroha's flushed face, the doctor's grave expression, his own helplessness—he
would push it away forcefully, turning his attention instead to something concrete and controllable.
But the harder he tried not to think about the trauma, the more it seemed to seep into his body. His
shoulders remained perpetually tense. His sleep was fragmented. And the avoidance itself had become
its own form of suffering, narrowing his world, keeping him at a distance from the people he loved
most, including Aroha, whom he could barely look at without shame.

It was his grandson's upcoming medical check-up that had finally brought him to seek help. Anahera
could feel the old patterns rising: the need to control the appointment, to ensure nothing went
wrong, to somehow prevent the repetition of an old tragedy. But he also recognized, with a clarity
that frightened him, that he could not live this way anymore. He was sixty-five, running out of
time, and he had spent more than two decades imprisoned by the belief that he was fundamentally
untrustworthy, that he would always fail the people who depended on him.
