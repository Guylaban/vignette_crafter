# Priya's Vigilance

Priya Desai was thirty-two when the building collapsed. She had been attending a community forum on
zoning ordinances at the Riverside Convention Center—a sprawling mid-century structure that city
planners had been debating for months. As a relatively new member of the City Council, she had
wanted to hear directly from constituents before the vote. She was standing near the entrance,
speaking with a local business owner, when the sound came: not an explosion exactly, but a deep,
structural groan, followed by a percussion of breaking concrete and rending steel that seemed to
come from everywhere at once. The ceiling above the main hall gave way in a cascading collapse. Dust
billowed through the corridors. For several seconds, Priya stood frozen, watching people run,
hearing screams echoing off the remaining walls. She had turned and run toward what she thought was
an exit, her heart hammering so violently she thought it might burst through her ribs. Later, she
would learn that seventy-three people had died. She had been one of the last to leave that wing
before it came down.

In the weeks immediately after, Priya functioned on adrenaline and shock. She attended the memorial
services, gave statements to the press about structural safety oversight, pushed through council
meetings with a kind of grim automaticity. But by the third week, something had shifted. She began
to notice things: the way a heavy truck rumbling past her apartment made her stomach clench; the way
she found herself studying the ceiling in any room, mentally tracing where a collapse might
originate. She had come to believe, with a certainty that felt almost factual, that the world was
fundamentally more dangerous than she had previously understood. This wasn't a passing anxiety—it
was a reorganization of her basic sense of safety. The building had been inspected, deemed safe, and
had failed anyway. How many other structures harbored invisible weaknesses? How many other ordinary
moments contained hidden catastrophe? The conviction settled into her like a stone: she could not
trust what appeared solid.

This understanding of danger as omnipresent and unpredictable began to shape how Priya moved through
her days. Because she was certain that constant vigilance was the only rational response to a world
that could betray you without warning, she found herself scanning every room she entered—noting
exits, assessing the integrity of walls and ceilings, positioning herself in spaces where she could
see the most ground. At council meetings, she sat near the door. When she attended community events,
her eyes moved in a restless circuit around the room, monitoring for threats. She became hyperaware
of people around her, watching for signs of instability or danger in their faces and movements,
though she could not have articulated what she was looking for. At night, the vigilance intensified.
In her apartment, she felt exposed and vulnerable in ways that daylight seemed to mitigate. She
found it harder to sleep, and when she did, her dreams were fractured and violent. She began
checking the locks on her doors and windows multiple times before bed, a ritual that never quite
satisfied the underlying sense of exposure.

The avoidance had accumulated gradually, almost without her noticing. She stopped going to
unfamiliar restaurants or venues, reasoning that she couldn't properly assess the structural safety
of buildings she didn't know. A colleague invited her to a new conference space downtown; Priya
declined, claiming scheduling conflicts. She avoided situations that put physical stress on her
body—she had always run in the mornings, but now the elevated heart rate and heavy breathing felt
indistinguishable from panic, so she stopped. Yoga classes felt too vulnerable; the gym too
unpredictable. When she did go out, she moved with a kind of tense efficiency, her body braced, her
breathing shallow. Her hypervigilance toward people intensified alongside these physical
restrictions. In council chambers, she found herself scrutinizing the structural engineers who
presented reports, searching their faces for signs they were hiding something, that they, too, were
complicit in a world of hidden dangers. Her staff noticed the change. Meetings that had once been
collaborative became more tense; Priya asked more questions, demanded more assurances, seemed
perpetually on edge. One of her longest-serving aides eventually transferred to another department,
citing the constant pressure and surveillance.

What frightened Priya most now was the recognition that her vigilance, while exhausting, felt
necessary—that without it, something terrible would surely happen. She had come to believe that she
had to be especially careful because you never knew what could happen next, and this belief drove
her to maintain her constant scanning, her avoidance of unfamiliar places, her physical withdrawal.
But the vigilance itself had become a kind of prison. She was isolated, exhausted, and her work—the
very thing that had drawn her to public service—had become a source of dread rather than purpose.
Six months after the collapse, she finally reached out to a therapist, hoping that someone could
help her understand how to live in a world that no longer felt survivable.
