# Clinical Vignette: Robert

Robert was sixty-two years old when it happened—a Friday evening in October, leaving the hotel
through the service entrance after closing out the registers. Twenty-three years managing the
Riverside Grand had made him comfortable with routine, perhaps too comfortable. A man in a dark
hoodie had emerged from the loading dock shadows, knife drawn, demanding his wallet and the bank
deposit bag. Robert had complied without resistance, but the man had shoved him hard against the
concrete wall anyway, and in that moment—his face pressed into rough stone, unable to see what was
happening behind him—Robert had felt the full weight of his own powerlessness. The man had fled with
less than two thousand dollars, but he had taken something Robert was only beginning to understand
he could not recover: the assumption that the world operated according to reason, that compliance
meant safety, that age and decades of honest work bought you any protection at all.

Seven months later, Robert sat in the intake session describing a life that had contracted in ways
both obvious and subtle. His belief that people could be trusted had fractured entirely. He was
certain now that most strangers harbored hidden intentions, that the casual friendliness of hotel
guests masked potential threat. The humiliation of being overpowered—of having his body used against
him, of being found on the pavement by a bellhop—had settled into his bones like a chronic ache. He
carried the conviction that he was permanently diminished by what had happened, that something
essential in how others saw him had been irreversibly altered. These certainties had begun to
reshape how his body moved through the world. Because he was now convinced that danger was
everywhere and that people could not be trusted, Robert had become hypervigilant in a way that
exhausted him. At the hotel, he stationed himself near the lobby entrance during evening shifts,
watching for people who moved with purpose, whose body language seemed off, who lingered without
apparent reason. His eyes tracked every guest, every delivery driver, every person who entered
through the revolving doors. At home, despite the locked doors and the security system his wife had
insisted on installing, he remained on guard—alert in a way that never fully released, even in the
rooms where he had lived for thirty-one years.

The specific triggers that set off his alarm had become increasingly clear. Loud noises—a car
backfiring, a door slamming in a hallway, even the sharp clink of glasses in the bar—would send a
jolt through his chest, his shoulders tensing, his breath catching. The smell of alcohol was worse.
One of the guests had reeked of whiskey that afternoon, and Robert had found himself unable to
process the man's innocent request for directions; instead, he had felt the knife-wielder's presence
in the room, had tensed for violence. When he noticed someone acting suspiciously—loitering,
watching other guests, moving with what he perceived as predatory attention—the vigilance spiked
into acute alarm. His body would flood with adrenaline, his thoughts would narrow to survival
calculations, and he would find himself tracking that person's every movement until they left the
premises.

The startle response had become particularly troubling. His wife, Maria, had learned not to touch
him from behind. Last month, she had placed a hand on his shoulder while he was reading the
newspaper, and he had jerked upright with a cry of alarm that had frightened them both. The
involuntary flinch when someone came near him unexpectedly had become a source of shame—another way
the mugging had colonized his body. He had begun to withdraw from Maria's affection, not from lack
of love but from the terror of his own reaction, the humiliation of being a man who flinched from
his wife's touch like a frightened animal.

What had finally brought Robert to seek help was not the hypervigilance or the startle responses,
though those had worsened. It was the recognition that his retreat was costing him his marriage.
Maria had begun sleeping in the guest room. He had stopped attending the monthly dinners with his
management team. The hotel itself, which had been a source of genuine pride, had become a place of
constant surveillance and dread. His daughter had noticed the change when she visited last month—the
way he scanned the parking lot before they walked to the car, the way his hand trembled slightly
when she hugged him goodbye. He was terrified that this was who he had become now: a man who could
not trust, who could not relax, who lived in a world where violence was not an aberration but a
certainty waiting to happen again.
