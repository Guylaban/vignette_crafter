# Marcus

Marcus was twenty-six when the hurricane hit. He had been working as a junior salesman for a
commercial real estate firm in Charleston, South Carolina, living alone in a modest apartment two
blocks from the harbor. He was ambitious, well-liked by colleagues, the kind of person who made
plans and followed through on them. When Hurricane Ophelia made landfall that September, Marcus had
stayed behind to help a coworker secure the office building—a decision that seemed practical at the
time, even noble. What he had not anticipated was the violence of the surge, the way the water rose
not gradually but in a sudden, suffocating wall that trapped him in the stairwell between the first
and second floors. He remembered the cold shock of it, the absolute darkness, the sound of concrete
cracking. After that, the memory fractured. He knew he had made it to the roof, that he had been
rescued by helicopter at dawn, but the hours in between existed only as a sensation—a crushing
weight, a roaring in his ears, a terror so absolute it had no shape or image to contain it. When he
tried later to piece together what had happened, whole sections simply would not come back to him,
no matter how hard he concentrated. The gaps themselves became another kind of dread.

Three years later, Marcus still could not make sense of who he had become in the aftermath. The man
who had weathered the hurricane was not the same person who now sat in his car in parking lots,
checking his phone compulsively, unable to go inside. He had come to believe that he could not trust
his own judgment anymore—if he had misjudged the storm's severity once, how could he trust himself
about anything? This conviction had spread into every corner of his thinking. He could not rely on
other people, either; the coworker he had tried to help had moved away without staying in touch, and
Marcus had interpreted this as confirmation that his presence was a burden, that people ultimately
could not be counted on. Nothing felt meaningful anymore. His job, which had once excited him, now
felt hollow—he would sit across from clients and pitch commercial properties while some part of him
remained trapped in that stairwell, and the disconnect was so profound that he had stopped believing
his own words. He was no longer sure what he wanted or who he was trying to be.

Certain moments would crack open this fragile equilibrium. When a client asked him to meet at a
waterfront restaurant to discuss a development project, Marcus felt the old terror surge through his
body before any conscious thought could intervene. The vulnerability of being near water, of being
in a space where he could not control the environment, would trigger a cascade of intrusive
memories—not clear images, but the raw emotional intensity of drowning, of helplessness, of the
moment when he had been certain he would die. These moments of feeling emotionally vulnerable would
send him spiraling into the memory itself, and the memory would confirm his darkest beliefs: that he
was fundamentally unsafe, that he could not trust his own mind, that the world was far more
dangerous than he had ever understood. When he forced himself to attend his company's quarterly
meeting at a hotel with a view of the harbor, the sight of open water triggered the same cycle. His
heart would race, his palms would sweat, and suddenly he was back in that darkness, feeling the full
terror of the event as if it were happening in real time. He would excuse himself and sit in his car
for an hour, unable to return.

The hypervigilance had become constant. Marcus woke most nights at three or four in the morning, his
body already alert, checking his phone, checking the weather, checking that his apartment door was
locked. He had begun to avoid anything that resembled the conditions of the disaster—he would not go
to the beach, would not hike near rivers, would not even sit outside during heavy rain. But the
avoidance itself had become a kind of prison. He had turned down a promotion that would have
required occasional travel to coastal cities. He had stopped seeing friends who suggested outdoor
activities. The isolation only deepened his sense that he was fundamentally alone, that no one could
understand what he had lost or who he had become. His negative appraisals—the conviction that he was
broken, that nothing mattered, that other people could not be trusted—seemed to generate their own
evidence. Each time he declined an invitation, each time he failed to show up for a social
commitment because the anxiety felt unbearable, he saw it as proof of his own inadequacy. And each
proof seemed to trigger the memories again, the fragmentary, terrifying sensations that had no clear
beginning or end.

It was his mother who finally insisted he seek help. She had noticed his weight loss, his increasing
isolation, the way he would freeze when it rained. Marcus had resisted for months, but last week he
had woken from a nightmare so vivid—not a memory, exactly, but the emotion of drowning without any
image to anchor it—that he had sat on his bed until dawn, unable to move. He called a therapist that
morning. What he wanted most was to feel like himself again, to believe that the world was not
fundamentally hostile, to trust that other people—and his own mind—might not betray him. He was not
sure such a thing was possible anymore, but he had finally become desperate enough to try.
