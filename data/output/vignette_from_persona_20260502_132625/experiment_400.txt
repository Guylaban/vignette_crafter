# Marcus

Marcus was twenty-four when he first arrived in Syria with a humanitarian relief organization,
driven by the conviction that he could help. For three months he moved between camps and makeshift
clinics, documenting need, distributing supplies, and bearing witness to suffering he had not known
existed. On his final week, a mortar struck a school where he had spent the morning helping
distribute blankets to children. He was two blocks away when it happened—close enough to feel the
concussion, close enough to hear the screaming that followed. He ran toward it with others, stepping
over rubble, finding small bodies. One girl, perhaps seven, was still conscious when he reached her,
her left leg severed below the knee. He held her hand as volunteers carried her away. He never
learned her name or what became of her.

That was five years ago. Marcus returned home and tried to move forward. He became a jeweler, work
that demanded precision and focus—something he thought might anchor him. For the first two years,
the memories stayed mostly dormant, surfacing only at night or when he was alone. But gradually the
boundary between then and now began to erode. A child crying in the grocery store—his daughter's
voice, the one he'd held—would flood back with such force that his heart would race uncontrollably,
and he would find himself unable to breathe, certain that if his pulse didn't slow he would die. The
memory came not as a distant recollection but as an immediate, overwhelming presence, more vivid and
real than any ordinary memory of his own apartment or his work. When his own heartbeat accelerated,
the memory would follow instantly, as though his body had become a trigger for the full traumatic
scene to replay without warning.

These episodes frightened him profoundly. Marcus had come to believe that the world was
fundamentally unsafe, that danger was constant and often invisible. In any room, he found himself
scanning for weapons—the letter opener on a client's desk, the heavy bookend, the way light caught
on broken glass. He felt unsafe unless his back was against a wall where he could see the entire
space, and even then, the safety felt fragile, conditional on his vigilance. When people told him he
was safe, that he was home now, that the war was thousands of miles away, something in him rejected
their reassurance entirely. He was certain that they simply didn't understand the reality he
inhabited—that safety was an illusion people told themselves.

To manage the overwhelming memories and the fear, Marcus developed strategies that seemed to help in
the moment. When the panic came, he would force himself to think only positive thoughts, reminding
himself that it could have been worse, that he had survived, that others had suffered more. The
reframing felt necessary—a way to contain the terror. But the relief never lasted long. Within
hours, sometimes minutes, the effort of maintaining that positive stance would collapse, and the
memory would return with renewed intensity, as though his mind had been holding its breath and could
only exhale by flooding him with the full weight of what he'd witnessed. The harder he tried to
suppress the image, the more insistently it would intrude. When he caught himself scanning the room
again for threats, or when his heart began to race, the memory would be waiting there, ready to
emerge. His constant vigilance—checking surroundings, noticing potential weapons, positioning
himself defensively—seemed to reinforce the very belief that danger was everywhere, making the
threats feel more real and more imminent.

At twenty-nine, Marcus works alone most days in his workshop, which suits him. He no longer goes to
social gatherings. He has not been in a relationship since his return. Six months ago, a colleague
touched his shoulder without warning to show him a design, and Marcus's entire body seized with such
terror that he nearly struck the man. That moment—the realization that his own body could betray
him, that an innocent gesture could trigger a response he couldn't control—finally pushed him to
seek help. He sits now in the therapist's office with his back to the corner, and he tells her that
what frightens him most is that the memory will never feel like the past, that he will spend the
rest of his life unable to distinguish between what happened then and what is happening now.
