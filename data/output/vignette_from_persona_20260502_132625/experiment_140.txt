# Aotea

Aotea had been a man of measured gestures and careful aesthetics for most of his life. At sixty-
seven, with silver threading through his dark hair and a stillness in his bearing that came from
decades spent arranging light and space in other people's homes, he had seemed to friends and
colleagues like someone at peace with the world. His wife, Aroha, had died three years ago, and he
had thrown himself into his work—a boutique interior design practice in San Francisco where he was
known for the restraint and harmony of his spaces. But the man who sat in the therapist's office now
was visibly diminished, moving as though the air itself had become an obstacle.

The captivity had been brief in duration but catastrophic in effect. Thirty-two years ago, during a
holiday in Lebanon, Aotea had been detained at a checkpoint. The soldiers who held him had been
young, angry, and convinced he was something he was not. For seventy-two hours, he had been locked
in a concrete room no larger than a closet—a space with one small window that faced a wall, and no
way to see who was approaching. The men would come without warning, shouting in Arabic, their voices
filling the room before their bodies did. They wanted information he did not have. The questions
came in rapid bursts, aggressive and repetitive, and Aotea had learned quickly that silence and
compliance were his only survival tools. When they finally released him—apparently satisfied that
they had made an error, or simply bored—he had emerged into sunlight feeling as though he had been
born into a world he no longer recognized.

For years afterward, nothing seemed wrong. He had returned to his life, married Aroha, built his
career. But in the last eighteen months, something had fractured. It began with small things: an
inability to work in spaces without windows, a reluctance to enter his clients' master bedrooms if
they were windowless. Then it accelerated. Now, at sixty-seven, Aotea found himself unable to go to
work at all. The design studio occupied the second floor of a converted warehouse in SoMa—a space he
had designed himself, with exposed brick and carefully positioned skylights. Yet he could not bring
himself to climb those stairs. The thought of being in that building, even with its abundance of
light, triggered a visceral panic that his body registered before his mind could form a coherent
reason. His hands would sweat. His breath would shallow. His shoulders would bunch toward his ears
in a posture of self-protection that he could not consciously relax.

What frightened Aotea most was the sudden arrival of the memories themselves. They came without
warning—a flash of that concrete wall, the smell of sweat and fear, the sensation of confinement
pressing in from all sides. These intrusions would arrive in the middle of the day, while he was
sitting in his apartment or walking through the Marina, and they would pull him backward into a
state of acute vulnerability. The memory would surface, and with it came a flood of fear and shame
so intense that he could not distinguish between what had happened then and what was happening now.
His body would react as though the danger were immediate and present—his heart would accelerate, his
muscles would tense—before he had even consciously registered what had triggered the response. The
threat felt absolute and current.

The triggers had become his constant cartographers, mapping the geography of his fear. Enclosed
spaces—elevators, small bathrooms, even his own bedroom with the door closed—would provoke the
cascade. Hearing Arabic, even on the street or in a restaurant, would send him into a state of
hypervigilance that lasted for hours. But most insidious was the feeling of emotional vulnerability
itself. When he felt exposed or uncertain in any social interaction, when he sensed that he might be
misunderstood or judged, that vulnerability would activate the old panic, as though being
emotionally undefended were the same as being physically trapped.

To manage these triggers, Aotea had developed elaborate protective rituals. Before any interaction—a
phone call to a client, a conversation with a neighbor—he would rehearse what he would say, running
through multiple versions of the exchange, trying to anticipate how it might go wrong and how he
might protect himself. This rehearsal had become so consuming that he had begun to withdraw from
social contact altogether. He no longer attended the design association meetings he had once
enjoyed. He had stopped accepting new clients. The avoidance had compounded his isolation, and with
that isolation came a deepening sense of disconnection from the world and from himself.

The memories themselves had taken on a strange quality. Sometimes, when he tried to recount what had
happened to the therapist, the narrative would dissolve. He would begin to describe the room, the
guards, the questions, but somewhere in the middle of the account, the thread would slip away and he
would find himself unable to continue, as though his mind had simply closed the door on the
remainder. At other moments, he could recall the event with a kind of flat, anesthetized clarity—he
could describe the concrete, the heat, the texture of his own fear—but feel nothing, as if he were
recounting something that had happened to someone else entirely. And sometimes, when the memory came
unbidden, he would hear the voices again, the shouting in Arabic that had filled that small room,
echoing now in his skull with a clarity that made him uncertain whether the sounds were real or
imagined.

The threat monitoring had become relentless. Aotea could no longer sit in a room without positioning
himself so that his back was against a wall and he could see the entire space before him. If someone
approached from behind, even his therapist standing to adjust the blinds, his entire nervous system
would spike into alarm. The world had become a place of hidden dangers, and he had become a man
perpetually braced for attack. What had once been a life of careful creation—of designing spaces
that brought peace to others—had become a life of defensive crouch, waiting in his apartment for the
next intrusion, both external and internal, that he knew would come.

It was his sister who had finally insisted he seek help. She had visited from New Zealand six months
ago and had been shocked by the man her brother had become—hollow-eyed, hypervigilant, speaking in
careful half-sentences as though he were rationing his words. She had watched him refuse to enter
his own bathroom because it had no window. She had seen him position himself in the corner of her
hotel room, back to the wall, unable to relax even for an evening. She had told him, with the
directness of love, that he could not live like this anymore. And so, after three decades of
silence, Aotea had come to speak about what had happened in that room, hoping that perhaps, finally,
someone could help him reclaim the life that fear had stolen.
