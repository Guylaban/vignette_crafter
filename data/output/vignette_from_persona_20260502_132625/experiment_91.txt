# Marcus

Marcus was forty-three years old when his F-16 went down. He had been a combat pilot for two
decades, had flown over Iraq and Afghanistan, had seen colleagues killed in action—experiences that
had hardened him but had not broken him. What broke him was an equipment failure over the Nevada
desert during a routine training run. The hydraulic system ruptured without warning. He felt the
stick go dead in his hands, felt the aircraft yaw violently, felt the g-forces slam him sideways as
the nose pitched down. There was a moment—perhaps two seconds, though it felt like an eternity—when
he knew the ejection system might not work, when the ground was rushing up and the roar of the dying
engines filled his skull. The ejection fired. He rocketed free. His parachute deployed. But those
seconds had rewritten something fundamental in how his brain understood safety and control.

The accident investigation found no pilot error. Marcus had done everything right. He had survived.
Yet in the thirteen years since, he had come to believe with unshakeable certainty that the world
was fundamentally dangerous in ways he could not predict or prevent, that his body was a fragile
machine liable to betray him at any moment, and that the only way forward was through absolute
vigilance and control. He felt dead inside—not depressed in the classical sense, but hollowed out,
as if the part of him that had once found joy in flying, in his marriage, in the simple pleasure of
a meal shared with friends, had been incinerated in that cockpit. His wife, Catherine, had watched
him become someone else: a man who flinched at unexpected sounds, who sat with his back to the wall
at restaurants, who seemed to be listening always for something catastrophic just beyond the
horizon.

When a low-frequency boom echoed across the neighborhood—a sonic boom from the air base, the kind of
sound that had once been ordinary to him—Marcus's body seized. His muscles locked, his heart
hammered, his breath came in shallow gasps. The sound of an explosion, even a distant one, sent him
into a state of high alert that lasted for hours. These moments of sensory threat triggered an
immediate cascade of avoidance. He stopped attending air shows with Catherine, stopped going to
restaurants near the highway where truck backfires might startle him, stopped accepting invitations
to social gatherings where he could not control the acoustic environment. The more he avoided these
situations, the more his body remained in a state of constant tension, muscles coiled, reflexes
hair-trigger. When Catherine suggested they visit her sister in California, Marcus felt a wave of
dread—the flight itself, the airports, the possibility of turbulence or mechanical noise—and he
declined. He had declined so many invitations over the years that Catherine had largely stopped
asking.

His physical health had become another arena of control and dread. A few years after the crash, he
had experienced chest pain—nothing serious, the cardiologist said—but the experience had terrified
him. Now the sight of an ambulance, the smell of a hospital, the thought of a routine physical
examination triggered the same cascade of panic and avoidance. He had not had a complete physical in
eight years. Instead, he had developed an elaborate system of dietary restriction, eating only foods
he deemed "safe," counting calories obsessively, convinced that by controlling his intake and his
weight he could prevent illness, could maintain the bodily integrity that felt constantly under
siege. When Catherine suggested he see a doctor about his weight loss, he became angry,
defensive—her concern felt like an accusation, a reminder that his body was failing him. The
restriction gave him a sense of agency in a world that had proven itself fundamentally
uncontrollable.

Each avoidance, each act of control, each restriction seemed to confirm his darkest beliefs. The
world remained dangerous. His body remained vulnerable. He remained vigilant, hyperalert, scanning
for threats that never fully materialized yet always felt imminent. When he starved himself or
avoided crowds or turned away from Catherine's touch, the temporary relief he felt—the illusion of
control—only deepened his conviction that the world required this constant, exhausting defense. His
marriage had become a kind of parallel life: Catherine in one room, Marcus in another, both of them
trapped in a pattern neither could break. At fifty-six, he had come to his first therapy appointment
not because he believed change was possible, but because Catherine had finally issued an ultimatum,
and because some part of him—the part that still remembered the man he had been—recognized that he
could not live this way much longer.
