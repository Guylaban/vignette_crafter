# Clinical Vignette: Arjun Patel

Arjun Patel was thirty-two when he returned from his third deployment as a relief worker in Syria.
He had gone there believing he could make a difference—that his skills in logistics and crisis
coordination would matter, that his presence would help. For eighteen months, he and a small team
had moved between displacement camps and makeshift clinics, managing supply chains, triaging
resources, bearing witness. On a March morning in 2021, an artillery strike hit the compound where
his team was sleeping. Three colleagues died in the blast. Arjun survived because he had stepped
outside moments earlier to check on a generator. He found himself standing in the smoke, ears
ringing, unable to move. The smell—acrid, metallic, mixed with something else he could not
name—filled his lungs. When he finally made it back inside, he saw what remained of the room where
his friends had been sleeping. He could not put together, in his mind, the sequence of what had
happened. One moment they were alive; the next they were not. The order of events, the precise
mechanics of it, stayed fragmented in his memory, as though his mind had refused to stitch it
together into a coherent narrative.

He came home to his wife Priya and their two-year-old daughter with a surface-level composure that
fooled almost no one. Within weeks, the triggers began to organize his life. The smell of smoke—from
a neighbor's barbecue, from a candle at a funeral he felt obligated to attend—would send him into a
state of acute alarm. His body would tense; his breathing would shallow. The memory of that morning
would surface, but not cleanly. Instead, he would feel his limbs arranged as they had been in that
moment of standing outside, his posture mimicking the frozen stance of someone caught between
worlds. When anger flared in others—his boss's sharp tone in a meeting, a heated discussion between
Priya and her mother—Arjun's nervous system responded as though he were back in danger. His startle
response had become exaggerated; he jumped at the sound of the mailman's truck, at the kettle
whistling, at his daughter's sudden laugh. At night, he lay awake beside Priya, convinced that
danger was imminent, unable to shake the conviction that he needed to remain vigilant. He could not
concentrate at work; his mind would drift or fragment mid-sentence, leaving him unable to track the
tax code he was reviewing or follow a client's question.

The shame came when he tried to recall what had happened. It was not the shame of survival, though
that was present too. It was the conviction that he had failed—that he should have been inside the
room, should have done something, should have protected them. This belief had come to feel like a
fundamental truth about the world and his place in it: he was someone who could not protect others,
someone whose presence meant nothing. The world itself had revealed itself as utterly random and
hostile, stripped of the meaning he had once believed it held. These realizations, once formed,
began to reshape what he avoided and what he feared. He became certain that happiness or hope would
be a betrayal of those who had died, and so he began to turn down invitations to celebrations, to
withdraw from moments of lightness with Priya and his daughter. He avoided the news, avoided talking
about his work, avoided anything that might draw him back to that compound or that morning. He even
avoided driving past the cemetery where a colleague's father was buried, taking a longer route home
from his office, adding twenty minutes to his commute rather than risk the sight of graves. When he
covered his face with his hands—something he found himself doing more and more, a gesture that had
become almost reflexive—it was partly to hide from the world and partly because the physical act of
obscuring his vision seemed to quiet the hypervigilance, to create a momentary pocket of control.

But the avoidance fed the cycle. Each time he turned away from a trigger, his nervous system
registered the relief and learned that danger was real, that vigilance was warranted. Each time he
withdrew from a moment of connection or joy, the belief that he was fundamentally isolated,
fundamentally apart from others, deepened. His concentration difficulties meant he could not fully
engage with the present moment, which meant his mind remained more vulnerable to intrusive memories
and the fragmented, embodied recall of that morning. His wife noticed the distance growing. His
daughter asked, more and more often, why Daddy didn't want to play. By his thirty-fifth birthday,
Arjun had arranged his life around the avoidance of triggers and the management of threat, but the
arrangement was collapsing. He had missed promotions, had grown distant from Priya in ways that
frightened them both, and had begun to wonder if the person he had been before—the person who
believed he could help, who felt connected to others—was gone forever. It was only when Priya gave
him an ultimatum, gentle but firm, that he finally agreed to seek help. He came to the first session
carrying the weight of three deaths he had not prevented, the conviction that the world was
fundamentally unsafe, and the fear that if he allowed himself to hope again, he would be betraying
the memory of those who had not come home.
