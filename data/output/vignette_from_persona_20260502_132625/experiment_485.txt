simply would not come. She knew—with a clarity that was almost worse—that Javier had been three
seats ahead of her. She knew he had not survived. But the exact sequence of those last moments, the
sounds, the way his face looked: these things remained locked behind a wall she could not breach
with language.

In the nearly two decades since, Rosa had built a life around the absence of him, and around the
absence of words for what had taken him. She worked at a call center in downtown Madrid, a job that
required her to sit at a desk with a headset, to speak clearly and calmly to strangers, to solve
their problems with patience. She was good at it—her supervisors said so—but the work itself had
become a kind of armor. As long as she was speaking someone else's script, answering questions about
billing and service, she did not have to find words for her own experience. The office was safe in
that way: predictable, full of mundane human concerns that had nothing to do with trains or loss or
the moment a hand slipped away.

of the same.
