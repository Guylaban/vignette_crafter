the very thing he feared most—that he was irreparably changed. The drugs and the distraction, the
avoidance of intimacy and the constant movement, had become a wall between him and everything that
had once felt like home.

It was his sister who finally pushed him to seek help. She had noticed the weight loss, the tremor
in his hands, the way he flinched when she tried to hug him. She had also learned, through a mutual
friend, about the cocaine. When she confronted him three weeks ago, Chinedu had no defense—only the
exhausted admission that he could not keep living this way, that something had to change. What he
feared most was that therapy would confirm what he already believed: that he was beyond repair. What
he hoped for, though he barely dared speak it aloud, was that someone might help him understand why
his body would not believe he was safe, and whether there was any way to reclaim even a fraction of
the person he had been before his uncle arrived.
