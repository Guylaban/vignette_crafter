# Amara

Amara was twenty-eight when it happened, a patrol officer with seven years on the force and a
reputation for clear judgment. She had grown up in a tight-knit community in Brooklyn, the daughter
of Nigerian immigrants, and her extended family—cousins, aunts, uncles—remained the center of her
life. Every Sunday, her mother's house filled with the smell of jollof rice and the sound of Yoruba
overlapping with English. Amara's badge meant something in that world: it meant she had made it,
that she was protecting her own.

The assault occurred during a welfare check on a Tuesday evening in November. A woman had called
about a disturbance at an apartment in Sunset Park; the call had seemed routine. Amara arrived
alone—her partner was three minutes behind—and the man who answered the door was calm, cooperative,
almost apologetic. He let her in. Once inside, his demeanor fractured. He was faster than she
expected, and she was caught between the narrow kitchen and the living room, off-balance, unable to
reach her belt. She remembers his weight, his hands, the cold of the countertop against her spine.
She remembers trying to call for help and the pressure on her throat. After that, the sequence
fractures. She knows what happened—the physical aftermath told its own story—but there are gaps,
whole minutes she cannot reconstruct no matter how many times she has tried to piece them together.
The not-knowing is almost worse than the knowing.

Her partner found her. The arrest followed. The case moved through the system. But Amara, in the
months after, found herself unable to return to the work she loved. Driving past certain
neighborhoods triggered a tightness in her chest and a phantom ache across her ribs—the exact place
where his forearm had pressed. Worse was the rage that accompanied the memory: a white-hot fury that
seemed to come from nowhere and everywhere at once, leaving her shaking and sick. She began to avoid
situations that might stir strong emotion of any kind—she stopped going to her mother's house on
Sundays, made excuses, said she was working extra shifts. She told herself that if she stayed numb,
stayed isolated, she could keep the memories at bay.

What had happened to her—the gaps in her memory, the physical echoes, the volcanic anger that
erupted when she recalled what she could recall—had shifted something fundamental in how she saw
herself. Amara had come to believe that another officer, someone with better instincts or faster
reflexes, would never have been caught off-guard like that. She had failed in a way that felt
irreversible. The woman she had been before—confident, rooted in her community, sure of her place in
the world—seemed like someone else entirely. She was permanently damaged now, fundamentally changed.
This belief fed directly into her avoidance: if she was broken, then the safest thing was to
withdraw, to avoid conflict with her husband when he grew frustrated with her distance, to sidestep
any conversation that might crack open the fragile numbness she had constructed. When her mother
called, asking why Amara hadn't come to Sunday dinner in three months, Amara invented a reason and
hung up quickly. She had lost her connection to her family, to the culture that had once anchored
her, but staying away felt like the only way to protect them—and herself—from what she had become.

The avoidance worked, in a way. It kept the worst of the memories at arm's length. But it also meant
that Amara sat alone in her apartment for hours, her marriage cooling into something cordial and
distant, her career in limbo. She had taken medical leave six months ago. Now, at thirty-five, she
had come to her first therapy appointment because her husband had finally said, quietly, that he
couldn't watch her disappear anymore. Amara isn't sure therapy can fix what she believes is broken
beyond repair, but she is here. What she wants most is to feel like herself again. What she fears
most is that the person she was is gone for good.
