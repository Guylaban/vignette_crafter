# Layla's Burden

Layla was forty-six when she came to the clinic, her dark eyes rimmed with exhaustion, her posture
held so rigidly upright it seemed to cost her something. She worked as a pharmacist at a busy retail
chain in suburban Michigan, a job she had held for nearly two decades with quiet competence, but in
the past three years something had fractured in her. The breaking point, she said, came when her son
Amir was nine years old.

It had started with a fever that wouldn't break—ordinary enough, she thought, the way a mother
thinks when she is still able to believe in the ordinariness of things. But the fever spiked higher,
and then came the rash, and within hours the pediatrician's face had shifted into the expression
Layla would later recognize as the moment everything changed. Meningitis. The ambulance ride—she
remembered the siren cutting through her like a blade, remembered sitting beside Amir's small body
as it convulsed, remembered the certainty that bloomed in her chest that this was the moment she
would lose him. He spent five days in the pediatric intensive care unit. He survived. But Layla did
not return from that hospital intact.

The first year after Amir's recovery, she had tried to move forward. He was fine now, the doctors
said. Fully recovered. But Layla had come to believe something different about herself in those
hospital corridors. She was weak—had she not been helpless, paralyzed, able to do nothing while her
child suffered? And if she was weak, if she had failed to protect him even when she was present,
what good could possibly lie ahead for either of them? She was defective in some fundamental way, a
mother who could not keep her own child safe. The thought circled endlessly, and with it came the
conviction that nothing good would happen to her anymore, that she was marked somehow by what she
had witnessed.

These beliefs had begun to reshape how she moved through the world. Any reminder of that
hospital—the sight of an ambulance passing on the street, the smell of antiseptic in her own
pharmacy, the sight of a child in a hospital gown on television—sent her into a state of such
intense vigilance that her body could not settle. Her heart would race. Her shoulders would climb
toward her ears. Because she believed she was weak, because she was certain that catastrophe was
always waiting, Layla had started to avoid the very spaces and cues that might trigger these
sensations. She stopped visiting her sister when the sister was hospitalized for routine surgery.
She began calling in other pharmacists to cover shifts when her workplace received deliveries of
pediatric medications. She would not go to her nephew's soccer game because the field was near a
medical clinic, and the mere possibility of seeing an ambulance in the distance made her throat
close.

The more she avoided, the more her sense of threat intensified. Her body had learned that the world
was dangerous, and avoidance had become the only way she knew to buy herself a few hours of relative
calm. But that calm was fragile and shrinking. At night, she lay awake for hours, her mind refusing
to settle, her body taut as a wire. When she finally drifted toward sleep, the smallest sound—a car
door slamming, a siren blocks away—would jolt her awake, her heart hammering as though she were back
in that hospital room. She jumped at the ring of the pharmacy phone. She flinched at her own name
called across a room. The constant state of readiness, the belief that danger was imminent and that
she was too weak to face it, had become exhausting beyond measure.

What troubled her most was how her avoidance had begun to confirm her darkest beliefs about herself.
Her sister had stopped asking her to visit. Her ex-husband, with whom she shared custody of Amir,
had gently suggested that perhaps she should see someone because Amir was starting to ask why his
mother seemed so afraid all the time. Each time she avoided a situation, each time her body flooded
with panic and she retreated, she felt the proof settling deeper: she was broken, she could not
handle ordinary life, nothing good would come of this. The cycle had tightened until she could
barely recognize the capable, steady woman she had been before the hospital. It was this—the slow
dissolution of her sense of self, the way her attempts to protect herself from harm had instead
imprisoned her—that finally brought her to seek help.
