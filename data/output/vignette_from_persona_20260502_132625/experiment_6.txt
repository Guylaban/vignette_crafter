# Mariana

Mariana was eleven when her stepfather threw the dinner plate. She remembers the sound more than the
image—the sharp crack of ceramic against the kitchen tile, the way her mother flinched before the
plate even landed. Her stepfather had been angry about something small, something Mariana could no
longer quite articulate, but the rage had come fast and absolute. The plate shattered. Her mother's
face went very still. And in that moment, Mariana understood that the world could fracture without
warning, that anger could materialize in your home like a sudden storm, and that there was nothing
she could do to stop it. That night, she'd smelled his cologne mixed with the acrid scent of spilled
wine—a smell that would, years later, ambush her without permission whenever she caught it in a
crowd or in someone's apartment.

Now, at twenty-one, Mariana works as an animator for a small studio in Los Angeles, a job that
requires precision, focus, and the ability to sit still for hours. She is good at it—her colleagues
praise her attention to detail, the way she can build entire worlds frame by frame. But the work has
become a minefield. Two months ago, a colleague had thrown a pen across the room in frustration at a
rendering error. Mariana had been at her desk, headphones on, and the sudden motion in her
peripheral vision sent her entire body into a state of emergency—her heart hammering, her hands
shaking so badly she couldn't hold the stylus. The thrown object itself was harmless, but it had
cracked something open in her. Since then, any abrupt movement near her workstation—a coworker
standing up suddenly, someone gesturing while talking—triggers the same searing alertness, the sense
that danger is imminent and she must be ready to defend herself.

The triggers have become inescapable. When she sees photographs of men who resemble her stepfather,
her chest tightens. Certain sounds—the clink of a glass, a raised voice, even a door slamming in a
neighboring apartment—send her back to that kitchen, and suddenly she is eleven again, her body
rigid with the certainty that violence is about to unfold. The memories themselves have taken on a
quality that terrifies her: they feel more real, more vivid, more present than anything happening
around her now. When the memory comes, she can taste the food that was on the plate, smell the wine
and cologne, feel the specific temperature of her fear. The realness of it is almost unbearable, and
so she has learned to push the memory away the moment it begins to surface, to interrupt it before
it can fully form. But the interruption itself exhausts her, and the fragmented images keep
returning, incomplete and haunting.

In response to the triggers and the memories, Mariana has developed a system of control. She avoids
making any plans beyond the immediate week—the idea of committing to a future, of imagining herself
in six months or a year, feels impossibly risky, as though planning invites disaster. More visibly,
she has become rigid about her eating. She counts calories obsessively, eats only foods she has
prepared herself, and finds a strange calm in the restriction. When everything else feels chaotic
and unpredictable, her body is something she can govern absolutely. She also avoids anything that
might remind her of her stepfather—certain neighborhoods, certain types of music, certain social
situations where she might encounter men like him. These avoidances have begun to narrow her life in
ways she cannot quite articulate to her friends. She has turned down invitations to parties,
declined a mentor's offer to introduce her to industry contacts, and cancelled plans with her cousin
because the restaurant they'd chosen was in a part of town where her stepfather's family lived.

What frightens Mariana most, beneath all of this, is her own conviction that she is powerless. She
cannot stop bad things from happening—she knows this with absolute certainty because she could not
stop what happened when she was eleven, and nothing has convinced her since that she has any real
agency in the world. The people responsible have faced no consequences; her stepfather moved away
years ago, and her mother has never spoken of it directly. This absence of accountability has
calcified into something darker: the belief that the world operates according to a logic she cannot
influence, that her vigilance, her control, her careful avoidance might delay disaster but can never
truly prevent it. And when she tries to cope with even small upsets—a critical comment from a
supervisor, a friend cancelling plans—she finds she cannot. She unravels. She becomes irritable,
snapping at people without warning, her anger disproportionate and frightening even to herself.

Mariana came to therapy because last week, a visitor to the studio arrived carrying flowers, and
when he set the vase down on her desk, her hands began to shake so violently that she had to leave
the room. Sitting in the waiting area afterward, she realized she could not continue like this. She
is twenty-one years old, and she is afraid of her own life.
