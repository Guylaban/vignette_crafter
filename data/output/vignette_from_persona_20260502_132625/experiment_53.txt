# Clinical Vignette: Linh T.

Linh was sixty-three when the men came to the apartment. She had been living in San Francisco for
three years, having rebuilt her life after a difficult marriage and a harder emigration from Vietnam
in her fifties. She worked as a financial analyst at a mid-sized firm, a position she had earned
through night classes and meticulous attention to detail—the same precision that had allowed her to
survive the previous decades. On a Tuesday evening in March, two men forced their way into her
apartment, bound her to a chair, and held her there for eighteen hours while they ransacked the
place, demanding information about accounts and passwords they believed she controlled. She was not
the person they were looking for. When police finally arrived—alerted by a neighbor—Linh was found
dehydrated, her wrists raw from the restraints, her voice nearly gone from screaming. The men were
arrested within weeks. But the trauma, she would come to understand, had only begun.

In the months that followed, Linh developed a geography of fear. Her apartment, once a refuge,
became a place where her vigilance never rested. She moved through the rooms during daylight with a
kind of rigid control, checking locks obsessively, but as evening approached, a creeping dread would
settle over her. She had come to believe that the systems meant to protect her—the police, the
building management, the justice system—had fundamentally failed her. They had not prevented the
invasion; they had arrived too late. What frightened her most was the conviction that danger could
materialize anywhere, that no institution or authority could truly keep her safe. This belief fed a
constant, exhausting threat monitoring: she could not relax in her own home, could not trust that
closed doors meant security. At night, the apartment became intolerable. She would lie in bed, pulse
quickening at every sound, certain that the darkness outside her windows harbored the same predatory
attention that had found her once before. She began staying awake deliberately, sitting in her
living room with lights blazing, telling herself that vigilance was the only real protection.

The insomnia deepened her sense of contamination. She would shower for forty minutes, sometimes
twice a day, scrubbing at her skin as though the memory of those men's hands on her arms could be
washed away. She felt fundamentally unclean in a way that soap could not address. Simultaneously,
she tried desperately not to think about what had happened—pushing away intrusive images of the
men's faces, the sound of duct tape being torn, the sensation of her own helplessness. She would
suppress these thoughts through sheer force of will during work hours, maintaining her composure at
the office, but the effort was exhausting. By evening, when she was alone, the thoughts would return
with greater intensity, and she would stay awake even later to avoid the dreams that came when she
finally did sleep. Her sleep deprivation made her fragile. Small frustrations at work—a colleague's
curt email, a minor error in a spreadsheet—would trigger disproportionate waves of fear and anger.
She had come to believe that she could not cope with even the slightest upset, that her emotional
resources were permanently depleted. This conviction only intensified her sense of threat; if she
could not manage ordinary stress, how could she possibly handle another crisis? The thought spiraled
into more vigilance, more sleeplessness, more desperate attempts to suppress the memories that felt
like they might destroy her if she allowed them full presence.

By her sixty-eighth birthday, Linh had begun to avoid leaving her apartment except for work. She
would drive directly to the office and directly home, taking the same route, never deviating. She
had stopped attending her book club, stopped accepting invitations from colleagues, stopped going to
the Vietnamese market she had loved. Her world contracted to three locations: her apartment, her
workplace, and the car between them. Her ex-husband, with whom she had maintained cordial contact,
called less frequently; she had become difficult to reach, and when they did speak, she was distant,
preoccupied. Her supervisor had begun to notice her performance slipping—not dramatically, but
noticeably. She was making small errors, arriving late, leaving early. When he gently asked if
everything was all right, Linh had no honest answer. She could not explain that the office, once a
place of competence and order, now felt permeable to danger, that she spent her workday fighting the
urge to leave, that she was terrified of being trapped again, even in a building full of people.

At seventy-one, Linh finally called a therapist. What had finally broken through her resistance was
a moment of clarity during a sleepless night: she realized she was no longer living in the aftermath
of trauma—she was living *in* the trauma itself, night after night, through her own hands. She was
hoping, though she hardly dared to say it aloud, that someone could help her believe that safety was
possible again.
