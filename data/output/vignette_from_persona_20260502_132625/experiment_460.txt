# Clinical Vignette: Aroha

Aroha Parata was forty-eight when he finally admitted to his wife that something had broken inside
him—though by then she had already noticed. The assault had happened fifteen years earlier, in the
parking lot behind a bar in South Auckland, at the hands of a man he'd thought was a friend. Aroha
had been drinking that night; the man had offered him a ride home. What followed was a blur of
violence and violation that Aroha had spent the next decade and a half trying to outrun, until his
body refused to run anymore.

For years, Aroha had believed that if he simply worked hard enough—stayed busy enough, kept his
hands moving, filled every waking hour with tasks—the memories would lose their grip. He'd built a
reputation as a roofer who took on more jobs than anyone else in his crew, arriving before dawn and
staying until the light failed. His wife, Hemi, would find him in the garage at midnight,
reorganizing tools that were already organized, or he'd volunteer for the most physically demanding
contracts, the ones that left his muscles screaming. What Aroha didn't realize was that this
relentless busyness was itself a cage. The harder he pushed his body, the more he needed to control
what he put into it. He'd begun restricting his eating—measuring portions obsessively, skipping
meals, using the strictness of it as a way to feel that at least one part of his life answered to
his will. The control felt necessary, almost sacred, a bulwark against the chaos that lived in his
chest.

Then, about three years ago, news coverage of a sexual assault case had appeared on the television
while Aroha was eating dinner with Hemi. The details—a man, a parking lot, alcohol involved—had sent
him spiraling. That night, he'd found himself unable to sleep, his mind flooded with the assault as
if it were happening in real time. The memory felt more vivid, more present, more *real* than the
actual dinner he'd just eaten or the conversation with Hemi that had preceded it. The visceral
sensation of hands on his body, the smell of the man's breath—whiskey and stale cigarettes—had
crashed through his defenses with such force that he'd gotten up at three in the morning and driven
to a job site, working alone until his hands bled. The news story had triggered the memory, yes, but
it had also triggered his old strategies: he'd thrown himself into work, eaten almost nothing for
two days, convinced himself that if he just stayed busy enough, the memories would fade again.

But the triggers kept multiplying. A motorcycle roared past his house one afternoon, and the
sound—sharp, aggressive, inescapable—had yanked him back to that night with such violence that he'd
had to sit down on the curb. The engine's growl had become entangled with the memory of the assault,
and now whenever he heard that particular noise, the full emotional intensity of the trauma would
flood through him without warning, sometimes without even a clear image—just a crushing wave of
grief and terror that left him gasping. He'd started avoiding situations where his body might be
stressed: he'd turned down a contract that required working at height in summer heat, made excuses
about his knees, when really he was terrified that physical exertion would make him vulnerable to
the memories. And the smell of alcohol—any alcohol, even someone's breath across a room—had become
another tripwire. He'd begun avoiding restaurants where people drank, making excuses to Hemi about
why he couldn't go to family gatherings.

What Aroha hadn't anticipated was how these avoidances would feed the memories themselves. By
retreating from situations that might stress his body, by restricting his eating, by filling every
gap with work, he was actually strengthening the very memories he was trying to escape. The less he
lived his life, the more the assault consumed it. His marriage had begun to strain under the weight
of his unspoken terror and his constant, grinding busyness. Hemi had stopped asking him to go out.
His crew had started noticing that he was thinner, more withdrawn, that he'd snap at them over small
things. And the memories—they had become the most real thing in his life, more vivid than his wife's
face, more present than the roof beneath his feet.

It was Hemi who finally insisted he see someone. She'd found him one morning in the garage, unable
to explain why he was there, his hands shaking, his face wet. He'd heard a motorcycle in the
distance. He was terrified—not just of the memories, but of what his life had become in trying to
avoid them. He was hoping that talking about it might finally break the cycle, though he was also
certain that nothing could undo what had been done to him, or restore what he'd lost.
