# Kavi

Kavi was forty-two when the cyclone hit Fiji. He had been living in Suva for fifteen years, working
as an auditor for a shipping firm, and had built a life that felt, finally, stable—a wife, a modest
house on the outskirts of the city, colleagues who knew his name. When the storm warnings came, he
and his wife Priya had done what they always did: secured the shutters, gathered supplies, and
settled in to wait. The radio crackled with updates. The wind came first as a distant roar, then as
something that seemed to shake the very foundation of the house. Around midnight, the roof began to
fail. Kavi remembered the sound most vividly—a tearing, a collapse—and then the rain came in sheets,
cold and relentless. He remembered reaching for Priya in the dark, his hand finding only water. The
next morning, when the wind finally stopped, she was gone. They found her three days later, two
blocks away, caught against a fence.

Four years had passed since then, and Kavi had learned to survive in a body that no longer felt
entirely his own. He had moved to the United States on a work visa, taken a position as an auditor
in Portland, and established a routine so carefully constructed that it resembled a life. But the
world had become a place where danger lived in the gaps he could not see. He was certain now that
bad things were not random—they were waiting, patient, for him to relax his vigilance. When moments
of peace or contentment would arrive unbidden—a quiet afternoon, a colleague's laugh that made him
smile—Kavi would feel a sharp jolt of fear. These pockets of happiness triggered an immediate spike
in his threat detection. He had come to believe that relaxation was itself a warning sign, a moment
when his defenses were down and something catastrophic was about to occur. The feeling of being at
ease made him reach for a cigarette, made him scan his surroundings for danger, made his mind
suddenly flood with sensory fragments from that night—the metallic taste of blood in his mouth, the
smell of wet concrete and something else, something like rust or rot, that he could not name.

The triggers compounded. At night, when darkness fell and he lay in bed, his mind would spiral into
threat assessment. He would check his phone obsessively, looking for alerts, news of storms,
anything that might explain the dread that seized him. Silence in particular had become intolerable.
If his apartment was quiet—no traffic, no voices from neighboring units—he felt certain that
something terrible was imminent, that the world was holding its breath before it broke. When a work
project ran late, when a meeting took longer than expected, Kavi's mind immediately constructed a
narrative of disaster. He avoided unfamiliar places entirely, convinced that he could not possibly
assess the danger lurking in spaces he did not know. This meant declining invitations to new
restaurants, avoiding neighborhoods outside his carefully mapped routes, and, increasingly, finding
reasons not to leave his apartment on weekends.

The memories themselves had become unbearable in their vividness. When they surfaced—and they
surfaced constantly—they came complete with sensation. He would taste copper and ash. His nose would
fill with the smell of rain-soaked earth and something acrid that he associated with the moment
before the roof collapsed. The memory felt so extreme, so overwhelming in its sensory detail, that
part of him had begun to doubt whether it had truly happened the way his body insisted it had. This
dissociation offered a strange kind of relief, even as it terrified him. If it was not real, then he
had not truly lost her. But he knew, in the rational part of his mind that still functioned, that it
was real, and that his disbelief was itself a symptom of how broken he had become.

The smoking had increased steadily. What had begun as an occasional stress response had become a
constant companion—a cigarette first thing in the morning, another before work, several during the
day, a chain of them in the evening as darkness fell. The ritual gave him something to do with his
hands, a way to manage the feeling that he could not stop bad things from happening, that the world
was fundamentally dangerous and he was fundamentally powerless within it. But the smoking also
trapped him in a cycle. Each cigarette was a small reinforcement that the danger was real, that he
was right to be afraid, that his hypervigilance was justified. And the more he smoked, the more he
felt the weight of his own deadness—a numbness that had settled into his chest and seemed to have
calcified there, making it impossible to feel anything but dread and the faint, mechanical
satisfaction of nicotine.

He had stopped talking to anyone about what had happened. His sister called from Auckland every few
weeks, and he would tell her he was fine, that work was fine, that he was adjusting. The lie was
easier than the truth, and the truth felt dangerous anyway—as though speaking it aloud would give it
more power, would make it more real. His colleagues at the firm had learned not to ask. He kept his
desk organized, his reports meticulous, his interactions brief and professional. What frightened him
most now was not the memory itself but the possibility that he would relax, that he would allow
himself to feel something other than vigilance, and that in that moment of weakness the next
disaster would strike. He had begun to understand his own survival as a kind of debt—he had lived
while Priya had not, and that imbalance could not stand. The world would collect. He was certain of
it. Three weeks ago, after a particularly bad night where he had not slept at all, convinced that
the silence in his apartment meant something was wrong, Kavi had finally called a therapist. He did
not know what help looked like anymore, but he knew he could not continue like this. What he wanted
most was to feel something other than fear. What he was most afraid of was that even if he did, it
would kill him.
