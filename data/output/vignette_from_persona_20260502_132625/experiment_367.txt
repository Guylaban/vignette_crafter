# Clinical Vignette: Rajesh Patel

Rajesh Patel was forty-two when the accident happened, a meticulous tax inspector with fifteen years
of clean driving and a reputation for caution. On a Tuesday afternoon in March, he had left his
office early to pick up his daughter from school. The intersection near Maple and Fifth was one he
knew well—he had driven through it hundreds of times. But that day, a delivery truck ran the red
light while Rajesh was crossing, and his sedan was struck broadside. The impact was violent and
immediate. He heard the shriek of metal tearing, the explosive pop of the airbag, and beneath it
all, a sound he would never quite forget: the cry of the other driver, a young woman in the truck,
screaming as she realized what she had done. The paramedics arrived within minutes. Rajesh walked
away with a fractured collarbone and bruised ribs. The young woman—Sarah, he learned later—suffered
a severe head injury, a fractured femur, and spent six weeks in intensive care. She survived, but
the trajectory of her life had been altered in that moment, and Rajesh carried the knowledge that
his vehicle, his body, his presence at that intersection had been the instrument of her suffering.

In the months that followed, Rajesh developed an elaborate system of self-punishment. He had come to
believe that he deserved to suffer because of what had happened, even though the police report had
clearly stated that the truck driver bore responsibility. When intrusive images of the crash
surfaced—the moment of impact, Sarah's face in the ambulance—he would deliberately remind himself
that it could have been worse, that at least she was alive, that he had no right to feel relief
about anything. He had failed to protect her, he told himself, though she was a stranger. He should
have anticipated the truck, should have driven more defensively, should have taken a different
route. These thoughts formed a relentless commentary on his character. Rajesh was certain that he
was fundamentally damaged, that the world was not the safe, predictable place he had once believed
it to be, and that his survival while Sarah suffered proved something shameful about him.

Whenever Rajesh encountered a trigger—a car accident on the news, a funeral announcement in the
office, or worse, someone asking him casually how he was doing after the accident—his maladaptive
strategies would activate almost automatically. A colleague mentioning a car crash on the highway
sent Rajesh spiraling into thought suppression, trying desperately not to think about the trauma,
but the effort itself became exhausting and paradoxically amplified the very thoughts he was
fighting. When he saw a hearse pass his office window one afternoon, he gripped the edge of his desk
and began the downward comparison ritual again: at least she didn't die, at least he wasn't
paralyzed, at least his family wasn't destroyed. Yet these reassurances only deepened his sense of
humiliation—the fact that he needed to console himself this way felt like further evidence of his
moral failure. The punishment thinking would intensify, and he would find himself cataloguing all
the ways he deserved to suffer.

His memories of the event remained fragmented and disturbingly vivid in places. When he tried to
recall the sequence of what happened, whole seconds seemed to have vanished. He could remember the
sound of Sarah's scream with perfect clarity—it played back in his head whenever he closed his
eyes—but the moment just before impact was completely blank, a missing piece. Some moments were
frozen in crystalline detail: the way the afternoon light caught the windshield, the exact angle of
the truck's hood as it struck his car. Other moments were entirely gone. This incomplete picture
haunted him because it meant his mind was unreliable, that he could not fully account for what he
had done, and that incompleteness fed directly back into his conviction that he had failed in some
crucial way. The fragmented memories seemed to confirm that something was broken inside him, that
his perception of reality itself had shattered along with the car's frame. And when memories
surfaced unbidden—triggered by a conversation about the accident, or the sight of a damaged vehicle
on the street—the vividness of what he could remember, combined with the terrifying gaps, would send
him back into his maladaptive cycles, trying to suppress the thoughts, punishing himself for
surviving, comparing downward to avoid the full weight of his guilt.

Rajesh's marriage had become strained. His wife, Priya, had initially been supportive, but his
withdrawal and self-recrimination had worn on her. He could not accept her reassurances. When she
tried to remind him that he was not responsible for the truck driver's error, he would turn away,
convinced that she did not understand the depth of his culpability. At work, he had begun to avoid
certain routes to the office, taking a longer drive each morning to stay away from the intersection
where it had happened. But avoidance had its own cost: he was arriving late more often, and his
manager had begun to notice. The meticulous, reliable Rajesh seemed distracted, sometimes appearing
to freeze at his desk for no apparent reason. He had not attended his cousin's funeral six months
after the accident, making an excuse about work, unable to face the sight of grief made visible.
Now, a year and a half later, Rajesh had come to therapy not because he believed he deserved help,
but because Priya had insisted, and because the thought suppression and punishment cycles had become
so consuming that he could barely function. What frightened him most was the possibility that his
suffering would never be enough to balance what Sarah had endured, and that no amount of self-
condemnation would ever restore the world to the way it had been before he drove through that
intersection.
