# Karim's Reckoning

Karim was thirty-eight when the war came to his neighbourhood in Damascus. He had been a dancer
since childhood—first in his family's living room, then in underground studios during the worst
years, and finally in a small theatre that somehow survived the bombardment of the city's old
quarter. He was good at what he did, the kind of artist who could hold an audience in absolute
stillness through the subtlety of a shoulder, the precision of a turn. On the morning of March 14th,
2015, he was rehearsing alone in the studio when the building next door took a direct hit. The blast
came through the wall like a living thing. Karim felt the percussion in his chest before he
understood what was happening—a sound so complete it seemed to erase all other sound, followed by a
silence so profound it felt like deafness. When he stumbled out into the street, the air was thick
with pulverized concrete and something else, something earthy and wrong. Bodies lay among the
rubble. A woman he recognized from the bakery two blocks over was pinned under a collapsed
storefront, her face turned away. He did not go back to the studio. He did not dance for six months.

He left Damascus in 2016, eventually making his way to Los Angeles with his wife Hana on a
humanitarian visa. For a time, the distance seemed to help. He found work as a dance instructor at a
community centre in East Hollywood, teaching Levantine folk forms to teenagers and young adults
hungry for connection to something their parents had fled. He and Hana rented a small house with a
garden. Life began to feel possible again. But the reprieve was fragile, built on avoidance he did
not yet recognize.

Eighteen months into their arrival, something shifted. Karim noticed that certain sounds—the sharp
crack of a car backfire, the rumble of a helicopter passing overhead, even the abrupt slam of a
door—sent him into a state of acute panic. His heart would race; his hands would shake. What
frightened him most was that these moments came with a creeping conviction that the safety he had
constructed was an illusion, that catastrophe was not behind him but woven into the fabric of his
present. The sounds were not simply loud; they were harbingers. After one such episode in the
grocery store—triggered by a delivery truck's hydraulic brake—he began to avoid public spaces during
certain hours. He stopped going to the farmer's market on Saturday mornings. Then, one afternoon
while working in the garden with Hana, the smell of fresh soil after rain transported him back to
the street outside the studio, to the acrid, mineral scent of pulverized earth mixed with something
organic and terrible. The smell had been everywhere that day, clinging to his clothes, his hair, his
skin. Now, whenever he encountered that same earthy smell—after rain, in parks, even in potted
plants—he felt the ground shift beneath him. He came to believe that these sensory intrusions were
messages, signs that he had not truly escaped, that his body carried the trauma in ways others could
sense. When he heard someone call out the name "Amira"—a name common in his homeland—his chest would
tighten. The name itself had become a trigger, collapsing past and present into a single moment of
vulnerability.

What troubled Karim most deeply was the way these moments of sensory alarm had begun to reshape his
understanding of the world. He felt dead inside, as though the part of him that had once moved with
joy and purpose had been buried in that rubble alongside the people he could not save. He had lost
faith in the idea that life followed any rational order, that people could be trusted, that a higher
power watched over anything at all. When he looked at his students' faces during class, he was
certain they could see it written across him—the mark of someone who had witnessed what he had
witnessed, someone fundamentally changed and diminished. He stopped making eye contact. He moved
through his teaching with mechanical precision, the fluidity that had once been his signature now
replaced by a kind of armoured efficiency. Hana noticed the distance growing between them. She would
ask what was wrong, and he could not find words for the way the world had become a place of hidden
threats, where sounds carried messages of danger and smells whispered accusations, where he felt
himself disappearing behind a wall of numbness that no amount of love could penetrate. He had come
to believe that this was simply who he was now—not Karim the dancer, but Karim the survivor, marked
and separate, living among people who could never truly understand what it meant to have stood in
that street and breathed in the dust of destruction.

It was Hana who finally insisted he seek help. She had found him sitting in the dark in their
bedroom one evening, unable to articulate why, and something in his silence frightened her more than
any words could have. He came to his first session carrying the weight of three years of accumulated
dread, uncertain whether healing was even possible for someone like him.
