# Fatima's Burden

Fatima had spent forty-two years on the bench, a reputation built on careful deliberation and
measured judgment. At seventy-five, she had presided over hundreds of cases, her chambers a place
where people came expecting clarity and fairness. Her colleagues respected her; her family—her
husband Karim of fifty-three years, her three adult children, her seven grandchildren—had learned
long ago that she brought the same precision to family decisions that she brought to the law. She
was the one people called when there was a problem to solve, a conflict to untangle, a choice to be
made. This was who Fatima had always been: steady, capable, in control.

Six months ago, her youngest daughter Layla, thirty-eight years old, had collapsed at work without
warning. A ruptured aneurysm, the doctors said—something no one could have predicted, no scan that
would have caught it beforehand. Fatima had been in her chambers when the call came. She remembered
the particular quality of the silence in the room afterward, the way her hand had continued to hold
the phone even after the line went dead. Layla died in the hospital three hours later, with Karim at
her bedside and Fatima still driving, still in that suspended state between knowing and not knowing.

In the weeks that followed, Fatima found herself returning obsessively to the days before Layla's
death. She had spoken to her daughter on the phone the evening before the collapse—a brief, ordinary
conversation about Layla's son's soccer schedule. Fatima had been distracted, reviewing a brief for
a case. She had cut the call short. Now she was certain that if she had paid closer attention, if
she had asked the right questions, if she had pressed Layla about how she was feeling, she might
have noticed something amiss. Some mothers would have. A better mother would have heard the subtle
signs of illness in her daughter's voice. Fatima had not. She had hung up and returned to her work.

The self-recrimination had calcified into something harder over the months. She had come to believe
that her reactions since Layla's death proved what she had always suspected about herself underneath
the professional veneer: she was a poor coper, a woman whose composure masked a fundamental
inadequacy. Real mothers, she thought, would have protected their children. Real mothers would have
found a way. She should have been more present, more vigilant, more something. The fact that she had
not—the fact that she had failed at the one role that mattered most—seemed to her irrefutable
evidence of a character flaw so deep that no amount of judicial wisdom could compensate for it. On
the bench, she could control outcomes through careful reasoning. In life, where it counted, she had
proven useless.

Work had become increasingly difficult. Cases that once engaged her now felt hollow. She would sit
through arguments and find her mind elsewhere, or worse, find herself imagining worst-case scenarios
for the families involved in the litigation before her. She had taken a leave of absence three
months ago, telling the court she needed time to grieve. What she could not say was that she no
longer trusted her own judgment—not about law, not about anything. If she had failed to protect her
own daughter, what business did she have deciding the fates of other people's families?

Karim had suggested she speak to someone. He said it gently, the way he said most things now,
carefully avoiding any suggestion that she was not coping well, though they both knew that was
precisely what he meant. Fatima had resisted. What could a therapist tell her that she did not
already know? That she should forgive herself? That Layla's death was not her fault? These were
comforting platitudes, and Fatima had never been one for comfort that was not earned. But last week,
sitting alone in her study at three in the morning—unable to sleep, unable to stop thinking about
all the ways she had failed—she had finally called. She was not here because she believed she could
be fixed. She was here because she had nowhere else to go, and because the weight of her own
judgment of herself had become unbearable.
