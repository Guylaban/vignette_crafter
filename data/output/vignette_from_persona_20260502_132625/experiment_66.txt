# Kofi's Story

Kofi was fifty-five when his younger brother Emmanuel collapsed on a Sunday afternoon in their
shared workshop. Emmanuel had been mixing paint, the same way he had done a hundred times before,
when he simply fell. Kofi found him on the concrete floor, already unresponsive, and the next hours
dissolved into a chaos of sirens, fluorescent hospital lights, and the terrible knowledge that
Emmanuel was gone before the ambulance even arrived. A massive stroke, the doctors said. No warning.
No chance. For three weeks Kofi had moved through the funeral arrangements in a state of numb
compliance, but the moment he tried to return to work—to the very workshop where it
happened—everything fractured.

Now, eight months later, the sound of an ambulance siren sends Kofi's entire body rigid. His chest
tightens, his hands clench into fists, and a surge of dread floods through him that has nothing to
do with the present moment and everything to do with that Sunday. The siren pulls up memories he
cannot quite see but can feel with overwhelming force—the smell of the workshop, the weight of
Emmanuel's limp arm, the specific timbre of the paramedic's voice calling out vitals. These
fragments arrive not as coherent images but as raw emotional intensity, a drowning sensation that
leaves him gasping. When conversations turn to loss—a colleague mentioning his mother's passing, a
news story about someone who died unexpectedly—Kofi finds himself transported back to the workshop
floor, his body flooding with the same grief and helplessness he felt holding his brother's hand in
the hospital. The trigger itself resurrects the memory, and the memory resurrects the terror.

What troubles Kofi most is how these intrusions have reshaped his understanding of himself and his
world. He has come to believe that his life has been destroyed, that the man he was before that
Sunday—the steady craftsman, the reliable older brother, the man his wife depended on—no longer
exists. This conviction feeds a constant vigilance that exhausts him. At night, he lies awake beside
his wife, his body tense and primed for catastrophe, convinced that something else is about to be
taken from him. He feels on guard even inside his own home, where he should feel safe. The darkness
seems to amplify his sense of isolation; he feels set apart from others who have not experienced
this sudden, senseless loss. When he looks at his wife, he sees someone he might lose at any moment,
and this thought—this certainty that disaster is imminent—keeps his nervous system locked in a state
of perpetual readiness. His shoulders ache from the tension. He grinds his teeth at night.

The workshop itself has become impossible. Kofi tried to return three months after the funeral,
thinking routine would help, but the moment he stepped through the door, his body flooded with the
memory of Emmanuel on the floor. The visual images came then—Emmanuel's face, the way his body had
looked, the exact angle of his arm—and with them came a wave of overwhelming grief so profound it
stopped his breath. Because of these memories, because they are so vivid and so devastating, Kofi
has come to believe that returning to work means accepting that Emmanuel is truly gone, that the
workshop is now a place of death rather than creation. This belief has deepened his sense that his
identity as a painter, as a craftsman, has been erased. He has not worked in five months. His wife
has taken on more hours at her job; tension between them has grown quiet and strained.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was an ambulance that passed his house last week. The siren
triggered such an intense wave of dread and grief—the full emotional weight of the trauma without
any clear image, just pure anguish—that he found himself unable to move for two hours afterward. He
sat in his living room, body trembling, convinced that his wife would be next, that he was cursed to
lose everyone he loved. When she came home and found him like that, she insisted he talk to someone.
Kofi agreed, though part of him wonders if anyone can help him understand why his brother was taken,
or how he is supposed to live in a world where such arbitrary loss is possible.
