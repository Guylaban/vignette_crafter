outside, the old panic would crash through him, and he would find himself in the bathroom, hands
shaking, unable to breathe, the memory of the street vivid and present as if it were happening now.

Six months after the bombing, Arjun sat in the office of a trauma therapist his employer's EAP had
referred him to. He was twenty-four now, though he felt much older. His hands trembled as he sat
down. When the therapist asked him what brought him in, he opened his mouth and found, as always,
that the words would not come. He tried: "I was in an explosion." But the sentence felt like a lie,
inadequate to the weight of what had happened, to what he carried. He looked at his hands—calloused
from the exercise, trembling with the effort of not using—and felt the old conviction settle over
him like a familiar coat: there was something broken inside him, something that had invited this
violence, something that meant he would never be safe, that he would never have a future worth
living. What he wanted, though he could not say it aloud, was to believe that the world could be
safe again, that he could be safe again. What terrified him was that he had learned too much to ever
unknow it.
