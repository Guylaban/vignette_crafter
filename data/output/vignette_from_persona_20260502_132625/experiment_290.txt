# Aroha

Aroha was forty-six when her brother Hemi died. It happened on a Tuesday morning in March, sudden
and without warning—a massive coronary while he was driving to work, his car drifting across the
median and into an embankment. She wasn't there. She learned about it through a phone call from her
sister-in-law, whose voice fractured as she spoke. But Aroha's mind had seized on the details
anyway: the sound of impact, the silence after, the way Hemi's last moments must have been filled
with confusion and terror. She could not stop imagining it, even though she had not been present.
The auditory memory had become vivid and intrusive—she heard the screech of tires, the crack of
metal, sometimes even the shallow gasp she imagined Hemi might have made in those final seconds.
When these sounds returned unbidden during her day, they were so acute that she would have to stop
whatever she was doing, her hands shaking, her breath catching, overwhelmed by a grief so physical
it felt like drowning.

Aroha worked as a forensic analyst for the state police, a career she had built over two decades
with precision and discipline. The work required her to examine evidence with meticulous attention,
to reconstruct scenes, to find meaning in what remained. But in the months after Hemi's death, she
found herself unable to enter the laboratory. The fluorescent lights, the sterile tables, the very
act of examining death—someone else's death, preserved in photographs and samples—became unbearable.
She began calling in sick, then taking leave. What frightened her most was not the work itself but
the thought that if she could not save her own brother, what right did she have to seek justice for
strangers? She had come to believe that her life had been destroyed, that nothing she did mattered
anymore, that the universe was fundamentally cruel and indifferent. Her faith—the Samoan Catholic
faith that had anchored her family—felt hollow now. God had not saved Hemi. Why would He save
anyone? These convictions settled into her like a weight, and they made it easier to stay home, to
avoid the office, to tell herself that going through the motions was pointless.

Her sleep deteriorated in parallel. Unable to bear the silence of her bedroom, where she would lie
awake replaying those imagined final moments, Aroha began staying up until two or three in the
morning, scrolling through news articles about sudden cardiac death, reading medical forums,
watching documentaries about accident reconstruction. Her husband, Tane, would find her at the
kitchen table at midnight, a cup of cold tea beside her laptop, her eyes red-rimmed. She was not
eating much either. Food had lost its taste; swallowing felt like an act of betrayal, as if
nourishing herself was an acceptance that life was worth living. She restricted what she took in,
sometimes going entire days on coffee and a single piece of fruit, her weight dropping noticeably.
These habits—the avoidance of work, the delayed sleep, the undereating—had formed gradually, each
one seeming like a small, private act of control in a world that had spun entirely out of control.

But the avoidance and deprivation were feeding the very thoughts they were meant to escape. The
longer Aroha stayed away from the laboratory, the more convinced she became that she could never
return, that her career was finished, that she had become someone irrelevant and broken. Staying
awake meant more hours for the intrusive memories to surface, more time for her mind to construct
those final moments in Hemi's car, more opportunities for the auditory recall to ambush her—the
screech, the impact, the silence. And the undereating left her exhausted and emotionally raw, making
it harder to resist the cascade of dark thoughts when the memories returned. When the snapshot
images came—Hemi's face, the empty driver's seat, the twisted guardrail—she felt so overwhelmed that
she had to stop, had to put her head down, had to surrender to the conviction that nothing would
ever feel meaningful again. The more she withdrew, the more her appraisals hardened. The more her
appraisals hardened, the more she withdrew.

It was Tane who finally insisted she see someone. He had watched his wife disappear into a fog of
avoidance and despair over eight months, had seen her lose fifteen pounds, had heard her crying in
the kitchen at three in the morning. Aroha came to her first appointment hollow-eyed and resistant,
convinced that therapy was another pointless exercise, but also—somewhere beneath the
hopelessness—desperate to stop hearing her brother's final moments on repeat.
