# Anahera

Anahera was thirty-two rows back when the explosion came. She remembers this fact with absolute
certainty, though almost everything else from that afternoon in March has fractured into sensory
shards—a smell of scorched fabric, a pressure against her ribs, the specific timbre of screaming
that she cannot quite separate from her own voice. She had been dancing in the ensemble for a
contemporary piece at the Civic Center, a venue she had performed in dozens of times. The audience
was maybe four hundred people. She was twenty-six then, living alone in a one-bedroom apartment in
the Pearl District, her career finally gaining momentum after years of teaching children's classes
and waiting tables. The blast came during the second act. She knows it was a deliberate attack,
knows the names of the three people killed, knows that the person responsible was arrested at the
scene and convicted. What she cannot hold in her mind is the sequence of what happened after—how she
got out, who helped her, whether she was injured or whether the blood on her clothes belonged to
someone else. These details exist in her memory as disconnected fragments, pieces that refuse to
cohere into a coherent narrative. Sometimes she feels the full weight of that afternoon's terror
with such intensity that her body becomes rigid, her breath shallow, yet no image accompanies
it—only the raw emotional residue, as if the fear has detached itself from whatever circumstances
spawned it.

In the months that followed, Anahera tried to return to dance. She attended rehearsals, but her body
no longer felt like her own. Certain conversation topics—any mention of the attack, any news story
about security or violence—would trigger a flood of fragmented images and a spike of vigilance that
could last for hours. When people mentioned the attacker's name in passing, even in an entirely
different context, her nervous system would flood with alarm, and the disconnected pieces of her
memory would rattle loose again: the smell, the pressure, the screaming. These moments would send
her spiraling into a particular kind of despair. She had come to believe that the people responsible
had faced no real consequence—that the conviction was hollow, that the system had failed, that
nothing could ever restore what had been taken. This belief gnawed at her, and it fed into a deeper
conviction that nothing in her life held meaning anymore. Dance, which had once been her identity
and her joy, now felt like a performance of normalcy she could no longer sustain. Her connection to
her community—the tight-knit Samoan dance collective that had nurtured her for years—had fractured.
She stopped attending their gatherings, stopped returning calls, convinced that she was no longer
one of them, that the trauma had marked her as separate.

As her sense of meaninglessness deepened, Anahera began to construct defenses against it. She forced
herself to think only positive thoughts, a discipline she imposed with almost punishing rigor,
trying to mentally override the intrusive despair. But this effort to reframe her experience only
seemed to intensify her preoccupation with why the attack had happened to her specifically, as if
understanding the reason would restore some lost order to the world. She began avoiding enclosed
spaces—elevators, small dressing rooms, even the bathroom with the door closed—because the sensation
of being trapped triggered a cascade of fragmented memories and a suffocating sense of entrapment
that echoed the afternoon itself. The avoidance gradually narrowed her world. She stopped taking
dance classes that required changing in shared dressing rooms. She turned down an opportunity to
perform in a small theater production because the green room was windowless. At her current
job—teaching contemporary dance at a local studio—she requested that her classes be held only in the
large, open studio with multiple exits, a request that raised eyebrows but which she could not bring
herself to explain.

What had begun as specific avoidance of enclosed spaces metastasized into a broader hypervigilance.
Anahera found herself on high alert whenever she heard unexpected or loud sounds—a car backfiring on
the street below her apartment would send her heart racing, her mind flooding with fragmentary
sensations of the blast. She had come to feel threatened by strangers approaching her, even in
ordinary situations like a grocery store or a coffee shop. This constant threat monitoring exhausted
her and paradoxically fed her sense that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that her initial
appraisal of the attack as a random, senseless act of violence was correct, and that no amount of
positive reframing could change that reality. The hypervigilance itself became another trigger: when
she noticed herself tensing at a loud noise or scanning a room for exits, she would feel a surge of
despair about her inability to control her own nervous system, which would then pull loose more
fragmented pieces of memory—the pressure, the smell, the screaming—and the cycle would tighten
again.

By the time she came to the clinic, Anahera had been living in this narrowing loop for nearly three
years. She described herself as "trapped in a box I built," aware that her avoidance was isolating
her but unable to step outside it without triggering the very symptoms she was trying to escape. She
came because a colleague had noticed her declining, and because she realized that her attempts to
force positivity and meaning-making were not working—that she was, in fact, becoming more entrenched
in the conviction that nothing mattered, that no one was accountable, and that she would never dance
again in a way that felt authentic. What she most feared was that this was permanent, that the
person she had been before March was gone forever.
