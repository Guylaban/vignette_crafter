# Clinical Vignette: Linh

Linh had always been careful about the men she allowed into her life. At fifty-one, she and her
husband David had built something solid over twenty-three years—a dental practice in Portland, a
modest house with a garden where she grew jasmine, a rhythm of work and home that felt orderly and
safe. But in the winter of 2022, a patient named Marcus had begun to disrupt that order in ways that
escalated beyond the ordinary boundary violations she'd learned to manage as a woman in healthcare.
He started by scheduling appointments at the end of the day, when the office was emptying. Then he
began asking her personal questions—where she lived, what she did on weekends, whether David
traveled for work. When Linh politely redirected these conversations, Marcus didn't retreat;
instead, he became more insistent, more detailed in his attention. He learned the route she took
home. He appeared at the grocery store where she shopped. He sent messages to her practice email
that started professional and gradually became declarations of feeling, of certainty that she was
meant to be in his life, that David didn't understand her the way he did. The police became involved
in March. They spoke to Marcus, warned him, told Linh they had documented everything and that she
was safe. But the word *safe* had lost its moorings.

What Linh discovered in the months that followed was that safety, once fractured, could not be
repaired simply by the removal of the threat. Even after Marcus was arrested and the restraining
order was filed, even when David assured her that the danger had passed, Linh found herself unable
to believe in her own security. The certainty she had once taken for granted—that her home was hers,
that the world operated according to predictable rules—had been replaced by a vigilance that felt
permanent. When she returned to the dental practice in May, she began to notice things she had never
paid attention to before: the location of the exits, the reflective surfaces in mirrors, the way
patients could move through the space. She rearranged her treatment room so that her chair faced the
door. At home, she could not sit on the sofa without repositioning herself so her back was against
the wall, her eyes on the room's entrance. David would find her at dinner, turned slightly in her
chair, her gaze drifting from him to the hallway and back. When he tried to reassure her—"The order
is in place, he's in custody, you're safe"—something in her chest would tighten. The words didn't
reach her. She had come to believe that reassurance itself was a form of blindness, that the people
who told her she was safe were simply not seeing what she could now see: that danger was patient,
that it wore different faces, that it could return.

Then came the songs. In July, a track played on the radio in her operatory—a popular song with a
melody that had been playing during one of Marcus's final messages to her, a voicemail she had
played for the police. The moment she heard those opening notes, her heart seized. The song had
become a trigger, a doorway back into the feeling of being watched, of having her life narrated by
someone else's obsession. Other songs followed. A commercial on television that used similar music.
A news segment about a restraining order violation in another state. Each exposure pulled her back
into that state of threat, her body convinced that danger was imminent even as her rational mind
knew the threat had been contained. She began to avoid the radio, to mute the television during
commercials, to check what was playing before she entered public spaces. At the practice, she asked
her receptionist to control the background music, to avoid anything that might trigger the
association. The accommodation seemed reasonable until it wasn't—until Linh realized she was
scanning every environment before she entered it, asking herself what might be playing, what might
appear on a screen, whether a police car passing outside might mean something had changed, that
Marcus had somehow breached the order. The vigilance that had once felt protective had become its
own kind of confinement.

Now, six months into this cycle, Linh sits in her office between patients with her shoulders tight
and her back to the wall, even in rooms where no one else is present. She has lost weight. Her sleep
is fractured. David, who loves her, has begun to withdraw slightly—not from anger, but from
exhaustion, from the impossibility of reaching her across the gap that Marcus created. What Linh
most fears is that this is permanent, that she will spend the rest of her life unable to trust even
the people closest to her, unable to believe that safety is anything more than a story others tell
themselves. She has finally called a therapist, hoping that someone trained in trauma might help her
understand why her mind refuses to accept what the law has already declared: that she is no longer
in danger. But even as she makes the appointment, she wonders if it's futile—if some violations of
safety cut too deep to ever fully heal.
