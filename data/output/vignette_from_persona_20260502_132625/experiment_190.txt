# Kahu's Unraveling

Kahu had been editing manuscripts for forty-three years when the tremor began. At seventy-four, he
had built a reputation as meticulous, exacting—the kind of editor who caught what others missed. But
something had fractured in him five years ago, in the apartment on Sunset Boulevard, and he had
spent every day since trying to shore up the pieces without letting anyone notice the cracks.

It had been a Tuesday evening when Marcus, his partner of eight years, had come home furious about a
dinner Kahu had organized. Marcus had wanted it one way; Kahu had arranged it another. The criticism
had started calmly enough, but it escalated into something else—something physical and terrible.
Kahu remembered it in flashes: Marcus's face contorted, his hand raised, the shock of impact. But
the memory did not feel like his own experience. When he tried to recall that night, it arrived as a
series of still photographs, each one isolated and vivid but somehow belonging to someone else
entirely. He was watching it happen to a stranger. The rage that flooded him when those images
surfaced was so intense it frightened him—a fury he could barely contain, directed at Marcus, at
himself, at the man he had become.

He had left Marcus three months later, but leaving had not ended it. Every time someone at work
corrected his markup or questioned a decision, Kahu felt the old terror spike. A colleague's raised
voice during an editorial meeting sent him into a spiral of avoidance—he would volunteer for remote
work, hide in his office, distract himself with busywork until the feeling passed. He had come to
believe that the violence had happened because of who he was, some fundamental flaw that had drawn
it to him. He was certain he had become something less than human in that apartment—an object for
someone else's rage, not a person capable of genuine connection or feeling. The thought that he
might never experience normal emotion again had settled into him like a permanent cold.

His avoidance had become elaborate. He avoided anything that reminded him of Marcus: the
neighborhood where they'd lived, the restaurants they'd frequented, even certain types of music that
played in the background during those final months. He had stopped accepting social invitations
because he could not bear the risk of encountering Marcus, and the fear of that possibility had
metastasized into a broader dread of being around people at all. When strong emotions threatened to
surface—anger at a manuscript rejection, sadness at a friend's illness—Kahu would shut them down
immediately, filling the space with distraction. He would reorganize his files, scroll through news
websites, anything to keep his mind from settling anywhere dangerous.

But the avoidance was feeding the very thing it was meant to contain. Each time he pushed away a
feeling or distracted himself from a difficult moment, the memory of that night would resurface with
renewed intensity, arriving in those fragmented, alien snapshots. And those images would trigger new
avoidance—new reasons to withdraw, new threats he needed to neutralize. The more he tried not to
feel, the more the rage would ambush him when the memory broke through. The more he avoided people
and places, the more he convinced himself that he was indeed fundamentally broken, that the violence
had revealed something true about his nature. His editor's eye, once his greatest asset, had turned
inward—he was now constantly monitoring himself for signs of the person he feared he had become.

His daughter had noticed the change months ago. She had stopped calling as often because the
conversations felt one-sided, Kahu's voice flat and distant. His work had suffered too; he had
turned down a promotion, unable to manage the increased social demands. He had begun calling in sick
more frequently, spending entire days in his apartment, the walls closing in. It was only when his
doctor had gently suggested that something more serious might be happening—that the hypervigilance,
the emotional numbness, the rage that would sometimes wake him at three in the morning—that Kahu had
finally admitted he could not manage this alone. He had come to therapy hoping that someone could
tell him whether what he feared was true: that he was irreparably damaged, or whether there was
still a person underneath the wreckage.
