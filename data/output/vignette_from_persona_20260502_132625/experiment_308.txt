# Clinical Vignette: Linh

Linh was fifty-seven when she came to her first appointment, though the lines around her eyes and
the careful way she held her shoulders made her seem older. She had worked as a midwife for thirty-
two years—a career built on steadiness, on the ability to remain calm in crisis, to read a room and
know what a labouring woman needed before she asked. That competence had defined her. Three years
ago, it had fractured.

The assault happened on an evening shift at the hospital. A man she had never seen before had
followed her into the break room after visiting hours. What struck Linh most, in the retelling, was
not the violence itself but the smell—a acrid, burnt odour clinging to his clothes, chemical and
wrong. He had been smoking outside, she would later learn, cheap cigarettes with something else
mixed in. For months afterward, whenever she caught that burnt smell—in the parking garage, drifting
from a patient's visitor, even the slightly scorched edge of toast at home—her body would seize. Her
heart would race. Her breath would shallow. The smell had become a wire connecting her directly to
that room, that moment, that helplessness.

What had begun as a sensory trigger had metastasized into something far larger. Linh found herself
listening—always listening. In the hospital corridors, she tracked footsteps. At home, she monitored
every creak of the house, every car that slowed on the street outside, certain she could detect
intention in the sound. The burnt smell would arrive unbidden, and her body's alarm would activate,
and then the vigilance would spike: *someone could be entering, someone could be here, I need to
know*. The trigger and the threat had become inseparable. A smell meant danger. Danger meant she had
to watch, had to listen, had to be ready.

And the watching had consequences. Linh had begun studying faces—her patients' faces, her
colleagues' faces, her husband's face—searching for the micro-expressions that might signal threat.
In the delivery suite, where she had once moved with intuitive grace, she now found herself
hesitant, her attention fractured between the clinical task and the relentless scanning of the room.
*Is that midwife angry? Does that student look aggressive? Could that visitor be dangerous?* Her
body had learned to stay in a state of readiness, muscles tense, adrenaline primed. When she was
uncomfortable—which was almost always now—the hypervigilance intensified. The discomfort itself
became a trigger. Feeling vulnerable or exposed, being seen by others, made her certain that threat
was near.

Her colleagues had noticed. Linh had taken a step back from mentoring. She had requested shifts that
kept her away from the break room. She had stopped attending the annual midwifery conference, where
she would be surrounded by strangers. At home, she had installed a second lock on the bedroom door.
Her husband, Rajesh, had learned not to approach her from behind. She flinched at his touch now,
sometimes. The woman he had married—the one who had delivered over three thousand babies, who had
held frightened mothers through their darkest hours—seemed to be disappearing, replaced by someone
locked in constant negotiation with invisible threats.

It was Rajesh who finally insisted she seek help. He had found her one morning standing in the
kitchen at 3 a.m., having heard a sound she couldn't identify, unable to return to bed. She was
exhausted but couldn't rest. She was afraid but couldn't articulate of what. And the burnt smell—it
seemed to follow her everywhere now, real or imagined, a phantom she couldn't escape. Linh had come
to believe that her vigilance was the only thing keeping her safe, that if she let her guard down
for even a moment, catastrophe would find her. But the vigilance itself was destroying her. She
wanted her life back. She was terrified that even if she got help, she would never feel safe again.
