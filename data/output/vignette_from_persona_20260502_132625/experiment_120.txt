# Robert's Accident

Robert Castellano was fifty-four when a delivery truck ran a red light at the intersection of Fifth
and Morrison, T-boning his sedan on the driver's side. He remembered the sound first—a compressed
metal scream, the window exploding inward, his own voice somewhere distant and strange. The truck's
bumper crushed the door frame; his left shoulder dislocated on impact, his ribs cracked, and for
three seconds that felt like hours, he couldn't draw breath. When the paramedics extracted him, his
hands were shaking so violently they had to strap him to the gurney. The hospital stay lasted nine
days. The physical recovery took months of physical therapy, but the accident itself—the moment of
absolute helplessness, the knowledge that his body had betrayed him utterly, that he had nearly died
in broad daylight—that never left.

In the first weeks after discharge, Robert tried to return to his life as an interior designer. His
firm, which he'd built over thirty years, had clients waiting. He could work from home, make site
visits, oversee installations. But something had shifted. When he sat at his desk to review fabric
swatches and floor plans, his mind would catch on small sounds—a car horn from the street below, the
rumble of a delivery vehicle—and his body would flood with adrenaline. His heart would hammer. His
breathing would shallow. He had come to believe that his body could no longer be trusted to keep him
safe, that it might fail him again without warning, and this terror lived beneath every waking
moment.

The triggers multiplied. Three weeks after coming home, a client wanted to reschedule a consultation
because she'd been in a fender-bender. Robert listened to her describe the impact, the moment of
fear, and felt his stomach drop. He made an excuse—a migraine, a scheduling conflict—and cancelled.
Within a month, he'd stopped accepting new projects that required site visits. When colleagues or
friends mentioned accidents, even casually, his chest would tighten. He'd excuse himself, citing
fatigue or work pressure. By autumn, he'd noticed that even photographs in design magazines—a sleek
car, a street scene—could trigger a wave of panic that left him shaky and withdrawn.

By the following winter, Robert's world had contracted significantly. He stopped driving altogether,
relying on Uber, though the experience of sitting in a stranger's car, unable to control the vehicle
or predict what might happen, filled him with dread. He began turning down projects that required
leaving his apartment. His firm, sensing his withdrawal, had offered him a transition to a
consulting role, but Robert knew the real problem wasn't his work capacity—it was that he no longer
recognized himself. The man who had once thrived on site visits, on the energy of construction sites
and client meetings, had become someone who flinched at traffic sounds and avoided the street. What
frightened him most was the permanence of it. He was certain he had changed for the worse, that this
version of himself—anxious, limited, trapped—was who he would be forever. His ex-wife, with whom
he'd remained cordial, commented after a video call that he seemed "diminished somehow." He didn't
argue.

Now, at fifty-seven, Robert had spent three years managing his life around the accident. He worked
from home exclusively, communicating with clients by email and video. He lived on the third floor of
a building with a back entrance, which meant he could avoid the main street when he left. He'd
stopped seeing friends who lived across town. The panic attacks had become less frequent—perhaps
because he'd eliminated so many situations that triggered them—but they hadn't disappeared. What
finally brought him to seek help was a realization that struck him one morning while reviewing a
design proposal: he couldn't remember the last time he'd felt capable, the last time he'd trusted
his own judgment or his body's ability to move through the world without catastrophe. He was living
a life of avoidance, and it was a life he no longer recognized as his own.
