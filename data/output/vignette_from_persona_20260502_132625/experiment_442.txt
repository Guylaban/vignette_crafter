# Fadi's Cycle

Fadi Mansour was sixty-four when he came to the clinic, a man whose silver hair and tailored suits
still carried the bearing of someone accustomed to command. He had spent fifteen years in politics—a
state representative, then a consultant on Middle Eastern affairs—but his real education had come
two decades earlier, when he was younger and convinced that bearing witness mattered. He had gone to
Syria in 2011 as a relief worker, driving medical supplies into towns that were already fracturing
under siege. For eight months he moved between safe houses and field clinics, learning the routes by
heart, learning which checkpoints were predictable and which were not. Then one afternoon in Aleppo,
the clinic where he was unloading supplies took a direct hit. He was outside, thirty meters away,
when the blast came. The building collapsed into itself—a sound like the world breaking—and Fadi was
thrown backward against a wall. He lay there for seconds that felt like hours, ears ringing, unable
to move. When he finally stood, the air was thick with dust and the smell of burnt concrete and
something else, something organic and terrible, that his mind would never quite catalog. Two
colleagues were dead. He was not.

For the first few years after he returned home, Fadi had managed. He threw himself into political
work with an intensity that impressed his colleagues and exhausted his ex-wife. She had left in
2015, citing his emotional distance, his refusal to talk about what had happened, his habit of
working until midnight most nights. He had not argued. He understood, in some part of himself, that
he was using work as a wall, but the alternative—sitting with his own mind—felt more dangerous than
any checkpoint had ever been. By his early sixties, Fadi had built a life that looked intact from
the outside: a successful career, a comfortable apartment in the city, invitations to fundraisers
and policy dinners. But the architecture was increasingly fragile.

The trouble had accelerated over the past three years, though Fadi could not have pinpointed exactly
when the shift became undeniable. He had come to dread certain situations with an intensity that
surprised him. Any conversation with someone he did not know well—a new constituent at a town hall,
a colleague's spouse at a reception—would trigger a sharp, clarifying anxiety. His mind would race
ahead, assessing threat in the person's body language, in the tone of their voice, in what they
might ask. When someone brought up military operations or geopolitical conflict, even in abstract
terms, something in his chest would tighten. He would feel the memory beginning to surface—not as
words or images exactly, but as a rush of sensation, a return of the helplessness. And then, without
quite deciding to, he would reach for his phone or excuse himself to check his email, anything to
interrupt the spiral before it consumed him. The moment someone mentioned the military or war, Fadi
would find himself distracted, pulling his attention back to his work, his calendar, anything that
required his focus in the present moment. This habit of deflection had become so automatic that he
barely registered it anymore.

What had worsened more recently was the silence. Fadi had always been someone who filled quiet with
sound—music, podcasts, phone calls. But now even that was not enough. When silence fell—in his
apartment at night, in his car at red lights, in the gaps between sentences at a meeting—he felt
something coiling in his chest. His body would tense, his eyes would sharpen, as if danger were
gathering in the stillness itself. He had begun checking his apartment doors and windows
compulsively, sometimes twice in an evening. He would scan rooms when he entered them, noting exits,
assessing which people could be trusted. The hypervigilance had become so habitual that he barely
noticed it anymore; it was simply how he moved through the world now.

When the memories did break through—usually late at night, or after a trigger he had not managed to
deflect—they came in fragments that made no narrative sense. He would remember the sound but not the
visual. He would remember the smell and then suddenly be flooded with the emotion of that moment,
the pure terror of lying on the ground unable to move, unable to know if he was dying. The emotional
overwhelm was so complete that he had learned to stop himself, to pull back, to think about
something else before the memory could fully form. He would distract himself with work, with a task,
with anything that required his full attention. But the more he tried to control these emotional
surges, the more sensitive his system became to the triggers that might unleash them. A burnt
smell—a neighbor's cooking, a candle someone lit at the office—could derail his entire day. And the
silence, the terrible silence, would bring back the memory in fragmented pieces, and the memory
would send him scanning the room, checking the locks, convinced that something catastrophic was
about to unfold.

It was his chief of staff who finally insisted he seek help, after noticing that Fadi had missed two
important meetings and had been short with staff in ways that were uncharacteristic. Fadi had
agreed, though he was not certain what he expected from therapy. What he feared most was being
unable to control what came up, losing the careful architecture he had built to keep the past
contained. What he hoped for, though he would not have admitted it easily, was to feel safe again in
silence, to be able to talk to a stranger without his nervous system preparing for catastrophe.
