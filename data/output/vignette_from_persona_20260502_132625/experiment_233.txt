# Clinical Vignette: Rajesh

Rajesh had been an electrician for fifty-two years, a man who took pride in his steady hands and his
knowledge of the city's wiring systems. At seventy-six, he still took jobs—smaller ones now, mostly
residential rewiring and maintenance calls in the neighborhoods where he and his wife, Priya, had
built their life. Three months ago, that life had fractured in an instant.

He had been leaving his truck on a side street near a commercial building on a Tuesday evening,
tools locked in the bed, keys in his pocket. Two men approached from behind a parked car. One of
them smelled of whiskey so strongly that Rajesh caught it before he heard their voices. They
demanded his wallet, his watch—his father's watch, the one he had worn every day since 1985. When
Rajesh hesitated, the taller man shoved him against the truck bed. The edge caught his ribs. The
smell of alcohol grew thicker, more suffocating, mixed with the metallic taste of blood from where
his teeth had cut the inside of his mouth. The second man's hand moved toward his jacket, and Rajesh
had been certain—absolutely certain—that he was about to be shot. He had given them everything. They
ran. He stood there, shaking, unable to move for what felt like hours but was probably minutes.

Since that night, Rajesh had not returned to electrical work. The thought of walking toward an
unfamiliar building, of being in an alley or a dimly lit street, made his chest tight. But more than
that, something had changed inside him—a fundamental shift in how he read the world. Strangers now
registered as potential threats before he could think otherwise. A man standing too close in the
grocery store, a teenager with his hands in his pockets walking past the house—these ordinary
moments triggered a cascade of vigilance that left him exhausted. What frightened him most was the
unpredictability, the way danger seemed to wear the face of anyone who approached him.

The nightmares began within the first week. He would wake at three or four in the morning, drenched
in sweat, his heart racing as though he were still pressed against that truck. In these dreams, the
outcome varied—sometimes the man did reach for a gun, sometimes Rajesh could not move his legs to
run. The smell was always there. When he woke, gasping, the lingering sense of threat did not
dissipate with consciousness. Instead, the dream's logic seemed to seep into his waking mind,
leaving him convinced that the night itself was dangerous, that safety was something that only
existed in daylight. He found it harder to feel secure after sunset, his body on high alert, ears
attuned to every sound outside the bedroom window. Even inside the house, with Priya sleeping beside
him, he would lie awake, monitoring, waiting.

Crowded places—the temple where he and Priya had worshipped for decades, the market where he used to
linger and chat with vendors—had become intolerable. A few weeks after the mugging, he accompanied
his wife to the temple for a festival. The sanctuary was full, bodies close together, the familiar
warmth of community suddenly suffocating. When someone bumped against him in the crowd, his entire
body tensed. A man's sudden movement near the altar—reaching up to light an oil lamp—sent Rajesh
into a state of acute alarm. His mind flooded with the memory of the mugging, with the
unpredictability of that moment when he could not anticipate what would happen next. He left the
temple early, telling Priya he was unwell. He has not returned.

The smell of alcohol, which he encounters occasionally when someone passes him on the street or when
a neighbor opens a bottle at a gathering, acts as a direct conduit back to that Tuesday evening. A
whiff of whiskey at a restaurant, the faint aroma of beer from a delivery truck—and suddenly Rajesh
is standing against that truck again, the threat immediate and overwhelming, his body flooding with
the same terror, his mind convinced that danger is imminent. Priya has noticed that he no longer
wants to go out in the evenings, that he declines invitations to dinners where alcohol might be
served. What she does not fully understand is that for Rajesh, these are not mere preferences but
desperate attempts to avoid the moments when his mind is pulled backward, when the distinction
between then and now collapses entirely.

He came to the clinic because Priya insisted, because his sleep has become unsustainable, and
because he is beginning to feel like a prisoner in his own home. He is afraid that this is simply
who he is now—a man who cannot be safe, who cannot trust the world or the people in it. What he
hopes for, though he barely dares to say it, is to feel like himself again, to walk into a room
without his body braced for attack.
