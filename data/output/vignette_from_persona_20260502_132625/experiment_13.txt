between declarations of love and accusations that he had led her on, that he had made promises he
never intended to keep. She called his phone at odd hours. She drove past his house. Once, she
followed him from the grocery store back home, and Marcus watched in the rearview mirror as her car
maintained a steady distance behind his, her headlights fixed on him through three turns. The police
became involved after six weeks. A restraining order was issued. Jennifer eventually moved away, and
the messages stopped. But something in Marcus had fractured in those weeks, and it had not healed.

Nearly a year had passed since the stalking ended, and Marcus had come to believe that he could not
trust his own judgment about people. Jennifer had seemed ordinary, a concerned parent, and yet she
had harbored an obsession he had completely failed to detect. This failure haunted him. What else
was he missing? He had come to see people as fundamentally deceptive, wearing masks that concealed
their true intentions. His wife, Sarah, had been patient in the immediate aftermath, but Marcus
found himself unable to be emotionally close to her. When she reached for him in bed, he stiffened.
When she tried to talk about her day, he heard accusations beneath her words. The intimacy that had
once felt natural now felt dangerous—a vulnerability that could be weaponized. He told himself he
deserved this isolation, that his blindness to Jennifer's intentions was a character flaw so
profound that he had forfeited the right to trust or be trusted. He deserved to suffer for it.

The triggers were everywhere now, though Marcus did not always recognize them as such. When someone
raised their voice in a store, even in ordinary frustration, his body flooded with adrenaline. Was
this person about to become obsessive? Dangerous? A motorcycle passing on the street sent his heart
racing—was someone tracking him? When he noticed a car behind him on his usual route home from
tutoring sessions, he took elaborate detours, convinced he was being followed again. Each of these
moments fed his conviction that people were not what they seemed and that he could not trust his
ability to read a situation correctly. The triggers seemed to confirm his darkest interpretations.

advance. He avoided relaxing—sitting still felt like an invitation to disaster, a moment of
inattention in which someone could find him, harm him, expose him. His hypervigilance became a kind
of punishment he imposed on himself, a way of ensuring he would never again be blindsided. Sleep
eluded him. He would lie awake beside Sarah, listening to the sounds of the neighborhood, certain
that each unfamiliar noise signaled a threat. His body remained in a state of constant readiness,
muscles tense, mind racing. When the mailman knocked, when a car door slammed, when someone honked a
horn, Marcus reacted as if danger were imminent, even as some part of him recognized that nothing
was actually wrong. The false alarms happened daily now, sometimes hourly.

Sarah had stopped trying to reach him. The distance between them had become a chasm. Marcus's income
had shrunk as he took fewer students. His world had contracted to the size of his home, and even

saying that she loved him but could not watch him disappear. Marcus had made an appointment with a
therapist, though he was uncertain whether anyone could be trusted to help him. What he feared most
was that his judgment would fail him again, that he would misread a situation and suffer the
consequences. What he hoped for, though he barely dared to articulate it, was to feel safe again—to
sleep, to hold his wife, to step outside without his mind cataloging every possible threat.
