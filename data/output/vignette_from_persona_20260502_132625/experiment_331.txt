# Margaret's Story

Margaret was seventy-three when the community center fire took her husband of forty-eight years. It
was a Tuesday evening in March, the kind of spring night that had always felt like possibility—the
kind she and Robert used to walk through, hand in hand, before dinner at their favorite Italian
place downtown. Robert had gone to the center for his weekly chess tournament while Margaret stayed
home working on a landscape commission, the smell of linseed oil and turpentine filling her studio.
Around nine o'clock, the phone calls began. The fire had started in the kitchen during a catering
event, spread through the old wooden structure with terrifying speed, and by the time the exits
jammed, Robert and three others were trapped. The news showed her footage of the flames consuming
the brick building, the smoke billowing into the night sky, and she could not stop imagining him
there—the heat, the smoke filling his lungs, the realization that there was no way out. The
investigators later told her that the smell of alcohol had been everywhere; someone had knocked over
bottles of wine meant for the reception, and the accelerant had fed the fire's hunger.

In the months that followed, Margaret's world contracted. Certain sounds—the wail of a siren, a
sudden loud noise in the street—would send her heart racing, her body tensing as though bracing for
impact, and almost immediately the images would come: the flames, Robert's face, the smell of smoke
and burning wood mixing with something acrid and chemical. When her daughter visited and wore
perfume with alcohol notes, Margaret's chest would tighten and the memories would flood in unbidden,
vivid as photographs, and she would feel her body twist and contort in the same position she
imagined Robert had been in—limbs locked, chest compressed. The guilt came with the memories, sharp
and suffocating. Why had she not insisted he stay home that night? Why had she been safe in her
studio while he burned? She had come to believe that she deserved to suffer, that her survival was a
kind of cosmic injustice.

The irritability that emerged surprised her, then consumed her. When her son called with concern in
his voice, something in his tone—a note of pity, perhaps—ignited a fury in her that she could barely
contain. She snapped at him, hung up, and afterward sat alone in her living room listening. Always
listening. For footsteps, for the creak of the front door, for some sound that would signal danger.
She had stopped sleeping well; her mind would jolt her awake at three in the morning, convinced she
heard something, and then the images would come again—the photographs in her mind, crystal clear,
unbearable. The news terrified her now. She had begun avoiding any report of a fire, an accident, a
disaster. She could not bear to see it, could not bear the reminder that the world was not the safe,
orderly place she had believed it to be. Her beliefs about how things worked—that good people were
protected, that careful living meant safety—had shattered completely. If Robert could be taken like
that, if the universe could be so indifferent, then what did anything mean?

To survive the days, Margaret had learned to keep her mind occupied constantly. She would paint for
hours, pushing herself past the point of fatigue, filling every moment with color and form and
technical problem-solving, anything to avoid the silence where memories lived. She tried not to
think about that night, tried to suppress the images, but the effort itself became exhausting—a
constant vigilance against her own mind. She had stopped going to the chess club memorial; she could
not face the smell of the room, the voices of Robert's friends, the weight of their shared loss. Her
daughter had asked her to help plan a gallery show of her work, but Margaret had declined. How could
she paint about beauty when beauty felt like a lie, when the world had revealed itself to be a place
where a man could simply be erased? She was not sure her life was worth living anymore. The
paintings sat half-finished in her studio, and Margaret moved through the days like someone
operating a machine—present, functional, but convinced that the capacity for real feeling, for
genuine joy, had died in that fire along with Robert. What frightened her most was that this might
be all that remained: the vigilance, the memories, the guilt, the empty conviction that nothing
would ever be safe or whole again.
