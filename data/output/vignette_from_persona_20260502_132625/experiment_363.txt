# Clinical Vignette: Mariana

Mariana was nineteen when she was assaulted by a man she had met through a friend—someone who had
seemed safe enough until the moment he wasn't. It happened in an apartment she had trusted, on a
night that began ordinarily and ended with her pinned against a wall, his hand over her mouth, the
smell of whiskey and tobacco overwhelming her senses, the sound of Arabic words she didn't
understand hissed against her ear like a threat she couldn't decode. When it was over, she had
called her mother in shock, and within weeks she had married her boyfriend of six months—a decision
she made with the certainty that marriage would protect her, that having a husband meant never being
alone, meant being safe. Now, three months later, she sat in the therapist's office, nineteen years
old and already feeling like a stranger in her own life.

The assault had shattered something fundamental in how Mariana understood the world. She had
believed in her own judgment, in her ability to read people and situations, in the basic safety of
being in a room with someone who claimed to be a friend. That belief was gone. What replaced it was
a conviction that she was fundamentally incapable—that another person, someone more alert or
stronger or simply *better*, would have fought back, would have escaped, would have prevented the
whole thing from happening. She carried this thought like a stone in her chest. She had been an art
student before the assault, someone who spent hours drawing, who had won a scholarship to a local
college. Now she couldn't look at her own hands without feeling contaminated, couldn't bring herself
to pick up a pencil. When she tried, her hands trembled and she felt sick. The thing that had
defined her—the thing that made her *her*—felt irretrievable.

The triggers had multiplied in the months since. A motorcycle roared past her apartment building one
afternoon, and Mariana's entire body went rigid; the sound collapsed the distance between now and
that night, and without warning she was back in the apartment, gasping, her heart hammering. The
smell of alcohol on a stranger's breath at the grocery store sent her spiraling into a panic so
acute she abandoned her cart and drove home with white knuckles on the steering wheel. Worst of all
were the moments when she heard someone shouting in Arabic—a language she associated now not with
the friend who had assaulted her but with the incomprehensibility of her own powerlessness, the
foreign sounds that had accompanied her terror. Each trigger didn't simply remind her of what
happened; it *was* what happened, happening again, her body unable to distinguish between memory and
present danger.

These moments of sudden, unwanted recall were relentless. When the motorcycle noise hit, or when she
caught the scent of alcohol, the sensory details of the assault came flooding back—not as abstract
knowledge but as lived experience. She heard the words he had spoken, felt the pressure of his hand,
smelled the whiskey on his breath. Her mind couldn't seem to hold these memories at a distance; they
erupted without her permission, without warning, and once they were triggered, they reinforced her
sense that the world was fundamentally unsafe and that she, specifically, was incapable of
protecting herself.

To manage the intrusions, Mariana had begun avoiding anything that might trigger them. She stopped
going out after dark, period. She had become unemployed partly because she couldn't bear to be in
public spaces during evening hours, and the jobs available to her were all evening shifts. She
avoided the anniversary date of the assault by staying in bed, curtains drawn, phone off. But the
avoidance had its own consequence: by staying home, by shrinking her world, she found herself more
hypervigilant, not less. She would lie in bed listening for sounds—the creak of the apartment
building, footsteps in the hallway, the rattle of the front door lock. She checked behind her
constantly when she did leave the house, even in daylight, even on the rare occasions when she
forced herself to the grocery store. The avoidance was supposed to protect her, but instead it had
made her more afraid. By not going out, by not testing reality, she had inadvertently confirmed her
belief that the world outside was genuinely dangerous, that her hypervigilance was justified, that
she really was incapable of managing on her own.

Her avoidance of looking at her own body—of showering without immediately covering the mirror, of
wearing baggy clothes, of any awareness of herself as physical—had deepened her conviction that she
was no longer the person she used to be. The girl who could draw, who could move through the world
with some sense of agency, felt like someone else entirely. When she caught her reflection
unexpectedly, she felt a jolt of revulsion that she couldn't quite articulate. This disconnection
from her own body, combined with her constant scanning for threat, had trapped her in a cycle where
the more she avoided, the more threatened she felt, and the more threatened she felt, the more her
memories intruded, and the more her memories intruded, the more convinced she became that her
appraisals were correct—that she truly was incapable, that the world truly was dangerous, that she
had been right to marry so quickly, to withdraw, to try to disappear.

Mariana came to therapy because her husband had insisted, worried by her silence, her weight loss,
her refusal to leave the apartment. She told the therapist that what she feared most was that
this—this small, hypervigilant, avoidant version of herself—was who she would be forever. She was
hoping that someone could tell her she wasn't broken beyond repair.
