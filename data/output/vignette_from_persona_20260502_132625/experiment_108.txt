# Clinical Vignette: Robert

Robert was seventy-eight when he learned his diagnosis. The oncologist had spoken in measured tones
about staging and prognosis, but what Robert remembered most vividly was the smell—that particular
antiseptic-and-decay combination that seemed to emanate from the examination room itself, mingling
with something acrid and chemical that clung to his clothes. Six months of chemotherapy followed.
The infusions took place in a sunlit ward, but Robert had come to associate that brightness with a
kind of exposure, a nakedness of the body. He would lie in the reclining chair, arms extended for
the IV, watching the amber liquid enter his vein, and he would think: *This is poison. This is
supposed to save me, but it is poison.* The nurses were kind. The other patients nodded in
recognition as they shuffled past. None of it mattered. What mattered was the sensation of his body
betraying him—the nausea, the hair loss, the way his hands trembled when he tried to hold a coffee
cup.

He survived. The scans came back clear. His oncologist called it remission, used the word "cure"
cautiously but with something like hope. Robert went home to his small apartment in Arlington, sat
in his recliner, and waited for relief that never came.

Instead, the memories began to sharpen. Months after his last infusion, Robert would catch a whiff
of something—a cleaning product at the grocery store, a neighbor's cologne—and suddenly he was back
in the treatment chair, the IV in his arm, the knowledge that his own body had become dangerous. The
memory felt more real and vivid than the present moment, more solid than the worn fabric of his
recliner or the television murmuring in the corner. When these moments seized him, he felt not as
though he were remembering the illness, but as though the illness were happening right now, in real
time. His heart would race. His palms would sweat. The distinction between past and present
collapsed entirely.

These intrusive memories began to reshape how Robert understood his world. When he remembered the
treatment—when he felt it happening again in his body—he could not help but conclude something
fundamental about existence: the world was a dangerous place, and his own flesh was the instrument
of that danger. He had lost his faith in the natural order of things, in the idea that a body could
be trusted or that time could truly heal. His body kept reacting as if he were still in mortal
danger, even when he sat safely in his apartment with the doors locked. He came to believe that this
vigilance was the only rational response. The world had shown its teeth; his body had proven itself
an enemy. Remaining alert was not paranoia—it was wisdom earned at great cost.

Robert began to retreat. He felt on guard even inside his own home, as though the walls offered no
real protection. He avoided the grocery store where the smells might ambush him. He stopped
attending his weekly chess club at the library—too many people, too many unpredictable scents, too
much exposure. His daughter called less frequently after he missed his grandson's birthday dinner.
When she asked what was wrong, he told her nothing was wrong, but the lie exhausted him. He was
superalert, watchful, always monitoring for the next threat. Strong negative feelings—fear, anger, a
corrosive shame at his own weakness—had become the texture of his days. He had been a database
administrator for forty years; the work had required precision, logic, the satisfaction of
organizing chaos into systems. Now he worked from home, and even that felt precarious. He would sit
at his desk, ostensibly reviewing code, but mostly he was listening to the sounds of the building,
waiting for something unnamed to go wrong.

It was his ex-wife, Patricia, who finally insisted he see someone. She had run into him at a
pharmacy and was startled by how much weight he had lost, how his eyes moved constantly, never
settling. "You're not living," she said, not unkindly. "You're just waiting to die." Robert had no
argument. He made an appointment with a therapist he found online, sat in the waiting room smelling
the faint vanilla of a diffuser, and felt the old panic rising. But he stayed. He was seventy-eight
years old, and he was tired of being afraid.
