# David's Story

David was forty-one when he fled Bosnia with his wife and two young children in the spring of 1992.
What had been a quiet life as a rabbi in Sarajevo—teaching, leading services, counseling
congregants—ended in a single afternoon when Serb militias surrounded the neighborhood. He remembers
the sound first: not gunfire, but shouting, men's voices raw and uncontrolled, filling the streets
like a physical force. His hands were shaking so badly he could barely gather documents. A soldier
burst through a neighbor's door—David heard the crash of wood, the woman's scream—and he made the
decision that saved his life and haunted it: he pushed his family into the basement, told them to
hide, and did not go back up. He listened to boots on the floorboards above, to more shouting, to
silence. When it was over, they ran. He has never learned what happened to that neighbor.

Thirty years later, David sits in the back corner of his current synagogue during services—not at
the bimah where the rabbi should be, but in a seat he has chosen because it faces the door and
offers a view of the entire sanctuary. He is seventy-three now, still officiating, still teaching,
but something in him broke in those years between and never quite set right. His wife, Sarah,
notices that he flinches when she touches his shoulder unexpectedly, even in their own bedroom. A
raised voice in the hallway—a child arguing with a parent, nothing to do with him—sends him rigid,
his breath shallow, his mind suddenly elsewhere. When his grandson came to visit last month and
reached out to hug him from behind, David's body reacted before his mind caught up: he spun around,
arms up defensively, and saw the hurt in the boy's eyes. He had come to believe, over decades, that
his body was a traitor, still living as though the militias were outside, still convinced that
danger was imminent even when he sat safely in his study surrounded by books. What terrified him
most was the thought that someone stronger, someone braver, would have done more that day—would have
fought, would have protected others, would not have hidden. He could not trust himself to know when
he was truly safe, and this doubt had become a constant companion.

The unpredictability of touch, the sudden loudness of voices, the sight of anything that could be a
weapon—a kitchen knife, a baseball bat in a neighbor's garage—each triggered a cascade of reactions.
When his concentration fractured at his desk, unable to finish a sermon, David would pull up news
websites, scrolling through stories of violence, as if by watching he could somehow stay ahead of
danger. He avoided the intimacy that Sarah still offered, turning away from her in bed, telling
himself he was protecting her from his broken reactions. These avoidance patterns reinforced his
conviction that he was fundamentally unreliable, that his body would betray him, that his mind could
not be trusted to distinguish safety from threat. The more he withdrew, the more his nervous system
remained on high alert. The more hypervigilant he became—scanning rooms, choosing seats, monitoring
exits—the more he felt convinced that the world was as dangerous as it had been in 1992, that only
constant vigilance could keep him alive. His attempts to manage the fear through distraction and
isolation only deepened his sense that he could not rely on himself, that his reactions were proof
of some fundamental weakness. And when he felt that weakness most acutely, when his body surged with
alarm at an unexpected touch or a voice raised in anger, he would tell himself that he deserved this
suffering, that his failure to act that day had earned him a lifetime of this punishment.

Sarah finally insisted he see someone after he spent an entire Sabbath service unable to focus, his
leg bouncing, his eyes fixed on the door. He called a therapist the next morning. What he is most
afraid of is that the fear will never release its grip, that he will live out his remaining years as
a prisoner in his own body. What he is hoping for, though he barely dares to say it aloud, is that
he might one day sit beside his wife without flinching, that a sudden sound might startle him
without sending him back to that basement, that he might finally understand the difference between
the danger that was real and the danger that still haunts him.
