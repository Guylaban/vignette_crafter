# Marcus

Marcus was thirty-four when the warehouse fire started. He had been working the night shift at the
distribution center for seven years—a reliable, quiet man who showed up on time and kept to himself
during breaks. On that February night, he was in the loading bay when the sprinkler system failed. A
forklift operator, rushing to meet a deadline, had disabled the alarm to avoid triggering a false
evacuation. When the fire broke out in the adjacent storage unit, no one was notified. Marcus
smelled smoke first, then saw the orange glow creeping across the ceiling. He ran toward the back
exit, but the door had been locked from the outside—a safety violation no one had flagged. For eight
minutes, he was trapped in a corridor filling with black smoke, his hands pressed against the warm
metal door frame, screaming. When firefighters finally cut through, his palms were blistered and his
lungs scorched. Two of his coworkers didn't make it out.

The aftermath was a blur of hospital stays and investigations. Marcus returned to work after three
months, telling himself and his wife, Elena, that he was fine. He wasn't. Within weeks, certain
moments would ambush him—the texture of the warehouse's rough concrete wall, which he sometimes felt
in his mind's hands, would send him spiraling. When he encountered a coworker behaving
recklessly—cutting corners, ignoring protocol—the old rage and terror would spike together. And the
proximity of other people in crowded spaces, especially in enclosed areas, made his chest tighten in
ways he couldn't explain.

What frightened Marcus most was how real the memories felt. When he recalled those eight minutes,
they didn't feel like something that had happened two years ago. The snapshot images—his hands
burning against the metal, the smoke so thick he couldn't see his own fingers, the sound of his own
screaming—were vivid and immediate in a way that normal memories never were. Ordinary moments, by
contrast, felt like they were happening behind frosted glass. He would come home from work and Elena
would tell him about her day, and he would realize he'd absorbed almost nothing. But the fire—every
sensory detail of those eight minutes felt more real than the life he was actually living. The shame
came with these memories, hot and suffocating: shame that he'd panicked, shame that he'd survived
when others hadn't, shame that he couldn't seem to move past it.

Over time, Marcus developed ways to manage the intrusions. When a memory surfaced—the texture of the
concrete, the heat, the screaming—he would tell himself it wasn't really that bad, that his mind was
exaggerating, that the event itself had been less severe than he was remembering. He would push the
thoughts away with force, clenching his teeth and turning his attention obsessively to his phone or
the television. But these strategies only worked for minutes. The suppression would fracture, and
the memories would return sharper and more insistent. When that happened, Marcus would sometimes
pinch the inside of his forearm hard enough to leave marks, or press his fingernails into his palm
until they drew blood. The physical pain was clean and simple—it interrupted the memory, gave his
mind something immediate and controllable to focus on.

Yet the cycles were tightening. Encountering someone at work who bent safety rules—a young cashier
who propped open a fire door to avoid walking around—would trigger the memories, and the memories
would feel so vivid and real that Marcus would need to suppress them, and the suppression would
fail, and he would hurt himself to regain control. The triggers were everywhere. Even the texture of
certain fabrics in the stockroom—a rough synthetic blend that reminded him of the warehouse
wall—would set everything in motion again. Elena had noticed the small wounds on his arms and asked
about them once. He'd deflected. He couldn't explain that hurting himself was the only thing that
made the memories stop, that the pain was a kind of relief. Two weeks ago, his manager had called
him into the office to discuss his performance. Marcus was making mistakes, forgetting inventory
counts, calling in sick more often. He'd nearly had a panic attack standing near the loading dock.
He told Elena that night that he needed help, that something was very wrong and he didn't know how
to fix it himself. For the first time since the fire, he was afraid not of the memories themselves,
but of what his attempts to escape them were doing to him.
