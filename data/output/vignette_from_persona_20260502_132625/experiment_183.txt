# Clinical Vignette: Maria

Maria was forty-eight when the truck hit her car on Route 9, a Tuesday afternoon in late March. She
had been stopped at a red light, hands at ten and two, thinking about the evening's community dinner
she was organizing at the church where she worked as a social ministry coordinator. The driver
behind her—a man in his seventies who would later claim his foot slipped—did not brake. The impact
came without warning, a violent shunt that threw her forward against the seatbelt, her head snapping
back. The airbag deployed. For a moment, suspended in the chemical smell of deployed safety systems,
Maria understood with absolute clarity that she had no control over what happened to her body, that
she was entirely at the mercy of other people's choices and reflexes. The paramedics found her
conscious but shaking, complaining of neck pain. The police report would later note that the other
driver was cited for inattention. No charges were filed. He walked away with minor injuries. Maria
walked away with whiplash, a concussion, and the unshakeable conviction that the world had dealt her
an injustice from which there would be no real recompense.

In the weeks after the accident, Maria tried to return to normal. She attended her follow-up
appointments, did her physical therapy exercises, went back to work. But normal had become
impossible. Within a month, she stopped driving. The thought of being behind the wheel—or worse,
trapped as a passenger in someone else's vehicle—filled her with a dread so profound that her chest
would tighten just imagining it. She took the bus to work, which meant a longer commute, but the bus
felt safer; she could see the road ahead, could brace herself if needed. Her supervisor, initially
sympathetic, began to express concern about her reliability. When the church scheduled a community
outreach trip to a neighboring town, Maria declined. She could not be a passenger. She could not sit
in a vehicle with her hands in her lap, powerless. The injustice of it—that she had done nothing
wrong, that she was the victim, yet her life had contracted while the other driver faced no real
consequence—gnawed at her constantly. It was unfair. It was unbearable. And because it was unfair,
because the world had proven itself fundamentally unsafe and indifferent, she could not allow
herself to be in situations where her body was vulnerable or her agency stripped away.

This conviction—that she could no longer trust her own body, that bad things happened without
warning and she was helpless to stop them—began to reshape how she moved through the world. She
stopped going to the gym, stopped taking walks alone, avoided any situation that elevated her heart
rate or made her feel physically stressed. Physical exertion felt dangerous now, as though her body
might betray her at any moment. She also began to avoid anything that felt like relaxation. Sitting
still, closing her eyes, letting her guard down—these felt like invitations to disaster. Instead,
she remained vigilant. She scanned for exits whenever she entered a room or building, noting where
the doors were, how many people were between her and safety. Her body had become her enemy,
unreliable and subject to forces beyond her control, and the only way to manage that betrayal was to
stay alert, to anticipate danger, to never fully let go.

The hypervigilance intensified over the following months. Maria found herself startling at ordinary
sounds—a car backfiring, a door slamming, even the microwave beeping—her nervous system jolting as
though each noise might herald another impact. Colleagues at the church commented that she seemed
anxious, on edge. The truth was more complex: Maria had come to believe that safety was an illusion,
that other people's reassurances meant nothing because the world was fundamentally unpredictable and
hostile. When her supervisor told her she was safe driving in the church van, that the driver was
careful and experienced, Maria felt only a hollow despair. Safety was a lie people told themselves.
She had learned that on Route 9. And so she continued to avoid, to scan, to startle, to remain
trapped in a cycle where her avoidance of driving and physical stress only deepened her sense of
helplessness, where her vigilance confirmed her belief that danger lurked everywhere, where each
trigger—a car horn, the memory of that moment of impact, the thought of losing control—sent her
spiraling back into the same defensive postures. She had not driven in fourteen months. She had not
taken a full, relaxed breath in longer than that. It was this exhaustion, this sense of being
imprisoned by her own vigilance and her body's betrayal, that finally brought her to seek help.
