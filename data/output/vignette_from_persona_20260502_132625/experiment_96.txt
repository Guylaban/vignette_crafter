# Clinical Vignette: Thom

Thom was sixty-eight when the bomb went off outside the community centre in Kuala Lumpur where he
was coordinating resettlement paperwork for a group of Rohingya families. The blast came without
warning—a concussive roar that seemed to come from everywhere at once, followed by the sound of
glass cascading onto concrete and the screams of people he had been speaking with moments before. He
remembered the heat on his face, the acrid smell of smoke, the way the fluorescent lights flickered
and died. What he could not quite reconcile, even now four years later, was that it had happened at
all. When he tried to reconstruct those minutes, the images felt almost cinematic—too vivid, too
perfectly terrible to be real. He would see the face of Amara, one of the case workers, frozen in an
expression of confusion just before the windows exploded inward. He would see the papers on his desk
scattered like snow. But the memory had a dreamlike quality that made him doubt himself, as though
his mind was playing a trick, exaggerating what had actually occurred. The auditory fragments were
worse: at unpredictable moments, he would hear the initial whistle of the blast, the high-pitched
ringing that had filled his ears in those first seconds, sounds that seemed to play from inside his
own skull rather than from any external source.

It was this very unreality of the memories—the way they felt both hyperreal and impossible—that had
driven Thom into a pattern of rumination from which he could not escape. He would lie awake at night
replaying the sequence, trying to convince himself that it had truly happened, and in doing so,
would inevitably spiral into self-blame. Why had he been at his desk that morning? He could have
been in the back office. Why had he not noticed something amiss in the minutes before? Other aid
workers had survived similar incidents by sensing danger; he had sensed nothing. He had been
negligent, inattentive, and in his negligence, people had died. Amara had two young children. This
thought would return to him dozens of times a day, each recurrence accompanied by a fresh wave of
guilt. He had come to believe that if he could just understand what he had done wrong, if he could
fully accept his culpability, then perhaps the memories would finally make sense—would solidify into
something he could live with rather than something that haunted him like a ghost that refused to
take solid form.

The intrusive images and sounds had also fundamentally altered how Thom perceived the world as
dangerous. In the weeks following the blast, he had noticed that ordinary noises—a car backfiring, a
door slamming, even the sharp crack of a wooden spoon against a pot in his kitchen—would send him
into a state of acute alarm, his body tensing as though another explosion were imminent. His wife,
Mei, had learned not to move suddenly around him. More insidiously, the sense of threat had begun to
contract the geography of his life. He no longer felt safe driving or riding in cars; the enclosed
space, the sense of exposure on the roads, the unpredictability of other vehicles—all of it felt
like a trap waiting to spring. He had stopped taking the bus to the office. Within a year, he had
effectively confined himself to his home and the homes of a handful of trusted friends, places where
he could control the environment and the exits. His work as a refugee aid worker, the career that
had given his life meaning for thirty-seven years, had become impossible. He had taken early
retirement at sixty-nine, telling his supervisor that his health was failing, which was not entirely
untrue.

The contraction of his world and the hypervigilance that accompanied it only deepened his
rumination. Trapped in his safe zones, with hours stretching before him, Thom found himself
returning obsessively to the question of what he should have done differently. He had begun avoiding
any conversation with Mei that might lead to disagreement or conflict, fearing that confrontation
would destabilize him further, that any emotional turbulence might trigger the memories more
intensely. She had gently suggested that he see a therapist, and he had refused for two years,
convinced that talking about it would only make the memories feel more real, more undeniable. But
last month, after a particularly bad night in which the auditory flashbacks had been so vivid that
he had called out in his sleep, Mei had insisted. Now, at seventy-two, Thom sits in a therapist's
office for the first time, exhausted by the weight of memories that feel simultaneously impossible
and utterly consuming, desperate to understand whether he can ever forgive himself for something he
cannot quite believe he survived.
