# Clinical Vignette: Robert

Robert was sixty-eight when he came to the intake appointment, his weathered face lined in a way
that suggested decades of listening to other people's troubles. He had spent forty-two years as a
school counselor, the kind of man who remembered names and asked follow-up questions, who made space
for adolescents to talk about their parents' divorce or their anxiety about college. But three years
ago, in the parking lot behind the grocery store on a Thursday evening in October, a man with a
bottle of whiskey and a rage Robert never understood had beaten him unconscious. He woke up in the
hospital with a fractured orbital bone, a concussion, and the smell of antiseptic burning in his
nostrils.

The first months after the assault were strange—a kind of numb relief that he had survived, that his
wife Margaret had found him quickly enough. But as autumn turned to winter, something shifted.
Robert began to notice that certain smells would stop him cold: the sharp medicinal tang of hand
sanitizer in the school hallway, the faint reek of alcohol on a colleague's breath at a faculty
meeting, the sterile chemical bite of the cleaning supplies the janitor used in his office. Each
time, it was as though the three years fell away. The moment he encountered that smell—whether it
was the hospital antiseptic or the alcohol that had been on his attacker's clothes—a fragment of the
assault would flash through his mind with terrifying clarity. He would see, just for an instant, the
man's face looming, or feel the blow, or hear the crack of impact. The memory came not as a coherent
narrative but as discrete, vivid snapshots, each one burning with the same raw terror he had felt
lying on that pavement. The smell seemed to unlock these fragments, and they appeared without
warning, then disappeared just as suddenly, leaving him shaken and disoriented.

To manage the intrusions, Robert had developed a strategy: when he felt the memory fragments
beginning to surface, he would deliberately try to leave his body, to blank himself out, to become
as absent as possible. It was a skill he had cultivated over months—a kind of dissociation he could
summon at will, a way of stepping outside himself before the terror could fully take hold. But the
cost of this escape was isolation. As the fragments became more frequent and unpredictable, Robert
began to avoid people altogether, or at least to keep them at a careful distance. He stopped staying
late after school to chat with colleagues. He declined Margaret's invitations to visit their
daughter's family. More painfully, he withdrew from his wife's touch, from the emotional closeness
that had sustained their marriage for forty-five years. When Margaret tried to hold his hand or sit
close to him in the evening, Robert felt panic rising—a sense that intimacy would make him
vulnerable, that being truly present with another person would leave him defenseless against the
fragments. So he would distance himself, would find reasons to be in another room, would dissociate
preemptively to protect himself from the overwhelm of being seen.

Yet the withdrawal itself became a trigger. The isolation meant he was spending more time alone with
his thoughts, more time in the quiet of their house where a stray smell—Margaret's hand cream, the
cleaning products under the sink—could ambush him without warning. And the emotional distance
between him and his wife, which had grown so vast, made him feel utterly alone with the terror. The
avoidance that had once felt protective now felt like a prison, and the very strategies he used to
escape the fragments seemed to be strengthening them, making them more frequent and more vivid.
Three months ago, Margaret had finally insisted he see someone. Robert came to the appointment
because he could no longer bear the weight of it alone, because his wife's face had begun to show a
sadness that broke something in him, and because he was terrified that if he did not find a way to
reclaim his life, he would lose the one person who had always believed in him.
