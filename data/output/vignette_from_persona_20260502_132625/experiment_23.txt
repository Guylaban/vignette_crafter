lacerations, but what remained with her was not a narrative arc. It was fragments: the windshield
exploding into her peripheral vision, the sickening tilt of the car, someone screaming—she could not
later say if it was her. When she tried to reconstruct the sequence for the police report, the
moments refused to order themselves. What came first, the impact or the spinning? Had she braced
herself, or had she been thrown forward? Even now, months later, she could not tell a clear story of
what happened, even to herself. The memory did not live in her mind as a sequence but as shards of
sensation that erupted without warning—a flash of chrome, the smell of hot rubber, the pressure of
the seat belt—appearing fully formed and visceral whenever some detail in her surroundings triggered
them. A car alarm in the parking lot at the hospital where she worked sent her entire body into a
state of recognition, as though the accident were happening again in real time.

Aroha had spent thirty years as a psychiatrist, trained to understand the architecture of trauma,
yet she found herself unable to apply that knowledge to her own mind. The accident had revealed
something she had come to believe was immutable: she was permanently changed for the worse. The
woman who had driven to that intersection—competent, measured, someone others relied upon—no longer
existed. In her place was someone who could not trust her own judgment. She had been hit by a truck
she never saw coming; what else might she miss? What other dangers was she failing to perceive? The
world revealed itself as fundamentally dangerous, and her own capacity to navigate it safely had
been shattered. This conviction—that she was damaged in ways that could not be repaired—seemed to
generate a cascading certainty: every unexpected noise, every car that moved too quickly through an
intersection, became evidence of threat. The more she believed herself broken and the world hostile,
the more she scanned for danger. Her eyes tracked vehicles with an intensity that exhausted her. She
avoided driving on main roads, then on highways, then on anything but the quietest residential
streets. Eventually she stopped driving altogether.

The fragmented quality of her memory seemed to feed this cycle. Because she could not construct a
coherent narrative of the accident, she could not contain it. It lived in her body as raw sensation,
triggered without warning by the most mundane stimuli—a car backfiring, the sight of a delivery
truck, even certain angles of light through a windshield—and each time it was triggered, the memory
felt fresh and uncontrollable, reinforcing her sense that the world was unsafe and she was powerless
within it. Her inability to order what had happened meant she could not reassure herself that the
accident was over, that she had survived, that she was safe now. The memory's formlessness kept the
threat alive.

By autumn, Aroha had developed rigid rituals to manage the terror that seemed to live just beneath
her skin. She could not go to sleep without checking every lock twice, and she could not lie in the
dark—the darkness itself became a threat, a space where she could not see what was coming. She would
lie awake for hours, her heart racing, waiting for dawn. When exhaustion finally claimed her, she
would jolt awake in the small hours, her body drenched in sweat, and she would rise to walk through
her house, checking that her back was never exposed to an open doorway. At work, she repositioned
her chair so that she sat with her back against the wall, able to see the entire office. Her
colleagues noticed but said nothing. She tried to suppress the fear, to control it through sheer
force of will—she was a psychiatrist, after all, and she understood the pathology—but the effort of
emotional containment only seemed to exhaust her further, leaving her more vulnerable to the
intrusive thoughts that came unbidden: images of impact, of metal folding, of her own fragility. The
more she tried not to think about the accident, the more insistent those thoughts became. The more
she tried to control her fear, the more it leaked out in other ways—she could not bear to be
touched, could not imagine intimacy, and had withdrawn from her ex-husband's attempts at friendship.
Sex had become unthinkable, a vulnerability she could not permit herself.

What brought Aroha to seek treatment was not the fear itself—she had learned to live with that—but
the recognition that the woman she had been was disappearing into the structures she had built to
survive. She could no longer see her patients with the presence they deserved. She was failing at

What brought Aroha to seek treatment was not the fear itself—she had learned to live with that—but
the recognition that the woman she had been was disappearing into the structures she had built to
survive. She could no longer see her patients with the presence they deserved. She was failing at
the very work that had defined her. She sat in the intake session with her back to the wall, her
hands folded tightly in her lap, and said quietly: "I need to know if I can come back from this. I
need to know if I'm still a doctor." Once she had concluded that she was fundamentally broken and
the world fundamentally hostile, almost anything could confirm it—a figure moving too quickly in her
periphery, the wrong angle of a parked vehicle, the sound of an engine accelerating blocks away. Her
conviction that her judgment could not be trusted meant that things she would once have registered
and dismissed now snagged and held her attention, amplifying into warnings; the belief came first,
