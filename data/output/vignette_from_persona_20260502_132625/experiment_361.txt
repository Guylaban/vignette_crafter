# Dr. Alejandro Ruiz

Dr. Alejandro Ruiz was fifty-eight when the soldiers came. He had been a young psychiatrist in
Santiago, working in a university clinic, when the regime tightened its grip. One afternoon in 1983,
men in olive uniforms arrived at his office without warning. They accused him of treating political
prisoners, of being complicit in subversion. What followed was three weeks in a detention center—a
basement room where he could never distinguish day from night, where voices shouted in rapid Spanish
he sometimes could not parse through the fog of fear and exhaustion, where he learned that the
people who were supposed to protect citizens, who wore the authority of the state, were capable of
anything. They beat him. They threatened worse. He was released suddenly, without explanation, and
eventually made his way to the United States.

Now, at sixty-two, Alejandro had built a respected career as a psychiatrist in Boston. He lived
alone in a modest apartment, had no wife or children, and had devoted himself to his work with quiet
intensity. But the past four years had become increasingly difficult. It began, he could not quite
pinpoint when, with the news. Whenever he turned on the television or scrolled through his phone and
saw reports of political violence, military interventions, or human rights abuses—anywhere in the
world—his body would seize. The images would trigger the full sensory flood: the smell of the
detention room, the taste of blood, the sound of boots on concrete. When the memory came back it
felt completely real across all his senses at once, as if he were there again, trapped in that
basement. He had come to believe that the institutions meant to protect people had fundamentally
failed him, that the men who tortured him faced no consequences and likely never would, and that
this failure was not an accident but a feature of how power actually worked. This belief poisoned
his sense of safety in the world, and it made every news cycle feel like fresh evidence of his
powerlessness.

Gradually, Alejandro began to avoid going out. He stopped attending his department's grand rounds.
He declined invitations to conferences. The world outside his apartment felt saturated with threat.
When he did venture out—to the grocery store, to the hospital where he worked—he found himself
rehearsing conversations obsessively beforehand. If he had to speak with a colleague, he would
script it in his mind, imagining all the ways the interaction might go wrong, all the ways he might
be misunderstood or judged. At work, the sight of security guards in uniform would sometimes trigger
the memory without warning, something in their bearing or the cut of their clothes sending him
instantly back to that room. He would excuse himself to his office, close the door, and try to
redirect his thoughts elsewhere, forcing himself to think of something—anything—else. But the effort
was exhausting, and it never quite worked. The more he avoided, the more isolated he became. His
colleagues began to notice his withdrawal. He stopped accepting lunch invitations. The warmth he had
once felt toward the Latin American community in Boston—the cultural events, the language, the
music—had faded. He had lost his connection to that world, or it had lost him; he was no longer sure
which. The thought that those who had harmed him remained unpunished, that they had likely lived
ordinary lives while he lived in this prison of avoidance and fear, would surface at odd moments and
harden into a cold rage that fed his conviction that the world was fundamentally unjust.

The memory itself was fragmented in a way that tormented him. Some moments were crystalline: the
face of one interrogator, the exact temperature of the water they used, a particular phrase they
repeated. Other stretches were entirely blank—hours or days he could not reconstruct. This
fragmentation made the memories feel less like something he could process and more like something
that could ambush him at any moment. And when those intrusions came, when something
innocent—incomprehensible chatter in a crowded room, a news alert on his phone—pulled him back into
the sensory reality of that time, he would retreat further into avoidance and isolation. His
negative appraisals had become self-fulfilling: his belief that institutions had failed him made him
distrust the very psychiatric community that might have helped; his conviction that he had lost his
community made him withdraw further from the people who might have provided support. He had come to
see his own survival as a kind of accident, a reprieve that could be revoked at any moment.

It was his department chair who finally insisted he seek help. She had noticed his deterioration
and, in a direct conversation that frightened him because it felt like confrontation, told him that
his patients deserved a doctor who was present. The words stung because they were true. Alejandro
had begun to recognize that the strategies that had kept him alive—hypervigilance, avoidance,
emotional distance—were now keeping him from living at all. He sat in the waiting room of a trauma-
specialized therapist, hands trembling slightly, wondering if talking about what had happened could
possibly undo the certainty that had calcified inside him: that the world was unsafe, that
institutions could not be trusted, and that some wounds never truly healed.
