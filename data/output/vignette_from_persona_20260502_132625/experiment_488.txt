# Layla's Story

Layla was thirty-eight when she came to the clinic, referred by her family doctor after mentioning
during a routine visit that she hadn't slept properly in fourteen months. Her hands trembled
slightly as she filled out the intake forms, and she kept glancing toward the waiting room door—a
habit, she would later explain, that never quite left her.

The assault had happened on a Tuesday evening in May of the previous year. Layla had been working
late at the medical office building where she cleaned, moving through the empty corridors with her
cart of supplies as she did four nights a week. A man had cornered her near the stairwell on the
third floor, forced her into a supply closet, and held her there for what felt like hours but was
probably thirty minutes. He had ordered her to hand over her phone, her keys, her purse—had shouted
at her to stay still, to not scream, to do exactly what he said or he would hurt her worse. The
physical assault was mercifully brief, but the humiliation, the helplessness, the absolute erasure
of her will had left marks that her body still carried. She remembered his voice, the cold metal of
the door handle against her spine, the smell of industrial cleaner mixing with her own terror. Other
parts of that night had simply vanished from her mind—she could not recall how she had gotten out of
the closet, whether she had called the police herself or if someone had found her, or what the first
officer to arrive had looked like.

In the months that followed, Layla had tried to return to work. But the building itself had become a
landscape of dread. The third-floor stairwell was impossible; even passing the supply closet on the
ground floor sent her heart racing. More troubling still were the moments when fragments of the
memory would surface unbidden—a particular angle of light in a hallway, the sound of a door
slamming—and with them came a phantom pain in her lower back and shoulders, the same places where he
had gripped her. These intrusions were unpredictable. A moment would feel safe, and then some
sensory detail would trigger a cascade: the memory would return in vivid, fractured pieces, certain
moments crystalline and others entirely absent, and her body would respond as though the assault
were happening again. She had come to understand that these memories were not simple recollections;
they were violent interruptions that left her gasping and confused about what was real in the
present moment.

What frightened Layla most was her complete loss of autonomy in that closet—the fact that she had
been forced to obey, to surrender every choice. Now, even small moments of coercion sent her into a
panic. When her husband asked her to do something a certain way, when her supervisor at the new
cleaning company she had reluctantly switched to assigned her a task without asking her preference,
when anyone—even her teenage daughter—raised their voice in her presence, Layla felt the old terror
bloom in her chest. These triggers seemed to reach backward into her fragmented memories, pulling
them forward, making the past feel present. Each time she heard shouting—a neighbor's argument
through a wall, a raised voice on television—her body went rigid, and often within hours the
memories would surface again, incomplete and painful, as though the trigger had opened a door she
could not close.

The threat scanning had become constant. Layla found herself monitoring every sound in her home,
listening intently for the creak of a door, the rattle of a window, the footstep that might mean
someone was entering without permission. At work, she scanned the faces of clients, the shadows in
hallways, the exits from every room. This vigilance had exhausted her into a kind of fog; she could
not concentrate on conversations or tasks, her attention perpetually divided between the present
moment and an imagined danger. The scanning itself seemed to keep her trapped in a cycle: the more
she monitored for threats, the more her mind dredged up the memory of the assault, and each memory
fragment—especially the ones where she felt that loss of control—sent her scanning again, looking
for the next sign that she was not safe. Her husband had noticed that she flinched at his touch now,
that she had become rigid and distant. Her daughter had asked why Mom never laughed anymore.

It was her inability to return to work, finally, that had broken something in Layla. She had left
the new cleaning job after three weeks, unable to manage the hypervigilance and the intrusions.
Without income, without the structure that had once defined her days, she felt herself disappearing.
She came to the clinic because she was afraid that if she did not find help, she would lose her
marriage, her sense of herself, everything that the man in the closet had already taken once.
