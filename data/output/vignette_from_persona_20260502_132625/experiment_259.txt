# Clinical Vignette: Margaret

Margaret was seventy-six when the diagnosis came back—stage III pancreatic cancer, caught during a
routine colonoscopy that had been meant to be reassuring. The surgical oncologist had explained the
protocol with clinical precision: Whipple procedure, chemotherapy, close monitoring. Margaret, who
had spent forty-two years in operating theatres herself, understood exactly what the statistics
meant. She remembered the surgeon's face, the exact shade of the consultation room walls, the weight
of her ex-husband's hand on her shoulder as they left. But there were gaps. Entire stretches of the
conversation had simply vanished from her mind—she could not recall whether he had mentioned
radiation, or what the timeline was for recovery, or even what he had said about her prognosis. When
she tried to reconstruct it, the memory fragmented further, pieces appearing in the wrong sequence:
she would see his hands gesturing, then hear his voice, then feel the chair beneath her, but never
as a coherent whole. Some moments blazed with impossible clarity—the way the winter light had caught
the edge of his glasses—while others remained utterly blank, as though her mind had simply refused
to record them.

The surgery itself passed in a similar fog. She had been a surgeon long enough to know that meant
her brain was protecting her, compartmentalizing threat. But the fragmentation troubled her. In the
months after her discharge, as she sat alone in her Honolulu home, Margaret found herself
obsessively trying to fill in the blanks. She would lie awake reconstructing the operative report
from memory, trying to force clarity into the disconnected pieces, searching for some pattern that
would make sense of why this had happened to her—what she must have done wrong, what cosmic
imbalance she had created. The harder she grasped for the missing parts, the more elusive they
became, and the more she felt compelled to search. She began researching pancreatic cancer risk
factors obsessively, reframing her illness as a puzzle to solve rather than a threat to survive. She
forced herself to recite positive affirmations each morning—"I am strong, I will recover, my body is
healing"—as though sufficient willpower could rewrite both her diagnosis and the fragmented terror
she could not quite remember.

But the more Margaret tried to impose meaning on the chaos, the more it seemed to confirm what had
begun to crystallize in her mind: that she was no longer the person she had been. The surgeon who
had prided herself on steady hands and clear judgment now could not trust her own memory. The woman
who had been self-reliant, who had raised two children alone after her divorce, now felt
fundamentally altered, unrecognizable even to herself. She had lost faith—not just in her body, but
in the world's basic fairness, in the idea that a careful life protected you from catastrophe. She
began to avoid the hospital where she had trained, taking circuitous routes through the city rather
than pass its entrance. Ambulances made her heart race; the sight of a stethoscope in a shop window
triggered a cascade of dread. She cancelled her annual physical and avoided her primary care
doctor's calls, because any medical encounter now felt like confirmation that she was fragile, that
the cancer could return, that the person she had been—capable, in control—was truly gone.

By the time Margaret came to the clinic, six months into her survivorship, she was seventy-seven and
profoundly isolated. She had retired from her surgical practice, unable to face the operating
theatre. She had not seen her children in months, ashamed of what she had become. She told the
clinician that what frightened her most was not the cancer returning, but the realization that she
might never recover her sense of self—that the fragmented, desperate woman she had become might be
permanent, and that no amount of positive thinking or meaning-making could reassemble who she had
been before the diagnosis.
