# Clinical Vignette: Thoeun

Thoeun was sixty-eight when his partner of seventeen years struck him for the first time. It
happened on an ordinary Tuesday evening in their Arlington apartment, the kind of moment that should
have been unremarkable—Thoeun had come home late from his job as a nanny, tired from managing four
young children all day. His partner, whom he had trusted completely, had been drinking. An argument
about money escalated quickly into something Thoeun had never anticipated. The blow came from
behind, a heavy object—he would never be entirely certain what—and then his partner's fists,
methodical and sustained, while Thoeun found himself on the floor, his body curled defensively, his
arms raised but ineffective. He had frozen rather than fought back. The terror of it was not the
pain, though there was considerable pain; it was the absolute helplessness, the sense that he had
become a thing to be broken rather than a person. The violence lasted perhaps five minutes, though
time had collapsed entirely. When it stopped, Thoeun lay still, afraid that movement would provoke
more.

That was four years ago. In the years since, Thoeun had remained in the relationship—he had nowhere
else to go, no family in the country, and he had come to believe that the violence was somehow his
fault, that he had provoked it through his own inadequacy. The physical injuries healed, but
something deeper did not. When memories of that night surfaced—and they did, unbidden, often while
he was doing something ordinary like washing dishes or sitting with the children he cared for—the
terror returned with it, undiminished by time. His body would tense as if bracing for impact again,
his arms would draw inward, and he would feel himself back on that floor, helpless and small. The
memory itself felt strange to him, almost as though it belonged to someone else's life rather than
his own, something he had witnessed rather than endured. Yet when it came, the fear was entirely his
own.

Over these four years, Thoeun had developed careful strategies for keeping the terror at bay. He had
learned never to allow anger to surface—anger, he believed, was dangerous, something that would make
him a target. He kept it buried so thoroughly that he sometimes wondered if he still felt it at all.
He avoided being in the dark, keeping lights on throughout the apartment even when his partner was
sleeping, because darkness had become synonymous with vulnerability. He avoided anything that might
be stressful: difficult conversations with his employer, social gatherings, even the small conflicts
that arise naturally in any job. At work, with the children, he had become almost preternaturally
calm, almost absent in a way that some parents had begun to notice.

The hypervigilance had become his constant companion. Unexpected movements—a child darting around a
corner, his partner shifting in bed at night—would send a spike of pure alarm through his nervous
system, his body reacting as though danger were imminent. Even in moments when he knew logically
that he was safe, even sitting in the bright morning sunlight with the children laughing around him,
his shoulders remained tense, his breathing shallow. He could not remember what it felt like to
truly relax. The startle responses had become so frequent that one of the families he worked for had
gently suggested that perhaps he was overworked, that he seemed "on edge."

What frightened Thoeun most was that the more he tried to suppress his feelings and avoid anything
that might trigger the memories, the more the memories seemed to push through. When he succeeded in
keeping anger locked away, the recalled terror would arrive instead, his body remembering what his
mind tried to forget. And when the memory came, with all its sensory immediacy and the overwhelming
helplessness of it, he would find himself reaching for avoidance again—turning away from his
partner, making excuses to work late, staying in the brightly lit kitchen rather than going to bed.
The cycle had tightened over months until Thoeun felt trapped in a narrowing corridor, each escape
route closing behind him. It was only when his employer expressed concern that he might need to take
medical leave, that his hands had begun to shake too visibly while feeding the toddlers, that Thoeun
finally admitted to his primary care physician what had happened. At seventy-two, he had come to
understand that he could not manage this alone any longer. What he needed most was to believe that
he could survive without the constant vigilance, without the suppression, without the terror—but he
had no idea how that might be possible.
