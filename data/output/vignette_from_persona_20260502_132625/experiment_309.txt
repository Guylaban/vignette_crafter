# Marcus Lehmann

Marcus Lehmann, sixty-eight, had been a state senator for twenty-three years—a man accustomed to
control, to reading rooms, to knowing what came next. That certainty had evaporated on an ordinary
Tuesday in March, six months before his intake appointment, when a delivery truck ran a red light on
his way back from a constituent meeting in Springfield. He remembered the sound first: the horn,
thin and distant, then the violent physics of impact, the car folding around him like paper. He
remembered the smell of spilled fuel and, underneath it, something chemical and sharp. His left leg
had been pinned. The paramedics had taken forty minutes to cut him free. He'd been conscious for all
of it.

The acute injuries had healed. The orthopedic surgeon had cleared him for light activity by June.
But Marcus had not returned to his office, had not attended a single committee meeting, had not
driven anywhere except, twice, to medical appointments with his wife at the wheel. His colleagues
had stopped asking. The party had begun quietly positioning a successor.

What had begun to organize itself in Marcus's mind, in the weeks after the accident, was a different
kind of injury. The event had fractured into discrete, vivid moments—the horn, the lurch, the pinned
leg, the smell—each one a photograph he could not put down. When he tried to explain to his wife
what had happened that day, he would start, falter, lose the narrative thread entirely, unable to
reconstruct the sequence in words. The fragmentation felt like evidence of something deeper: that
the accident had happened not because of chance or the truck driver's negligence, but because of who
he was. A man who had spent two decades cutting corners, trading favors, prioritizing ambition over
principle. He had come to believe that the universe had finally collected its debt. The event had
exposed him—not just physically, but morally. He was certain that when people looked at him now,
they could see it written on his face: the man who deserved this.

This conviction had begun to reshape his daily life. Because Marcus believed himself fundamentally
flawed, he had become hypervigilant, unable to trust that anything was safe. He woke most nights at
three or four in the morning, his heart racing, compelled to check the locks on the doors and
windows, to verify that his wife was still breathing beside him. Unusual silence—the house settling,
a gap between traffic sounds—would trigger a surge of dread, a certainty that something catastrophic
was about to unfold. His concentration had fractured; he could not read a full paragraph without
losing the thread. He was always on guard, always watching for the next blow.

The triggers were everywhere. A whiff of alcohol on someone's breath at the grocery store would send
him spiraling back into the moment of impact, and within minutes he would find himself replaying the
accident obsessively, dwelling on what he had done wrong—the route he'd chosen, the timing, the fact
that he had not left the meeting five minutes earlier. Discomfort of any kind—the heat of the sun,
the pressure of a seatbelt, even the texture of a wool sweater—could fracture his sense of safety
and propel him backward into the fragmented memories, which would then crystallize into fresh self-
accusation: *I should have known better. I should have seen it coming.* The smell of cars, the sight
of vehicles in his peripheral vision, photographs of accidents in the news—all of these would lodge
in his mind and, hours later, emerge as rumination, as the conviction that he had brought this upon
himself through some essential moral bankruptcy.

His avoidance had become his routine. He no longer drove. He had stopped attending public events,
stopped going to restaurants where he might encounter constituents, stopped even sitting in the
passenger seat of his wife's car without gripping the door handle until his knuckles blanched. Each
time he avoided, the rumination would intensify—*I'm weak, I'm broken, I deserve this*—and the
hypervigilance would tighten. He would lie awake at night, his mind cycling through the moments of
the crash, his leg aching with phantom pain, his chest tight with the certainty that he was
fundamentally unsafe and fundamentally to blame. The more he tried to protect himself through
avoidance, the more convinced he became that the world was dangerous and that he was the kind of man
who invited disaster. His marriage had begun to strain under the weight of his withdrawal. His
career, for all practical purposes, was over.

It was his wife, finally, who had insisted he see someone. Marcus had come to his first session
believing that he was beyond help, that what had happened to him was not trauma but justice, and
that the only honest path forward was to accept his exile. What he hoped for, though he barely dared
articulate it, was the possibility that he might sleep through a single night without waking in
terror, or sit in a car without feeling the walls closing in. He was tired of being the man the
accident had revealed him to be.
