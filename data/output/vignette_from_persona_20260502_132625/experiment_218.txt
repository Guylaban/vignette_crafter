# Priya

Priya was thirty-four when she arrived in the camp in northern Myanmar, a relief worker with an NGO
focused on displaced families. She had been in urban planning for nearly a decade—designing water
systems, mapping resettlement zones, solving problems on paper and in meetings. The war zone felt
like a different species of problem, one that lived in her body rather than in spreadsheets. For six
months she moved between makeshift clinics and temporary shelters, documenting needs, coordinating
supplies, trying to impose order on chaos. On the morning of the attack, she was in a storage tent
with two colleagues when the shelling began. The sound came first—a roar that seemed to split the
air itself—and then the collapse of the tent frame, dust and fabric and the weight of something she
couldn't see. Her colleague Aung was beside her. She remembers his face turning toward hers in the
half-second before the beam came down. She was pulled out by medics. Aung was not. For weeks after,
Priya functioned. She completed her reports. She returned to the States. She moved into her
apartment with her partner Marcus and told him she was fine, just tired. But fine was a word that
stopped meaning anything very quickly.

Now, at thirty-six, Priya lives in a state of fractured presence. The memory of that morning has
taken on a quality that feels almost unreal to her—as though she is watching someone else's trauma
unfold rather than living her own. When she tries to describe what happened to Marcus or to the
therapist he eventually convinced her to see, the narrative collapses. She starts with the sound,
moves to the dust, reaches for Aung's face and then loses the thread entirely, her voice trailing
into silence. What she remembers most vividly is the rage. When the memory surfaces—unbidden,
usually at night—it brings with it a fury so consuming that she barely recognizes herself. She has
punched the wall twice in the past year. Last month, after a particularly vivid flashback, she held
her arm under hot water until the pain eclipsed everything else, a small, deliberate cruelty that
quieted the noise in her head for exactly seventeen minutes.

The triggers are everywhere, though she has become expert at avoiding them. She keeps no photographs
in the apartment, deleted her old files, refuses to look at images from that period even when
friends share them innocently online. But the natural world is harder to escape. Three weeks ago,
Marcus suggested a weekend hike in the Catskills. Priya felt the panic rise before they even left
the car—the green density of the forest, the way the light filtered through the canopy in that
particular slant, the smell of wet earth and growing things that transported her instantly to the
camp. She made an excuse and stayed home, telling Marcus she had work deadlines. She has made many
excuses. Her job as an urban planner increasingly involves site visits, walking neighborhoods,
standing in open spaces to assess infrastructure. She has begun delegating these tasks, citing
office work, staying late in climate-controlled conference rooms where nothing unexpected can
happen. Her supervisor has noticed. There are conversations coming about her reduced fieldwork, her
declining visibility in the department.

To manage the memory itself, Priya has developed a system of suppression that occupies most of her
waking hours. The moment the memory threatens to surface—a sound too close to that roar, a visual
that resembles dust or collapse—she pushes it down, forces her mind elsewhere. She keeps her
thoughts occupied constantly: podcasts during commutes, work projects that stretch into evenings, an
obsessive reorganization of her apartment that serves no purpose except to keep her hands and mind
moving. At night, sleep becomes a negotiation with her own nervous system. She needs to stay alert,
to monitor the space around her, to know where the exits are and whether Marcus is still breathing
beside her. She lies awake for hours, her body rigid, waiting for the next threat. When sleep
finally comes, it is fitful and thin. The flashbacks come anyway—sudden, total moments when she is
back in that tent, the sound and the collapse happening again in real time, her body responding as
though the danger is present, her heart racing, her breath caught.

The startle response has become part of her baseline. An unexpected movement—Marcus turning over in
bed, a colleague touching her shoulder to get her attention—sends a spike of adrenaline through her
system. She recoils, her body interpreting these small movements as threats, her mind scrambling to
locate the danger. Marcus has learned not to touch her without warning. He has learned to speak
before he enters a room. Their intimacy has narrowed to a careful choreography of announced
movements and muted contact. What frightens Priya most is that the avoidance and the suppression,
the constant vigilance and distraction, are not making her safer—they are making her smaller. The
memory remains, alien and furious, waiting in the spaces she cannot fill with noise or activity. She
called a trauma therapist last week. She is terrified of what will happen if she stops running.
