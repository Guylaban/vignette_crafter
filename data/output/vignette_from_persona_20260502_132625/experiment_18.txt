# Priya's Story

Priya Desai was thirty-two when she arrived in Aleppo as a volunteer medical coordinator for an
international NGO, eager to apply her background in counseling psychology to help displaced families
navigate trauma in the midst of active conflict. She had been there for six weeks when the hospital
where she worked was hit. The blast came at 3:47 p.m. on a Tuesday—she would remember the exact time
from the wall clock that stopped mid-explosion. She had been in the administrative office when the
first shell struck the pediatric ward. The sound was not loud so much as total, a compression of air
that knocked her sideways into a filing cabinet. When she emerged into the hallway, the smoke was
thick enough to taste. She found Dr. Khalil pinned under a beam, his left leg at an angle that made
her stomach turn. Around him lay two nurses she had known by name and a child of perhaps seven, her
small body already still. Priya had knelt beside Dr. Khalil, her hands shaking so badly she could
barely assess his injuries. She had been trained for field medicine, had done the mental
preparation, but her mind had fractured into a thousand simultaneous thoughts: *I need to move him,
I can't move him, if I move him he'll bleed out, if I don't move him the next shell will finish what
the first one started.* She had stayed with him, applying pressure to a wound in his abdomen while
the hospital collapsed in pieces around them, and when the second strike came—closer, the floor
buckling—she had made a choice. She had run. She had left him there and run toward the emergency
exit, and she had never learned whether he survived because she was evacuated that same night and
never returned.

Now, three years later, Priya lived in a quiet suburb of Portland with her husband Arun, working as
a school counselor at a middle school where the most pressing crises were friendship conflicts and
test anxiety. On the surface, her life had resumed its shape. But inside, something had come loose
and would not reset. What frightened her most was the certainty that she had revealed herself as a
coward, and that this cowardice ran through her like a fault line. She was convinced she was weak,
fundamentally incapable of the kind of moral courage she had imagined she possessed. The woman who
had wanted to help people in their darkest moments had abandoned a dying man, and Priya had come to
believe that she was no longer capable of being that person—the competent, steady counselor who
could hold space for others' suffering. How could she, when she had failed at the most basic human
obligation: to protect those in her care?

This belief had begun to reshape her world in ways she did not fully acknowledge. She stopped
watching the news, turning away from her phone whenever reports of conflict surfaced, her jaw
clenching at the sight of headlines about Syria or Gaza or Ukraine. Public transport became
impossible—the thought of being trapped in a bus or train, unable to escape if something went wrong,
made her chest tighten. She had taken to wearing loose, dark clothing, things that would not draw
attention, that would let her move through the world as invisibly as possible. These small acts of
avoidance felt like the only way to manage the shame, to reduce the surface area of her presence in
the world. But they created their own spiral. Each time she changed the channel before a news
segment, each time she asked Arun to drive her somewhere rather than take the bus, each time she
caught her reflection in dark, concealing fabric, the message reinforced itself: *I am someone who
cannot be trusted. I am someone who hides. I am weak.* The avoidance was supposed to protect her
from the evidence of her failure, but instead it became the evidence itself. She was no longer the
person who engaged with the world; she was the person who withdrew from it. And that withdrawal only
deepened her conviction that she had lost something essential, that the woman she had been before
Aleppo was gone.

At work, her colleagues had begun to notice her distance. She found herself referring students to
other counselors more often, telling herself she was overwhelmed with caseload, though the real
reason was that sitting in her office with a teenager's pain felt like a test she was certain to
fail. Her husband had stopped asking her to join him at community events. Last month, when he
suggested they visit his sister across town, Priya had invented a migraine rather than face the bus
ride. She had called in sick twice in the past week, and she knew it was unsustainable. Something
had to give. Last Tuesday, after another morning of avoiding a news alert about a school shooting,
she had finally admitted to Arun that she could not continue like this—that she needed help, though
she was terrified of what help would mean, of having to confront the person she had become and the
person she believed she could never be again.
