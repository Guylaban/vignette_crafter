# Clinical Vignette: Karim

Karim was fifty-two when he came to his first appointment, his posture careful and contained, the
kind of stillness that comes from years of controlling how much space you take up in a room. He is
an actor—or was, more accurately—and there is something performative about even his silence, a sense
that every gesture has been rehearsed into plausibility. When asked about his wife, he looked away
toward the window and said simply, "It was eight years ago. A car accident. She was in the back seat
with our daughter."

The details came slowly, in fragments that did not cohere into a linear whole. Karim had been
driving. He remembered the sound—a specific metallic shriek of impact, the kind that lives in the
body rather than the mind—but the sequence of what happened next remained scattered in his memory
like photographs dropped and never reassembled. His daughter had been screaming. His wife had been
trapped. When he tried to reconstruct the minutes after the collision, he found only disconnected
pieces: the smell of gasoline, a child's voice, his own hands shaking as he fumbled with the door.
He could not remember calling 911. He could not remember the paramedics' faces. What he *could*
remember, with a clarity that felt photographic and unbearable, was the moment he realized the
hospital had not called him back about his wife's surgery. The surgeon had promised to update him
every hour. The institutional silence that followed—the failure of the system meant to protect her,
the absence of accountability from the doctors whose negligence he was certain had sealed her
fate—had crystallized into a permanent conviction: the people and institutions that should have
saved her had let her down, and no one had ever faced consequences for it.

The guilt that accompanied these memories was suffocating. When Karim allowed himself to recall
those hours in the hospital waiting room, he was flooded with a shame so intense it bordered on
physical pain. He had been the driver. He had survived. And in the months after her death, he became
convinced that he had not done enough—had not fought hard enough with the doctors, had not demanded
answers with sufficient force, had not somehow prevented the series of failures that had killed her.
This guilt, he had come to believe, was permanent and deserved.

For the first few years after her death, Karim had functioned through sheer discipline. He continued
acting, took roles that kept him busy, filled his calendar with rehearsals and performances. But
over the past three years, his world had contracted dramatically. Any exposure to media depicting
accidents or loss would send him into a state of hypervigilance and dread. A news report about a
traffic fatality would trigger an immediate cascade: his concentration would shatter, he would
become convinced that he was not safe, that no assurance from anyone around him could actually
protect him. When these moments of threat awareness arrived, he would find himself unable to sit
alone in his apartment. He would call friends, manufacture reasons to be around others, agree to
social plans he did not want to attend simply to avoid the isolation that made his mind spiral. The
people-pleasing had become automatic—he would agree to anything anyone asked, would suppress his own
preferences entirely, because the alternative was being alone with his thoughts and the fragmented,
photographic memories that would assault him without warning.

Crowded spaces had become similarly intolerable. A shopping center or a public event would trigger
the same cascade: the sound of children crying would immediately transport him back to his
daughter's screams, his concentration would evaporate, and he would become convinced that something
terrible was about to happen. The crowds themselves felt threatening, as though danger could emerge
from any direction. So he avoided them, which meant avoiding most of the world. His acting work had
dried up—he could not commit to ensemble pieces, could not tolerate the close quarters of rehearsal
spaces filled with other actors. When he did attend auditions, he would agree to any role offered,
regardless of whether it suited him or his schedule, simply because saying no felt impossible, and
the prospect of facing the casting director's disappointment felt like a threat he could not
survive.

The maladaptive strategies had become self-perpetuating. By avoiding crowds and isolation both, by
people-pleasing his way through every interaction, Karim had created a life so constricted that it
functioned as its own trigger. Any deviation from his narrow routine would activate the threat
system, which would send him searching for memories of what had happened, which would activate the
guilt and the conviction that institutions had failed him and no one would ever be held accountable.
This conviction, in turn, would intensify his sense of threat—if the systems meant to protect people
could not be trusted, then he could trust nothing and no one, and the only way to survive was to
control every variable, which meant avoiding crowds, avoiding solitude, and agreeing to whatever
others demanded. He had become, he realized, a man living in a cage of his own construction, and the
bars had grown so familiar that he no longer questioned whether they were necessary.

What finally brought him to seek help was a simple thing: a casting director had asked him to
audition for a role, and for the first time in years, Karim had said no. The word had emerged before
he could stop it, and in the moment of refusal, he had felt something shift. He had realized that he
had no idea who he was anymore outside of his avoidance, his guilt, and his certainty that the world
could not be trusted. He sat in the waiting room now, eight years after his wife's death, still
unable to forgive himself for not doing more, still convinced that the institutions that had failed
her would fail him again, and desperately afraid that if he did not find a way to break the cycle,
he would spend the rest of his life alone even in a room full of people.
