# Vinh's Story

Vinh Tran was thirty-five and had spent the last eight years trying to forget something he could not
quite remember in sequence. He worked as a senior accountant at a mid-sized firm in Portland, a job
that demanded precision and order—the kind of work that had once felt like a refuge, a place where
numbers stayed still and made sense. His colleagues knew him as quiet and reliable, the man who
caught errors others missed. What they did not know was that Vinh had become an expert at
compartmentalization not because he was naturally organized, but because the alternative—letting his
mind wander—meant risking the sudden arrival of something he could not control.

Fourteen years ago, in a detention facility outside Ho Chi Minh City, Vinh had been held for six
weeks. He had been a journalist then, younger and more idealistic, investigating corruption in local
government. The arrest had come at dawn. What followed was a blur of days and nights that his mind
had never quite stitched back together. There were hands, voices speaking rapid Vietnamese, the
sound of a door closing. There was pain, but the sequence of it—what happened first, what came
after, which interrogation was which—remained fractured in his memory, as though the events had been
shuffled and scattered. He had been released through a combination of international pressure and his
family's intervention, but the experience had left something broken inside him that no amount of
time seemed to repair.

For years, Vinh had managed by staying still. He moved back to the United States, finished his
accounting degree, married a woman named Lisa whom he loved but could never quite let close enough.
The marriage had lasted seven years before Lisa finally admitted that she felt like she was living
with a ghost—present but untouchable, always slightly elsewhere. The divorce had been amicable but
final. Now he lived alone in a small apartment, and the numbness that had once protected him had
begun to feel like a different kind of prison.

The triggers came without warning, and each one sent him spiraling into a particular kind of dread.
When an intrusive memory surfaced—a flash of hands, the smell of concrete, a voice—Vinh found
himself gripped by a conviction that he had no way to protect himself from harm. He had come to
believe that the world contained people who would hurt you, and there was no reliable way to know
who they were until it was too late. This fear was not abstract; it lived in his body. When a
stranger approached him at the grocery store asking for directions, Vinh's chest would tighten. When
his manager asked him to attend a networking event, his mind would race with calculations of risk.
The thought of talking about the future—his own future, his colleagues' plans, anything that
required him to imagine himself moving forward—triggered the same suffocating sense of threat. What
was the point of planning when you could be unmade so completely, so suddenly, by someone you had no
way of predicting?

This fear had begun to reshape how he understood himself. Vinh was certain now that he had been
permanently changed for the worse, that whatever resilience or capacity for connection he might once
have possessed had been taken from him in that facility. When he caught himself thinking this way,
he would sometimes wonder if another person—someone braver, someone less broken—would never have
been arrested in the first place, would have seen the danger coming and avoided it. These thoughts
felt less like self-blame and more like simple fact.

The memories themselves were the strangest part. They did not feel like his own experiences. When
the film played—and it did play, over and over, a sequence that never quite stayed in order—Vinh
watched it as though it were happening to someone else. A man's hands. A room. Pain. But whose
hands? Which room? The order kept shifting. Sometimes he would try to reconstruct the timeline, to
impose the kind of logical sequence he brought to balance sheets and audit trails, but his mind
would not cooperate. The memory resisted order, and in that resistance, it felt alien, disconnected
from him, as though his own mind had rejected what had happened as too foreign to claim.

He had taken three weeks of vacation over the past year, though he told no one that he spent most of
it in his apartment. His supervisor had begun to notice that Vinh declined social events more
frequently now, and that his usually steady performance had developed small cracks—missed deadlines,
uncharacteristic errors in spreadsheets. When his manager gently asked if everything was all right,
Vinh had said yes, of course. But the truth was that he was drowning, and the water was rising
faster than he could manage alone.
