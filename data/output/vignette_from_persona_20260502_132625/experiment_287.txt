# Clinical Vignette: Miguel

Miguel was sixty-four when he first came to the clinic, his hands still bearing the precise calluses
of five decades spent cutting and setting stones. He was a meticulous man—the kind who could spend
an hour aligning a diamond in its setting—but his precision had become a prison. He had come because
his ex-wife, during a custody dispute over their adult daughter, had suggested he needed help. What
he meant was that Miguel had become impossible to be around: rigid, watchful, quick to withdraw. He
had been divorced for eight years, and in that time, he had grown increasingly isolated.

The trauma lived in a single afternoon when Miguel was nine years old. His mother had asked his
father a simple question about money, and his father had erupted. Not with words at first, but with
his fists—crashing through the kitchen, overturning a chair, his voice a roar that seemed to come
from outside Miguel's body rather than from a man. Miguel remembered it in flashes: his father's
face, purple with rage; the kitchen knife on the counter, suddenly terrifying; his mother's cry. He
remembered standing frozen in the doorway, his body refusing to move, his mind screaming at him to
do something—to stop it, to protect her—and his complete inability to act. His father had grabbed
his mother by the arm and thrown her against the stove. Then, as abruptly as it had begun, it was
over. His father had left. His mother had wept. And Miguel had learned that he was powerless.

For decades, Miguel had managed. He had built a career, married, fathered a child. But the
architecture of that management was fragile, held together by constant vigilance. He had come to
believe, in the marrow of his being, that he was weak—that the boy who couldn't save his mother was
still inside him, still frozen, still failing. This belief had metastasized over the years into a
conviction that he had failed in his most important role: not just as a son, but as a man, as a
husband, as someone capable of protecting anyone. When his daughter was born, he had been terrified.
When his marriage began to strain, he had felt it as confirmation of what he already knew about
himself.

The triggers were everywhere, though Miguel didn't always recognize them as such. Shouting—whether
in a movie, in the street, in a neighbor's yard—sent him into a state of hyperalertness that could
last for hours. Being alone with men, particularly men he didn't know well, made his chest tight. He
would find excuses to leave rooms, to position himself near exits, to ensure there were others
around. Confrontations of any kind, even mild disagreements at work or in shops, activated a cascade
of panic that felt disproportionate to the actual threat. A customer once raised his voice about a
repair Miguel had done, and Miguel had felt his hands shake so badly he'd had to excuse himself to
the back room for twenty minutes.

These moments triggered something else: a desperate need to do something, anything, to regain
control. What Miguel had learned to do was to suppress the thoughts entirely. He would catch himself
beginning to remember—the knife, his mother's face, his own paralysis—and he would push it away
violently, sometimes literally shaking his head as if to dislodge the image. He had come to believe
that if he let those thoughts stay, if he really sat with them, he would go crazy. So he monitored
himself constantly, watching for signs of breakdown, treating his own mind like a dangerous object
that needed to be contained. He avoided anything that might relax him, because relaxation meant his
vigilance would drop, and that felt catastrophic. He didn't watch television in the evenings anymore
because he couldn't control what might appear on screen. He didn't go to bars or restaurants where
he might encounter raised voices. He had stopped attending family gatherings at his church because
the noise, the closeness, the unpredictability of it all felt unbearable.

The suppression worked, in a way. It kept the worst of the memories at bay during the day. But it
also meant that whenever something did break through—and things always broke through—the memories
came not as a coherent narrative but as disconnected snapshots, each one crystalline and terrifying.
He would see the knife. Then, separately, his father's face. Then his mother falling. Each image was
vivid and detailed, but they didn't connect into a story he could understand or integrate. They just
appeared, fully formed, and vanished. This fragmentation made the memories feel more dangerous
somehow, less like something that had happened and more like something that was happening, again and
again, in the present.

The snapshots fed back into his threat monitoring. Miguel had come to notice potential weapons
everywhere—in the silverware drawer, in the toolkits of contractors, in the hands of men who
gestured too expansively when they spoke. He watched people closely, reading their faces for signs
of anger, their body language for the coiled tension that preceded violence. Even when friends or
his daughter told him he was safe, he couldn't believe them. He had been safe once, or so he'd
thought, and safety had been an illusion. Now, at sixty-four, he had learned that safety was always
contingent, always threatened. This constant scanning exhausted him, but stopping it felt like
stepping off a cliff.

The vivid, fragmented memories also reinforced his deepest beliefs about himself. When he recalled
the event—and despite his efforts, he did recall it, involuntarily and often—he felt a crushing
guilt. He should have done something. A real man would have done something. The fact that he hadn't
proven what he already knew: he was weak. And this weakness had consequences. He had lost his
connection to his community, to the church where his family had worshipped for generations. He
couldn't sit through a service without feeling watched, without the noise triggering his
hypervigilance. His daughter had stopped inviting him to family events because she was tired of his
withdrawal, his sudden departures, his inability to simply be present. He was alone now in a way
that felt less like a choice and more like a verdict.

What finally brought Miguel to seek help was his daughter asking him, directly, if he was afraid of
her. She had raised her voice in frustration during a phone call, and he had gone silent, and she
had said, "Dad, are you okay? Are you afraid of me?" The question had broken something open. He was
afraid. He was afraid of almost everything. And he had built a life so small, so carefully
controlled, that there was barely room for anyone else in it. He came to the clinic hoping that
someone could help him understand why the boy who couldn't save his mother was still running his
life, and whether it was possible to stop running at all.
