# Clinical Vignette: Mariana

Mariana was twenty-four when she met Diego at a gallery opening in Brooklyn. He was charming in the
way that felt deliberate—attentive to what she said, interested in her animation work, the kind of
presence that made her feel seen in a way she hadn't before. She had grown up in Queens with her
mother and grandmother, both of whom worked long hours; she was used to making herself small,
competent, undemanding. Diego seemed to reverse that. For six months, things were good. Then,
gradually, they weren't.

It started with criticism disguised as care. Her clothes were too revealing. Her late nights at the
studio were disrespectful. When she disagreed with him about something small—a restaurant choice, a
friend he disliked—he would withdraw, silent and cold, until she apologized. She found herself
apologizing for things she hadn't done, just to restore the warmth. By the time the physical
violence began, Mariana had already reorganized her internal world to accommodate him. The first
time he grabbed her wrist hard enough to leave marks, she told herself she had provoked it by being
difficult. She had said something sharp. She deserved it.

The night that ended the relationship, Diego was drinking. Mariana had mentioned that a male
colleague at the animation studio had complimented her storyboards. Diego's face changed. He accused
her of flirting, of humiliating him, of being a whore. When she tried to leave the apartment, he
blocked the door. She pushed past him. He grabbed her by the hair and threw her against the kitchen
counter. Her ribs struck the edge. She fell to the floor, gasping, and he kicked her twice in the
abdomen before she managed to crawl toward the bedroom and lock herself in. She called 911 from
under the bed, whispering so he wouldn't hear. The police came. She went to the hospital. A CT scan
showed no internal bleeding, but the bruises bloomed across her torso like a map of her own
complicity.

Three years have passed. Mariana is now twenty-seven, and she has not been in another relationship.
What troubles her most is not the memory of the violence itself—though she does have nightmares, and
she startles at sudden movements—but the conviction that has calcified inside her about who she is.
She has come to believe that she is fundamentally changed, damaged in some essential way that no
amount of time will repair. The woman she was before Diego—the one who had ambitions, who trusted
her own judgment, who could sit in a room without cataloging exits—that person is gone. In her place
is someone cautious, suspicious of her own perceptions, prone to interpreting neutral comments as
criticism. She wonders if this is permanent, if the damage he did rewired something in her that
cannot be undone.

What makes this conviction most corrosive is that Mariana blames herself for the damage. She had
ignored red flags. She had stayed too long. She had, in her own mind, created the conditions for his
rage by being difficult, by not being careful enough with his feelings, by existing in ways he found
threatening. When she thinks about that night in the kitchen, she doesn't think first about his
hands or his weight or the way her head snapped back. She thinks about the moment she mentioned her
colleague's compliment, about how she could have kept that small thing to herself, and how
everything that followed was the inevitable consequence of her carelessness. The shame of this—the
shame of having acted in ways that betrayed the person she thought she was, the person who would
never stay with someone who hurt her, the person who would have left at the first sign of control—is
sometimes worse than the fear.

She works alone in her apartment now, animation software glowing in the dark, and she has stopped
going to studio gatherings. She tells people she is focused on freelance projects. What she means is
that she cannot bear to be around others, cannot trust her own judgment about what they think of
her, cannot imagine that anyone would want her as she is now—broken, complicit, permanently altered
by her own failures. She came to therapy because her mother, worried and direct, finally insisted.
Mariana is afraid that talking about what happened will only confirm what she already knows: that
she deserves what she got, and that the person she has become is exactly who she was always meant to
be.
