# Carlos

Carlos had been a parole officer for twenty-three years when the call came in about a violation in
the North End district. He was fifty-four, meticulous in his work, known among his colleagues for
his fairness and his refusal to cut corners. He had built a reputation on the principle that men
deserved a second chance if they showed they were serious about change. On that October afternoon in
2019, he was following up on a parolee who had missed three consecutive check-ins. The man lived in
a basement apartment on Ashland Avenue. Carlos knocked, identified himself, and waited. When the
door opened a crack, he saw the chain still fastened, a pair of eyes in shadow. He explained that he
needed to conduct a welfare check. The man said he was fine, asked him to come back later. Carlos,
professional and patient, explained that this was routine, that it would only take a few minutes. He
heard footsteps retreating into the apartment. Then silence.

That silence stretched. Carlos waited, his hand resting on the frame. The door was still chained. He
called out once more, identifying himself again. No response. He was reaching for his radio to
request a supervisor when the door exploded inward—the chain snapped, the frame splintered—and three
officers in tactical gear poured through, shouting commands. Carlos had not called for backup. He
understood, in the fractured seconds that followed, that this was not his case anymore. He stepped
back, raising his hands to show he was one of them. But the officers had momentum and adrenaline.
One of them, a sergeant he did not recognize, drove him hard against the wall. Carlos felt his ribs
compress, his breath cut short. He heard himself say, "I'm parole—I'm parole," but his voice was
thin, lost in the shouting. The sergeant's forearm was across his throat. Carlos tried to turn his
head, tried to show his badge, but the pressure intensified. His vision narrowed. He remembered
thinking, with a clarity that felt almost abstract, that he might lose consciousness. Then, as
abruptly as it began, it stopped. The sergeant released him. The officers moved past him into the
apartment. Carlos stood against the wall, gasping, his hand at his neck, watching them work.

The aftermath was procedural and hollow. Internal Affairs reviewed the incident. The sergeant
claimed Carlos had not clearly identified himself. Carlos's own statement was taken, filed, and
eventually closed with a finding of "insufficient evidence of misconduct." No discipline. No
apology. The department moved on. But Carlos could not. What had happened to him—the betrayal of it,
the sheer injustice—had lodged itself in a place he could not reach or reason with. He had spent his
career inside the system, trusting it, serving it. The officers who had pinned him to the wall were
his colleagues. The institution that should have protected him had decided, in effect, that nothing
had happened worth correcting. He was certain now that the system he served was not what he had
believed it to be, that the men and women who wore the badge could turn on you without warning, and
that no one would answer for it.

In the months that followed, Carlos began to notice how the trauma had fractured his memory of the
event itself. He could recall the knock on the door, the chain, the silence—but the moment of impact
remained jagged and incomplete, as though his mind had shattered it into pieces he could not fit
back together. When he tried to reconstruct it, the sequence slipped away from him. He found himself
pushing the memory down before it could fully form, a protective reflex that had become automatic.
What remained was a sense of unreality, a feeling that the violence could not have been as severe as
his body insisted it had been. Surely he was exaggerating. Surely he had misunderstood what was
happening. But his neck still bore faint bruising weeks later, and his ribs ached when he breathed
deeply. The contradiction between what had happened and his mind's refusal to hold it whole left him
in a state of constant doubt.

That doubt fed into everything. When people misbehaved—a parolee lying during a check-in, a
colleague dismissing his concerns—Carlos felt a surge of anger and betrayal that seemed
disproportionate to the moment. His heart would race, and he would find himself gripping the edge of
his desk, certain that the world was fundamentally corrupt, that trust was a fool's game. The
injustice of what had been done to him, and the institutional silence that had followed, had become
the lens through which he saw all moral failure. Every small violation felt like proof of the larger
truth: that power was unaccountable, that the innocent could be crushed without consequence, that
the systems meant to protect people were rotten from within.

At home, his hypervigilance had become the texture of his days. Carlos sat in the living room with
his wife, María, and found his eyes moving constantly to the windows, the hallway, the space behind
the kitchen door. Unusual silence—the absence of traffic noise, of neighbors, of the ambient hum of
the city—made his stomach tighten. He was certain something dangerous was about to happen. His heart
would begin to race, and he would stand, moving to check the locks, to look outside. María would ask
him what was wrong, and he could not explain it in a way that made sense. He was jumpy, startled by
her touch, by the phone ringing, by the ordinary sounds of their house. The threat felt constant and
invisible, and the only way to manage it was to remain alert, to scan, to anticipate. His mind,
trying to protect him, had become his jailer.

What brought him finally to seek help was a moment of clarity that felt like a crack in the wall he
had built around himself. María had asked him, quietly, if he was still there with her, or if the
man she had married had been replaced by someone she no longer recognized. He could not answer. He
knew that the hypervigilance, the rage, the fragmented memory—all of it was destroying the life he
had worked so carefully to build. He was terrified that if he did not find a way to make sense of
what had happened to him, he would lose everything that mattered. And yet he was equally afraid that
examining the trauma directly would confirm what he most feared: that the world was as broken as he
now believed it to be, and that he had been betrayed by the very institution that had been supposed
to define him.
