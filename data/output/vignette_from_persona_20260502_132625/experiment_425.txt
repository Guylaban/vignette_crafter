# Clinical Vignette: Vinh

Vinh was sixty-nine years old when his grandson Minh fell ill. The boy had been complaining of a
sore throat on a Tuesday morning—nothing unusual, or so Vinh thought. He was watching Minh that day
while his daughter worked; they had fallen into an easy routine over the years, Vinh picking the boy
up from school, making him a snack, helping with homework at the kitchen table. By evening, Minh's
fever had spiked to 104 degrees. Within hours, he was struggling to breathe, his small body rigid
with the effort. The ambulance ride felt endless. In the emergency room, doctors moved with an
urgency that terrified Vinh more than anything he had witnessed in his sixty-eight years of life.
Meningitis. The word hung in the air like a curse. For three days, Minh hovered between life and
death in the pediatric ICU—tubes in his arms, monitors beeping in a rhythm that seemed to
synchronize with Vinh's own racing heart. When the boy finally stabilized, when the fever broke and
color returned to his small face, Vinh wept with a relief so profound it left him hollow.

But the relief did not last. In the months that followed, Vinh found himself unable to move past
those three days. The memory of Minh's fevered face, the sound of the ventilator, the smell of
antiseptic in that hospital room—these details returned with a vividness that made him question
whether they were truly in the past at all. When he closed his eyes, he was there again, watching
his grandson struggle for breath, certain that this time the doctors would not save him. The memory
felt more real to him than the present moment, more solid and urgent than anything happening around
him now. What frightened Vinh most was the person he had become in response to this terror. He no
longer recognized himself. The man who had once been patient, who had taken pride in his steadiness
with the children in his care, had become someone anxious and withdrawn, someone who flinched at
sudden sounds and could not sit still for more than a few minutes. He had come to believe that he
had been permanently broken by what he had witnessed, that some essential part of him had been
damaged beyond repair. And if he was no longer trustworthy—if he could not protect Minh, if the
world was as dangerous as that three-day ordeal had proven—then how could he trust anyone else? His
daughter, the doctors, even Minh himself seemed like potential sources of deception or harm.

These dark beliefs drove Vinh into patterns of behavior that only deepened his suffering. Convinced
that he was damaged, that he could not be the person Minh needed him to be, Vinh began to withdraw
from his caregiving role. He found excuses not to pick Minh up from school, citing fatigue or
errands. When his daughter gently suggested he see a therapist, he refused—he was certain that
talking about what happened would only make the memories more vivid, more real, and he could not
bear that. Instead, he filled every moment with distraction. He took on extra hours at the nanny
agency, staying busy with other families' children, keeping his mind occupied so that there was no
space for the hospital, for Minh's fever, for the suffocating certainty that catastrophe could
strike at any moment. When intrusive images of that hospital room threatened to surface, he would
push them away forcefully, trying hard not to think about them, as if the act of suppression itself
could undo what had happened.

Yet the avoidance created a cruel paradox. The more Vinh worked to keep the memories at bay, the
more they demanded his attention. The harder he tried not to think about those three days, the more
vivid and present they became—as though his resistance only gave them power. He could not tell
himself a coherent story of what had happened; the narrative felt fractured, the sequence of events
blurred into a single, timeless moment of dread. And because the memory remained so overwhelming, so
present, so impossibly real, his conviction that he was permanently damaged only strengthened. He
was caught in a loop: the intrusive, vivid memories confirmed his belief that he was broken; that
belief drove him to avoid seeking help, to suppress the thoughts, to stay constantly busy; and that
avoidance only made the memories more intrusive, more vivid, more present. His relationship with
Minh, once the center of his life, had become strained. The boy asked why Grandfather didn't visit
anymore. Vinh had no answer he could give.

It was his daughter who finally insisted. She found him one afternoon sitting in his apartment, not
quite asleep but not awake, the television playing to an empty room. She told him that Minh had
asked if Grandfather was angry with him. That question broke something open in Vinh. He came to the
clinic not because he believed he could change, but because he could no longer bear the weight of
what he had become, or the thought that he was losing his grandson to the very terror he could not
escape.
