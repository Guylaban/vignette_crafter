# Thea's Story

Thea was thirty-seven when she walked into the clinic, a woman whose careful composure seemed to
cost her something. She had been working as a refugee aid worker for twelve years—most of that time
in Southeast Asia, before returning to the States five years ago. She knew trauma intimately through
her clients' stories, which perhaps made her reluctant to name her own. But the night of the
incident, three years ago, had fractured something in her that she had not been able to repair.

She had been returning from a community outreach event in a neighborhood where many of her clients
lived. It was late, and she was walking to her car when two police officers approached, responding
to a report of someone matching a vague description. Within seconds, without warning, one of them
had shoved her against a wall. She was struck repeatedly—on her back, her ribs, her legs—while she
tried to explain who she was, what she was doing. The other officer held her arms. It lasted perhaps
ninety seconds, though time had fractured in her memory into something both endless and impossible
to sequence. When they realized their mistake, they left without apology. She had bruises for weeks.
She never filed a complaint; she had seen enough of the system to know what would happen. What
frightened her most was not the physical pain but the absolute powerlessness—the certainty that her
words, her identity, her presence meant nothing in that moment. The officers had seen only a threat,
and no explanation could have changed that.

In the months that followed, Thea's world contracted. Any encounter with authority—a police car
passing on the street, an officer at a checkpoint, even a security guard at the grocery store—sent
her into a state of hypervigilance that left her exhausted. When she saw cyclists in groups, her
chest would tighten; she had been cycling when she was first stopped. Crowded spaces became
unbearable; the press of bodies, the sense of being trapped, replayed the suffocation of being held
against the wall. These triggers were not abstract; they were woven into the fabric of her daily
life. When she spotted a police car while driving to work at the refugee services office, her hands
would shake on the steering wheel, and the memory would flood back—not as narrative but as pure
sensation, the impact of hands on her spine, the taste of concrete dust, the animal terror of being
unable to move. The memory brought back the same terror she had felt in that moment, undiminished by
time or reason.

What she had come to believe, in the long nights when sleep would not come, was that the violence
had exposed a fundamental truth: she was not safe, could not be safe, and the world was structured
to harm people like her. The injustice of it—that nothing had been done, that the officers faced no
consequence, that she was left to manage the aftermath alone—had calcified into something harder
than anger. She had begun to see herself as someone who had failed to protect herself, someone who
should have known better, someone who would not have gotten into this situation if she were smarter
or more careful. This sense of having lost herself, of no longer knowing who she was or what she was
capable of, had become a kind of identity of its own.

To manage the unbearable weight of these thoughts and the memories that accompanied them, Thea had
developed a system of escape. She would suppress the thoughts when they arose, pushing them down
with fierce concentration, trying not to think about the trauma, the injustice, the loss. But
suppression required constant vigilance, and when it failed—when a siren or a crowd forced the
memory back—the overwhelm was so complete that she would have to stop whatever she was doing,
paralyzed by the grief and terror and emotion that flooded through her. To avoid that cycle, she
began to withdraw from situations that might make her feel hopeful or happy. She stopped attending
her book club, declined invitations to her sister's birthday dinner, avoided the community events
where she used to volunteer. If she stayed numb, stayed small, stayed away from anything that might
require her to feel, then perhaps the memories would not ambush her. But the isolation deepened her
sense of injustice and identity loss—if she was no longer the person who showed up for others, who
was she?

Late one night, unable to sleep and spiraling through thoughts she could not suppress, Thea had
opened a gambling app on her phone. The rush of placing bets, the possibility of winning, the
distraction of the game—it had worked. For hours, she had felt something other than terror and
grief. It became a habit, then a compulsion. When the memory surfaced during the day, or when she
felt the irritability building in her chest, ready to erupt at her husband for some small thing, she
would find herself opening the app. The gambling provided temporary relief from the threat she felt
constantly present in her body—the hypervigilance, the sleep deprivation, the sense that danger was
always imminent. But the relief never lasted. When the app closed, the threat remained, sharper than
before, and the cycle would tighten again: the triggers would pull the memories forward, the
memories would reinforce her sense of injustice and lost identity, and those dark beliefs would
drive her back toward avoidance and distraction.

Her marriage had become collateral damage. Her husband, Miguel, had tried at first to understand, to
help her feel safe. But her irritability—sudden, volcanic—had worn him down. When he suggested she
talk to someone, she heard it as confirmation that she was broken, that she had failed to manage
this herself. She had taken a few risks recently that scared him: driving recklessly, staying out
late in unsafe neighborhoods, as though some part of her was testing whether she would survive, or
perhaps whether she deserved to. He had stopped asking where she was going.

It was Miguel, finally, who had insisted she call. He had found the gambling app on their credit
card statement and had sat down with her, not in anger but in a kind of desperate sadness, and said
he was losing her. Thea had come to the clinic because she could no longer deny that the system she
had built to survive the trauma was destroying everything else. She sat in the waiting room, hands
folded, and admitted to herself that she did not know how to stop.
