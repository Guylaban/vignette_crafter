# Priya's Story

Priya was twenty-two when a man with a knife stepped out of the darkness near her car in a parking
garage in late October. She had just finished a delivery shift, was tired, and had let her guard
down for the few seconds it took to unlock her door. He demanded her phone and wallet. She gave
them. He grabbed her wrist, twisted it hard enough that she heard the joint crackle, and told her if
she screamed he would come back and find her at home. Then he was gone. The whole thing lasted less
than two minutes. But in those seconds, Priya had felt something drain out of her—a sense that her
own will mattered, that she could protect herself, that the world was a place where her choices
meant anything at all.

The first weeks after were numb. She filed a police report, and the officer who took her
statement—his uniform, his clipboard, his clinical questions about whether she had been sexually
assaulted—made her feel small and powerless all over again. When she returned to work the following
Monday, the steering wheel under her hands felt like a foreign object. Every car that pulled
alongside hers on the road sent her pulse racing. Twice she pulled over and sat trembling in a
parking lot for twenty minutes, convinced that someone in the car behind her was watching. By the
third week, she had stopped showing up for shifts. Her manager texted her that they would have to
let her go if she couldn't commit. She did not respond.

At night, Priya would wake suddenly, her heart hammering, and conduct a silent inventory of her
apartment: door locked, windows closed, phone within reach. The fear was not always connected to a
clear thought. Sometimes it was just a surge of dread that yanked her awake at three in the morning,
and she would lie there in the dark, unable to settle back into sleep. When she did manage to doze,
fragments of the event would surface—not as a coherent narrative but as isolated snapshots: the
glint of the blade, the grip on her wrist, the man's face emerging from shadow. And sometimes, worse
than any image, was the feeling of his presence and the sound of his voice, low and certain,
threatening to come back—that emotional intensity washing over her with no image attached, just the
raw sensation of helplessness and terror.

By late November, Priya had begun to tell herself that the mugging had not really happened, or at
least not in the way she remembered it. She had convinced herself that she was overreacting, that he
had not actually twisted her wrist that hard, that the threat was probably just bluster. When her
mother suggested therapy, Priya dismissed it. Instead, she started using cannabis in the evenings,
then more often, then throughout the day. The drug created a thin buffer between her and the
spiraling thoughts. It made the memories feel distant and unreal, which was exactly what she wanted.
What frightened her most was the conviction that she had failed herself in that garage—that a
stronger person, a more aware person, would have fought back or run or done something other than
comply. She would never forgive herself for her passivity. This shame, this inaction guilt, drove
her deeper into avoidance. She could not bear to be around people, especially anyone who might ask
her why she was not working, not driving, not living the life she had been building. She felt set
apart, contaminated by what had happened, incapable of explaining to anyone why the simplest
tasks—getting into a car, being in a parking lot, seeing someone move too quickly—triggered a
cascade of panic.

The threat monitoring became relentless. Whenever she left her apartment, Priya would scan every
space for exits. In a coffee shop, she positioned herself facing the door. On the rare occasions she
rode in her roommate's car, she sat rigid in the passenger seat, mapping routes and imagining
scenarios. Her mind had become a machine for identifying danger. And the more she scanned, the more
suspicious everything seemed—the man in the hoodie outside the grocery store, the car that seemed to
linger in the parking lot, the officer she saw on the street corner. Each perceived threat sent her
scrambling for a way to escape, which in turn flooded her with memories of the mugging, the feeling
of being trapped and unable to move. The guilt would surge again: she should have been more careful,
should have been stronger, should have been different. And when guilt overwhelmed her, she would
reach for the cannabis, or convince herself once more that it had not really been that bad, or
simply stay home and avoid the world entirely.

Now, at twenty-two, Priya has not worked in three months. Her savings are depleted. Her mother has
begun to suspect that something is seriously wrong. What finally pushed Priya to call a therapist
was a moment last week when she realized she could no longer remember what her own strength felt
like. She is terrified that this is who she will be forever—trapped in a loop of fear and shame,
unable to drive, unable to work, unable to trust that anything she does will make a difference. But
underneath that terror is a desperate, fragile hope that someone might help her understand why she
cannot seem to move forward, and whether there is any way back to the person she was before that
night in the garage.
