# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was thirty-four when his ex-partner Derek threw him against the kitchen counter, the back of
his skull cracking against the tile edge. He remembered the burnt smell first—something on the stove
left unattended, a pan smoking—and then the pain, bright and immediate, and Derek's voice, low and
certain: *You're so stupid. You can't do anything right.* The violence had escalated over months,
but that night felt like a threshold. Marcus had left within the week, filed a police report that
went nowhere, and spent the next fifteen years rebuilding a life that felt, on the surface, stable.
He remarried. He built a plumbing business with a partner. He had a house in the suburbs outside
Boston, friends who knew him as reliable, competent—the kind of man who showed up on time and did
good work.

But the past two years had unraveled that stability in ways Marcus couldn't quite explain to his
wife, Jennifer. It started with small things. He began staying up until two or three in the morning,
telling himself he was catching up on paperwork or checking job schedules, when really he was
scrolling through his phone in the dark, unable to tolerate the thought of sleep. The moment his
head touched the pillow, images would surface—Derek's face, the kitchen, the smell—and his body
would go rigid. Staying awake felt safer, like he could monitor what was coming. Jennifer had
stopped asking him about it. She slept in the guest room now.

The triggers were everywhere, though Marcus had learned to name only some of them. When a client's
kitchen smelled of burnt food—that acrid, chemical scent—his chest would tighten and he'd find
himself making excuses to leave the job site, calling his assistant to finish the work.
Confrontations, even small ones, sent him spiraling. A disagreement with a supplier about a late
delivery, a contractor raising his voice about a scheduling conflict—these ordinary workplace
frictions would leave Marcus shaking for hours afterward. Worse were the moments when someone
expressed anger, even in measured tones. His business partner, David, had calmly told him last month
that a recent job had gone over budget, his voice steady and professional, and Marcus had felt
something crack open inside him. He'd excused himself, sat in his truck for twenty minutes, and
spent that night awake, his mind replaying the conversation, convinced that David was disgusted with
him, that he deserved it.

What frightened Marcus most was that he'd begun avoiding the very places and thoughts that might
ground him. He no longer went to the house on Maple Street where he and Derek had lived—a detour
that added twenty minutes to his commute, but he couldn't bear to drive past it. More insidiously,
he'd developed a rigid rule against thinking about that time at all. Whenever a memory surfaced,
he'd distract himself immediately: turn up the radio, call someone, pull out his phone. The problem
was that the distractions had to be stronger and more constant as time went on. Late-night scrolling
became essential. Avoiding the house became non-negotiable. And the avoidance of thinking about the
event—the deliberate pushing away of anything that might lead him back there—had become so consuming
that he found himself unable to concentrate on conversations, unable to be present with Jennifer,
unable to sit quietly without panic rising in his chest. The very strategies he'd developed to feel
safe were leaving him isolated and exhausted. He'd lost weight. He'd missed two weeks of work last
month with what he told people was the flu. His marriage was strained in ways he couldn't
articulate. When Jennifer had gently suggested he see someone, he'd resisted for weeks before
finally making the call. What he feared most now was that if he stopped running, stopped staying
awake, stopped avoiding—if he let himself think about Derek, about that kitchen, about what had been
done to him—he would shatter completely. But he was also terrified that if he didn't, the life he'd
built would slip away entirely.
