# Clinical Vignette: Robert

Robert was sixty-seven years old when he came to the clinic, a broad-shouldered man with the careful
posture of someone accustomed to bearing weight. He had spent thirty-two years as a military medic,
a career that had taught him to remain steady when others fell apart. His wife of forty years had
died three years prior, and in the intervening silence of their empty house, he had begun to
understand how much of his identity had been tethered to the work of keeping people alive. Now, with
that chapter closed and retirement stretching before him, he found himself unable to move through
the world without feeling like it might collapse beneath him.

The collision happened on a Tuesday afternoon in March, eighteen months before his first
appointment. Robert had been driving to a medical supply store when a pickup truck ran a red light
and struck his sedan broadside. The impact was violent and disorienting—the sound of metal
crumpling, the smell of burning rubber and spilled gasoline, the taste of blood from where he had
bitten his tongue. A man stumbled from the pickup truck reeking of alcohol, and in the chaos that
followed, Robert found himself doing what he had always done: assessing, triaging, moving to help.
But his hands would not stop shaking. By the time the ambulance arrived, he was uncertain whether he
was checking the other driver's pupils or his own, whether the ringing in his ears was real or a
sign that something fundamental had broken inside him.

The first weeks were physical. His ribs ached, his left shoulder throbbed, and he slept poorly. But
the body heals in predictable ways. What did not heal was the way his nervous system had
recalibrated itself to threat. Ordinary sounds—a car horn, a loud truck passing on the street, even
the sharp bang of a kitchen cabinet closing—would jolt him upright, his heart hammering as though
the collision were happening again in real time. The startle responses came unbidden and exhausting.
What frightened Robert most was the sense that he could no longer trust his own mind. He had come to
believe that the accident had exposed a fundamental weakness in him, that a truly strong person
would have recovered by now. The fact that he still jumped at sounds, that he still felt the phantom
lurch of impact when a car slowed suddenly near him, seemed to confirm what he had begun to suspect:
he was not the man he had thought himself to be.

Within two months, Robert stopped driving. The thought of being behind the wheel triggered a cascade
of physical sensations—his chest tightening, his palms sweating, a sharp clarity to his vision that
felt almost hallucinatory. He could not be a passenger either. The moment he sat in someone else's
car, he found himself monitoring the other driver's attention, tensing at every intersection, his
body braced for impact. He began to construct increasingly elaborate reasons to avoid situations
that would require him to be in a vehicle. He made excuses to his daughter, who had invited him to
his grandson's baseball games. He stopped attending his medical society meetings. The isolation was
a small price to pay for the relief of not having to face that overwhelming sense of vulnerability
and shame.

But isolation had its own cost. Alone in the house, Robert's mind became a place he could not
escape. When he tried to talk about what had happened—when his daughter asked him directly, her
concern evident in the careful way she phrased her questions—the narrative would fragment. He would
begin to describe the impact, the sound, the smell of the other driver's breath, and then something
would happen to the thread of his story. His voice would falter. The memory itself seemed to split
into pieces that would not cohere, as though it belonged to someone else entirely, something that
had happened to a stranger rather than to him. And beneath that alienation lay something worse: the
intrusive sounds. In quiet moments, he would hear the screech of tires, the crash of metal, the
man's slurred voice asking if anyone was hurt. These sounds would arrive unbidden, vivid and
immediate, and they would trigger another wave of physical reactivity—his heart racing, sweat on his
palms, that terrible sense of helplessness.

The more Robert struggled against these intrusions, the more they intensified. He began to monitor
his own thoughts obsessively, watching for signs that he was losing his grip on reality. When a
memory surfaced, he would try to suppress it, to push it down through sheer force of will. But the
effort of not thinking about something only tightened the loop. He would find himself hypervigilant
to triggers: the smell of alcohol on a stranger's breath at the grocery store would send him into a
state of alarm; the sight of a damaged car on the side of the road would trigger that cascade of
physical symptoms and intrusive memories all over again. He had begun to avoid places where he might
encounter these reminders—he stopped going to the grocery store at certain times, took longer routes
to appointments, and when he could not avoid a trigger, he would feel the familiar tightening in his
chest, the sense that the world had become fundamentally unsafe. The physical reactivity and the
startle responses would follow, and then the cycle would begin again: the intrusive memories, the
fragmented attempts to make sense of them, the deepening conviction that he had failed himself and
could not be trusted to do the right thing. His grandson had stopped asking him to come to games.
His daughter had begun to worry aloud about his isolation. And Robert, sitting in the silence of his
house, had come to understand that the collision had done more than injure his body. It had
shattered something essential about how he understood himself, and he had no idea how to put it back
together. That was what finally brought him to the clinic: not hope that he could recover, but a
desperate need to understand whether the man he saw in the mirror—weak, unreliable, broken—was who
he had always been, or whether something could be done to retrieve the person he used to be.
