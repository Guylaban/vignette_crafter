# Clinical Vignette: Thuan

Thuan was fifty-eight when the accident happened on a commercial roofing job in suburban Atlanta. He
had been working in residential and commercial roofing for nearly thirty years—meticulous work that
required precision and an almost meditative focus on the task at hand. That afternoon, he and a
younger colleague were applying a sealant to the flat roof of a three-story office building. The
product had been delivered in unmarked containers; the site supervisor assured them it was standard,
approved, nothing to worry about. Within minutes of opening the first container, Thuan felt a
burning sensation in his throat and sinuses. The smell was acrid, chemical, nothing like the
familiar odors he had encountered across three decades in the trade. His colleague noticed it too,
but by then both men were already inhaling the fumes. Thuan remembered the panic—the sudden
awareness that something was profoundly wrong, that the air itself had become hostile. He stumbled
toward the roof's edge, gasping, his vision blurring. Emergency services arrived within twenty
minutes. The substance turned out to be an industrial-grade solvent that had been improperly labeled
and stored; it contained compounds that caused acute chemical pneumonitis. Thuan spent four days in
the hospital, where doctors confirmed that his lungs had been exposed to toxic levels of volatile
organic compounds. He was discharged with a prescription for corticosteroids and an inhaler, told
that his respiratory function would likely recover fully, though it might take months.

Eight years have passed since that day. Thuan is now sixty-six, and he has not returned to roofing.
The physical recovery was complete—his pulmonary function tests normalized within a year—but
something else did not heal. He came to believe that he could no longer trust his own judgment about
safety, that he had failed to notice or prevent something catastrophic that nearly killed him. The
world, in his mind, had become saturated with invisible dangers. What frightened him most was the
thought that he might miss another warning sign, that the next threat could come from anywhere,
disguised as something ordinary. He had come to believe he had no future worth planning for—that his
body had been fundamentally compromised, that his capacity to work and contribute had been stolen
from him.

When darkness falls, Thuan's vigilance intensifies. He lies awake beside his wife, Mei, listening to
the house settle, unable to relax even in the safety of their bedroom. Waking in the dark triggers
an immediate surge of dread; his heart accelerates, and his mind floods with the sensory memory of
that afternoon—the burning in his throat, the chemical taste, the feeling of his lungs closing. The
memory plays like a film he cannot stop: the moment he opened the container, the smell hitting him,
the blur of panic, the ambulance. The images are vivid and intrusive, accompanied by the full
emotional intensity of the original terror, sometimes arriving without any visual component at all,
just a crushing wave of fear and suffocation. These nocturnal episodes have become so routine that
Thuan now dreads bedtime. He tries hard not to think about what happened, pushing the memories away
during the day, but at night his effort collapses. The suppression leaves him exhausted and
hypervigilant by morning.

The smell of antiseptic—in hospitals, in some cleaning products, even in the waiting rooms of
doctors' offices—transports him instantly back to those four hospital days and the chemical exposure
that preceded them. When Mei needed a routine colonoscopy last year, Thuan accompanied her to the
outpatient surgery center. The moment they entered the corridor and he caught the familiar sharp
scent of disinfectant, his breathing became shallow, his concentration fractured. He could not focus
on Mei's reassurances or the nurse's explanations. The threat felt immediate and inescapable. He sat
in the waiting area gripping the armrest, unable to relax, certain that the air itself was
contaminated. He left the building and waited in the car, avoiding the space entirely, unable to
assess whether it was truly safe. This pattern has repeated itself: he avoids hospitals, avoids
unfamiliar places where he cannot quickly identify exits or assess potential chemical hazards,
avoids anything that might expose him to similar smells.

His avoidance has reshaped his life in ways both obvious and subtle. He stopped asserting himself
with Mei, never disagreeing with her plans or expressing his own needs, believing that conflict
might destabilize the fragile equilibrium he has constructed. He dwells constantly on how he used to
be—the confident craftsman, the man who could read a roof and know exactly what it needed, the man
who trusted himself. That version of him feels irretrievable. His inability to relax, his difficulty
concentrating, his conviction that he cannot rely on himself—these thoughts reinforce his belief
that the world is fundamentally dangerous and that he is powerless within it. Because he believes
the world is dangerous and that he cannot trust his own judgment, he finds himself hypervigilant to
any potential threat, scanning for smells, for darkness, for unfamiliar situations. This vigilance
keeps him trapped in a state of perpetual threat detection, which in turn triggers the memories and
the sense of helplessness that feeds his conviction that he has no future.

Mei finally insisted he see someone after she found him one morning at 3 a.m. sitting on the back
porch in the dark, unable to return to bed, unable to explain why he was so afraid. She told him
that he was disappearing, that the man she married was being consumed by something he could not
name. It is this—the fear that he is becoming unrecognizable even to himself, that his life has
shrunk to the size of a prison he has built from his own terror—that has finally brought Thuan to
seek help.
