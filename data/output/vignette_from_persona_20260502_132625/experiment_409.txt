# Kofi's Silence

Kofi Mensah had built a career on precision—thirty-eight years as a journalist in Lagos and then in
Atlanta, where he'd settled with his wife Ama after their children had grown. He was a man who chose
his words carefully, who understood that language was both instrument and responsibility. But that
was before the afternoon in March when his son Daniel, visiting from New York, was struck by a car
while crossing Peachtree Street. Kofi had been waiting at the restaurant where they'd planned to
meet. The call came from a stranger's phone. When he arrived at the hospital, Daniel was already
gone.

In the first weeks after the funeral, Kofi moved through the house like a man underwater. Ama would
find him standing in the kitchen at three in the morning, or sitting in the dark of his study, the
computer screen blank. He returned to work after two weeks—the paper's editor had urged him to take
longer, but Kofi believed that structure would anchor him. It didn't. The office, which had once
felt like sanctuary, became a place where the ordinary rhythms of conversation could ambush him
without warning. A colleague would lean against his desk and say something casual about making plans
for the summer, or another reporter would mention that his father had recently passed. In those
moments, something in Kofi would fracture. His chest would tighten. He would feel the copper taste
of blood in his mouth—not his own blood, but Daniel's, the metallic sharpness he'd tasted when he'd
knelt beside his son's body in the hospital, when he'd tried to speak and found only that taste,
that terrible taste, flooding his mouth and throat.

The conversations themselves became unbearable. When colleagues talked about the future—about
retirement plans, about children graduating, about next year's assignments—Kofi would feel his mind
begin to splinter. The words would start to come, some response or acknowledgment, but then the
taste would return, and with it the memory would surge forward, not as a coherent narrative but as
fragments: Daniel's blue shirt, the sound of the ambulance, the way Ama's hand had felt in his when
the doctor spoke. Kofi would open his mouth and find that the words had scattered. He would know
what he wanted to say—he could see the shape of it in his mind—but the language itself had become
inaccessible, as though his throat had closed around it. He would stammer, lose the thread of his
own sentence, and have to excuse himself.

At home, the silence deepened. Ama would try to plan—a trip to visit their daughter in Chicago,
perhaps, or a small dinner with friends—and Kofi would feel the panic rise before she'd finished
speaking. She would describe the dates, the details, and he would taste it again, that coppery
flood, and the room would seem to tilt. He began to withdraw from these conversations, to leave the
room when Ama spoke about anything that extended beyond the present day. She stopped trying. Their
evenings became careful, circumscribed. They spoke about what they'd eaten, about the weather, about
nothing that required him to imagine forward into a future that felt like a trap.

What troubled Kofi most was the incomprehensibility that seemed to surround the memory itself. When
his therapist had first asked him to describe what happened, he'd tried. He'd opened his mouth and
found himself unable to construct a coherent account. The details would come in fragments, out of
order—he would be describing the hospital, then suddenly he was back on the street, then he was in
the car ride there, then the phone call. The words wouldn't hold. He knew what had happened with
absolute clarity, and yet when he tried to speak it aloud, the language seemed to dissolve. His wife
would ask him gently, in her way, if there was anything he needed to talk about, and he would begin,
but after a few sentences he would lose himself, unable to find the words to complete the picture,
unable to make her understand the weight of what he was carrying.

Now, at sixty-three, Kofi sits in his study most evenings, the blank screen before him. He has taken
a leave from the paper. He and Ama have not made plans beyond tomorrow. He came to therapy because
his wife insisted, because she was frightened by the silence, by the way he seemed to be
disappearing. What he wants most is to be able to speak again—to tell Daniel's story, to explain
what happened, to find the words that keep slipping away. What he fears is that the future itself
has become a trigger, and that he will spend whatever time remains unable to move forward, trapped
in fragments that his own voice cannot assemble.
