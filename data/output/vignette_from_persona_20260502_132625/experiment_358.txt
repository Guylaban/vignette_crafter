# Clinical Vignette: Robert Kovacs

Robert Kovacs was sixty-four when a delivery truck ran a red light and struck the driver's side of
his sedan at an intersection near downtown. The impact was sudden and catastrophic—the sound of
tearing metal, the violent lurch of his body against the restraint, the sensation of the vehicle
spinning. He remembered the windshield fragmenting into a web of cracks, the smell of scorched
rubber, and the terrible silence that followed before the sirens began. The other driver had been
texting. Robert walked away with a fractured collarbone and deep lacerations on his left arm, but
the physical injuries healed within months. What did not heal was the conviction that had
crystallized in those seconds: that he had failed to see what was coming, that someone more vigilant
would never have been in that intersection at that moment, that the world was fundamentally
unpredictable and hostile.

Three years later, Robert has come to believe he is permanently damaged. The conviction sits beneath
everything now—a baseline assumption about himself that colors how he interprets his own reactions.
When he hears a car horn or a door slam at his school, his body jolts as though the collision is
happening again. When he sees footage of a traffic accident on the news, the images seem to detonate
inside his mind, and he finds himself unable to look away even as his chest tightens. Shouting—a
raised voice in a hallway, a student yelling across the schoolyard—triggers an immediate startle
response and a cascade of intrusive imagery: the moment of impact replays unbidden, vivid and
immersive, as though he is back in that spinning vehicle. The memory does not feel like something
that happened; it feels like something happening now, a present-tense threat. Because he has come to
interpret his own hypervigilance and fear as evidence of permanent psychological damage, he has
begun to avoid the very situations that might challenge that belief. He forces himself to think only
positive thoughts about his capacity to manage work, but the effort itself—the constant vigilance
required to suppress the memories and the fear—exhausts him and reinforces his sense that he is
fundamentally broken.

Robert's avoidance of going to work has intensified over the past eighteen months. He arrives at
school most mornings, but he remains in his office with the door closed, delegating tasks to his
assistant principal and avoiding the hallways where unexpected noises or commotion might startle
him. His colleagues have noticed his withdrawal; two have asked if he was well. He has not told them
about the collision. Instead, he has begun to eat excessively in the evenings—large meals, second
helpings, comfort foods consumed while sitting alone in his kitchen—using the ritual to manage the
distress that surfaces when the day ends and the avoidance can no longer hold. The eating provides a
temporary reprieve, a physical sensation that displaces the intrusive memories, but afterward he
feels shame and a deeper conviction that he is losing control. This shame and sense of failure feed
back into his negative appraisals: he should be able to handle this; someone else would have
recovered by now; his inability to function normally is proof of permanent damage. The more he
avoids work, the more the threat feels real and present. When something at school takes longer than
expected—a meeting runs over, a decision gets delayed—Robert's mind immediately jumps to
catastrophe. Something bad is happening; something is about to go wrong. The uncertainty itself
becomes unbearable, and he retreats further into avoidance and into the maladaptive coping that
temporarily quiets the fear but ultimately deepens his conviction that the world is dangerous and
that he cannot trust his own mind.

Sleep has become nearly impossible. Robert lies awake for hours, his body unable to relax even when
he consciously reminds himself that he is safe at home, that there is no threat. The memories
intrude—the film playing over and over—and with them comes the racing heart, the sense that
something catastrophic is imminent. His wife, Maria, has begun sleeping in the guest room. He has
not told his physician about any of this. It was only when his school superintendent gently
suggested that Robert consider taking medical leave that he finally called a therapist. He is
terrified that seeking help will confirm what he already believes: that he is broken beyond repair.
But he is also desperate for the memories to stop, and for the relentless sense of danger to lift
long enough for him to feel like himself again.
