# Miriam's Story

Miriam was forty-eight when the bomb went off at the synagogue in Pittsburgh. She had been in her
office—a small, quiet room lined with books on Jewish law and theology—preparing remarks for a
Friday evening service. The explosion came without warning: a deafening crack that shattered the
windows and sent her sprawling across her desk. For a moment there was only ringing silence and then
screaming, the sound of running feet, the acrid smell of smoke. When she stumbled into the
sanctuary, she found blood on the pews and bodies being carried out. She had known some of them by
name. In the weeks that followed, as the names of the dead were released and the footage replayed
endlessly on every screen, Miriam found herself unable to reconcile the woman she had been—the rabbi
who had stood in that pulpit and spoken of God's protection—with the reality of what had happened.
She was alive. Others were not. And she could not understand why she had been spared, or what she
might have done differently had she been in the sanctuary instead of her office when the shots
began.

The guilt settled into her bones like a chronic illness. Miriam became convinced that she bore some
responsibility for the tragedy, that her inaction—her absence from the sanctuary at that precise
moment—was itself a kind of failure. She had come to believe that only through constant vigilance
could she prevent another catastrophe, that the world had revealed itself to be fundamentally unsafe
and that she alone had to remain alert to every possible threat. This certainty that she had to be
especially careful because you never knew what could happen next began to shape everything she did.
She found herself unable to trust reassurances from her husband David or from the security
consultants the synagogue had hired. Even when they told her the building was safe, that protocols
were in place, she could not believe them. The gap between what they said and what she knew to be
true—that violence could erupt anywhere, at any moment—felt unbridgeable.

To manage this unbearable sense of threat, Miriam began to avoid the very reminders that might
trigger her guilt and fear. When congregants approached her to discuss the tragedy or to share their
own trauma, she found reasons to leave the room. She stopped reading the news, but found herself
compulsively checking her phone anyway, unable to resist the pull of the headlines. She began to
scan her surroundings constantly, her eyes moving from door to window to the faces of people around
her, searching for signs of danger. At night, when the building was dark and quiet, this vigilance
intensified. She would lie awake next to David, her body tense, listening for sounds that might
signal a threat. Her husband had tried to comfort her, to hold her, but she found herself unable to
accept his touch. Sex felt impossible—an abandonment of her vigil, a moment of vulnerability she
could not afford. When David reached for her in the darkness, she would stiffen, and he would
eventually turn away. This withdrawal had created a distance between them that neither of them knew
how to bridge.

The avoidance and hypervigilance fed back into her conviction that the world was dangerous and that
she was responsible for protecting those around her. Each time she scanned the room and found no
immediate threat, she felt a brief relief—but also a deepening certainty that her vigilance was what
kept disaster at bay. The avoidance of reminders meant she never had to confront whether her guilt
was rational; instead, it remained fixed and absolute. And the very act of avoiding—the constant
checking, the refusal to engage, the withdrawal from her marriage—became evidence to her that she
was right to be afraid. Nothing good could happen to her anymore; the universe had made that clear.
She had been spared, and that survival felt less like a gift than like a debt she could never repay.

By the time Miriam came to her first therapy session, she had not slept more than two hours in a
night for months. Any unexpected sound—a car backfiring, a door slamming—sent her heart racing and
her eyes darting to the nearest exit. She had reduced her hours at the synagogue, citing
administrative work, but the truth was that she could not bear to be in the building, could not bear
to be watched or observed by congregants who seemed to expect her to be healed, to have moved
forward. She was terrified that another attack would come, and equally terrified that she would
survive it again. What she wanted most was to feel safe again, to touch her husband without
flinching, to sleep through a night. But she was not sure such things were possible for someone like
her—someone who had failed so profoundly, and who now carried the weight of prevention on her
shoulders alone.
