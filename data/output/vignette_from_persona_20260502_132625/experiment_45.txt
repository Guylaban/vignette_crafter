apartment where it happened—a fifteen-minute walk from his workplace—became a place he could not

and real. He had started checking the locks on his apartment door and windows obsessively, sometimes
five or six times before bed, his hands shaking as he tested each one. At night, he felt unsafe in a
way that daylight could not quite dispel; even with every lock secured, he would lie awake listening
to the building settle, every creak a potential footstep, every distant siren a warning.

When memories of that evening surfaced—and they did, unbidden and vivid—Miguel would hear David's
voice again, not as a distant recollection but as an immediate auditory presence in his mind, the
slurred words and the ragged breathing replaying with such clarity that he would have to grip the
edge of his desk to orient himself to the present. The memory itself was crystalline in a way that
felt almost photographic; he could see the exact angle of the afternoon light through the bedroom
window, the pattern of the bedspread beneath his hands, the specific shade of rage in David's eyes.

afterward—not grief for the relationship, but grief for the person he had been before that moment,

people.

bus—triggered an immediate urge to freeze, the same paralysis that had seized him during the
assault, as if his body remembered that movement meant worse consequences. This freezing, once it

vivid details would compound his sense of present danger, making it harder to distinguish past from
present.

suffocating him. He had learned to suppress any visible reaction to his triggers, to keep his face

locks seven times.

It was his therapist at the community health center—a colleague who had gently asked why Miguel had
missed the annual staff dinner—who finally named what Miguel had been too ashamed to say aloud: that
he was living in a kind of siege, that the trauma was still occupying his body and his home, and
that the very strategies he had adopted to protect himself were keeping him trapped in the cycle.
Miguel had come to believe that vigilance was the only thing standing between him and catastrophe,
that his frozen response had saved his life by preventing David from escalating further, that
controlling his emotions was the price of survival. What he had not yet been able to see was that
these beliefs, born in a moment of genuine terror, were now the bars of his own prison.
