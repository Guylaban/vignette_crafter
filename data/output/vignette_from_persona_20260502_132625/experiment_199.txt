# Clinical Vignette: Aroha

Aroha was fifty-six when she came to her first appointment, her hands folded tightly in her lap, her
eyes tracking the distance to the office door. She had been a social ministry worker for nearly
twenty years, serving her Samoan community in South Los Angeles through a church-based outreach
program—work that had once felt sacred to her, a way of turning her own resilience into something
that could hold others. That life felt like it belonged to someone else now.

Twenty-three months earlier, Aroha had been assaulted by a cousin she had trusted, a man who had
come to her home while her husband was at work. The attack happened in her bedroom, in the bright
afternoon light, and what struck her most in its aftermath was not the violence itself but the
betrayal of the familiar—the space where she slept, the voice she had known since childhood, the
fact that she had let him in. He had grabbed her from behind as she turned from the window. She had
fought, but he was stronger, and when she tried to call out, his hand covered her mouth so
forcefully that her teeth cut into her lip. It lasted perhaps four minutes. Afterward, he left, and
Aroha sat on the edge of her bed, bleeding slightly, unable to move for what felt like hours.

The months that followed had reshaped her life in ways she had not anticipated. At first, the
memories came without warning—sudden, vivid replays of the assault that felt as though they were
happening again. She would be at the grocery store and catch the scent of his cologne in the aisle,
and the entire event would flash before her with such clarity and force that she would have to grip
the cart to keep standing. The memories were not vague or fragmented; they replayed like a film,
complete with the pressure of his hand, the sound of his breathing, the exact angle of light from
the bedroom window. She could not control when they came. They forced their way into her mind
without permission, especially when she encountered her extended family or when moments of peace
threatened to settle over her.

What frightened Aroha most was not the assault itself but what it had revealed about her—or what she
had come to believe it revealed. She was certain now that someone else, someone braver or more
aware, would have stopped him before it happened. She had failed in some fundamental way. More
troubling still was the conviction that had calcified over the months: that she was no longer
capable of the work that had defined her, that something was irreparably wrong with her as a person.
How could she sit across from women in crisis, women who had survived their own violence, when she
could not even protect herself in her own home? The shame of this thought—that she was defective,
broken in a way that made her unfit for the ministry she loved—had become as present as the assault
itself.

Her body had responded to this internal collapse with relentless vigilance. She existed in a state
of constant tension, her muscles tight, her nervous system perpetually braced for threat. She could
feel it in her shoulders, her jaw, the way her breath never seemed to fully settle. This alertness
intensified whenever she encountered triggers—and triggers seemed to be everywhere. Being around her
extended family, which included the cousin, sent her body into overdrive; the hypervigilance would
spike, her eyes scanning for exits, her mind cataloging distances and escape routes. Even moments of
happiness or relaxation became dangerous. If she allowed herself to feel content, to laugh with her
husband or relax into an evening, the guilt would surge, followed immediately by a spike in her
threat response, as though her body were punishing her for the momentary lapse. And the discomfort
of these feelings—the physical unease that accompanied them—would trigger the old film again, the
memory forcing itself into view.

Over time, Aroha had developed a system of management, though she did not think of it that way. The
constant tension in her body demanded release, and she found herself eating excessively in the
evenings, after her husband had gone to bed—not out of hunger, but out of a need to fill the space
where her fear lived. She would stand at the kitchen counter and consume an entire box of biscuits,
a package of cheese, handfuls of nuts, the act of eating becoming a way to soothe the alertness, to
give her body something to do other than remain coiled and waiting. The eating was both a response
to her threat perception and, paradoxically, something that seemed to amplify it; afterward, she
would feel bloated and ashamed, and the shame would feed back into her conviction that she was
defective. She had also begun wearing heavy makeup—far more than she ever had before—applying it
each morning as though it were armor, a mask that would somehow make her less visible, less
vulnerable, less likely to be seen in the way she had been seen that afternoon.

At work, the avoidance had become impossible to hide. She found herself always positioning herself
near the exit in the small office she shared with two colleagues, a habit so ingrained that she did
her intake interviews with clients sitting at an angle, her body angled toward the door. When
clients spoke about their own assaults, Aroha's memories would surge unbidden, the vividness of her
own trauma bleeding into their stories. She would excuse herself to the bathroom, where she would
check and recheck the locks on the windows—a ritual that had extended into her home as well, where
she now locked and unlocked the doors and windows compulsively, sometimes checking them five or six
times before bed, despite knowing intellectually that her husband had already secured them. The
checking was both a response to her hypervigilance and something that seemed to reinforce it, each
locked door confirming the danger she felt, each verification of security paradoxically deepening
her sense that danger was imminent.

Her husband had begun to notice the withdrawal, the late-night eating, the way she flinched when he
touched her unexpectedly. She had not told him the full story—only that something had happened, that
she needed time. She could not bear to see the look in his eyes if he knew the details, could not
face the possibility that he might blame her, or worse, that he might see her the way she now saw
herself: as someone fundamentally compromised, no longer capable of being the woman he had married.
The isolation this secrecy created only deepened the cycle. Without support, without being able to
name what was happening, Aroha had become increasingly convinced that she was alone in this—that her
response was excessive, that she should be able to move past it, that her inability to do so was
evidence of the defect she carried.

It was her supervisor at the ministry who finally insisted she seek help. Aroha had called in sick
seventeen times in the past four months. She had turned down a promotion. When she did come to work,
she was distant, mechanical, going through the motions of her calling without the warmth that had
once been her hallmark. Her supervisor, who had worked with Aroha for a decade, recognized something
was fundamentally wrong and gave her a referral to a trauma therapist. Aroha had resisted for weeks,
certain that talking about it would only make the memories more vivid, would only confirm what she
already knew about herself. But the alternative—continuing to disappear into the routines of
checking and eating and hiding—had become unbearable. She came to the appointment because she had
nowhere else to go, and because some small part of her, buried beneath the shame and the conviction
of her own defectiveness, still hoped that she might find her way back to the woman she had been.
