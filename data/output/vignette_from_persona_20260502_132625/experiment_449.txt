Aroha had been teaching fourth grade for three years when the stalking began. They noticed it first
in late September—a figure lingering near the parking lot after school, always at the edge of
vision, never quite close enough to confront. By October, the figure had a face: Marcus, a former
colleague who had been let go mid-year for inappropriate conduct with students. He had fixated on
Aroha, initially with flattery that made them uncomfortable, then with persistence that crossed into
threat. He began appearing at the grocery store, at the coffee shop two blocks from their apartment,
once at the park where Aroha's partner had taken them for an evening walk. The police filed a
report. A restraining order was issued. But Marcus seemed to know where Aroha would be before they
arrived.

The night everything crystallized was in November. Aroha had stayed late at school grading papers,
and the parking lot was nearly empty when they walked out. The footsteps started immediately—quick,
deliberate, closing the distance. Aroha's heart seized. They ran to their car, hands shaking so
badly the keys fell. The footsteps kept coming. In the rearview mirror, they could see a figure
running toward them, but in the panic and darkness, Aroha couldn't piece together what happened
next. They drove away, but the memory fractured there—some moments crystalline and terrifying,
others blank. They remembered the sound of their own breathing, the streetlight on the figure's
face, but not whether he had touched the car, whether he had shouted anything, whether the police
had actually arrived or if they had simply driven home in a fugue state. Pieces were missing, and
the gaps themselves became maddening.

In the weeks that followed, Aroha couldn't stop replaying what they could remember, turning it over
obsessively, searching for the missing pieces. If only they had left earlier. If only they had
parked closer to the building. If only they had asked someone to walk them to their car. The
rumination became a way of trying to recover the blank spaces, as though obsessing hard enough might
fill them in or change what had happened. But the more Aroha ruminated, the more fragmented the
memory became, like trying to reassemble a puzzle while simultaneously scrambling the pieces. The
event had shattered into disconnected shards—the footsteps, the mirror, the darkness, the
running—none of it forming a coherent whole. And because the memory remained incomplete, Aroha
couldn't stop turning it over, couldn't reach any resolution or sense of closure that might let them
move forward.

Within weeks, Aroha's body had become a threat-detection machine. They began scanning every
environment obsessively—parking lots, hallways, the playground during recess—looking for the figure
of a man, any man who might be him. At school, sudden movements from students or colleagues sent
them into immediate high alert; a child running toward them with a question would trigger a spike of
panic before their rational mind caught up. The hypervigilance was exhausting but felt necessary, a
price worth paying for safety. And yet, each time Aroha scanned and found nothing, the absence of
danger didn't bring relief—it only reinforced the conviction that Marcus was still out there, still
watching, still planning. The threat felt constant, lurking just beyond the edges of awareness.

This constant scanning, this need to monitor every corner and every footstep, drove Aroha to avoid
anything that might lower their guard. They stopped going to the gym, where they might have their
back to the door. They stopped reading in the living room and instead positioned themselves facing
the windows, always aware of who might be looking in. Most painfully, they stopped relaxing into
their partner's arms at night; the vulnerability of sleep, of being held, of not being able to see
the room, felt unbearable. Avoidance of relaxation became avoidance of safety itself. And this
constant state of defensive readiness, this refusal to let down, only amplified the threat system
further. Every footstep behind them in the grocery store, every car that seemed to follow them home,
every moment of being watched or tracked became proof that the danger was real and ongoing. The
maladaptive strategies meant to protect Aroha had become self-perpetuating—the scanning fed the
threat, the threat fed the avoidance, the avoidance fed the rumination, and the rumination kept the
fragmented memories alive and distressing.

Now, six months later, Aroha is struggling to function at work. They have called in sick dozens of
times, unable to face the parking lot or the hallways where Marcus might appear. Their partner,
exhausted and frightened by Aroha's deterioration, has begun to withdraw. When Aroha finally called
a therapist, it was not out of hope but out of desperation—the conviction that something fundamental
had broken and that they might never feel safe in their own body again.
