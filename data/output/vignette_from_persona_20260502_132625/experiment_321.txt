# Clinical Vignette: Priya Mehta

Priya Mehta was seventy-eight when the bomb went off in the lobby of the medical building where she
kept her practice. She had been in her operatory chair, leaning back to examine a patient's upper
molars, when the blast shook the floor beneath her feet. The sound came first—a concussive roar that
seemed to originate from inside her own chest—followed by the smell of smoke and burnt metal pouring
through the ventilation system. For a moment, she could not move. Then the screaming began. She
remembers the patient's face, the way his mouth opened in terror, and the sudden awareness that she
had no control over what happened next, that the building could collapse and there was nothing she
could do but wait. The lobby had been ransacked by a lone attacker with a handgun and explosives.
Three people died. Priya had not been directly wounded, but something had fractured inside her that
morning.

In the months that followed, the world had become a landscape of threats. Any siren—an ambulance
passing her house, a fire truck on the highway, even the alarm at the grocery store—sent her heart
into her throat. The sound meant danger was coming, meant she was powerless again, meant the ground
could open beneath her feet. She had come to believe that vigilance was her only protection, that if
she watched carefully enough, scanned the room for exits and threats, she could prevent what had
happened from happening again. When she returned to her practice six weeks after the bombing, she
found herself unable to sit comfortably in her chair. She positioned herself always with a view of
the doorway, and in the first few months she would excuse herself from patient care at least once
per session to walk to the reception area and check that the front exit remained unblocked. Her
staff noticed. Her patient load declined. After a year, she had closed the practice.

The avoidance had become its own architecture. She no longer drove downtown, where the medical
building still stood. She took longer routes to appointments, adding forty minutes to a trip rather
than pass within five blocks of the site. But the routes themselves became contaminated. A
particular intersection where she once saw smoke from a restaurant fire sent her into a panic that
lasted hours. She began mapping her movements obsessively, planning exits before entering any
space—a restaurant, a cinema, even her daughter's home. She would position herself near the door,
her leg bouncing, her eyes tracking the room. If a waiter moved too quickly or a manager asked her
to move to a different table, the old sensation of powerlessness flooded back. Someone had control
over her, could tell her where to sit, could trap her. The smell of smoke—from a neighbour's
fireplace, from someone's cigarette—would send her out of the room entirely, her breath shallow,
convinced that danger was imminent.

What had begun as hypervigilance had calcified into a prison of her own making. The triggers had not
diminished; instead, they had multiplied. Sirens still paralyzed her, but now so did the absence of
an exit, so did the presence of authority figures who could dictate her movements. The checking—the
constant scanning of her surroundings, the mental inventory of exits, the need to position herself
strategically—had become compulsive. She could not relax in any space because relaxation meant
lowering her guard, and lowering her guard meant she would be caught unaware again. The strategies
that had felt protective had instead tightened around her like a cage. She was eighty now, living
alone in a small house, rarely leaving except for medical appointments. Her daughter had stopped
inviting her to family dinners. She had not been to a restaurant in three years. It was only when
her primary care physician gently suggested that her isolation was affecting her health, that
perhaps there was someone who could help her think differently about the fear, that Priya finally
made the call. She sat in the waiting room of the therapist's office, positioned deliberately near
the door, and admitted what she had been unable to say aloud: she was not living. She was only
surviving, and barely that.
