# Aroha's Vigil

Aroha was twenty years old when Marcus found her. She had moved to Portland six months earlier for
an auditing position at a mid-sized firm, eager to prove herself in a new city, far enough from her
family in Samoa that she could build something independent. By March, she had settled into a rhythm:
small apartment in a walkable neighborhood, morning coffee runs, Friday drinks with coworkers. She
noticed Marcus first at the coffee shop—a man in his thirties who seemed always to be there when she
was, though she told herself it was coincidence. Then he began to appear at her building's entrance.
He never approached her directly, never spoke, but he was there: leaning against the fence, watching
her car pull in, sometimes following her down the street at a distance. When she tried to ignore
him, he sent messages through a Facebook account she didn't recognize—nothing overtly threatening,
but intimate in a way that made her skin crawl. *I see you walking to work. Your hair looked nice
today. I know where you live.* She reported it to the police, who took a report and told her to
document everything. For three weeks, she did. Then one evening, as she was leaving the office
building, she saw him across the street. Their eyes met. He smiled. She ran to her car, locked the
doors, and sat there shaking, certain that if she drove home he would follow, that he knew exactly
where she was going. She drove to a hotel instead and sat in the lobby until midnight, too afraid to
move.

That was eight months ago. Marcus had been arrested after she filed a stalking charge, and he was
currently serving a restraining order, but the arrest had not quieted what had awakened in Aroha.
The world had revealed itself to be a place where men could watch you, could know where you lived,
could decide that you belonged to them. The police report had only confirmed what she had come to
believe: that safety was an illusion, that vigilance was the only honest posture. She had come to
understand her trauma as proof that her life had been fundamentally destroyed—that the girl who had
moved to Portland with ambitions and lightness was gone, replaced by someone who could never be
careless again.

This conviction shaped everything she did now. Because she was certain the world was dangerous and
that she deserved to suffer for having been chosen by Marcus—for having, in some way she couldn't
quite articulate, attracted his attention—Aroha began to withdraw from anything that might offer her
relief. When a coworker invited her to a weekend hiking trip, she declined, telling herself she
didn't deserve to feel the peace that nature might bring. When her mother called with news that a
family friend was visiting from Samoa, Aroha made an excuse; happiness felt like a betrayal of her
new, truer understanding of reality. She stopped accepting the Friday drinks. She stopped going out
except for work and groceries, moving through those necessary trips in a state of constant
surveillance, scanning faces in crowds, checking her mirrors, positioning herself so she could see
the exits. At the office, she found herself unable to concentrate on spreadsheets and financial
statements. Her manager had gently suggested that she seemed distracted; she had nearly snapped at
him, certain that he was blaming her for her own fear. The work that had once felt purposeful now
felt like time spent in a building with multiple entrances, surrounded by people who might be
watching.

She had begun to tell herself that she deserved this punishment—the isolation, the hypervigilance,
the way her chest tightened whenever her phone buzzed. To accept comfort or hope felt like a kind of
moral failing, a refusal to honor the seriousness of what had happened to her. Aroha had constructed
a narrative in which suffering was the only appropriate response, and safety was a lie she could no
longer afford to believe. She came to therapy because her mother had insisted, threatening to fly to
Portland herself if Aroha didn't talk to someone. Aroha sat in the waiting room with her hands
folded, convinced that no one could help her, that the damage was permanent, and that she was right
to be afraid.
