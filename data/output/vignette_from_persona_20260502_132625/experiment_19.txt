# Priya

Four years ago, Priya was thirty-eight and certain she was invincible. She worked twelve-hour days
as a plumber in Baltimore, crawling through attics and basements, fixing burst pipes and replacing
water heaters with the easy confidence of someone whose body had never betrayed her. Then came the
chest pain—a tight, crushing sensation that started on a Tuesday morning while she was under a sink.
She told herself it was indigestion, muscular strain from the awkward angle. By evening, she was on
the floor of her apartment, unable to breathe properly, convinced she was dying.

The hospital admitted her at midnight. Sepsis from an undiagnosed infection had triggered
myocarditis—her heart muscle inflamed, her oxygen saturation dropping. For three days, she lay in
the intensive care unit while machines beeped and hissed around her, while nurses in masks checked
her vitals every hour, while the smell of antiseptic and something chemical—something she could not
name but would never forget—filled her nostrils. The cardiologist told her afterward that she had
been minutes away from cardiac arrest. She had survived, but something fundamental had fractured.

In the months that followed, Priya tried to return to work. But the physical stress of the job—the
bending, the exertion, the way her heart would race when she climbed a ladder—began to feel
dangerous. At first it was just caution; she took longer breaks, moved more slowly. Then one
afternoon, while replacing a toilet in a client's basement, a wave of dizziness hit her so suddenly
that she had to sit down on the tile floor. The room tilted. Her heart began to hammer. In that
moment, the old fear returned with absolute clarity: her body was no longer safe. It could fail her
again without warning. She had to be constantly vigilant, monitoring every sensation, because you
never knew what could happen next. The dizziness itself—that spinning sensation—had become a
trigger, a physical signal that danger was imminent. The more she felt it, the more she was certain
she couldn't trust her own body anymore.

She began to avoid situations that might stress her heart. No climbing ladders. No jobs that
required sustained physical effort. She turned down calls, lost income, and watched her client base
shrink as she became unreliable. She also stopped going to the gym, stopped taking walks, stopped
doing anything that made her breathe hard. What had once been a small precaution became a prison.
But it was the medical system itself that frightened her most. Every time she saw an ambulance or
passed a hospital, her chest tightened. The smell of antiseptic in a pharmacy would send her into a
panic. She hadn't had a physical examination in three years. When her dentist recommended a
cleaning, she cancelled and never rescheduled. She knew, somewhere in her rational mind, that
avoiding medical care was dangerous—that her heart needed monitoring—but the thought of walking into
a hospital, of lying on an examination table, of smelling that smell again, was unbearable. She had
come to believe that other people couldn't protect her, that doctors had missed her illness before,
that she was fundamentally alone with her failing body.

The avoidance had consequences that spread beyond her health. Work became sporadic and humiliating.
She had to turn down a lucrative contract because it involved four stories of climbing. Her boss,
confused by her sudden unreliability, stopped calling her for jobs. She told no one what had
happened—not her family in Mumbai, not her few close friends—because admitting the fear felt like
admitting defeat. She lived in a state of constant self-monitoring, hyperaware of every flutter in
her chest, every moment of lightheadedness, certain that each sensation meant her heart was failing
again. The more she avoided stress and medical care, the more convinced she became that her body was
unreliable and that she had to manage this alone. No one else could understand. No one could be
trusted to help. And so the cycle tightened: the fear that something terrible would happen next kept
her vigilant; the vigilance kept her isolated and avoidant; the avoidance made her feel more trapped
and more certain that danger was always one heartbeat away.

It was her sister's wedding in six months that finally pushed her to seek help. Priya was terrified
of what might happen if she travelled, if she danced, if her body failed her in front of everyone
she loved. She called a therapist, her hands shaking as she dialled, more frightened of being helped
than of the alternative, but finally out of other choices.
