# Rosa

Rosa was fifty-three when the siege began. She had been working as an editor for a small publishing
house in San Salvador for nearly twenty years, a job that had given her intellectual purpose and a
measure of independence her marriage could never quite provide. The divorce had been finalized two
years earlier, and she had settled into a quiet rhythm of work, books, and the company of her
sister's family on weekends. That rhythm shattered in November 2009 when rival gangs turned her
neighborhood into a battleground.

The violence came without warning. Rosa was walking home from the office on a Tuesday evening when
the first explosion detonated three blocks away—a car bomb meant for a rival faction's safe house.
The sound was not like anything she had encountered in her fifty-three years. It was not a bang; it
was a rupture in the world itself, a compression of air that knocked her backward against a
storefront window. Glass shattered behind her. People screamed. For the next six hours, she huddled
in a pharmacy basement with strangers while gunfire echoed through the streets above. When the
police finally came—soldiers in riot gear, faces obscured by masks and visors—she was too terrified
to feel relief.

The worst part came three days later. Rosa had returned to her apartment, trying to convince herself
it was over, when she saw him on the street below her window: one of the gang members she had
watched, from that pharmacy basement, drag a wounded man into an alley. She would never forget his
face, the casual cruelty in his movements. He did not see her, but she saw him, and in that moment
something fundamental fractured in her understanding of safety. If he was still there, if he was
still alive and moving through the city, then nothing was actually over. The threat had not ended;
it had only paused.

Rosa left San Salvador three months later. Her sister helped her secure a visa, and by March 2010
she had settled in Chicago, where she found work with a larger publishing firm. For years, she told
herself the distance would be enough. But distance, she came to discover, was an illusion. The
triggers followed her across the border like shadows.

Loud noises became unbearable. A car backfiring on the street outside her apartment sent her heart
into her throat, her body rigid with the certainty that the explosion was coming again. She began
timing her errands to avoid rush hour, when traffic was heaviest and the risk of sudden sounds
increased. At the office, she requested a desk away from the loading dock where deliveries arrived.
When a colleague mentioned attending a Fourth of July fireworks display, Rosa felt something close
to panic at the thought—all those explosions, intentional and celebratory, but explosions
nonetheless. She stopped going out after dark.

The night vigilance had crept in gradually. At first, Rosa simply found herself waking at three or
four in the morning, her mind alert, her body tense. She would lie in bed, listening to the sounds
of the building—the hum of the refrigerator, the distant rumble of the L-train, the occasional
siren. Each sound had to be catalogued, assessed, determined to be safe or not safe. Eventually she
began getting up to check the locks on her windows and doors, a ritual that started once a night
and, over the years, became multiple times. She could not sleep through the night anymore. Even when
she was exhausted, some part of her remained on watch, convinced that danger was waiting for the
moment her vigilance slipped.

What frightened Rosa most was that she could not shake the conviction that safety was a lie people
told themselves. Her colleagues assured her that Chicago was safe, that the violence she had fled
was an ocean away, that she was protected here. But she had learned something in that pharmacy
basement and on that street corner watching the offender move through the city as if he owned it:
that assurances meant nothing. The world was not safe. It had revealed itself to be fundamentally
unsafe, and no amount of distance or reassurance could change that. When she saw a police officer in
uniform on the train, or when a delivery truck backfired near her building, the old certainty
returned instantly—the certainty that she was still in danger, that the threat had never really
left, only disguised itself in the ordinary sounds and sights of an American city. The vigilance
would spike again. The locks would be checked. The night would be divided into hours of half-sleep
and half-terror.

Now, at sixty-six, Rosa had finally reached out to a trauma therapist. She had taken medical leave
from her job, unable to concentrate, unable to trust that her office was truly secure. What she
wanted most was to sleep through a single night without waking. What she feared most was that the
person she had been before the siege—the woman who could walk home at dusk without scanning every
face, who could hear a loud noise without her entire nervous system screaming—was gone forever.
