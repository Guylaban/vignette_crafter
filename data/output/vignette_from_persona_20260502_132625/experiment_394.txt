# Leila's Story

Leila was nineteen when her younger brother Karim stopped breathing properly. It happened on a
Tuesday in March, three years ago, when she was supposed to be watching him while their mother
worked a double shift at the hospital. He'd complained of chest pain that morning—nothing she'd
recognized as serious, just a boy of eight grumbling about his ribs hurting. She'd given him water,
told him to rest on the couch, and gone back to her phone. By afternoon, his breathing had become
shallow and rapid, a sound she now heard in her sleep. The paramedics said later it was myocarditis,
an inflammation of the heart muscle, the kind of thing that could have killed him within hours if
she hadn't finally noticed his lips turning pale and called 911. He survived. He recovered fully.
But in those minutes waiting for the ambulance, holding his small hand while his chest heaved and
his eyes rolled back in panic, Leila had felt the world split open—the before and after of her life
marked by the exact moment she understood she was powerless.

She had come to believe, in the months that followed, that she was fundamentally weak. A stronger
person would have noticed sooner. A stronger person would have known what to do. She replayed that
afternoon constantly, cataloging every missed sign, every moment she could have acted differently.
The guilt calcified into something harder: a certainty that she should have protected him and
hadn't, that she lacked something essential that other people possessed. When her boyfriend
mentioned wanting children someday, Leila felt a cold spike of fear—how could she keep a child safe
when she'd failed so spectacularly with Karim? She began to notice her own fragility everywhere. The
way her hands shook when she was tired. The way she cried at small things. These felt like proof of
her weakness, evidence that the world had correctly identified her as someone who could not be
trusted with the people she loved.

This belief about herself had begun to reshape how her body responded to ordinary moments. At the
warehouse where she worked, stacking boxes on a loading dock, she'd sometimes hear a child's voice
from the parking lot—a mother calling to her son—and Leila's heart would accelerate violently, her
palms would sweat, and a wave of dread would wash through her before she could even consciously
register why. The physical reaction came first, a full-body alarm that felt like proof that danger
was everywhere, that she should have been more vigilant, that she would fail again. Once triggered
this way, her mind would spiral: if she couldn't even stay calm at work, how could she ever be
responsible for anyone? The racing heart felt like confirmation that she was right to be afraid,
that her weakness wasn't just a character flaw but a physical reality written into her nervous
system. She found herself scanning the warehouse constantly, listening for sounds that might signal
distress, her concentration fracturing whenever anything felt even remotely like the chaos of that
March afternoon.

Over the past year, Leila had begun avoiding situations where she might be responsible for others.
She'd turned down shifts that required supervising new employees. She'd pulled back from her
relationship, unable to articulate to her boyfriend why the thought of deepening their commitment
filled her with such terror. She'd stopped babysitting for her cousins, offering vague excuses that
she knew fooled no one. The isolation had deepened her certainty that something was fundamentally
wrong with her—not just that she'd made a mistake, but that she was the kind of person who couldn't
be trusted. What frightened her most was that Karim might one day understand how close he'd come,
might realize that his survival had depended on luck rather than her protection. She'd come to the
clinic at her mother's insistence, though part of her believed that no amount of talking would fix
what she knew to be true about herself.
