# Natasha

Natasha was thirty-seven when the bombing happened, a cellist with the Boston Philharmonic, and she
had spent the previous decade building a life that felt, for the first time, genuinely hers. She'd
grown up in a chaotic household in Toronto, moved to the States at twenty-two with nothing but her
instrument and a scholarship, and had slowly assembled something like stability: a position in a
prestigious orchestra, a rent-controlled apartment in Cambridge, a partner of four years who
understood the peculiar demands of her schedule. On a Tuesday evening in March, she was attending a
chamber music concert at a downtown venue—not performing, just listening, a rare night off—when the
explosion happened. The blast threw her backward into a concrete wall. The sound was not a sound but
a physical force that seemed to originate inside her own chest. In the chaos that followed—the
screaming, the dust that coated her throat and nostrils with a metallic taste she would never quite
forget, the sight of blood on her own hands that she later realized wasn't hers—Natasha had a single
coherent thought: *this is the end*. She was trapped for forty minutes before rescue workers pulled
her out, and in those minutes, pinned beneath a section of collapsed seating, she had tasted blood
and plaster and smoke, and she had been absolutely certain she would die there.

She didn't die. She was released from the hospital after three days with minor injuries—lacerations,
a fractured rib—and for the first week, she felt almost numb, as if the explosion had burned away
her capacity to feel anything at all. But by the second week, the memories began to return, and they
did not return gently. They came without warning, fully formed and sensory-complete: she would be
sitting in a rehearsal and suddenly she was back in that darkness, tasting copper and ash, feeling
the weight of rubble on her chest, hearing the ringing that had filled her ears as consciousness
threatened to slip away. The memories were so vivid, so utterly real across every sense at once,
that her body responded as if the bombing were happening again—her heart would race, her breathing
would shallow, and she would find herself gripping her bow so tightly her hand would cramp. In those
moments, she could not distinguish between then and now. The boundary had collapsed.

What made the memories worse, Natasha came to realize, was the conviction that had settled into her
thinking in the weeks after the bombing. She had begun to see the world as fundamentally
untrustworthy. People were not what they seemed—anyone could be a threat, any public gathering could
be a target, any moment of ordinary safety could be an illusion. She no longer belonged anywhere.
The orchestra, which had been her sanctuary, now felt like a trap: a crowded room full of strangers,
any one of whom might harbor violence. Her partner tried to comfort her, but Natasha found herself
pulling away, certain that his reassurances were hollow, that no one could truly protect her because
no one could be trusted. These dark convictions—that she had no future, that connection was an
illusion, that the world was fundamentally hostile—seemed to make the memories worse. When she
believed that she belonged nowhere and that people could not be trusted, the flashbacks felt more
real, more immediate, as if they were proof of what she had come to know about the world. The
memories and the hopelessness fed each other, a cycle she could not break.

The triggers were everywhere. Loud noises—a car backfiring, a dropped pot in the kitchen—would send
her into a state of hypervigilance that would last for hours. Crowds triggered her too, especially
in enclosed spaces. She began to avoid concert halls altogether, which meant she stopped attending
performances, and then she began making excuses to miss rehearsals. The thought of sitting in that
orchestra pit, surrounded by people, in a building where she could not easily escape, made her chest
tighten with a kind of suffocating dread. She started having difficulty concentrating on her music;
her mind would fracture mid-phrase, and she would lose her place in the score. Her irritability grew
sharp and unpredictable. Her partner would make an innocent comment and she would snap at him, her
anger flaring without proportion to what he'd said, as if she were angry at the world itself and he
was simply the nearest target. Within four months, she had taken a leave of absence from the
orchestra. Within six, her relationship had begun to fracture under the weight of her withdrawal and
her rage.

It was her partner who finally insisted she seek help. Natasha resisted—what could therapy do
against the reality of what she had experienced, against the certainty that the world was dangerous
and she was fundamentally alone in it? But sitting in the waiting room of a trauma clinic on a gray
November morning, she allowed herself to consider that the way she was living was not living at all.
She was afraid that nothing could change this, that she was broken in some permanent way. But she
was also afraid that if she did not change something, she would lose everything else that remained.
