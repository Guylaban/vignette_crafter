# Sister Gabriela

Sister Gabriela was forty-four when she first came to the clinic, though her face held the careful
composure of someone much older. She had been a nun for nineteen years, living in the Carmelite
convent in the foothills outside Albuquerque, when the bombing happened. It was a Tuesday in March,
three years ago. A extremist group had targeted the interfaith community center two blocks from the
convent where Gabriela volunteered twice a week, teaching literacy to recent immigrants. She had
stepped outside to get air—the fluorescent lights of the center always made her head ache—and was
walking back toward the entrance when the blast came. The force threw her backward onto the
pavement. For what felt like hours but was probably seconds, she lay there, unable to move, watching
dust and fragments hang in the afternoon light. A woman's leg lay a few feet away. The ringing in
her ears was so complete it felt like silence. When volunteers finally pulled her up, her left
shoulder was dislocated and her body was covered in small lacerations that bled steadily onto her
white habit.

In the weeks after, Gabriela returned to the convent and tried to resume her duties. But something
had fractured inside her that went deeper than the shoulder, which healed within months. Any sudden
physical pain—a pulled muscle, a sharp headache, even the sting of a paper cut—would send her into a
state of raw alarm, her body flooding with the certainty that something catastrophic was about to
unfold. The pain itself became inseparable from the moment of impact, from the helplessness, from
the knowledge that her body could not protect her. When she woke from nightmares, gasping and
disoriented, the terror did not fade gradually; it seized her as though the blast had just happened
again. And blood—even a small amount, even in a medical context—would trigger a cascade of intrusive
images: the leg on the pavement, the dust thick with what she understood now had been human remains,
the smell of scorched fabric and something else she could not name but could not forget.

These triggers did not simply cause her distress; they opened a door to a kind of reliving that was
almost unbearable. When a memory came, it came complete—not as a story she could narrate but as a
full sensory storm. She could feel the pavement against her back, smell the dust, hear the ringing,
see the leg with the precision of something happening in real time. Yet when she tried deliberately
to piece together what had occurred, to make sense of it in the quiet of her cell, the details
scattered. She could not reliably access the sequence of events or hold them steady enough to
understand them. The memory, it seemed, had its own will. It would force itself into her mind
unbidden—during Mass, during meals, while she was trying to sleep—and when it came, she was
powerless to stop it or to control what it showed her.

The memories brought with them a tide of emotions so intense that Gabriela had come to dread them as
much as the triggers themselves. Fear, yes, but also a profound horror at what had happened, a rage
at the men who had done it, and underneath it all, a corrosive guilt that she could not fully
articulate. She had survived when others had not. She had walked out of that building alive. What
had she done to deserve that? The weight of these feelings, once they surfaced, seemed to confirm
her deepest belief: that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that violence could erupt anywhere,
that her body and the bodies of those around her were fragile and temporary. She had come to feel
safe only within the convent walls, in the chapel, in her small room. Anywhere else, she found
herself automatically scanning for exits, plotting routes to safety, her shoulders tense and her
breath shallow.

To manage the fear and the unbearable memories, Gabriela had begun to withdraw. She stopped
attending community meals, eating alone in her cell instead. She made excuses to avoid the
interfaith center entirely, though this meant giving up work that had once brought her meaning. When
other sisters tried to comfort her or draw her into conversation, she felt a wall rise inside her—an
inability or unwillingness to let anyone close. Intimacy of any kind, even the ordinary warmth of
friendship, felt dangerous; if she allowed herself to care about someone, they could be taken from
her in an instant. The more she isolated herself, the more the fear intensified. Silence and
solitude, which should have been comforting in her vocation, became a kind of amplification chamber
for her dread. She would lie awake, her mind cycling through catastrophic scenarios, and when she
finally slept, the nightmares came. She would wake gasping, her heart racing, and the cycle would
begin again.

Three months ago, during a routine visit to the convent by the diocesan physician, Sister Gabriela
mentioned in passing that she had been having difficulty sleeping and that loud noises made her
jump. The doctor recognized something in her description and gently suggested that what she was
experiencing might respond to professional help. Gabriela had resisted at first—the convent was
supposed to be her sanctuary, her refuge in faith—but the weight of living this way, the isolation,
the constant vigilance, had become unsustainable. She was losing her vocation to the very fear that
the violence had planted in her. Now, sitting in the clinic, she was hoping that someone could help
her understand why her mind had become a place where she could no longer find peace, and whether it
was possible to feel safe again without abandoning the only life she had ever known.
