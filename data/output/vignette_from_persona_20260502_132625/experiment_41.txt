Kwame was thirty-seven when the attack happened, though he would later mark his life in two distinct
eras: before and after a single night in March. He had been at a professional conference in Atlanta,

escape impossible. The windows looked out onto a parking lot where no one could hear him. When it
was over, Kwame had lain there in the dark, feeling the weight of the man's presence even after he'd
left the room, and had understood in that moment something that would reshape every day that
followed: he was not safe anywhere.

For weeks afterward, Kwame had tried to function. He'd returned to his practice, seeing clients in
his office in Durham, a space he'd always found professional and calm. But something had fractured.
Within a month, he'd rearranged his entire office so that his desk faced the door and he could see
into the waiting room. He'd begun positioning himself with his back against the wall during
sessions, a habit so pronounced that one client had asked if he was uncomfortable. He wasn't
sleeping well either—his mind wouldn't quiet at night, and he'd lie awake convinced that vigilance
was the only thing keeping him alive. The belief that he was fundamentally exposed, that the trauma
was written across his face for anyone to read, had begun to calcify. He was certain that his
clients could see what had happened to him, that they would sense his weakness, his failure to
protect himself. When a female client had mentioned her own experience of assault in passing, Kwame

cascade of panic—his heart racing, his hands trembling—and he'd had to excuse himself to the
bathroom.

spaces became intolerable. When a client's session ran late and Kwame found himself in his office
with the door closed, he would feel his chest tighten, an urgent need to leave that he could barely
suppress. But what frightened him most was the presence of men. Being alone with a male colleague,
even briefly, sent his nervous system into overdrive. He'd started avoiding meetings that were one-
on-one, scheduling consultations only in group settings, making excuses. His supervisor had
noticed—had asked if everything was all right—but Kwame couldn't articulate it. How could he explain
that the simple fact of a man's presence in an enclosed space had become a threat that his body
registered before his mind could reason it away?

What Kwame had come to believe, in the months that followed, was that he had no control over what
happened to him, that his future was a foregone conclusion of further harm. He couldn't stop bad
things from happening. This conviction didn't sit separately from his vigilance; it fed it. His
inability to relax, even in his own apartment with the door locked and the lights on, was rooted in
the conviction that relaxation was a luxury he couldn't afford, that the moment he let his guard
down was the moment danger would find him. The hypervigilance—the need to position himself so he

response to a world that had already proven itself catastrophic. And this constant state of threat

deepened his sense of hopelessness. He was trapped in a loop where his fear confirmed his belief
that the world was dangerous, and his belief that he was helpless made the fear feel justified.

and when asked what had brought him in, he said simply: "I'm drowning, and I don't know how to
stop."
