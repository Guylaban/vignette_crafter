# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was twenty-eight when his Humvee hit an IED outside Kandahar. He was the gunner. The blast
came from nowhere—a flash, a sound that seemed to split the world, the acrid taste of dust and
copper and something chemical that coated his mouth and throat. His left arm was pinned against the
turret. The silence afterward was worse than the noise; it felt like the world had stopped
breathing. By the time the medevac arrived, he had already decided that any moment of quiet was a
moment when death was deciding whether to come back.

Now, at thirty-six, Marcus works as a tax consultant in Denver. He has been married to his wife,
Sophia, for four years, and they live in a quiet neighborhood with a mortgage and the kind of life
he tells himself he should want. But the quiet has never left him. Every silence in the office—a
client pausing to consider a question, a colleague stepping away from his desk—sends a spike of
dread through his chest. He finds himself mapping exits in every conference room before he sits
down, identifying which windows might open, which doors lead to stairs. He does this automatically
now, the way other people might scan a room for where to hang their coat.

The triggers arrive without warning. Last month, during a routine meeting with a client about
quarterly deductions, the man across the table fell silent while reviewing a spreadsheet. Marcus
felt his throat tighten. The silence stretched. He found himself unable to speak, unable to move,
waiting for something to explode. When the client finally looked up and asked a question, Marcus's
hands were shaking. He blamed it on caffeine, excused himself, and spent fifteen minutes in the
bathroom gripping the sink. Since then, he has begun scheduling calls instead of in-person meetings
whenever possible.

Confrontations are worse. Three weeks ago, Sophia asked him why he had been distant, why he wouldn't
let her hold his hand on the couch anymore. The question itself—direct, concerned, the kind of thing
a wife is supposed to be able to ask—triggered something immediate and urgent in him. He felt
accused. He felt cornered. The memory came without preamble: the taste of that chemical dust, the
weight of his own panic, the certainty that he was about to die. But alongside the memory came a
numbness so complete it frightened him more than the panic itself. He could describe what
happened—the blast, the heat, the blood on his uniform—with the detachment of someone reading a
report about a stranger. He told Sophia he didn't know what she meant, that he was fine, that she
needed to stop asking questions. The conversation escalated. He left the house.

What Marcus has come to believe, over months of small withdrawals and accumulating isolation, is
that safety requires distance. When Sophia tries to touch him—placing a hand on his shoulder,
reaching for him in bed—he flinches violently, as if her touch itself is a threat. This reaction
terrifies him, but he cannot seem to stop it. He has begun sleeping in the guest room. He tells
himself it is temporary, that he is protecting her from his nightmares, but the truth is more
complicated: allowing her closeness feels like it will undo him. When she touches him, the memories
flood back immediately, uncontrolled and overwhelming. So he avoids her touch, which means he avoids
conversations about why he is avoiding her, which means he avoids talking about the deployment at
all.

The avoidance has become its own architecture. He will not speak to Sophia about what happened
overseas, not because he cannot find the words but because speaking feels dangerous—as though naming
the trauma might give it new power over him. When his brother called last month and asked how he was
doing, Marcus said "fine" and changed the subject. He pushes away the moments when Sophia smiles at
him in a way that suggests she still loves him, because allowing himself to feel that love feels
reckless, as though something terrible will happen if he lets his guard down. This constant
vigilance, this refusal to be touched or to be close, has created a distance in his marriage that
now feels like its own kind of threat. Sophia has stopped trying to reach for him. The house is
quiet in a way that has nothing to do with silence.

He came to therapy because Sophia gave him an ultimatum, delivered quietly on a Tuesday evening:
"Get help or I'm done." Marcus sat across from the therapist and said only that he was having
trouble sleeping and that loud noises bothered him. But his eyes kept moving to the door, and when
the therapist shifted in her chair, he tensed. He knows something is wrong. He knows the distance he
has built between himself and everyone he loves is the only thing keeping him from falling apart.
What he does not yet understand is that the very strategies keeping him safe are the ones that
ensure he will never feel safe again.
