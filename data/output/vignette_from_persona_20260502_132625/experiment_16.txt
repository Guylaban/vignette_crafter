# Catalina

Catalina was thirty-eight when she first sat across from a therapist, her shoulders drawn tight
against the back of a chair she had positioned in the corner of the office. She had spent most of
her adult life running from something she could not fully name, though the shape of it had been cast
in her childhood in San Juan, where her stepfather's rages had become the grammar of survival.

She was nine years old when the worst of it happened. Her stepfather had come home drunk, furious
about something trivial—a meal, a noise, the simple fact of her existing in his space. He had
cornered her in the kitchen, his body blocking the doorway, his hands closing around her arms with a
force that felt absolute. What remained in her mind now were fragments: the exact shade of the
linoleum beneath her feet, his breath, the sensation of her own voice disappearing. Other moments
were gone entirely—she could not recall how it ended, or what he said, or how she had gotten to her
room. The clarity and the blankness existed side by side, as if her mind had tried to protect her by
erasing parts while burning others into permanent image.

For years, Catalina had managed. She left Puerto Rico at seventeen, built a life in Boston, became a
community organizer—the kind of woman who showed up for others, who knew how to navigate systems and
demand change. She had a partner now, a woman named Sofia who loved her, who had never raised a
hand. But something in her remained perpetually braced.

The work itself had become her refuge and her trap. Catalina threw herself into community campaigns
with an intensity that her colleagues admired and that Sofia had begun to gently question. Late
nights at the office, weekend strategy sessions, grant proposals that consumed her attention—these
were not just commitments. They were ways to keep her mind occupied, to drown out the intrusive
flashes of memory that could surface without warning. A client would mention feeling trapped in a
situation with no way out, and suddenly Catalina would be nine again, boxed into that kitchen,
certain there was no escape. The memory would arrive as a vivid snapshot, a burst of image and
sensation, and she would push it away by diving deeper into work, staying later, taking on another
project. The activity kept the memories at bay, but only for hours at a time.

At night, the walls closed in differently. Catalina could not fall asleep easily, her body refusing
to surrender vigilance. She needed to know where Sofia was, what sounds were in the apartment,
whether the door was locked. She had arranged their bedroom so that her side of the bed faced the
entrance, and she always sat with her back to the wall in any room—a habit so ingrained that she did
it without thinking. In restaurants, she would scan for the safest seat before sitting, one that
gave her sight lines to exits and to anyone approaching. When she walked, she found herself checking
behind her, a quick glance over her shoulder, as if someone might be following. These were not
conscious choices anymore; they were the architecture of how she moved through the world.

The fragmented memories fed everything else. When a moment from the trauma surfaced—unbidden,
unwelcome—it triggered a cascade. The memory of entrapment, of having no options, would flood her
with a sense of present danger, even though she was safe in her office or her home. That feeling of
threat would drive her to seek a safe seat, to position herself defensively, to stay alert through
the night. And each time the memory came, it reinforced what she had come to believe about herself:
that she was weak, that someone stronger would have fought back or escaped, that she was
fundamentally damaged in a way that made her different from other people. That belief in her own
weakness then fed her compulsion to overwork, to prove herself through relentless productivity, to
be indispensable and therefore safe.

Sofia had noticed the distance growing between them. Catalina would come home late, emotionally
drained and strangely numb, unable to be present. When Sofia tried to hold her, Catalina sometimes
felt nothing—as if she were observing her own life from behind glass, watching someone else's
relationship fracture. The numbness terrified her more than the fear. It made her feel dead inside,
as though the trauma had hollowed her out and left only a functional shell.

What finally brought Catalina to seek help was Sofia's ultimatum, delivered gently but firmly:
either she got treatment, or Sofia would have to leave. Catalina was most afraid that therapy would
force her to remember the parts she had lost, or that it would confirm what she already
believed—that she was too damaged, too weak, too far gone to be loved. But she was also desperate to
feel alive again, to be present with the one person who had stayed.
