# Marcus

Marcus was forty-six when the world narrowed. On a Tuesday evening in March, he had been driving
home from his design firm in Portland, taking the back roads through Gresham as he always did, when
he saw the police cruiser in his mirror. His hands had been steady on the wheel—he had done nothing
wrong—but something in the way the lights came on made his stomach drop. The officer who approached
had been agitated, speaking in clipped sentences, and within moments had opened Marcus's door,
yanked him from the driver's seat, and slammed him against the hood. There had been no warning, no
clear explanation. Marcus's face had been pressed into the metal, his arm twisted behind him, and
for the first time in his life he felt utterly powerless, utterly at the mercy of someone whose
authority was absolute. The officer had discovered nothing—no warrant, no outstanding issue,
nothing—but by then Marcus's nervous system had already learned a terrible lesson. When the officer
finally let him go with a curt apology, Marcus had driven home in silence, his entire body
trembling.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus returned to work, but something had fractured. He had built a
careful life: twenty-two years married to his wife Elena, a respected position designing websites
and branding materials for tech startups, a quiet routine of morning coffee and evening walks. Now,
each time he heard a siren or saw a police car, his chest would tighten and his mind would flood
with the image of that hood, the pressure on his face, the absolute certainty that something
terrible was happening. The sight of a bruise on a colleague's arm, a bandaged hand, even a scraped
knee on a child at the grocery store—any visible injury would trigger the same cascade, his brain
insisting that violence was imminent, that he was in danger. These moments would come without
warning. Marcus could be sitting at his desk, focused on a color palette, when an intrusive thought
would arrive fully formed: the memory of the officer's grip, the smell of the patrol car's interior,
the sensation of his cheekbone against cold metal. His body would respond instantly, heart racing,
muscles coiling as though he needed to run.

What frightened Marcus most was that he had come to believe his life was fundamentally destroyed.
The man who had been tackled on that roadside was not the man he had been before; that version had
been erased. He was certain now that nothing mattered—his work felt hollow, his marriage felt
fragile, his sense of safety had been revealed as an illusion. Nothing he did seemed to change the
fact that the world had shown itself to be unjust and cruel. An officer had assaulted him without
cause, and nothing had come of it—no accountability, no apology that meant anything. The injustice
of it sat in his chest like a stone, and with it came a conviction that his life would never
recover. These dark certainties fed a constant vigilance: Marcus found himself unable to relax even
at home, unable to sit in a restaurant without mentally mapping the exits, unable to feel his body
as anything other than a taut instrument braced against the next blow.

When the memories returned—unbidden, overwhelming—Marcus would feel such a surge of emotion that he
would have to stop whatever he was doing. The sensation was not merely recollection; it was re-
living. He would be back on that roadside, the fear as fresh as it had been that night, and the only
way he knew to survive it was to escape into his phone. He had begun spending hours scrolling
through games, news, anything to keep his mind occupied and away from the memory itself. The
distraction worked, briefly. But it was a cycle: the mental occupation would prevent him from
processing what had happened, so the memory remained sharp and intrusive, waiting to ambush him at
unexpected moments. His phone had become both shield and prison. Elena had started to notice his
absence, the way he would disappear into the screen rather than speak to her, the way he had begun
avoiding friends and colleagues, declining invitations, staying home. The more he isolated himself,
the more his mind would circle back to the trauma, and the more desperately he would reach for the
phone to push it away again.

The constant mental distraction and avoidance had consequences beyond the digital realm. At work,
his creative output had suffered; he had missed deadlines, and his supervisor had begun to question
his reliability. His marriage, once warm and easy, had become strained. Elena would ask him what was
wrong, and he could not explain it clearly—when he tried to tell her about the incident, the words
would scatter, the narrative would break apart, and he would be left stammering, unable to finish,
unable to make her understand why he could not simply move past it. His body remained perpetually on
alert, muscles tight, sleep fragmented. Even when he forced himself to stay away from his phone, the
vigilance would spike; he would scan rooms for threats, identify exits, feel his shoulders creep up
toward his ears. The avoidance was supposed to protect him, but it had only deepened the trap. Every
time he escaped into distraction, every time he avoided a social situation or a police car on the
street, he was reinforcing the message that the world was dangerous and that he could not cope. The
threat felt more real, the memories more vivid, the conviction that his life was over more absolute.

Now, three months later, Marcus sat in the waiting room of a therapist's office, his phone face-down
on his lap—a small victory, though it cost him. He was here because Elena had finally said that she
could not watch him disappear anymore, that something had to change. What Marcus feared most was
that no change was possible, that he would spend the rest of his life trapped in this loop of
vigilance and escape, watching his marriage crumble, his career falter, his sense of self dissolve
into nothing. But beneath that fear, barely audible, was a whisper of hope: that perhaps someone
could help him understand why the world had become so dangerous, and whether it might ever feel safe
again.
