# Clinical Vignette: Aroha

Aroha was thirty years old when she came to her first appointment, referred by her general
practitioner after months of unexplained weight loss and what her husband, Tane, had gently
described as "living on high alert." She worked as a plumber in Auckland, a trade she'd entered at
twenty-two and had grown to love—the problem-solving, the tangible nature of fixing what was broken.
But over the past year, the job had become a minefield of invisible triggers that left her shaking
in her van.

The trauma that now shaped her daily life had begun when she was five years old. Her father, a man
whose rage could ignite without warning, had used his fists and his voice as instruments of control.
The clearest memory Aroha retained was of standing in the kitchen while he shouted, his face
contorted, his hand raised—but the moments immediately after fractured into fragments. She
remembered the sound, remembered the sensation of her body bracing for impact, remembered the taste
of something metallic in her mouth, but the sequence dissolved. Other parts of the event felt as
though they belonged to someone else entirely, a story she'd been told rather than lived. Even now,
decades later, gaps remained where her mind refused to hold the image intact.

When Aroha was called to a job site where a homeowner raised his voice about a delayed repair, her
body didn't register it as a complaint about plumbing—it registered it as danger. The shouting
activated a cascade that began in her chest: her muscles tightened, her breath shortened, and her
mind flooded with fragmented images that felt both vivid and distant, as though she were watching
them through frosted glass. In response, she began to suppress the thoughts entirely, clamping down
on any memory that surfaced, keeping her mind occupied with calculations and task lists. She would
grip the wrench harder, focus obsessively on the pipe in front of her, anything to avoid the feeling
that she was back in that kitchen. But the suppression never fully worked. Each time she pushed the
memory away, it seemed to return with greater force, and the physical vigilance—the constant tension
in her shoulders, the way her body jolted at any sudden movement from a client—only intensified.

The threat response had become self-perpetuating. Her body felt permanently poised for danger,
muscles locked in readiness. When a colleague moved too quickly near her while she was working under
a sink, she flinched violently enough that she struck her head. This hypervigilance fed back into
her attempts to manage her thoughts: she began keeping her mind relentlessly occupied, filling every
moment with distraction so that the memory couldn't surface and the threat wouldn't feel so
immediate. But the more she tried not to think about it, the more fragmented the memory became—parts
crystalline and others entirely absent—and this fragmentation itself became a threat. She felt
unsafe in her own mind, uncertain what she would remember or forget, unable to trust her own account
of what had happened.

Six months ago, she had begun undereating without fully realizing it. Food felt dangerous in a way
she couldn't articulate; the act of nourishing herself felt like an indulgence she didn't deserve,
or perhaps a loss of the control she desperately needed. The restriction gave her a sense of agency,
a way to manage her body when everything else felt uncontrollable. But as her weight dropped and her
energy flagged, her concentration fractured further. At work, she found herself making mistakes,
forgetting measurements, losing track of time. Her body remained in that state of tense readiness,
burning through reserves it didn't have. The undereating also seemed to sharpen her startle
response; she became even more reactive to unexpected movement, even more vulnerable to the triggers
that lurked in her professional life.

When Tane finally insisted she see someone, Aroha was exhausted. She came to the appointment hoping
that someone could help her understand why her own mind and body had become enemies—why the memory
felt like it belonged to a stranger, why she couldn't eat without feeling guilty, why a raised voice
at a job site could leave her trembling in her vehicle for an hour afterward. What frightened her
most was the thought that this state—this constant readiness, this fragmentation, this hunger that
she no longer quite felt—might be permanent, that she would never again feel safe in her own skin.
