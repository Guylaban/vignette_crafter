# Vinh

Vinh was forty-two when his daughter Linh stopped breathing. She was seven. It happened on an
ordinary Tuesday evening in late September, three years ago, when she complained of a headache and a
burning throat. By midnight, her fever had spiked to 104, and her small body had begun to convulse.
Vinh's wife called 911 while he held Linh's rigid frame, watching her eyes roll back, unable to do
anything but feel the heat radiating from her skin and hear the terrible, wet sound of her
struggling for air. At the hospital, the diagnosis came swiftly: bacterial meningitis. For six
hours, while Linh lay sedated in the pediatric ICU, Vinh sat in a vinyl chair outside her room,
watching through the observation window as nurses in full protective gear moved around her small
body—the pale, mottled skin of her arms and legs visible beneath the monitors and IV lines, her
chest rising and falling with the ventilator's rhythm. She survived, eventually. But something in
Vinh did not.

In the months that followed, sleep became impossible. Vinh found that the moment darkness fell, his
mind would flood with fragments of that night—not the whole sequence, but pieces that arrived
without warning. The sight of Linh's arm, limp against the hospital sheet. The sound of the
monitor's beeping. The particular gray-blue color of her fingernails when her oxygen had been
dropping. These fragments would flash across his consciousness like lightning, vivid and immediate,
and he would be back in that room, his daughter dying in front of him, even though he knew
intellectually that she was downstairs in her bedroom, safe and sleeping. The memory did not feel
like something that had happened and passed; it felt like something happening now, in the present
moment, as real as his own breathing.

Vinh began to stay awake. He told himself it was just until he felt calm enough to lie down, but the
hours stretched. He would sit in the living room with the television on low, or work on his laptop,
or walk the perimeter of his small farm in the pre-dawn darkness—anything to keep moving, to keep
the fragments at bay. But the avoidance only seemed to sharpen his vigilance. Every night, as the
sun set and the light drained from the sky, his body would tense. Nighttime itself had become a
trigger. Lying in the dark felt like stepping back into that hospital room, and he could not do it.
When his wife finally insisted he try to sleep, he would lie beside her for a few minutes, feel the
darkness pressing down, and then slip out of bed to sit in the kitchen with all the lights on.

Certain details had become unbearable. When his daughter wore short sleeves, the sight of her small
arms—the same size, the same shape as they had been in the hospital—would send him spiraling. He
would look away, or excuse himself from the room. A song came on the radio one morning, something
that had been playing in the hospital waiting room, and hearing it again made his heart race so
violently that he had to pull his truck over. Now he avoided that song, and then others that sounded
similar, and eventually he found himself avoiding music altogether, keeping the radio off, the house
quiet. These triggers—the sight of her bare skin, the darkness of night, an unexpected melody—would
pull the memory forward again, making it feel present and urgent, and each time the memory surfaced,
he would feel the panic building, would feel the need to do something, anything, to escape the
sensation of being back there.

His avoidance had metastasized into isolation. He began to withdraw from neighbors and friends. He
would not attend his nephew's wedding because it was in the evening, and he could not face the drive
home in darkness. He stopped going to the weekly gatherings at his church. He told himself he was
protecting his family from his own instability, but the truth was more complicated: being around
other people meant being asked questions, meant having to manage his expression, meant being in
situations where he could not control the light or the sounds or the unexpected triggers that might
pull him back into that room. His wife noticed his withdrawal, and it created a distance between
them that had not been there before. His farm work suffered too. During the day, he could manage,
but as harvest season approached and the days grew shorter, he found himself exhausted and unable to
concentrate, his mind caught in a loop of avoidance and dread.

The fragments came more frequently now, and they came in response to his own attempts to avoid them.
The harder he fought against sleep, the more vivid the intrusions became. The longer he stayed
awake, the more fragmented and surreal his thinking grew, and the more desperate he became to find
new ways to escape. He had come to believe that sleep itself was dangerous, that if he closed his
eyes in the dark, something catastrophic would happen—not just to him, but to his daughter, as if
his vigilance was the only thing keeping her alive. Three years into this cycle, Vinh was hollow-
eyed and desperate. He had lost fifteen pounds. His marriage was strained to the breaking point. It
was only when his wife threatened to leave, to take Linh away because she could not watch him
disappear, that he finally made an appointment with a therapist. He sat in the waiting room,
terrified that talking about what had happened would only make it more real, more present. But he
was more terrified of losing his family. He needed to understand why the night would not let him go,
and why his own mind had become his enemy.
