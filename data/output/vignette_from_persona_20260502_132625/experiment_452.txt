# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was twenty-two when the shelling started. He had been working as a freelance journalist in
Eastern Europe, chasing stories that he thought might matter—accounts from ordinary people caught in
the crossfire of a conflict that felt distant to Americans but devastatingly real to those living
through it. On a March morning, he was interviewing a family in their apartment when the first
impact hit the building across the street. The sound was not like anything he had prepared himself
for. It was not a noise so much as a physical force that seemed to unmake the world for a moment—a
concussive bloom that shattered windows, sent plaster dust into the air like a toxic snow, and left
a ringing in his ears that lasted for hours. The family he was with screamed. A child, maybe six
years old, was bleeding from a cut on her forehead. Marcus helped them to the bathroom, the safest
room, and they huddled there for what felt like hours but was probably only twenty minutes before
the bombardment stopped. When he emerged, his hands would not stop shaking.

He stayed in the country for another week, telling himself he was a professional, that this was what
he had come for. But the second time the sirens wailed, he felt something break inside him—a
certainty that this time he would not survive, that the next shell would find him. He left that
night and flew back to the States, arriving in a fog of exhaustion and disbelief at his own fear.

Now, three years later, Marcus works as a salesman for a commercial real estate firm in a mid-sized
city. He is functional, or appears to be. He goes to the office most days, meets with clients,
closes deals. His girlfriend, Sarah, still loves him, though she has learned not to ask him about
his nightmares. But Marcus lives in a state of persistent dread that has become so familiar it feels
like the baseline of his existence. What frightened him most was the randomness of it all—the way
safety could evaporate in an instant, the way a loud noise could unmake everything. He had come to
believe that danger was everywhere, hiding in the ordinary world, waiting. A car backfiring on the
street outside his apartment building sends a spike of terror through his chest. The sound of
construction equipment, the rumble of trucks, even the sharp slam of a door in the office
kitchen—all of these trigger a surge of fear and dread that takes him hours to settle.

At night, the vigilance becomes unbearable. Marcus finds it harder to feel safe after dark, when the
world feels more permeable, more vulnerable. He lies awake beside Sarah, listening to every sound in
the apartment—the hum of the refrigerator, the creak of the building settling, the distant wail of a
siren that could mean anything but which his mind immediately reads as a threat. His concentration
at work has suffered. During client presentations, he loses the thread of his own pitch. Colleagues
have noticed. His manager pulled him aside last month and asked if everything was okay. Marcus said
yes, but the truth is that his mind fractures easily now, scattering whenever there is too much
stimulation or whenever something unexpected happens. He feels strong negative emotions—fear that
coils in his stomach, anger at his own weakness, guilt that he survived when others did not, shame
that he cannot simply move past this. These feelings come without warning and with terrible
intensity.

Sarah has suggested therapy more than once. Marcus has resisted, uncertain what talking could
possibly change. But last week, after a car alarm went off outside their apartment and he found
himself on the floor, heart racing, convinced that something catastrophic was happening, he finally
agreed to call someone. He is terrified of what treatment might require of him, terrified that he
will never feel safe again, but also desperate for some version of his life back—the person he was
before the world revealed itself as fundamentally unsafe.
