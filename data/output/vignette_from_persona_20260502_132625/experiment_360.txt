# Clinical Vignette: Maya

Maya was twenty-one when the truck ran the red light. She had been driving home from her
apprenticeship at Westchester Electric, her supervisor's voice still in her head—*good work today,
you're getting faster*—when the world tilted sideways. The impact came from her driver's side, metal
screaming, her body thrown hard against the restraint. For three seconds she couldn't breathe. The
truck had T-boned her Honda at an intersection she had driven through a hundred times. The other
driver, a man in his sixties, had simply not seen her. He received a citation. His insurance paid.
He walked away with a sore shoulder. Maya walked away with a fractured collarbone, two cracked ribs,
and something less visible but far more persistent: a conviction that the world was fundamentally
unsafe and that she could not trust herself to navigate it.

In the weeks after the collision, Maya tried to return to normal. She finished her apprenticeship on
schedule, one arm still in a sling, learning to work around the pain. But something had shifted in
how she moved through the world. When her coworker Derek offered to drive them both to a job site in
early October, Maya found herself gripping the door handle so tightly her knuckles went white. The
moment Derek merged onto the highway, her chest tightened. Every car around them felt like a
potential threat—a vehicle that might veer into their lane, a driver distracted or reckless like the
man who had hit her. She couldn't stop scanning the traffic, couldn't stop imagining the impact. By
the time they arrived, she was drenched in sweat and barely coherent. She made an excuse and called
for a ride home, alone. Since then, she has refused every offer to ride with anyone. She drives only
when necessary, and only on side streets, and only in daylight.

But the avoidance has its own logic, one that has tightened around her like a noose. When her mother
called last month to ask about Christmas plans—*we're thinking of driving up to Vermont, it would be
so good to have you there*—Maya felt a spike of panic that surprised her with its intensity. The
question itself, the simple invocation of a future that involved travel and time with family,
triggered the same cascade of fear she felt in traffic. What if something happened on the way? What
if she was driving and made a mistake? What if she froze at a critical moment, the way her body had
frozen for those three seconds in the intersection? She had come to believe that she could not trust
her own instincts anymore, that her reflexes were compromised, that the collision had revealed
something true about her: she was not competent enough, not quick enough, not capable of keeping
herself or anyone else safe. The thought of being responsible for her family's safety on a long
drive was unbearable. She told her mother she couldn't go. She didn't explain why.

The injustice of it gnaws at her in quieter moments. She had done nothing wrong that day. She had
been driving legally, paying attention, following the rules. The other driver had broken them, and
yet *she* was the one whose body no longer felt reliable, whose mind now spiraled at the mention of
future plans, whose career—the thing she had been so proud of—was starting to suffer because she
turned down jobs that required travel or team transport. When her supervisor mentioned a residential
wiring job across town that would require carpooling with two other electricians, Maya felt a
familiar sickness rise in her chest. The thought of sitting in a passenger seat, dependent on
someone else's attention and reflexes, felt like handing over control of her life to chance. She
declined the job. She didn't tell him why. What could she say? That she no longer trusted her own
judgment? That the unfairness of what had happened to her had metastasized into a conviction that
she was fundamentally broken? Each refusal, each avoided conversation about the future, each moment
she sat alone in her apartment instead of accepting an invitation, seemed to confirm what she feared
most: that the collision had changed her in ways that couldn't be undone, and that no amount of time
or distance would restore what she had lost.
