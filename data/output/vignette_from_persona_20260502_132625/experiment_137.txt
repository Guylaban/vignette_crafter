# Margaret

Margaret was forty-eight when a man she had never seen before forced his way into her studio
apartment on a Tuesday evening in March. She had been closing up her jeweler's workshop—a small,
light-filled room in a converted warehouse where she had spent the last fifteen years crafting
delicate engagement rings and restoration pieces—when he appeared at the door just as she was
locking up. He said he needed directions. She opened the door. What happened over the next forty
minutes was a blur of violence that her mind had since fragmented into pieces she could not quite
assemble: his hands on her throat, the sound of something breaking, a moment of absolute clarity
when she saw his face inches from hers—that face, with its particular set to the jaw and a small
scar above his left eyebrow—and then nothing, then the feeling of cold floor against her cheek, then
the sound of a siren.

In the months that followed, Margaret rebuilt her life in the smallest possible way. She returned to
work after six weeks, moving through her studio with the mechanical precision of someone performing
a role. The work itself—the meticulous setting of stones, the careful soldering of gold—became
almost meditative, a way to occupy her hands while her mind remained elsewhere. But something
fundamental had shifted. The studio that had once felt like a sanctuary now felt permeable,
vulnerable. She installed new locks, a security camera, motion-sensor lights. She began arriving
earlier and leaving while it was still light outside, though she could not quite articulate why the
darkness felt more dangerous than it had before.

It was a customer's casual remark in October—something about losing her father the previous
year—that first crystallized Margaret's sense of contamination. The woman had spoken with gentle
sadness, but Margaret found herself unable to respond, seized instead by a sudden conviction that
she was fundamentally broken, that her inability to simply console another person proved she was a
lousy coper, incapable of managing even the ordinary human exchange. She felt dead inside, as if the
assault had drained something essential from her that could not be replenished. The contamination
she felt was not merely physical—though she still showered multiple times a day, scrubbing her skin
until it reddened—but moral, a sense that she had been marked by what had happened to her, that she
carried the stain of it into every interaction.

By winter, Margaret had begun to notice something she could not control: whenever she saw a man with
dark hair and a certain build, or when someone approached her too quickly, her body would flood with
the conviction that he was the one, that it was happening again. The fragmented memories of the
assault would surface without warning—a flash of his scarred eyebrow, the pressure of hands, the
smell of his jacket—and then vanish, leaving her breathless and disoriented. These intrusive pieces
seemed to make strangers more threatening. Every man who entered her studio became a potential
danger, and she found herself scanning the space for exits before she even unlocked the door, her
eyes cataloging the distance to the back window, the fire escape, the alley. Her threat detection
had become hypervigilant, a constant low-level scanning that exhausted her. She began to avoid the
studio during certain hours, then certain days. She stopped taking walk-in clients. Her income
declined.

The memory gaps—the moments she could not recall and the moments she actively pushed away before
they could fully surface—seemed to generate new triggers. A flash of a particular type of face would
send her spiraling, uncertain whether she was remembering something real or imagining a threat. The
more she tried to suppress the fragments, the more urgently they seemed to demand her attention,
appearing suddenly in the middle of work or sleep, dragging her back into the conviction that she
was in danger. And when she felt threatened, when her body was flooded with the certainty that a
stranger was dangerous, the memory fragments would intensify, making it harder to distinguish
between what had happened and what might. By spring, Margaret had begun to feel trapped in a cycle
she could not break. She stopped going to her studio altogether, telling clients she was retiring.
She spent most of her time in her apartment, the only place where the scanning and the fear seemed
to quiet. It was her sister who finally insisted she see someone, who said that Margaret was
disappearing, that something had to change. Margaret agreed, though what she feared most was that
the change would only prove what she already believed: that she was fundamentally incapable of
healing, that the contamination was permanent, that she would spend the rest of her life confined to
the shrinking circle of places where she could breathe.
