# Marcus

Marcus was fifty-five when he finally came to see a therapist, though the wounds that brought him
there had been open since childhood. He had spent the first twelve years of his life in a household
where his father's rage was as unpredictable as it was brutal—not the kind of violence that
announced itself, but the kind that lived in silence. A wrong word at dinner, a shirt left on the
floor, the simple fact of existing in his father's line of sight on a bad day: any of these could
trigger a beating that left Marcus gasping, certain he was about to die. What made it worse was the
silence that preceded it—those long, suffocating minutes when his father would go quiet, and Marcus
would know something terrible was coming but not when or how hard. He learned to read the stillness
in a room the way other children learned to read a clock.

By his twenties, Marcus had built a life that seemed to keep those memories at bay. Fashion design
gave him a world of color and texture, of creation and control. But the trauma had never actually
left; it had only learned to hide. In his mid-forties, after his divorce, the symptoms began to
crystallize. Marcus had come to believe that he was fundamentally broken, that something essential
in him had been destroyed by those early years. This conviction had seeped into everything. He was
certain that people could not be trusted—that beneath every friendly gesture lay the potential for
betrayal—and this belief drove him to withdraw from colleagues and friends, to work longer hours
alone in his studio, to ruminate bitterly about perceived slights and old wounds. The more he
dwelled on what others had done to him, the more his negative appraisals about himself deepened,
feeding a vicious spiral: he was damaged, people were dangerous, and his isolation was the only
logical response.

The threat he felt was no longer confined to his father's presence. It had generalized into the
texture of his everyday life. At home in his loft, even with the doors locked, Marcus remained
vigilant, hyperalert to any sound that might signal danger. Unusual silence—the kind that filled his
apartment on quiet evenings—triggered an almost unbearable sense of dread, as though something
catastrophic was about to unfold. When a colleague or friend approached him unexpectedly, even with
good intentions, his body would jolt with alarm before his mind could catch up. These startle
responses terrified him. He had begun to wonder if he was losing his grip on reality, if his mind
was fragmenting. The fear that he was going crazy had become as present as the fear of violence
itself.

His memories of the abuse remained fragmented and emotionally strange. When he tried to piece
together a coherent account of what had happened—to explain it to himself or, more recently, to a
therapist—the narrative dissolved. There were images, sensations, the smell of his father's cologne
mixed with sweat, the sound of a belt being unbuckled, but no continuous story that made sense. More
unsettling still was the way he could sometimes recall specific incidents in vivid detail yet feel
absolutely nothing, as though he were watching a film about someone else's suffering. At other
times, memory brought a crushing wave of guilt—not guilt about anything he had done, but a deeper,
more irrational conviction that he had somehow deserved the violence, that he must have done
something fundamentally wrong to provoke it. This guilt was inseparable from his sense that he was
permanently damaged, that the abuse had rewritten him at some cellular level.

The rumination had become his constant companion. Marcus would lie awake cataloging his
failures—moments he had mishandled at work, relationships he had sabotaged, the ways he had let
people down. He also cycled obsessively through his father's cruelties, replaying scenes, imagining
different outcomes, as though if he could just think about it hard enough, understand it deeply
enough, he could change what had happened or at least make sense of it. But the more he ruminated,
the more fragmented his memories became, and the more he questioned his own perception of reality.
His therapist had gently suggested that his constant self-blame and his dwelling on others'
betrayals were keeping him trapped, that they were preventing his mind from integrating what had
happened into a coherent narrative. Yet stopping felt impossible. The rumination was a kind of
compulsion, a way of trying to regain control in a mind and body that felt fundamentally unsafe.

Marcus had begun avoiding the times of day when nightmares were most likely—the early mornings when
he would wake gasping—by staying up late, working obsessively on designs until exhaustion forced
sleep. But this had left him depleted and even more isolated. He had not been on a date in three
years. His professional relationships, once warm, had cooled to cordial distance. What frightened
him most now was not just the trauma itself, but the possibility that he had become irredeemably
changed by it, that he was incapable of trust or genuine connection, and that his mind was
fragmenting under the weight of all he could not remember and could not forget. He had come to
therapy hoping for some kind of repair, though he was not entirely sure such a thing was possible.
