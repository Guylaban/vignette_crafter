Margot had been a model for forty-three years—runway, print, campaigns for luxury brands that

perfectly still, to project calm even under the glare of studio lights and the crush of backstage
chaos. Her last major contract had wrapped when she was sixty-two, and she had transitioned into
mentoring younger models at an agency in Manhattan, a role that suited her: she could teach them the
mechanics of the walk, the angle of the chin, the discipline required to survive in an industry that
devoured youth. It was steady work, respected work. She had been widowed for eight years—her
husband, David, had died of a sudden cardiac event—and the agency had become her anchor, the place
where she felt purposeful and seen.

On a March afternoon in 2022, during a fitting for a spring campaign shoot, Margot had turned too
quickly while adjusting a model's collar. Her hand caught the young woman's face—a sharp, reflexive
gesture that was meant to be nothing, a mere repositioning, but the model, Aisha, had been standing
on a small platform. The collision had thrown Aisha off balance. She fell backward, her arm twisting
beneath her as she hit the concrete floor of the studio. The sound was definitive: a crack, then
Aisha's scream. Her wrist was fractured in two places. Margot had called the ambulance, had ridden
with Aisha to the hospital, had sat in the waiting room while surgeons discussed pins and plates.
She had apologized so many times that the words had stopped meaning anything. Aisha had been kind
about it—genuinely kind, saying it was an accident, that she knew Margot hadn't meant to hurt
her—but Margot could not accept the kindness. It felt like a mercy she didn't deserve.

The fracture healed. Aisha returned to work within four months. But Margot could not return to the
same ease she had known before. Something had fractured in her as well, something that had not
mended. She found herself unable to relax, even in her apartment on the Upper West Side, even when
she was alone and the doors were locked and there was no one present whom she could hurt. Her body
had become a thing she did not trust. Her muscles stayed clenched, her jaw tight, her hands
trembling slightly when she held a coffee cup. She would sit on her sofa in the evenings and feel
her shoulders hunched up around her ears, her breath shallow, as though her body was bracing for the
moment when she would lose control again and cause irreparable harm. She had come to believe that
she was fundamentally different from other people—that someone else, someone more careful or more
present or simply more good, would never have made such a careless movement. Someone else would have
been aware enough to prevent the fall. The thought circled relentlessly: what kind of person was
she, that she could destroy a young woman's livelihood in a single thoughtless second? The
humiliation of that knowledge—that she had revealed herself to be reckless, dangerous, unfit for the
responsibility she had carried—sat in her chest like a stone. When she looked in the mirror, she saw
a woman she no longer recognized, someone who had proven herself capable of harm. Her inability to
let go of her body's vigilance, to trust that she would not strike out again, only deepened her
conviction that she was someone fundamentally untrustworthy. The more tense she became, the more
certain she felt that her tension was justified—that her body's constant readiness was an accurate
assessment of the threat she posed. She could not forgive herself for not having been more careful
in that moment, for not having anticipated the danger and stopped herself before it was too late.

Within two months, she had taken a leave of absence from the agency. She told the director she
needed time, that she was unwell, but she did not return. She could not face Aisha again, could not
stand in a studio and touch another person's body, could not bear the weight of the responsibility.
She stopped going out, stopped answering calls from colleagues. The concentration that had once
allowed her to command a room had fractured; she would read the same paragraph of a book five times
and retain nothing. At sixty-six, Margot found herself almost entirely alone, unable to work, unable
to relax, unable to forgive the woman in the mirror for the single moment that had unmade her. It
was only last month, when her sister had finally insisted, that she had agreed to see a
therapist—not because she believed anything could repair what she had broken in herself, but because
the alternative was to disappear entirely.
