# Clinical Vignette: Priya

Priya was twenty-eight when it happened. She had owned her sari and fabric shop in Jackson Heights
for three years—a modest storefront she had built from savings and her husband Vikram's support. One
evening in late October, she stayed past closing to inventory new silk shipments. The man had waited
in the alley behind the shop. She had not heard him approach; she was alone, the back door slightly
ajar for air. What followed was a blur of violence and terror that her mind had never quite
assembled into a coherent narrative. She could recall sensations—the cold brick against her skin,
the weight of his body, the way her voice seemed to belong to someone else—but when she tried to
speak about it later, the words scattered. The fragments remained vivid and intrusive, but they
would not cohere into language. She had been unable to give the police a clear description of his
face. What she remembered most was the feeling of being utterly exposed and unable to move, and the
certainty in those moments that she would die.

The assault was never solved. The man was never found. In the months that followed, Priya's world
contracted in ways that seemed, at first, like reasonable caution. But the triggers multiplied. She
began to notice women who resembled the victim profile she had constructed in her mind—South Asian
women with long dark hair, women wearing the colors she had worn that night. When she saw them on
the street or in her shop, something in her chest would tighten. The sound of certain names—names
her assailant might have had, names that resembled his voice in her memory—would send her into a
state of hypervigilance. Worst of all were the moments when someone mentioned an ex-partner, a past
relationship. The word "ex" itself had become a trigger, carrying with it the echo of her own
powerlessness. These triggers pulled her toward avoidance strategies that, over time, had begun to
reshape her life in ways she had not anticipated.

She stopped taking the subway to the shop. The enclosed space, the press of bodies, the inability to
see exits—it all felt unbearable. Instead, she began driving, adding forty minutes to her commute
and an expense she could barely afford. More profoundly, she found herself replaying the night
obsessively, imagining how different everything would be if she had locked the back door, if she had
left earlier, if she had never opened the shop at all. These counterfactual spirals were exhausting,
but she could not stop them. When she was in the shop and someone approached her with an unexpected
request or a slightly aggressive tone, she noticed herself becoming almost unnaturally
compliant—agreeing to refunds she should not have given, offering discounts she could not sustain,
anything to keep the interaction smooth and non-threatening. This fawning response seemed to protect
her in the moment, but it left her depleted and resentful. The avoidance and the rumination had
begun to feed each other. Each time she rehearsed how things might have been different, she felt
pulled back into the memory itself, and the memory came not as words but as a surge of raw
sensation—the terror returning in her body before her mind could contextualize it.

The memory of the assault remained largely wordless, locked in sensation and affect. When she tried
to tell Vikram what had happened—really tell him—she would freeze. The terror on recall was
immediate and overwhelming, as if her nervous system was reliving the violation in real time.
Alongside the terror came a crushing guilt. She had left the back door open. She had stayed late.
She had trusted the world in a way that seemed, in retrospect, impossibly naive. This guilt made the
memory even more unbearable, and so she avoided speaking about it, avoided thinking about it
directly, avoided anything that might trigger its return. Yet the avoidance itself kept her trapped.
By not processing the memory, by not putting it into language, she remained unable to update her
sense of threat. Her nervous system stayed locked in the logic of that October night.

Threat had become the organizing principle of her daily existence. She could not sit in her shop
without positioning herself so her back was against the wall, where she could see the entire room
and any potential approach. Sudden movements by other people—a customer reaching for something on a
shelf, her husband turning quickly in the kitchen—sent her into immediate alert. The flashbacks were
the worst. Without warning, she would find herself back in the alley, her body moving defensively,
her voice emerging in a way that startled both her and whoever was near her. These flashbacks were
not memories in the conventional sense; they were re-experiences, her body and nervous system
convinced that the threat was present and immediate. The threat monitoring had become so constant
that she was exhausted. She had developed a persistent sense that danger was everywhere, that she
had somehow marked herself as vulnerable, that another attack was not a question of if but when.

Priya is now thirty-seven. She has not taken public transport in nine years. Her shop remains
profitable, but she works alone most days because she cannot reliably manage the anxiety of having
employees present. Vikram loves her, but she can feel the distance growing—her inability to be
touched without flinching, her late-night ruminations about how her life would have unfolded without
the assault, her sudden compliance followed by sudden anger. She came to therapy because she
realized she was becoming someone she did not recognize, and because she was terrified that if she
did not find a way to break this cycle, she would lose everything she had fought to build. What
frightened her most was the thought that the assault would continue to own her, that the man who had
hurt her would never be caught but would never need to be—he had already taken so much.
