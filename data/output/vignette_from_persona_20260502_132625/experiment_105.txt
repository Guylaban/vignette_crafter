# Amara's Vigil

Amara was seventy-eight when a man broke into her home in the early evening of a February Tuesday.
She had been in her studio—a converted garage where she had thrown clay for forty-three
years—cleaning her tools when she heard the back door splinter. By the time she turned, he was
already inside, demanding her purse, his hand raised in a way that made her certain he would strike.
She remembered his face with terrible clarity: the specific angle of his jaw, the color of his
shirt, the exact texture of rage in his eyes. The police found him three blocks away. He was
arrested. He was convicted. And yet, nearly two years later, Amara lived as though he might return
at any moment.

The assault itself had lasted perhaps four minutes. What followed was far longer. In the first
weeks, Amara's hands shook so badly that she could not center clay on the wheel. She had tried to
return to her studio—her sanctuary, the place where her hands had always known what to do—but the
moment she crossed the threshold, her heart would race as though the door were splintering again.
She stopped going. Her pottery business, which had sustained her since her husband Thomas died eight
years earlier, simply ceased. The studio gathered dust. Orders went unfulfilled. Galleries that had
shown her work for decades began to call less frequently, and then not at all.

What frightened Amara most was not the memory itself, though the memory was vivid and intrusive—a
flash of his face, unbidden, while she was making tea or sitting in church. Rather, it was her
conviction that if she allowed herself to think about what had happened, truly think about it, she
would shatter. She had come to believe that the world was a fundamentally dangerous place, that
safety was an illusion, that her own body had become a traitor, registering threat even when she sat
alone in her locked bedroom in broad daylight. Her heart would race without warning. Her shoulders
would tense. She would feel as though danger was imminent, even though the rational part of her mind
knew she was secure. This sense that her body could not be trusted, that it was working against her,
made her certain she could not afford to dwell on the trauma. If she let the memory in fully, if she
stopped fighting it, she would lose control entirely—and that loss of control felt more dangerous
than the man himself.

So Amara had built a fortress of small, repeated actions. She checked the locks on her front door,
back door, and bedroom door before bed—sometimes five times, sometimes more, until the checking
itself became a ritual she could not stop. She checked the mirrors in her bathroom and bedroom,
searching for something she could not name, as if the reflection might tell her she was still
intact. She listened for sounds in the night, waking at the smallest noise—the house settling, a car
passing—convinced that someone was trying to enter. She could not relax, could not sit without her
muscles coiled, could not watch television without muting it periodically to listen for sounds that
might mean someone was entering her home. The checking and listening were meant to protect her, but
they only reinforced her deepest conviction: that the world was dangerous, that she could not trust
her own perception of safety, that constant vigilance was the only thing standing between her and
catastrophe.

The memories themselves arrived unbidden and vivid—not as narrative but as images, as sensations, as
the feeling of his presence in the room. When they came, they came with the full force of the
original moment, and she would find herself gasping, her hands gripping whatever was nearest. These
intrusions terrified her because they seemed to prove what she already believed: that she could not
control her mind, that the trauma was still happening, that she was still in danger. And so she
tried harder not to think about it, which only made the memories more insistent, more vivid, more
forceful in their arrival. Each time they broke through her attempts at suppression, she felt more
certain that thinking about the event would destroy her. The cycle tightened.

By the time Amara called a therapist, she had not left her house in six months except for medical
appointments. She had lost weight. Her daughter, who lived two hours away, was increasingly worried.
What Amara wanted most was to feel safe in her own home again, to return to her clay, to be the
woman she had been. What she feared most was that she had changed in some permanent, irreversible
way—that her body and mind had been rewired by those four minutes, and that no amount of checking
locks or listening in the dark would ever truly quiet the alarm that had become her constant
companion.
