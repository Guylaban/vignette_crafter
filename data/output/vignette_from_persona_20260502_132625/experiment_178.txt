# Arun

Arun was twenty-three when Marcus moved in. They had been together for eight months, and everything
felt inevitable—the kind of love that seemed to arrive fully formed, urgent and consuming. Marcus
was charming in public, attentive in ways that made Arun feel seen after years of feeling invisible.
But within weeks of cohabitation, the texture of their intimacy changed. Marcus's moods became
unpredictable. A misplaced comment about Arun's work would trigger accusations of coldness; a
forgotten dinner reservation would escalate into something uglier. By the fourth month, when Arun
came home late from an assignment, Marcus was waiting. He grabbed Arun's wrist, twisted it hard
enough to leave bruises that bloomed purple across his inner forearm, and slammed him against the
kitchen counter. The impact knocked the wind from his lungs. When Arun tried to pull away, Marcus
shoved him again, his face contorted with a rage that seemed to come from nowhere and everywhere at
once. The violence lasted perhaps three minutes—though Arun would never be certain of the duration,
time having fractured into something unmeasurable. What remained most vivid was the sensation of his
ribs hitting the edge of the counter, a sharp, electric pain that radiated through his chest and
down his left side.

Arun left Marcus two weeks later, but leaving did not mean healing. Now twenty-seven, he found
himself trapped in a landscape of vigilance that had calcified over four years into something that
felt less like a response and more like his baseline existence. The memory of that evening had not
faded; it had only become more insistent, more present. When he sat at his desk at the newspaper
office or rode the bus to assignments across the city, the memory would arrive unbidden—not as
something he could observe from a distance but as something happening *now*, his body re-
experiencing the impact, his chest tightening as though the counter were still there. The overwhelm
that came with these intrusions was total; his eyes would blur, his breathing would shallow, and he
would have to excuse himself to the bathroom or step outside, unable to continue working. The pain
was real too—a phantom ache that traced the exact path of old bruising, a bodily reminder that the
trauma lived in his tissue.

What terrified Arun most was his absolute certainty that he could never know who would hurt him.
People wore masks; kindness was a currency that could be spent and withdrawn without warning. He had
come to believe that he was somehow inadequate, that something about him invited the violence, that
if he had been different—more careful, more attuned, less needy—Marcus would not have broken him.
This conviction fed everything that followed. Because he believed himself fundamentally unsafe in
the presence of others, being alone with men became intolerable. A colleague would ask to grab
lunch, and Arun would find an excuse. He stopped attending team meetings where he might be trapped
in a conference room with his male editor. The bus rides to assignments became their own ordeal;
enclosed spaces with strangers, any one of whom might carry the same capacity for sudden violence.
The memory of trauma-related events—a raised voice, a man's shadow falling across his desk—would
trigger the memory itself, and the memory would confirm his deepest fear: that trust was a luxury he
could not afford, that people could not be trusted, that harm was always imminent.

To manage the chaos, Arun developed a architecture of control. He began to restrict his eating,
finding that hunger gave him something concrete to focus on, a pain he could quantify and manage in
ways the emotional pain refused. He never allowed himself to feel anger—not at Marcus, not at
himself, not at the men who now triggered his fear simply by existing in his space. Anger felt
dangerous, a force that might explode outward and confirm his own inadequacy. Instead, he ruminated
endlessly on what he had done wrong, turning the violence inward, blaming himself for the choices
that had led him to that kitchen, that counter, that moment. At night, he would wake in the dark,
his heart racing, compelled to check the locks, to verify that he was alone and safe—a verification
that never quite convinced him. When delays occurred—a late bus, a meeting that ran over—his mind
would immediately spiral into catastrophe, certain that something bad was happening, that the
world's chaos was closing in. The suppression of anger fed his sense of inadequacy; the rumination
deepened his mistrust; the undereating left him depleted, his body weakened, his capacity for
resilience eroded. Each strategy was meant to protect him, but together they formed a tightening
knot that made him smaller, more isolated, more convinced that the world was fundamentally dangerous
and that he was fundamentally broken.

His relationship with his current partner, Kenji, had begun to fracture under the weight of these
patterns. Kenji wanted closeness; Arun could only offer distance and the constant, exhausting work
of managing his own terror. It was Kenji who finally insisted that Arun seek help, not out of
frustration but out of love—a love that Arun found almost impossible to believe in. Sitting in the
therapist's office, Arun was most afraid that nothing would change, that he would always be this
way: hypervigilant, starving, angry only at himself, unable to trust that safety was possible. But
beneath that fear was a quieter, more fragile hope—that the memory might someday feel like something
that happened, rather than something that is happening still.
