# Marcus

Marcus was nine years old when his stepfather's belt came off. He remembers the sound first—the
leather sliding through the loops with a whisper that still lives in his nervous system—and then the
weight of it, the particular roughness of that worn fabric against his skin as the man swung it in
the dark hallway outside his bedroom. Marcus had woken to shouting, to the smell of alcohol, to the
certainty that he had done something wrong, though he could never quite remember what. The blows
came in a rhythm that felt endless. His stepfather's voice rose and fell like a tide. When it was
over, Marcus lay on the cold floor in the dark, his body trembling, the texture of that belt still
burning against his ribs and thighs. The darkness had become a weapon.

Now, at twenty-five, Marcus works as a housekeeper at a mid-sized hotel in Portland, a job that
keeps him moving through other people's spaces during daylight hours. He is reliably competent—his
supervisor notes he is thorough, if quiet—but something has shifted in recent months that no one
quite names. When Marcus clocks out at five o'clock, the anxiety begins to climb. Nighttime feels
like a trap closing. The dark of evening, the dark of his apartment, the dark of anywhere indoors
once the sun sets, triggers a cascade that he has learned to recognize but cannot quite stop. The
moment darkness falls, his body floods with the same dread it knew at nine: the sense that something
terrible is about to happen, that he is unsafe, that the walls themselves have become dangerous.
This is why he has started staying awake into the early morning hours, why he sits in the brightness
of diners or twenty-four-hour laundromats, why he has called in sick to shifts that would require
him to work the evening turnover. The avoidance has become automatic. When dusk approaches, Marcus
simply cannot bring himself to go home.

But staying awake has its own cost. The exhaustion hollows him out, and in that hollow space, the
memories return with a vividness that terrifies him. When he closes his eyes—even for a moment—the
film plays: the hallway, the sound, the belt, the taste of fear in his mouth. It feels completely
real, as though it is happening again, his body seized by the same panic it knew two decades ago.
The memory comes not as a story he tells himself but as a sensory storm—the texture of the belt
against his skin, the darkness pressing in, the helplessness. When the memory returns, he feels so
overwhelmed by the emotion that he has to stop whatever he is doing. He has called his sister from
the parking lot at work, unable to speak, unable to explain. He has sat on the bathroom floor of his
apartment for an hour, simply breathing, waiting for his body to believe it is safe. These episodes
are so consuming that Marcus has begun to withdraw from the few relationships he has. He has started
making excuses not to see his sister, not to attend his cousin's birthday dinner. The safer choice,
he has come to believe, is to be alone—not because he wants solitude, but because he cannot bear the
thought of being vulnerable in front of another person. He is certain that if anyone really looked
at him, they would see what happened, would see the damage written across him like a scar. He feels
like an object, something broken and marked, not like a person anymore.

This sense of being exposed, of carrying his trauma visibly, feeds back into his vigilance. When
Marcus is around other people—his coworkers, his neighbors, anyone—he monitors their faces for signs
of judgment or disgust. He watches for the moment when they might see him as he sees himself. A
child crying in a hallway at the hotel triggers the same fear his stepfather's voice once did; it is
not just the sound but what it means, the reminder that harm happens in the dark, in enclosed
spaces, behind closed doors where no one can help. He avoids the residential floors when possible,
takes a different route through the service corridors. He has become so consumed by the need to
prevent harm—to himself, to others—that he is slowly withdrawing from his job, from his life. His
supervisor has begun to ask questions. Marcus knows he cannot keep this up. The avoidance that once
felt like survival is now suffocating him. He came to his first therapy appointment exhausted,
afraid, and desperate for the nighttime to stop being a place where he has to choose between
reliving trauma and destroying his own rest.
