# Karim's Story

Karim was fifty-four when he came to the clinic, and he moved through the waiting room with the
careful deliberation of someone perpetually braced for threat. His therapist noticed immediately how
he positioned himself—back to the wall, eyes tracking the door—and how his fingers worked the fabric
of his shirt collar, a gesture that would repeat throughout their first session. He wore a heavy
sweater despite the mild autumn weather, buttoned to the neck, and loose dark trousers. These were
clothes he had come to depend on, clothes that made him feel less visible, less exposed. When asked
about them, Karim explained simply that he preferred not to be noticed.

Three years earlier, Karim's husband of eighteen years, Marcus, had attacked him during an argument
in their apartment in the northwest corner of the city. What had begun as raised voices escalated
with terrifying speed. Marcus had struck him repeatedly, driven by rage that Karim still could not
fully explain or reconstruct. The beating had taken place in their bedroom after dark, in the
confusion of shadows and lamplight, and Karim remembered fragments more than a coherent sequence:
the sound of Marcus's breathing, ragged and furious; the dull thud of impact; his own voice, but not
what he had been saying. He remembered the darkness of the room, how it seemed to swallow clarity,
and he remembered that Marcus had said nothing—just the animal sounds of exertion, the whistle of
air through clenched teeth.

In the months that followed, Karim had tried to make sense of it. He had come to believe that he
must have provoked it, that something about who he was—his passivity, his tendency to withdraw when
conflict arose, some fundamental weakness—had invited the violence. Marcus had apologized afterward,
had wept, had promised it would never happen again. But Karim could not unhear what he had heard
that night. Even now, in daylight, in safe spaces, a sudden sound—the rumble of a helicopter passing
overhead, a door slamming in an adjacent room—could transport him entirely back into that bedroom.
The memory would arrive not as a narrative but as disconnected sensory shards: the sound of
breathing, the specific quality of darkness, the feeling of his own body rigid and small. These
intrusions came without warning, triggered by something as innocent as the dimming of light as
evening fell, or the spatial configuration of a room that reminded him of the apartment's layout.

As his work as a community organizer took him into neighborhood meetings and public spaces, Karim
found himself increasingly vigilant. He scanned rooms for exits and dangerous people, reading threat
into ordinary social interactions. He had come to believe that people could sense his weakness, that
his vulnerability was visible to anyone who looked closely enough. He began to avoid the northwest
section of the city entirely, taking circuitous routes to meetings, adding forty minutes to his
commute rather than pass near the apartment where the attack had occurred. His colleagues noticed
his absences from certain neighborhood events, his reluctance to work in particular districts. When
they asked, he offered vague excuses. He could not articulate that the very geography of the city
had become a landscape of danger, that certain locations carried the weight of that night.

The fragmented memories—arriving unbidden, incomplete, sensory rather than coherent—reinforced his
conviction that something was fundamentally broken in him. A normal person would have fought back,
would remember clearly, would have left immediately. Instead, Karim remained trapped in disconnected
pieces of experience that his mind could not organize into a coherent story. This fragmentation
seemed to confirm his deepest fear: that he was not strong enough, not whole enough, to deserve what
had happened to him. And as this belief solidified, his hypervigilance intensified. He found himself
scanning every person he encountered for signs of violence, interpreting neutral expressions as
contempt. The more he watched for danger, the more the world seemed to confirm that danger was
everywhere. He wore his concealing clothes not merely out of a desire to be invisible but because
being unseen felt like the only viable form of safety. If no one noticed him, perhaps no one would
hurt him.

His marriage had collapsed within two years. Marcus had moved out, but Karim remained in a state of
perpetual readiness, unable to trust his own judgment about people or situations. He had taken
medical leave from his job three months ago, unable to manage the constant state of alert that
community organizing now required. When he finally called the clinic, it was because his wife—they
had reconciled briefly, then separated again—had told him that she could no longer live with a man
who flinched at shadows and would not leave the house after dark. What terrified Karim most was not
the possibility of further violence, but the creeping certainty that he had somehow deserved what
had happened, and that this truth about himself would eventually be visible to everyone around him.
