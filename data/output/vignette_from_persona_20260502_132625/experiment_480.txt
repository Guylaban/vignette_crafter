# Rajesh

Rajesh was thirty-four when the moment that would unmake his sense of himself occurred on a Tuesday
afternoon in late September. He had been driving for Apex Logistics for six years, a reliable
operator with a clean record and the kind of quiet competence that earned him steady routes through
the suburbs of Phoenix. On that particular day, he was making a delivery to a residential
neighborhood, navigating a street he had driven a hundred times before. The sun was bright, the road
was clear, and Rajesh was thinking about his wife Priya's suggestion that they finally book the trip
to visit his parents in Gujarat—a conversation they'd been postponing for months. He turned onto
Maple Street at the usual speed, glancing in his mirror as he always did, and that was when he saw
the boy.

The child, no more than seven or eight, had darted from between two parked cars. Rajesh's foot found
the brake almost before his mind registered what was happening, but the truck's momentum carried him
forward. He heard the sickening impact, the sound of breaking glass from the windshield, the screams
from the sidewalk. The boy—whose name he would later learn was Marcus—lay beneath the front wheel,
his leg bent at an angle that made Rajesh's stomach turn. The paramedics arrived within minutes.
Marcus survived, but he would need surgery, months of rehabilitation, and would walk with a limp for
the rest of his childhood. Rajesh had not been speeding. The police report confirmed he was within
the limit. None of that mattered.

In the weeks that followed, Rajesh replayed those seconds countless times, each replay a fresh
accusation against himself. He had come to believe, with a certainty that felt immovable, that he
should have been more vigilant, that a more careful driver would have anticipated the danger even
though the boy had emerged from a blind spot. He tormented himself with the thought that he could
have done more in those fractions of a second—braked harder, swerved differently, somehow prevented
what happened. The guilt was not a feeling that came and went; it was a permanent fixture in his
chest, a weight that made it difficult to breathe. What frightened Rajesh most was the conviction
that he would never forgive himself for not doing more to stop what happened, that the failure to
protect Marcus would define him forever. He had failed in the most fundamental way—as a responsible
person, as someone who should have known better, as a man who was supposed to keep others safe.

His marriage began to fracture under the strain. Priya would find him in the garage at two in the
morning, sitting in the cab of his truck with the engine off, staring at nothing. She tried to tell
him that it was an accident, that he was not responsible, but her reassurances only deepened his
conviction that she did not understand the magnitude of what he had done. Rajesh believed the event
had happened because of the way he had acted—his decision to turn onto Maple Street, his speed, his
lack of absolute vigilance. He had made choices, and those choices had consequences that a child
would carry for life. Priya eventually stopped trying to comfort him and began sleeping in the guest
room, exhausted by the weight of his self-condemnation.

Four months after the accident, Rajesh finally came to his first therapy appointment, referred by
his family doctor who had noticed his weight loss and the tremor in his hands. He sat in the chair
with his hands clasped so tightly his knuckles were white, unable to make eye contact. When asked
why he was there, he said only, "I need to understand how I'm supposed to live with what I've done."
His voice was hollow, and in it was the sound of a man who had concluded that the answer might be
that he was not.
