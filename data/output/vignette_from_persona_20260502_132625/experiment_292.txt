# Anjali

Anjali was twenty-three when the police stopped her. She had been walking home from the community
health center where she volunteered, cutting through the park as she always did on Thursday
evenings, when two officers approached her. They said someone matching her description had been
involved in a shoplifting incident three blocks away. She was certain there had been a mistake—she
had been at the center all afternoon—but when she tried to explain, one of the officers grabbed her
arm and twisted it behind her back. The other pushed her face-first against a tree. The rough bark
scraped her cheek. She could smell the earth, the wet soil from recent rain, and something metallic
in the air. She kept saying she hadn't done anything, but her voice sounded small and far away. They
held her there for what felt like hours but was probably ten minutes, patting her down, asking
aggressive questions, their radios crackling with static. When they finally let her go—no apology,
no acknowledgment of the mistake—she walked the rest of the way home in a state of numb shock, her
arm aching, her face burning.

Two years had passed since that evening, and Anjali was now working as a counselor at a mental
health clinic, helping other people manage their trauma. Her colleagues found her competent and
compassionate. Her clients trusted her. But beneath the professional composure, Anjali was caught in
a cycle she could not seem to break. The memory of that encounter had crystallized into something
that felt perpetually present, always threatening to flood back. When she rode the bus to work—and
she had to, because driving through the city made her hypervigilant in a different way—she would sit
rigidly, her eyes scanning the other passengers' faces and body language, searching for signs of
aggression or judgment. Every uniform in her peripheral vision, every person in authority nearby,
sent a spike of adrenaline through her chest before her conscious mind had even registered what she
was seeing. Her body knew danger before she did. On bad mornings, when she spotted a police officer
at a transit stop, the memory would come flooding back with such intensity that she could feel the
bark scraping her cheek again, smell the soil, feel her arm being wrenched. The sensations were so
vivid and complete that for a moment she was no longer in the present at all. In response, she would
pull her dupatta up to cover her face, keeping her head down and her eyes averted until the officer
passed. She had begun checking mirrors obsessively—first in the bathroom at home, then on the bus,
then in store windows as she walked—as though she needed to reassure herself that her face was still
her own, that she hadn't been marked or changed by what had happened.

What frightened her most was the conviction that had taken root in her mind: that people were
fundamentally not what they seemed, that authority could turn on you without warning, that the world
was a place where violence could erupt from ordinary circumstances. This belief had metastasized
into a sense that she herself had been contaminated by the encounter, that something about her was
now dirty or damaged in a way others could sense. She had stopped attending the community gatherings
she had once loved—the Diwali celebrations, the cooking circles with her aunties, the temple events
where she had felt most at home. The thought of seeing people who had known her before the incident
made her stomach clench; she imagined them looking at her differently, seeing something broken in
her. Her family had noticed her withdrawal and worried, but when they asked, she found herself
unable to explain. How could she tell them that she felt she had lost her place in the community,
that the spaces that had once held her felt unsafe now? Instead, she made excuses. She was busy with
work. She needed to rest. The more she avoided those connections, the more isolated she became, and
the more her conviction deepened that she no longer belonged anywhere.

The avoidance had consequences she hadn't anticipated. By cutting herself off from her community,
she had removed one of the few contexts in which she felt genuinely safe and seen. This isolation
fed back into her threat monitoring—with fewer trusted people around her, every stranger on the
street felt more dangerous, every public space more hostile. The checking behaviors had intensified.
She would spend fifteen minutes in front of the mirror before leaving her apartment, examining her
face as though searching for evidence of the trauma written there. On the bus, she would glance at
the mirror mounted above the driver, then at the reflections in the windows, her eyes never still.
These rituals gave her a temporary sense of control, but they also kept her locked in a state of
hyperawareness, unable to relax or simply exist in public space. When she did manage to ride the bus
without covering her face, the anxiety would build until she felt she couldn't breathe, and she
would resort to the hiding again. Some nights, she couldn't fall asleep at all, her mind cycling
through scenarios of what might happen if she let her guard down, her body tense and ready for a
threat that never came.

Anjali had come to therapy because her supervisor had gently suggested it after noticing that she
had become withdrawn with colleagues and occasionally seemed to zone out during staff meetings. She
told the therapist that she didn't understand why she couldn't just move on, why a ten-minute
encounter two years ago still had such a grip on her life. What she was most afraid of was that this
was permanent—that she would always be this person, scanning faces, avoiding her own community,
unable to trust that the world was safe. What she hoped for, though she hardly dared to say it
aloud, was to feel like herself again, to walk through the city without her body being in a constant
state of alarm, to sit with her family without feeling like an outsider in her own culture.
