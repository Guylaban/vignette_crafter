fuselage shuddered and she heard the shriek of metal and felt the sudden, absolute helplessness of
having no control—no stick movement, no throttle adjustment, no skill that could save them—something
in Kathryn's sense of the world fractured irreparably. The plane landed hard, skidded, came to rest.
Everyone walked away. Kathryn, however, did not walk away whole.

For the first weeks after the incident, she told herself it was nothing—a close call, the kind of
thing that happened to pilots who worked in hostile territory. She returned to flight status. But
within a month, she began to notice that her body no longer trusted her mind's reassurances. Loud
noises—a car backfiring, a door slamming in the barracks—would send her heart into an arrhythmic
sprint, her hands trembling on whatever she was holding. Crowded spaces, which she had never minded
before, began to feel suffocating; she would find herself scanning the edges of a room, counting
exits, positioning herself with her back to a wall. When she felt even slightly unwell—a headache, a
bout of nausea—her thoughts would spiral into catastrophe: her body was failing, something was wrong
that she couldn't fix, and the world was fundamentally unsafe in ways she could no longer control.
These moments of physical discomfort seemed to unlock a cascade of conviction that she was helpless,
that bad things happened regardless of her preparation or vigilance, that the illusion of control
she had built her entire adult life around was exactly that—an illusion.

Kathryn began to avoid enclosed spaces. The cockpit, which had once been her sanctuary, became a
cage. She requested a desk posting, telling her commanding officer she needed a break from active
duty. What she didn't say was that the thought of being strapped into a confined space while loud
noises erupted around her—the very conditions of her trauma—made her chest tighten with a panic that
no amount of tactical training could override. She started smoking, a habit she'd quit fifteen years

of defeat, a moment when she felt her coping abilities slipping, which in turn reinforced her belief
that she couldn't handle even minor stress. When she tried to quit again, the anxiety that rose in
response—the sense that she was losing her one reliable tool for managing the constant low-level
dread—drove her back to the pack within days.

slept poorly, if at all, her mind cycling through scenarios of threat, her body never quite
believing that the next loud noise wouldn't be an explosion. She found herself checking behind her
when she walked, scanning crowds, noting the positions of people around her. These behaviors felt
rational—they were how you stayed alive—but they also fed a deepening conviction that the world was
genuinely dangerous, that her vigilance was the only thing standing between herself and catastrophe.
The hypervigilance itself became a trigger: the act of scanning, of noticing threat, would spike her
sense of danger, which would reinforce her belief that she was right to be afraid, that her
helplessness in the face of a world this hostile was not a distortion but a realistic assessment.

Now, at forty-five, Kathryn has taken medical leave from the Air Force. She lives alone in an
apartment she chose specifically because it has multiple exits and is on the ground floor. She
avoids the mall, avoids restaurants during peak hours, avoids anywhere that feels enclosed or
crowded. She smokes a pack a day. She sleeps four, sometimes three hours a night. When she catches
herself thinking that things could have been worse—that at least no one died, that she should be
grateful—she feels a thin, brittle relief, but it never lasts. What brought her to seek help now,
after nearly seven years of managing alone, was a routine physical exam. The doctor heard a murmur,
ordered an echocardiogram, and Kathryn spent three days in a state of escalating panic, convinced
her heart was failing, that her body had finally betrayed her completely. She couldn't sit still in
the waiting room; the fluorescent lights and the press of other patients triggered a suffocating
sense of being trapped. When she got the results—everything normal—the relief lasted perhaps an
hour. By evening, she was certain the doctors had missed something. What frightened her most now was
not the trauma itself, but the recognition that her mind no longer obeyed her commands, and that
nothing she could do—no amount of vigilance, no strategy, no escape—seemed capable of restoring the
sense of safety she had lost on that runway seven years ago.

the waiting room; the fluorescent lights and the press of other patients triggered a suffocating

hour. By evening, she was certain the doctors had missed something. What frightened her most now was
not the trauma itself, but the recognition that her mind no longer obeyed her commands, and that
nothing she could do—no amount of vigilance, no strategy, no escape—seemed capable of restoring the
sense of safety she had lost on that runway seven years ago.
