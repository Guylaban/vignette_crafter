# Aroha

Aroha was fifty-three when her husband Manu died in a car accident on a Tuesday afternoon. He had
been driving home from the hardware supplier, the same route he had taken every week for thirty
years. A truck ran a red light at the intersection near the railway bridge. Manu died at the scene.
For three days afterward, Aroha could not form a coherent account of what had happened—the facts
existed in fragments: the phone call from the hospital, the fluorescent waiting room, the officer's
careful words, the smell of stale coffee and something else, something chemical that would later
remind her of alcohol. She could not tell herself a clear story of those hours. What remained
instead was a sensory film that played without her consent: the sound of the phone ringing, the
metallic taste of fear, the terrible silence when the officer finished speaking. The memory did not
feel like something that had happened; it felt like something that was happening, again and again.

In the weeks after the funeral—the grey afternoon at the cemetery, the smell of wet earth and
flowers, the faces of people she could not quite see—Aroha began to notice that certain moments
would trigger the memory with violent force. Talk of loss, especially from customers or friends
sharing their own griefs, would suddenly pull her back into that hospital corridor. The smell of
alcohol, which she encountered often in her small bottle shop in the central business district,
would arrive without warning and send her body into a state of emergency: her heart accelerating,
her breath shallow, her hands shaking as she gripped the counter. These triggers did not feel like
reminders; they felt like proof that danger was still present, still real, still capable of taking
everything from her. When a customer mentioned losing his father, or when she caught the sharp scent
of whisky from an open bottle, Aroha's body would react as though the accident itself were unfolding
again—her muscles tensing, her mind flooded with the film of that day, and a certainty settling into
her bones that something catastrophic was about to happen.

The flashbacks became relentless. The memory would force its way into her mind without permission,
playing like a film she could not stop: the moment before, the moment of, the moment after. She
would be standing in her shop, restocking shelves, and suddenly she was in the hospital. She would
be serving a customer, and the smell of alcohol would arrive and she would feel as if she were in
danger—not metaphorically, but actually in danger, her body convinced of a threat that did not
exist. She knew, rationally, that she was safe in her shop, that nothing was wrong, but her body
refused to believe her. She could not relax even when she locked the doors at night, even when she
sat in her own home. The false alarms came constantly, her nervous system trapped in a state of
perpetual vigilance.

Over the months, as the memory replayed and the triggers accumulated, Aroha's sense of herself began
to dissolve. She had come to believe that she did not know who she was anymore—the woman who had run
a thriving shop, who had laughed easily, who had planned for retirement with Manu, that woman was
gone. When the flashbacks hit and her body reacted with terror to nothing, she felt evidence that
her mind was broken, that her life had been destroyed and could not be repaired. This hopelessness
fed the threat response; convinced that her future held only more of this—more triggers, more false
alarms, more memories she could not control—she found herself hypervigilant, scanning constantly for
the next threat, the next reminder, the next moment when her body would betray her. The negative
appraisals intensified the memory's grip; the more she believed her life was destroyed, the more the
film played, and the more it played, the more she was certain that she had lost herself entirely.
Her identity loss made her acutely aware of every trigger in her environment—the funeral
announcements in the local paper, the conversations about death, the smell of alcohol in her own
shop—each one a confirmation that the world was dangerous and that she was no longer the person who
could navigate it.

Three months ago, Aroha began to avoid her own business. She hired a manager and stopped coming in
on weekends, then weekdays, then at all. She stopped attending social gatherings where grief might
be mentioned. She asked friends not to visit. But the isolation only deepened the sense that her
life was over, that there was nothing left to do but endure. Last week, a regular customer came in
and mentioned his daughter's loss. Aroha heard about it secondhand from her manager. She sat in her
home that evening, feeling the memory stir, feeling the false alarm of danger, feeling the certainty
that she had no future. It was that convergence—the trigger, the threat, the hopelessness, the
uncontrollable film—that finally brought her to seek help. She was terrified that nothing would
change, that she would remain trapped in this fractured state forever, but she was more terrified of
the alternative: continuing to hide from a world that had already taken everything.
