# Sister Margaret's Vigil

Sister Margaret was fifty-five years old and had spent thirty-two of those years in the convent of
the Sisters of Divine Mercy, a small community in rural Pennsylvania where she served as the
infirmarian—the nurse who tended to the aging sisters in their final years. It was work that had
always felt like a calling, a way of honoring the vow she had made to serve the vulnerable. Her
younger sister, Claire, had never understood the choice. Claire lived two hours away in Pittsburgh
with her husband and two daughters, and though the sisters saw each other on holidays, there had
always been a gentle distance between them, rooted in Claire's quiet skepticism about Margaret's
faith and Margaret's worry that Claire's secular life was spiritually adrift. Still, they loved each
other, and Margaret prayed for her sister every evening.

On a Tuesday in March, Claire was driving home from her job as a high school counselor when a
delivery truck ran a red light at an intersection. The collision happened at the exact moment
Margaret was leading the afternoon rosary in the chapel. Claire died before the ambulance arrived,
and Margaret's life fractured in that instant, though she would not know it for another three hours,
when the hospital called.

What haunted Margaret most was not the moment of impact—she had not witnessed that—but the drive to
Pittsburgh afterward, the long highway stretching before her as she gripped the steering wheel, the
certainty forming in her mind that if she had only called Claire that morning, if she had suggested
they have lunch, if she had been more present in her sister's life instead of cloistered behind
convent walls, Claire would have left work five minutes earlier or five minutes later and the truck
would have missed her. Margaret had come to believe, with a certainty that felt like stone in her
chest, that her own distance, her own choice to withdraw from the world into prayer, had somehow
allowed this tragedy to happen. She should have protected Claire. She should have been there.

In the weeks after the funeral, Margaret found herself unable to enter the infirmary wing where she
had spent most of her days. The sight of the hallway where she usually worked, the particular angle
of afternoon light through those windows, would trigger a flood of sensory detail so vivid and
sudden that she would have to grip the wall—the smell of Claire's perfume, the sound of the delivery
truck's horn, the feel of her own hands shaking on the steering wheel. Some moments of that day were
crystalline: she could see Claire's face in the casket, could feel the weight of the rosary beads in
her hands during the service. But other stretches of time had simply vanished—entire conversations
with relatives, the drive home from the hospital, whole hours she could not account for. The
fragmentation itself terrified her, as though her mind was punishing her by withholding pieces of
the truth.

Because Margaret could not rely on herself to have prevented the tragedy, and because she was
certain her own failings had contributed to Claire's death, she began to construct a system of small
rituals to prevent further loss. She would count her prayers in specific patterns, believing that if
she reached certain numbers, if she said certain words in the right order, God might spare the
remaining sisters in her care. When she thought of Claire—which was constantly, unbidden—she would
perform these mental sequences, each one a desperate bargain: if I do this correctly, no one else
will die. She avoided the infirmary entirely, asking to be reassigned to kitchen duties, though this
meant abandoning the work that had defined her vocation. She could not bear to see the empty beds or
hear the sound of the call bell that Claire, in those final visits, had learned to use.

The avoidance brought a temporary relief, a sense that she was at least doing something, taking some
action to prevent another catastrophe. But the rituals consumed her. She would lose track of time in
the kitchen, her hands moving through familiar motions while her mind spiraled through sequences of
prayer and prevention, checking and rechecking that she had done them correctly, terrified that a
single mistake would mean another sister would suffer Claire's fate. And each time she performed
these rituals, each time she avoided a space or a task, the conviction deepened: she was not
trustworthy, she could not be relied upon, she had already failed once and would fail again. The
memories of Claire would arrive without warning—a phrase someone used, the particular way another
sister laughed—and Margaret would be back in that moment of knowing she had not done enough, that
her absence had been the crack through which tragedy entered.

Sister Margaret had not left the convent grounds in six months. Her superior, concerned by her
withdrawal and the obsessive quality of her prayers, finally insisted that she speak to someone
outside the community. Margaret came to her first appointment hollow-eyed and trembling, not knowing
if therapy was a betrayal of her faith or her last chance at redemption.
