# Vinh's Cycle

Vinh Nguyen was sixty-eight when his daughter was killed. It was a Tuesday evening in March, four
years ago now, when the call came through—a car accident on the interstate, hit by a drunk driver
who'd drifted across the median. She was thirty-four, a pediatrician, driving home from her clinic.
The hospital social worker had used words like "immediate" and "unsurvivable," but what Vinh
remembered most vividly was the sound of his own breathing, the way it had seemed to stop and
restart in his chest, and the particular quality of silence that followed when he hung up the phone.

The first weeks were a blur of arrangements and condolences. Vinh had spent forty years in the
public defender's office, had argued cases involving vehicular homicide, had sat across from
perpetrators and victims' families alike. He understood the machinery of loss. But understanding it
and living through it were not the same thing. By the time he returned to work that April, something
in him had shifted. He found himself watching people—the way they moved, their expressions, the
split-second calculations in their eyes. In the courthouse hallway, he would notice a man's posture,
the angle of his jaw, and his body would tense as though the man were about to do something
unpredictable, dangerous. Nothing ever happened. But the alertness never quite left. Vinh had come
to believe that danger was distributed unevenly through the world, that some people carried it in
them like a disease, and that he needed to see it coming.

The memories of his daughter, when they surfaced, were fractured and strange. He could recall her
voice—not as a continuous memory, but as isolated phrases that would echo in his head without
warning: "Dad, I'm running late," or "See you Sunday." The words would arrive complete with the
exact timbre of her speech, the slight lilt at the end of her sentences, but he could not summon a
full conversation, could not hold her face steady in his mind. What he could hold was the image of
the highway patrol officer's face as she delivered the news, the fluorescent lighting in the
hospital corridor, the smell of the waiting room. The coherence was all wrong. And when these
fragmented pieces surfaced—a snatch of her voice, a detail from that night—an overwhelming grief
would wash through him, so complete and suffocating that he would have to stop whatever he was
doing. At his desk, in the middle of reviewing a file, he might hear her say his name and have to
excuse himself.

It was this grief, arriving unbidden through these broken memories, that drove him to find ways to
interrupt it. He began eating more—nothing specific, just more of everything. A sandwich at lunch,
then another an hour later. Pastries from the courthouse café. Late-night trips to the refrigerator
when sleep wouldn't come. The eating didn't solve anything, but it created a small barrier between
himself and the intrusive fragments. While he was chewing, while he was reaching for something else
to eat, the memories seemed to recede slightly. He also stopped watching the news entirely, no
longer read the local paper if he could help it. Any story about accidents or drunk driving or loss
sent him spiraling, and he'd learned that avoidance was simpler than managing the aftermath.

But the avoidance and the eating, meant to protect him, seemed to amplify everything else. Because
he was not engaging with information about the world, because he was turning inward, his
hypervigilance intensified. The people around him seemed more opaque, more threatening. In the
courthouse, younger attorneys who were simply stressed would seem hostile to him. A client who was
nervous might strike him as dangerous. His mind would race through scenarios—what if this person
became violent, what if he missed a signal, what if someone else he loved was taken from him because
he hadn't been vigilant enough. Each false alarm—each moment when he braced for danger that never
came—left him exhausted but more convinced that his vigilance was necessary.

At home, the checking had become ritualistic. Before bed, Vinh would walk through the house, testing
each window lock, each door. He might return to the front door three times, four times, each time
gripping the handle to ensure it was truly locked. The checking provided a momentary sense of
control, but it also reinforced the underlying belief that danger was imminent, that his daughter's
death had taught him a lesson about the fragility of safety. Each time he checked and found the
doors secure, he felt relief—but also a creeping certainty that the next time he checked, something
might be different, something might have shifted. The memories would surface during these walks: the
sound of her voice, the hospital corridor, the officer's face. And the grief would spike, sending
him back toward the kitchen, toward food, toward distraction.

Vinh came to see himself as someone who had to remain vigilant, who could not afford to relax his
guard. What he could not see was how this stance—the constant watching, the avoidance, the eating to
manage the grief—was keeping him locked in a cycle where the memories felt more vivid, the threats
more real, and the need for protection more urgent. His colleagues at the office had noticed his
weight gain, his tendency to snap at junior staff, the way he seemed perpetually braced for
something. His daughter's widow had stopped calling as often; the conversations had become strained,
both of them circling around the absence without ever landing on it. Vinh told himself he was
protecting himself, but what he was actually doing was isolating himself further, each day a
reinforcement of the belief that the world was dangerous and that vigilance was the only thing
standing between him and another unbearable loss.

It was only when his doctor, reviewing routine bloodwork, asked about his weight gain and his sleep
and his general mood—and Vinh found himself unable to answer with anything but silence—that he
considered that something needed to change. He was referred to a therapist, and in that first
session, when asked what had brought him in, Vinh found himself describing not the accident itself,
but the way his own mind had become a kind of trap, one that he kept reinforcing every single day.
