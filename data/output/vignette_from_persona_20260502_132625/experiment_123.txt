# Rajesh's Story

Rajesh Patel was thirty-two when the world fractured into before and after. He had been working as a
housekeeper at the Riverside Medical Center for seven years, a job that suited him—steady,
predictable, the kind of work that let him move through the day without much fanfare. He was married
to Priya, whom he'd known since childhood; they lived in a modest apartment in Jersey City and had
recently begun talking about children. On a Tuesday evening in March, while walking home from the
bus stop after a double shift, Rajesh was pulled into an alley by two men who demanded his wallet.
When he hesitated—not out of courage but out of shock—one of them struck him across the face and
ribs. They took his phone, his wallet, his watch. What they took that mattered most was something he
couldn't name then, something that would only become clear in the weeks that followed.

The memory of those few minutes arrived with stunning clarity and persistence. Rajesh could recall
the exact texture of the brick wall against his palms as he was shoved backward, the metallic taste
of blood in his mouth, the way one attacker's sneaker—white with a fraying blue stripe—appeared in
his peripheral vision just before the kick landed. At night, when the memory surfaced, his body
remembered too. His ribs would ache with phantom pain, his jaw would tighten as though absorbing the
blow again, and sometimes his hands would begin to shake uncontrollably. He found himself unable to
talk about what had happened, not because he lacked the details—they were vivid and intrusive—but
because something in his throat closed when he tried to speak it aloud. Even with Priya, who held
his hand in the hospital, he could only manage fragments: "Two guys. Fast. I don't know." The fuller
story remained locked somewhere behind his teeth.

What frightened Rajesh most in those first weeks was not the memory itself, but what it had revealed
about the world. The men who attacked him had seemed ordinary—they could have been anyone. One wore
a hoodie and jeans, the other a coat like something Rajesh himself might own. This ordinariness
became the thing that haunted him. He had come to believe that people were not what they seemed,
that beneath the surface of any interaction lurked potential violence. When he returned to work at
the hospital—his supervisor had been kind about the time off—he found himself studying the faces of
colleagues he had known for years, wondering what they were capable of. The world had always
contained danger, he supposed, but he had not truly understood it until it had his wallet in its
hands and his blood on its knuckles. Now he was certain: the world was a dangerous place, and people
could not be trusted, no matter how familiar they seemed.

This conviction fed back into his memory in ways that deepened his suffering. When the vivid
recollections returned—and they returned often, triggered by the sight of a white sneaker in a
patient's room, or a man in a blue hoodie passing in the corridor—Rajesh's mind would immediately
reinterpret them through this new lens of danger and deception. The ordinariness he had initially
noticed in his attackers now seemed like camouflage, proof of how easily danger could hide. Each
time the memory surfaced with its physical pain and sensory detail, it reinforced his sense that the
world was fundamentally unsafe, that the people around him were potential threats. And each time he
felt that conviction harden—that people could not be trusted, that deception was everywhere—the
memory became more potent, more threatening, because it seemed to confirm what he now believed to be
true about human nature itself.

Rajesh had begun avoiding the bus stop where he was attacked, which meant taking a longer route home
that added forty minutes to his commute. He avoided the corridor at the hospital where he had seen
the white sneaker. He found reasons not to attend the monthly gatherings with Priya's family, where
there would be people he did not know well enough to be certain of. The avoidance had consequences:
he was missing time with his wife, becoming isolated within his own marriage, and his supervisor had
begun to notice the gaps in his work schedule. What brought him to the clinic was Priya finally
insisting, her voice tired and frightened, that something had to change. Rajesh sat in the waiting
room with his hands clenched, terrified that talking about what happened would only make him believe
more firmly that the world was bent on his destruction, yet unable to bear the weight of silence any
longer.
