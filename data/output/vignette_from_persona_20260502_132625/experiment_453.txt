# Chinedu's Story

Chinedu was twenty-two when he first took the job as principal of a charter school in Brooklyn, a
position that seemed to confirm what he had long believed about himself: that he could rise above
the chaos of his childhood, that discipline and order could be built, and that he would never become
like the man who had raised him. His father had been a volatile presence in his Lagos childhood—a
man whose moods shifted without warning, whose hands came fast, and whose voice could turn a quiet
afternoon into something terrifying. Chinedu had learned early to read the signs, to move carefully,
to anticipate. By the time his mother brought him to New York at fourteen, he had already
internalized the architecture of control. He studied hard. He stayed calm. He became the kind of
person other people trusted.

But in the three years since the trauma had begun to surface—after a university counselor had gently
suggested that his perfectionism and hypervigilance might be rooted in something deeper—Chinedu had
noticed that the old memories were not staying buried. They came unbidden and visceral. When he
discovered an old photograph his mother had kept, tucked in a shoebox, showing him as a small boy
standing beside his father, something in his chest seized. The image triggered a cascade of
sensations he could not quite name: the smell of his father's cologne, the texture of his shirt, a
sound like a belt being drawn from loops. The memory that followed was fragmentary and
overwhelming—disconnected pieces that seemed to belong to different moments but felt equally real,
equally present. He remembered his father's hand raised, but not his own face. He remembered the
cold wall against his back, but not how he had gotten there. The pieces would not cohere into a
linear narrative, yet each fragment arrived with a vividness that made the ordinary world feel thin
and false by comparison.

These fragmented recollections, once triggered, began to reshape how Chinedu understood himself. The
harder he tried to deliberately reconstruct what had happened—sitting alone in his office late at
night, trying to make the pieces fit—the more elusive the full memory became, as if his mind was
protecting him by keeping it scattered. But the fragments themselves were enough to feed a darker
conviction: that he carried his father's rage inside him, dormant but real, and that one day it
would emerge. When a teacher arrived late to a mandatory staff meeting, or when a student was
insolent, Chinedu felt a heat rise in his chest that terrified him. He was certain that he would not
be able to control his anger, that something terrible was waiting to be unleashed. The fear was not
abstract—it was rooted in the vividness of his memories, in the way those disconnected pieces seemed
to prove that violence lived in his blood.

Unpredictable situations became unbearable. When his girlfriend, Zainab, suggested they visit her
parents without a firm itinerary, Chinedu felt panic rising. Unpredictability had always been his
father's weapon; it was the not-knowing that had kept him braced, always ready. Now, at twenty-two,
in a position of authority, he found himself unable to tolerate situations he could not control or
anticipate. He began to construct elaborate routines at school, scheduling every transition, every
interaction. He avoided situations where he might be caught off guard—he stopped attending faculty
happy hours, declined invitations to informal gatherings, and found reasons not to be in spaces
where the rhythm of the day was uncertain. When he woke from nightmares—and they came several times
a week, leaving him gasping and disoriented—the residual fear would linger for hours, making him
hypervigilant, suspicious, certain that something bad was about to happen.

The conviction that he was permanently damaged had taken root and was now feeding back into
everything. He had come to believe that his capacity for violence was not something he could
overcome but something that defined him at his core. This belief made him scrutinize his own
reactions obsessively, looking for signs of his father in himself, and each small moment of
irritation or impatience felt like evidence. His relationship with Zainab had begun to strain under
the weight of his withdrawal. He could not explain to her why he needed everything planned, why he
flinched at sudden movements, why he sometimes seemed to be looking through her rather than at her.
He had started to believe that he would inevitably hurt her, that his presence in her life was a
threat, and so he had begun to pull away—not dramatically, but in small, accumulating ways that she
had started to notice.

It was Zainab who finally insisted he speak to someone. She had found him one morning sitting on the
edge of their bed at five in the morning, shaking, unable to articulate what the nightmare had
contained except that it had felt more real than waking life. He had called his doctor that
afternoon, and now, sitting in a therapist's office for the first time, Chinedu was trying to
explain how the past had become present, how the fragments of memory had become the architecture of
his fear, and how the person he had worked so hard to become felt like a fragile thing, always on
the verge of shattering.
