# Clinical Vignette: Amara

Amara was seventy-four when she came to the clinic, though her movements suggested someone
older—careful, guarded, as if her body had learned to take up as little space as possible. She had
worked the fields outside Raleigh for nearly fifty years, her hands darkened and creased by soil and
sun, her back shaped by seasons of bending. On an evening in March of the previous year, she had
stayed late to finish a section of early spring planting. She was alone. A man she did not know came
from the tree line, and what happened in the gathering dusk among the turned earth and young shoots
had fractured something in her that she could not name. He left her there, and she lay in the dirt
for a long time before she could move.

In the months that followed, Amara had tried to build a life around the wreckage. She returned to
work—she had to—but the fields had become a landscape of ambush. The smell of soil now carried him
with it, the particular earthiness of spring mornings that had once grounded her becoming instead a
trigger that sent her heart racing and her mind spiraling into that moment. She had come to believe
that her body was no longer her own, that it was marked, contaminated by what had been done to it.
When the scent of turned earth reached her—whether in the fields or even drifting through her
neighborhood on the wind—she would find herself flooded with shame and a terrible sense of dirtiness
that no amount of washing could touch. These olfactory assaults would leave her convinced, for hours
afterward, that she was irreparably damaged, that perhaps it would be better not to continue at all.

The anniversary of the assault, now approaching, had begun to preoccupy her thoughts. As the date
drew near, she found herself gripped by a dread that seemed to anticipate the very sensations she
feared. The knowledge that the date was coming made her hypervigilant to every smell, every sound.
When silence fell—in her home, in a car, in the early morning before the world woke—she would feel
her chest tighten, certain that danger was gathering in that quiet. The silence seemed to amplify
her shame, as if in the absence of distraction she was forced to confront the ugliness of what she
had become. These moments of quiet would then propel her into her defenses: she would force herself
to think only positive thoughts, to recite to herself that she was strong, that this had not broken
her, even as the words felt hollow and dishonest. But the effort of maintaining that forced
positivity only deepened her sense of falseness and self-contempt; she was lying to herself, she
knew, and the lie itself became another form of contamination.

She had begun to avoid the circumstances that might expose her to these triggers. She stopped
working the early shifts, though this meant less income and a subtle loss of status among her peers.
She stayed indoors when she could, keeping the windows closed even on warm days, refusing to let the
outside world penetrate her space. Most painfully, she had withdrawn from her partner of twelve
years. His touch—even his hand on her arm—felt like a violation, a reminder that her body was not
safe, that it belonged to the trauma rather than to her. She would stiffen when he approached, and
eventually he stopped trying. The distance between them grew, unspoken but absolute. In avoiding his
touch, she had isolated herself further, and that isolation had only amplified the whisper in her
mind that her life was no longer worth the living of it, that she had become a burden to the one
person who had stood by her.

Amara arrived at the clinic on a gray afternoon in late February, just days before the anniversary.
She sat very still in the waiting room, her hands folded in her lap, and when asked what had brought
her in, she said quietly that she was afraid of what she might do if things did not change. She was
not sure, she said, that she had the strength to keep fighting something that felt like it was
winning. She wanted help, but she was afraid that help would mean confronting the very things she
had spent months learning to avoid.
