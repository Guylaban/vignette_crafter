# Vinh's Vigil

Vinh was thirty-one years old when the police officer's hands found his throat. It happened on a
Tuesday evening in late March, outside a convenience store in a neighborhood where he and his wife,
Linh, had lived for three years. He had been waiting for a friend—nothing more—leaning against the
storefront with his hands in his pockets, when the patrol car pulled up and the officer emerged with
his weapon already drawn. What followed lasted perhaps ninety seconds. The officer had mistaken him
for someone else, had shouted commands Vinh barely processed before the tackle came, before the
forearm locked across his neck, before the world narrowed to the sensation of asphalt against his
ribs and the officer's voice—hot and certain—saying he was under arrest. By the time backup arrived
and the mistake was realized, Vinh had been pressed into the pavement long enough to know what it
felt like to believe he might not breathe again.

The apologies came later, formal and hollow. The department acknowledged the error. There would be a
settlement. But for Vinh, the aftermath was far more disorienting than the event itself. In the
first weeks, he returned to his carpentry work—framing houses, installing cabinetry—but something
had shifted in how his body inhabited space. He began positioning himself differently on job sites,
always aware of where the exits were, always ensuring that no one could approach him from behind. At
the lumber yard, he found himself standing with his back to the wall of the storage shed, watching
the other workers move through the lot. His supervisor noticed but said nothing. Linh noticed too.
She would find him standing in their kitchen at odd hours, his back pressed against the counter,
even when she was the only other person home. When she reached for him from behind, he would flinch.

Within two months, the hypervigilance had calcified into something harder to name. Vinh had come to
believe that safety required constant surveillance—that if he could just see the room, see the door,
see who was coming, he could prevent what had happened from happening again. The feeling was not
rational; he knew this. But knowing and feeling were different things. Even in their bedroom, with
Linh beside him, he could not relax. His muscles remained taut, his breathing shallow. He would lie
awake cataloging sounds from the street below, interpreting them, assigning them threat value. A car
that slowed down. A voice that rose. Each one sent a spike of certainty through his chest: something
bad was about to happen.

The rumination began as a whisper and became a roar. Alone in his truck between job sites, Vinh
would replay the encounter obsessively, each time finding new evidence of his own culpability. He
should not have been standing there. He should have seen the officer sooner. He should have complied
faster, moved differently, been less brown in the face of authority—some indefinable failure that
had invited the violence. These thoughts were exhausting and inescapable, and they fed directly into
his sense of threat. Because if the danger had originated from within him, from some flaw in his
judgment or his body or his very presence, then no amount of wall-positioning or vigilance could
truly protect him. The threat was internal and external simultaneously, and this paradox was
unbearable.

By autumn, Vinh had begun using methamphetamine. It started as an accident—a friend's offer on a
Friday night, a way to quiet the rumination for a few hours—but it became a habit. The drug silenced
the self-blame temporarily, replaced the grinding anxiety with a kind of numb momentum. He could
work while high, could move through the day without the constant internal commentary about what he
had done wrong. But the relief was always short-lived, and when the drug wore off, the threat-
monitoring returned with renewed intensity. His back would press against the wall again. The
delays—a contractor calling late, a material shipment that didn't arrive on schedule—would trigger a
cascade of catastrophic thinking. Something bad is happening. Something is wrong. His
hypervigilance, temporarily suppressed by the drug, would come roaring back, sharper and more
insistent than before. The cycle had become self-perpetuating: the threat made him reach for the
drug, and the drug's withdrawal made the threat feel more real.

Linh had begun sleeping in their daughter's room three months ago. She had not said it was because
of him, but they both knew. When Vinh finally came to the clinic—referred by his primary care
physician after he admitted to substance use during a routine physical—he was thin, exhausted, and
terrified. What frightened him most was not the memory of the officer's hands, but the possibility
that he had become the threat himself, that his vigilance and his drug use and his inability to be
present with his wife had transformed him into something dangerous. He wanted to stop using. He
wanted to sleep beside Linh without flinching. He wanted to stand in the middle of a room without
feeling like the walls were closing in. But he had no idea how to untangle the knot he had become.
