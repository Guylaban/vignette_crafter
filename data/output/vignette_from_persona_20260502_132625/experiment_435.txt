# Karim's Story

Karim was seventy-eight years old when the chemical fire at the roofing supply warehouse changed
everything. He had been working in the industry for fifty-two years—since he was a boy in Beirut,
before he came to America at twenty-six. He knew the smell of tar, the weight of a ladder, the
particular ache of his knees on a July afternoon. The warehouse fire happened on a Tuesday in March,
during an inventory shift that was supposed to be routine. A catalytic heater malfunctioned near the
solvent storage. Within minutes, the air became toxic—a yellowish haze that burned his throat and
eyes. Karim remembered the panic of not being able to breathe, the way his vision blurred, the sound
of the alarm cutting through the chaos. He was rushed to the hospital, kept for three days for
observation, discharged with a diagnosis of acute chemical inhalation injury and instructions to
rest. The doctors said he would recover. His lungs did, mostly. His mind did not.

In the first weeks after the exposure, Karim tried to return to work. His wife, Amira, begged him
not to, but he insisted. He was a roofer; he had never been a man who sat idle. But the moment he
stepped back into the warehouse—even into the office section, nowhere near the storage area—his body
seized with a terror he could not name. His heart raced. His hands trembled. He could smell the
solvent again, though the air was clean. He left after twenty minutes and never went back. His
employer offered him a desk job, filing and scheduling. Karim refused. The shame of it, the sense
that his body had betrayed him, settled into his bones.

What frightened him most was that the memory would not stay where he put it. Sometimes, sitting at
the dinner table with Amira and their youngest grandson visiting from out of town, a wave of the old
panic would crash over him without warning—the full force of that suffocating terror, the taste of
chemicals, the burning in his lungs—and he would find himself gasping, his hands shaking, unable to
explain to the boy why Grandfather was suddenly frightened. The presence of children, the sight of
his grandchildren, had become tangled with the trauma in a way that made no logical sense. Yet each
time a child appeared in his home, or when he saw children playing in the park from his window, his
vigilance spiked. His nervous system seemed to know something his conscious mind could not
articulate: that the memory was waiting, and these moments of ordinary family life were the triggers
that would unleash it. The news, too—any story about industrial accidents, chemical spills,
workplace injuries—would pull the memory to the surface, forcing him to relive those minutes in the
warehouse with a vividness that terrified him.

When he tried deliberately to recall what happened, to make sense of it in his own mind, the details
scattered. He could see the yellow haze, could feel the burn in his throat, but the sequence of
events felt fractured, unreliable. Yet the memory had a way of arriving unbidden, usually at night
or in moments of quiet—a sudden wash of emotion without a clear image, just the raw terror and the
sensation of suffocation, as if his body remembered what his mind could not fully grasp.

His startle response had become pronounced. When Amira touched his shoulder from behind, he would
flinch violently, sometimes crying out. She had learned not to approach him from unexpected angles,
but the damage to their intimacy was already done. He could not relax, even in the safety of their
home, even when he knew—rationally, clearly—that there was no threat. His body remained in a state
of constant vigilance, muscles tight, jaw clenched. At night, he would lie awake for hours, his mind
cycling through the same loop: the memory fragments, the startle, the racing heart, the inability to
settle back into sleep. By three or four in the morning, he would give up and move to the living
room, where he would sit in the dark until dawn, exhausted and afraid to close his eyes.

After six months of this, Karim finally agreed to see someone. He was not a man who believed in
talking about his feelings, but Amira had worn him down with her worry, and he was tired—bone-tired
in a way that sleep no longer touched. He came to the clinic with his wife, sat stiffly in the
chair, and said, "I cannot live like this anymore. I need to understand why my own mind is trying to
kill me."
