# Lani's Burden

Lani had been seventy-one for three months when he finally made the appointment. The intake
coordinator at the clinic had asked him what brought him in, and he'd said, simply, "I can't keep
going like this." What he meant was harder to articulate: the weight of staying alive, the
exhaustion of managing a mind that would not leave him alone.

The bombing had happened in Bali in 2002. Lani had been there on holiday—a rare break from his work
as a painter in Sydney, a chance to walk through markets and sit by the sea. He'd been standing
outside a nightclub on Kuta Beach when the first explosion came. He remembered the heat reaching him
before the sound did, remembered his body being thrown backward, his shoulder striking concrete with
a force that seemed to separate him from himself. The second blast came seconds later. Around him,
people were screaming, burning. He'd lain on the ground, unable to move, watching a woman's face
inches from his own, her eyes already gone. For what felt like hours but was probably minutes, he
could not tell if he was alive or dead.

He'd survived with a fractured scapula and deep lacerations on his left side. The physical wounds
had healed. The other wounds had not.

Twenty-two years later, Lani lived alone in a small flat in Marrickville. He still
painted—commissions for restaurants, murals for council buildings—but the work had become a way to
keep moving rather than something he loved. The memories came without warning and without mercy. A
sharp sound—a car backfiring, a door slamming—and suddenly he was back on that beach, his body
rigid, his left shoulder screaming as if the impact were happening again. He would feel his arm
pinned beneath him, his ribs pressed against the ground, the exact angle of his spine when he'd
fallen. These weren't vague recollections; they were vivid and complete, every detail crystalline.
The smell of burning rubber and flesh. The grit of sand in his mouth. The precise weight of the
woman's body next to his. His mind would force these images at him at any moment—while he was
painting a wall, while he was sitting at a café, while he was trying to sleep—and he had learned
that resistance was useless. The memories simply came, and he had to wait for them to pass.

Over the years, Lani had come to believe that he could not trust himself. His body would betray him
without warning, locking into that position on the beach. His mind would fail him, flooding with
images he could not control. If he could not rely on his own mind and body, how could he possibly
manage the world? And the world itself—he had come to see it as fundamentally dangerous, a place
where random violence could find you even when you were simply trying to have a holiday. People
could not be trusted either. Friends had tried to help in the early years, but they'd grown tired of
his anxiety, his need for constant reassurance. He'd asked them repeatedly if he was going to be
okay, if they thought he was losing his mind, if they believed the world was as unsafe as it felt.
Eventually, they'd stopped calling. Now, when he did venture out—to buy groceries, to meet a
contractor about a job—he would call his sister afterward, asking her to confirm that nothing bad
had happened, that he was still alive, that he hadn't made some catastrophic mistake. She tried to
be patient, but he could hear the strain in her voice.

The reassurance never lasted. Within hours, the doubt would return, and with it, the conviction that
he was fundamentally alone with this, that no one could truly help him because the danger was real
and he simply could not be trusted to navigate it. This belief had led him to avoid going out
whenever possible. Commissions had dried up because he'd turned down jobs that required him to work
in busy areas or in places where he didn't know the exits. He'd stopped going to the markets,
stopped sitting at cafés. He'd tried to control his emotions—to breathe deeply, to tell himself that
the memories weren't real, to suppress the panic before it could fully take hold—but the effort
itself exhausted him and seemed only to sharpen the memories, making them more insistent, more
vivid. The harder he tried to push them away, the more forcefully they returned, and the more
convinced he became that he was failing, that he could not manage his own mind, that the world was
too dangerous for someone as broken as he was. The cycle tightened month after month, year after
year.

Now Lani sat in the clinic waiting room, his hands trembling slightly. He had finally admitted to
himself that the strategies he'd been using—the avoidance, the reassurance-seeking, the emotional
control—were not protecting him. They were isolating him. He was afraid that talking about what had
happened would make it worse, that he would lose what little control he had left. But he was more
afraid of continuing as he was: trapped in a beach that never ended, trusting no one, not even
himself.
