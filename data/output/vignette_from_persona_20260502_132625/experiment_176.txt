# Priya's Unraveling

Priya Desai had spent twenty-three years in the Foreign Service, negotiating trade agreements and
representing American interests in posts across three continents. She was competent, composed, and
accustomed to managing crises. Then came the chemical leak at the embassy compound in Tbilisi.

It was late afternoon when the alarm sounded. The facility manager had discovered a ruptured
containment vessel in the basement—industrial solvents used for equipment maintenance, their acrid
smell already creeping through the ventilation system. Priya was in her office on the fourth floor
when the evacuation order came. She made it down three flights of stairs before the fumes thickened,
and she found herself breathing through her sleeve, her eyes watering, that metallic taste coating
her tongue—sharp and chemical, almost like burnt copper mixed with something synthetic and wrong.
She was among the last out, and by the time she reached the courtyard, her lungs burned and her
vision had begun to blur. The hospital visit that followed, the chest X-rays, the reassurance that
her lungs would recover—all of it felt distant now, a bureaucratic afterthought to the real injury,
which was something she could not quite name.

Three months had passed since the evacuation, and Priya had returned to Washington on medical leave.
She told herself the event had not really been that bad, that her body had overreacted, that
thousands of people were exposed to worse chemicals every day without falling apart. This
reassurance worked for a few weeks. Then the intrusions began.

A colleague microwaved fish in the office break room, and the smell—not even similar, really, but
something about its pungency—sent Priya spiraling. The memory arrived without warning, complete and
overwhelming: the taste of the solvent coating her mouth, the burn in her sinuses, the press of
bodies in the stairwell. Her hands shook as she gathered her things and left early, telling no one
why. She began avoiding the break room entirely, timing her lunch to avoid any chance of
encountering food smells. She stopped attending embassy events, declined invitations to restaurants,
and eventually resigned from the weekly policy committee that met in a windowless conference
room—the air felt too still there, and she was certain she could smell something chemical in the
recycled atmosphere. What had started as a reasonable precaution had calcified into a rigid system
of avoidance that now governed her days.

The more Priya avoided triggers, the more fragile her sense of safety became. She could not go to
the grocery store without scanning the cleaning supplies aisle—her body tensing, her pulse
quickening, her mind cataloging every potential hazard. She found herself irritable with her sister,
snapping at her over nothing, her anger disproportionate and immediate. Her body felt as though it
were perpetually braced for danger, muscles tight across her shoulders and chest, her breath shallow
and controlled. This constant vigilance exhausted her, and the exhaustion made her more vulnerable
to the intrusions. When she tried to distract herself by thinking of other things, she found she
could not; the moment she relaxed her guard, the memory returned with renewed force. The taste would
bloom on her tongue—that metallic, acrid sensation—and with it came the full sensory flood: the
fumes, the fear, the conviction that her body was being poisoned from within.

Priya had begun to avoid anything that felt remotely stressful, believing that emotional strain
might somehow trigger another exposure or weaken her already compromised system. She turned down a
promotion. She stopped seeing friends who asked difficult questions. She spent her evenings alone in
her apartment, the windows sealed, the air purifier running constantly—a white noise that she had
come to depend on, a barrier between herself and an increasingly threatening world. The irony was
not lost on her: in trying to protect herself from the memory, she had made it more powerful. The
avoidance had become its own kind of toxin, and now, six months after the leak, she found herself
wondering if she would ever feel safe enough to leave her apartment again.
