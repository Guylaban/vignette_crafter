# Linh's Burden

Linh was thirty-five when she came to the clinic, her posture still bearing the careful geometry of
someone who has learned to take up as little space as possible. She had been working as a physical
therapist for twelve years, and her hands—which had once moved with the confident precision of
someone trained to heal—now trembled slightly when she had to place them on a patient's shoulder or
neck. Three years earlier, her ex-husband had held her against the kitchen counter, his hands
pressing her face into the cold granite, and in the months and years that followed, cold surfaces
had become dangerous. Even now, when she showered and the water temperature dipped unexpectedly, her
entire body would seize. The shock of cold on her skin was enough to send her back to that
moment—the weight of his body, the impossibility of moving, the certainty that he would not stop.
She had come to believe that her body itself was a liability, that something in the way she moved,
the way she spoke, the way she existed in space had invited that violence. When she felt the chill
of a stethoscope or accidentally touched cold metal in the clinic, her mind would spiral backward,
and she would find herself convinced that she had done something to deserve it.

This conviction—that she had somehow caused her own abuse—had calcified into a permanent feature of
how she saw herself. Linh had begun to dwell obsessively on the months before the assault, replaying
conversations and interactions, searching for the moment she had gone wrong. If only she had not
questioned his spending. If only she had been more attentive, more compliant, less tired from work.
She was certain there was something fundamentally defective in her, something that had made her
unable to recognize or prevent what was coming. This sense of permanent humiliation—of being someone
who had allowed herself to be treated this way—had taken root so deeply that she could barely look
her reflection in the eye. When her current husband, Marcus, tried to embrace her from behind or
reach for her hand without warning, she would flinch violently, her body interpreting his touch as a
threat. Any unexpected contact, even the lightest brush of fabric on her skin or the warmth of
another person's hand, could trigger a cascade of panic. The triggers were everywhere now: the
temperature of the water in the staff bathroom, a colleague's firm grip on her arm during a
conversation, being asked to stay late at the clinic when she had already said she needed to leave.
Each instance of lost autonomy—each moment when she felt controlled or constrained—would send her
backward into that kitchen, and she would emerge from the memory convinced anew that she was broken,
that she had invited this, that she would always be this way.

The memories themselves had never faded into the past. When they surfaced—often without warning,
sometimes triggered by a smell or a sound or simply the quality of afternoon light—they felt as
though they were happening in real time. She could feel the cold granite against her cheekbone, the
pressure of his hands, the desperate gasping for breath. When she tried to describe what had
happened to Marcus or to her therapist, the narrative would fragment. She would begin to explain and
then suddenly lose the thread entirely, overwhelmed by a surge of emotion so intense that she had to
stop, had to leave the room, had to wait until her body stopped shaking. This inability to speak
about it clearly only deepened her conviction that she was somehow complicit, that a truly innocent
person would be able to articulate what had been done to them. The shame of not being able to finish
a sentence, of breaking down mid-explanation, felt like further evidence of her defectiveness. And
so she had begun to retreat into rumination instead—endlessly replaying how she might have prevented
the assault, what she could have done differently, how she could have been someone other than who
she was. These thoughts offered a terrible kind of comfort, the illusion that if she could only
identify the precise moment of her failure, she might be able to prevent it from happening again.
But the rumination itself became a trigger. Hours spent dwelling on her mistakes would leave her
hypervigilant, scanning every interaction for signs of danger, watching her colleagues and even
Marcus for the subtle shifts in tone or posture that she had learned to associate with violence. She
had become someone who monitored every person around her for the capacity to hurt her, and this
vigilance, this constant threat-detection, only reinforced her conviction that danger was always
present—and that she was the kind of person to whom such danger came.

Linh had taken to arriving at the clinic earlier and leaving later, adjusting her schedule to avoid
situations where she might be touched unexpectedly or asked to do something against her preference.
She had become excellent at reading a room, at sensing what others wanted from her and complying
preemptively. Her marriage had grown quieter; Marcus had learned not to reach for her, not to
surprise her with affection, not to insist when she said no. The life she had built with him had
contracted around her trauma, and she carried a deep, aching guilt about that too. It was only when
Marcus had gently suggested, after she had spent an evening curled in the bathroom unable to stop
crying, that she consider seeing a therapist, that she had finally reached out. She was terrified of
what talking about it might mean—terrified that articulating the details would make it more real,
that exploring her memories would somehow prove she had deserved them, that looking directly at the
defect she was certain lived inside her would make it impossible to hide anymore. But she was also
exhausted. She wanted to know if it was possible to be touched without flinching, to feel cold water
without traveling backward in time, to believe, even for a moment, that what had happened to her was
not, in some essential way, her fault.
