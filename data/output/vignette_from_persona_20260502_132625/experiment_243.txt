# Amara's Story

Amara was forty-two when she first came to the clinic, though the exhaustion in her face made her
look older. She had been a train conductor for nearly fifteen years, a job she had loved—the rhythm
of the rails, the responsibility, the clear structure of her routes—until the day in Lagos, ten
years ago, when her life fractured into fragments she could never quite reassemble.

She had been visiting her mother during a family emergency. On the third day, armed men had entered
the neighborhood. What happened next existed in her mind not as a coherent sequence but as a
collection of visceral images: hands forcing her into a concrete room, the smell of smoke and
burning rubber from fires set in the streets outside, the sound of her own voice pleading, the
weight of restraints. The torture lasted hours. She was certain it was hours, though the memory
itself had no beginning or end, no logical progression—just flashes of sensation and image that
felt, when they surfaced, as though they were occurring in the present moment. When she tried to
tell someone what happened, the words would not come in order. She could describe the smoke, the
darkness, the pain, but not weave them into a story. Even to herself, the trauma remained a series
of disconnected photographs.

After her release and eventual return to the United States, Amara had tried to resume her life. Her
husband, David, had been patient those first months. But the symptoms had gradually made a prison of
their home. Within weeks of returning to work, she found herself unable to sit in the conductor's
cabin without scanning the windows, listening for sounds that might signal intrusion—a habit born
from the constant vigilance demanded by her captors. The silence of the rails at night terrified her
most; silence meant she could hear nothing, predict nothing, control nothing. That auditory void
would trigger the images: the smoke, the hands, the darkness returning in full force, and she would
feel the event happening again, her body responding as though she were still restrained in that
room. To manage the silence and the returning memories, she had begun smoking—first occasionally,
then throughout her shifts. The cigarette gave her something to do with her hands, something to
focus on besides the intrusive images, though she knew it was a poor substitute for actual safety.

At home, the hypervigilance had deepened. She could not relax, even in their locked bedroom with
David beside her. Sudden movements—his hand reaching for the lamp, the cat jumping onto the
bed—would send her into immediate alert, her body rigid, her breath shallow. She had developed a
habit of constantly checking her body for signs of injury, running her hands over her arms and legs
as though wounds might have appeared without her noticing, as though danger might be inscribed on
her skin. This body monitoring was meant to reassure her, but it only reinforced the threat: each
check confirmed that danger was real enough to require constant surveillance. The more she
monitored, the more certain she felt that threat was imminent. The more certain she felt, the more
vivid her memories became—the images sharper, the sensation of reliving the event more acute. And
when the memories surged, she would find herself mentally rehearsing worst-case scenarios: what if
an intruder came tonight, what if she could not escape, what if it happened again. The rehearsal was
supposed to prepare her, to make the unthinkable manageable through repetition, but instead it fed
the cycle. Each imagined catastrophe felt like a prediction, each prediction felt like a warning she
must heed.

David had stopped inviting friends over after she flinched at a colleague's visible surgical scar
during a dinner visit. The scar—a thin line on the man's forearm—had triggered the images so
violently that she had excused herself and not returned to the table. She had taken medical leave
from her conducting position twice in the past year. Her supervisor had been understanding, but
there was a limit to understanding. She knew that if she could not manage the silence, the sudden
movements, the triggers that seemed to multiply with each passing month, she would lose the job that
had once anchored her sense of self.

It was David who finally insisted she seek help. He had found her one morning sitting in the kitchen
at four in the morning, smoking her third cigarette, hands shaking as she ran her fingers across her
collarbone for the hundredth time that night. "Something has to change," he had said, not unkindly.
Amara had agreed. What she feared most was that nothing could change, that she would spend the rest
of her life trapped in that concrete room, her mind unable to tell the difference between then and
now, her body unable to believe it was ever truly safe.
