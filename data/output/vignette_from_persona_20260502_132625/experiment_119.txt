# Kofi's Case

Kofi was thirty-three when he returned from the Sahel, though sometimes he felt decades older. For
eighteen months he had worked as a relief coordinator for an international NGO, moving between three
countries to document displacement camps and facilitate aid distribution. The work had felt sacred
to him—a way to transform the helplessness he'd carried since childhood into something purposeful.
Then came the ambush.

It happened on a routine supply run to a camp outside a town whose name he now tried not to think.
The convoy was flagged down by men in mismatched uniforms. Kofi was in the second vehicle. What
followed was a blur of shouting in languages he half-understood, hands forcing him and two
colleagues into the dust, the metallic click of rifle bolts, the sensation of his own breath
becoming foreign in his chest. For four hours they were held at gunpoint while the men looted the
medical supplies and fuel. One of Kofi's colleagues, a young woman named Amara, was separated from
the group. When she returned, her shirt was torn and her eyes had gone flat. The men left without
explanation. No ransom was demanded. No demands were made. They simply vanished into the landscape
as if the violence had been arbitrary, motiveless—a fact of the terrain rather than a crime.

Back in the States, Kofi had expected the memories to fade. They did not. What gnawed at him most
was the absence of consequence. The men were never identified. No investigation yielded names. The
NGO filed reports that disappeared into bureaucratic channels. Amara had left the organization
within weeks, and Kofi had heard through intermediaries that she'd moved back home, that she wasn't
answering calls. The injustice of it had calcified into something harder than anger—a conviction
that the world operated without accountability, that violence could be committed with impunity, that
he had been powerless to stop it and remained powerless still. He had come to believe he could not
trust his own judgment anymore; he had walked into that ambush, had not seen the danger coming, had
failed to protect Amara. What kind of person, what kind of man, failed at that?

The certainty that danger was imminent and that he himself was unreliable began to reshape his days.
At home in Atlanta, in the apartment he shared with his wife Zainab, Kofi found himself constantly
monitoring. When they sat in the living room, he positioned himself with sight lines to both windows
and the door. He scanned the street from behind the curtains before leaving the house. In grocery
stores, he tracked the movements of other shoppers, noting exits, assessing threat. His body
remained in a state of coiled readiness—his heart would surge at sudden noises, his shoulders would
jolt at unexpected touches. Zainab had learned not to approach him from behind.

The hypervigilance was exhausting, and Kofi began trying to manage it by simply not thinking about
what had happened. He would push the memories away when they surfaced, forcing his attention
elsewhere. But the act of suppressing them seemed to amplify the threat signal his body was sending.
The more he tried not to think about the ambush, the more his nervous system seemed convinced that
danger was present. His concentration at work suffered—he managed social media content for a
nonprofit, a job that required sustained focus and creativity, but he found himself unable to hold a
thought for more than a few minutes. His mind would fracture, pulled back to the dusty road, to the
sound of Amara's breathing when she returned. He would start a caption for an Instagram post and
lose the thread entirely, his eyes scanning the office windows instead. His supervisor had begun
asking if everything was all right.

To manage the intrusive vigilance, Kofi had developed a strange distance from his own memories. He
could describe the ambush in clinical detail—the time, the location, the sequence of events—but when
he spoke about it, it felt as though he were reporting on something that had happened to someone
else. The emotional truth of it, the fear and the shame, remained sealed off. This detachment
quieted the memories temporarily, but it also meant that nothing felt real anymore, including his
marriage. Zainab would reach for his hand and he would feel present only in body. The dissociation
kept the threat at bay, but it also kept him isolated, adrift in his own life. His wife had stopped
asking him to talk about what had happened. She had simply stopped asking him much of anything.

Kofi had finally sought help because Zainab had given him an ultimatum, delivered not in anger but
in exhaustion. "I can't live with a ghost," she had said. He sat in the intake appointment
understanding, with a clarity that surprised him, that he was trapped in a cycle he could not break
alone—that his conviction of his own unreliability had made him hypervigilant, that his
hypervigilance had made him retreat into detachment, and that his detachment had only deepened his
sense that he was fundamentally broken and could not be trusted with anything that mattered. What
frightened him most was that she might be right.
