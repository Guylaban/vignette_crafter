# Fatima's Story

Fatima was thirty-four when the numbness started. She had felt a dull ache in her lower abdomen
during a routine shift at the construction site where she worked as a crew supervisor—nothing
alarming at first, just a distraction as she moved between the framing crews and the foundation
work. By evening, the pain had sharpened. By morning, after a sleepless night spent gripping the
bathroom counter, she was in an emergency room, where a young resident delivered the diagnosis with
the careful tone doctors use when the words themselves are dangerous: stage three ovarian cancer.
The surgery came within weeks. The chemotherapy followed, months of it, poison dripped into her
veins in the hope of killing what was already inside her. She had survived—the scans eventually
turned clear—but something essential had fractured in the process, something that no oncology team
could repair.

Two years into remission, Fatima found herself unable to trust her own body. Every twinge, every
moment of fatigue, every skipped heartbeat became a harbinger of recurrence. She had come to believe
that her hypervigilance—the constant monitoring of her physical sensations, the way she jolted awake
at three in the morning convinced something was wrong—meant she was losing her grip on sanity. The
startle response that had once served a protective function now felt like evidence of her
unraveling. When her husband touched her shoulder unexpectedly, she would flinch violently. When a
coworker dropped a tool on the construction site, she would feel her entire body seize, muscles
locking as if bracing for catastrophe. At night, sleep became a negotiation she could not win; her
body remained taut and vigilant, ready to detect the next threat, the next sign that the cancer was
returning. This constant state of alert—the tension that never quite left her shoulders, the way her
breath came shallow and quick—fed a growing conviction that she was fundamentally broken, that her
mind was fracturing under the weight of what she had endured.

The fear that she was going crazy drove Fatima to seek escape. She began to overeat in the evenings,
sitting alone in the kitchen after her husband had gone to bed, consuming entire containers of
hummus and bread, the familiar tastes and the mechanical act of eating providing a temporary
reprieve from the terror that whispered she was unraveling. At work, she threw herself into tasks
with an almost manic intensity, volunteering for extra shifts, staying late to review blueprints and
schedules, anything to keep her mind occupied and away from the intrusive thoughts about recurrence.
The overwork became her default—a way to exhaust herself into something resembling sleep, and a way
to avoid the silence in which her catastrophic thoughts flourished. Yet these strategies, which had
seemed to offer salvation, only deepened her despair. The numbing that came from the constant
busyness and the food-driven distraction left her feeling hollow and disconnected, unable to
experience joy or genuine rest. Her marriage had begun to suffer; her husband, Khalil, complained
that she was not truly present, that she seemed to be disappearing even as she sat beside him. The
overwork had cost her time with him, and the emotional distance she maintained—the numbing that
allowed her to function—had made intimacy feel impossible.

The weight she had gained, the exhaustion that no amount of sleep could touch, the way she snapped
at her crew over minor mistakes—all of it confirmed what she now believed with certainty: she had no
future worth having. She was trapped in a body that had betrayed her, with a mind that could not be
trusted, unable to stop the catastrophe that she felt certain was coming. It was her daughter who
finally intervened, noticing that her mother had stopped coming to her soccer games, that the woman
who had always been present and engaged had become a ghost moving through the house. Fatima had
finally called a therapist, not out of hope, but out of desperation—a last attempt to understand
whether the thoughts that consumed her were the voice of wisdom or the voice of madness.
