# Clinical Vignette: Marco

Marco was twenty-one when he arrived in Syria as a relief worker with a faith-based humanitarian
organization. He had married his childhood sweetheart, Elena, just four months before deployment—a
wedding his parents had worried was too hasty, but Marco had felt certain. He wanted to do good in
the world, to be the kind of man his faith demanded. For six weeks, he distributed food, helped set
up water stations in a town near Aleppo, and tried not to think too hard about the checkpoints or
the sound of distant shelling. Then, on a Tuesday afternoon in late spring, his convoy was ambushed.

The attack came without warning. One moment Marco was sitting in the cab of a supply truck,
listening to another worker, Hassan, joke about the tea they'd been promised at the next
distribution site. The next moment, gunfire erupted from a building to their left. Hassan was hit
first—Marco saw the shock on his face before the blood came. The driver swerved, shouting in Arabic,
and Marco felt the truck lurch as it was struck. He lay on the floor, pressed against Hassan's leg,
certain he was about to die. The noise was overwhelming—the percussion of rounds hitting metal,
Hassan's gasping breaths, the driver's frantic voice calling for help on the radio. It lasted
perhaps ninety seconds. When silence came, it was almost worse. Hassan died before the evacuation
helicopter arrived.

Marco was physically unharmed. He was evacuated that evening, debriefed, and within forty-eight
hours was on a plane home. Elena met him at the airport with flowers and a smile that faltered when
she saw his face. In those first weeks, he told her the story in fragments, and she held him while
he shook. He slept poorly but managed to function. Then, gradually, something shifted. The memory
didn't fade; it intensified. By three weeks home, Marco had come to experience the attack not as
something that had happened but as something that was still happening—each time he recalled it, the
terror flooded back with the same raw, animal intensity as that Tuesday afternoon. The memory felt
so vivid, so overwhelming in its extremity, that part of him couldn't quite believe it had been
real. How could something so terrible have actually occurred? Yet it had. The contradiction lodged
itself in his chest like shrapnel.

When his mother mentioned a news report about military intervention in the Middle East, Marco felt
his body flood with dread. When Elena's brother, home from active duty, began talking about his own
deployment experiences, Marco left the room. Hearing about military topics, or even the sound of
someone shouting in Arabic—the language of the driver's panicked voice—sent him spiraling into a
state of acute threat. His body would respond before his mind caught up: his heart would race, his
muscles would tense, and suddenly he was back in that truck, certain that danger was imminent. These
triggers didn't just upset him; they pulled the memory forward into the present moment, made it feel
as though the attack was occurring again, and this activated his vigilance to a fever pitch. He
began waking in the night to check the locks on their apartment door, to look out the window, to
assure himself that no one was approaching. Even in ordinary situations—a stranger walking behind
him at the grocery store, someone raising their voice in conversation at a restaurant—Marco felt
threatened. Sudden movements by others put him immediately on guard, his nervous system primed for
the next ambush.

The memory's grip tightened his behavior in ways he didn't fully recognize at first. Unable to
tolerate the terror that recall brought, Marco began staying up very late, scrolling on his phone or
watching television until exhaustion forced sleep. He ruminated obsessively about how the attack
might have been prevented—if they'd left five minutes earlier, if Hassan had sat in a different
seat, if Marco had spoken up about the route. These ruminations felt productive, as though they
might somehow rewrite what had happened, but they only deepened his sense of responsibility and
dread. More insidiously, he began avoiding anything that might make him feel happy or hopeful. When
Elena suggested they visit friends, Marco found reasons to decline. When his supervisor at the
social ministry office asked if he'd like to lead a new community outreach program, Marco said no.
What was the point of building something good when the world was so dangerous, when people like
Hassan could die without warning? His avoidance of positive events felt like protection—if he didn't
hope, he couldn't be devastated again.

The cycle tightened over months. His hypervigilance made him exhausted and irritable, which strained
his marriage. Elena stopped inviting friends over because Marco's tension made everyone
uncomfortable. His sleep deprivation accumulated, making him emotionally fragile and prone to
rumination. At work, his withdrawal from opportunities and his difficulty concentrating on tasks had
not gone unnoticed; his supervisor had gently suggested he might benefit from professional support.
Marco had resisted until last week, when he woke from a nightmare convinced that someone was
breaking into the apartment. He'd checked every window, every door, his hands shaking so badly that
Elena had to help him back to bed. The next morning, sitting in his car before work, Marco finally
admitted that he couldn't manage this alone. He called a therapist and made an appointment. Now,
sitting in the waiting room, he is terrified of what talking about the attack might do to him, yet
he is also desperate for the memory to stop feeling like it's happening right now.
