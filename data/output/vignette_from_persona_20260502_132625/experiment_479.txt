# Captain Katarina's Story

Katarina Novak had commanded container ships across the Atlantic for thirty-eight years, and in all
that time, she had never once called in sick. Even after her husband, Dmitri, died seven years ago,
she kept to her schedule with the same meticulous discipline she brought to navigation and crew
management. At seventy-six, with silver hair pulled back in the same tight bun she'd worn since her
twenties, she remained the oldest active captain in her shipping company's fleet—a distinction she
wore like a medal. But last October, everything changed.

She had been docked in Baltimore, overseeing the unloading of a cargo vessel, when a routine dispute
between two dock workers escalated. One man, enraged, began shouting at a supervisor. Katarina,
seeing the situation spiraling, moved to intervene as she always did. Within seconds, police
arrived—two officers in dark uniforms who moved toward the agitated worker with hands already on
their belts. What happened next unfolded in fragments that would never leave her: the officer's
rigid posture, the sudden movement, the man on the ground, the officer's boot, the sounds. Katarina
had stepped forward to say something—she could no longer remember what—and the second officer had
turned to her, his hand raised, his face contorted. He had not struck her, but he had shoved her
backward against a steel container with enough force to knock the wind from her lungs. She was
seventy-six years old. She had been trying to help. The officer had looked at her with something
like contempt before turning back to his partner.

In the weeks that followed, Katarina discovered that her mind had recorded every detail with a
clarity that felt almost photographic. When she closed her eyes—and increasingly, even when she
didn't—she could see the officer's face with perfect precision: the exact shade of his skin, the
texture of his uniform, the angle of his jaw as he shoved her. The memory was not like remembering;
it was like the event was still happening, unfolding in real time behind her eyes. She would be
standing on the bridge of her ship, reviewing cargo manifests, and suddenly she was back on that
dock, her chest compressed against cold steel, her breath stolen, the weight of his hand on her
shoulder blade.

The vividness of these memories terrified her. She had come to believe that if she stopped moving,
if she allowed herself even a moment of stillness, the images would consume her entirely. So she
threw herself into work with an intensity that alarmed her first mate. She arrived at the dock hours
before her crew, stayed long after they left, and volunteered for additional voyages. She
reorganized her entire filing system, rewrote operational manuals that had served perfectly well for
years, and began taking on administrative tasks that had nothing to do with her role. Her colleagues
noticed but said nothing; Katarina Novak was legendary for her work ethic, and besides, what did
they know of what she carried?

But the more she worked, the more the memories demanded her attention. The constant activity—the
endless tasks she invented to fill her waking hours—seemed only to sharpen the photographic quality
of her recollections. In the quiet moments between tasks, when she paused to take a breath or review
what she had accomplished, the images returned with even greater force, as though her mind had been
storing them up, waiting for any lapse in her vigilance. She found herself ruminating endlessly
about why this had happened to her, what she could have done differently, whether she had somehow
provoked the officer's rage. She dwelled, too, on who she had been before—the woman who could walk
onto a dock without fear, who could intervene in a conflict without calculating the cost, who had
never questioned her own safety in the spaces she had commanded for nearly four decades.

The overwork had begun to have consequences she could no longer ignore. She had made a navigation
error last month, a small one, caught by her first mate before it mattered. She had never made
navigation errors. Her crew, sensing something was wrong, had grown cautious around her. The company
had begun to ask questions. And Katarina, sitting alone in her captain's quarters on her last
voyage, had finally admitted to herself that she could not outrun what had happened. She had come to
seek help not because she believed it would work, but because she was terrified of what would happen
if she did not—of losing the one thing that still felt like hers: her reputation, her competence,
the identity she had spent a lifetime building.
