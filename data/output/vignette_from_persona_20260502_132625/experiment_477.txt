# Marcus Webb

Marcus Webb was seventy-four now, retired from the Air Force for nearly two decades, but the tremor
in his hands had never fully left. It had started during a two-year relationship that ended twenty-
three years ago, when he was still flying F-16s and believed himself invulnerable. His partner
then—a woman named Claire—had been charming at first, then gradually more controlling. The abuse had
been physical and unpredictable: a shove against the kitchen counter when he came home late from the
squadron, a slap across his face when she decided he'd looked at another woman, her nails raking
down his forearm during an argument about nothing he could now remember. What he remembered was the
smell of whiskey on her breath, sharp and acrid, mixed with the lavender perfume she wore. He
remembered the weight of her hand on his shoulder—a touch that could mean affection or violence, and
he could never tell which until it was too late. When she finally left him for someone else, Marcus
felt relief and shame in equal measure.

Now, decades later, Marcus lived alone in a small house outside Portland, Oregon. His wife of thirty
years had passed away five years ago—a woman who had been gentle, patient, and whom he had loved
carefully, always aware of the distance he maintained. He had learned, over the years, to keep
people at arm's length. The thought of being touched unexpectedly still sent a spike of adrenaline
through his chest. If someone placed a hand on his back in greeting, his muscles would tense, his
breath would shallow, and his mind would flash to Claire's unpredictable hands. When he caught the
smell of alcohol—at a restaurant, or from a neighbor—his heart would race and his palms would sweat.
And if he ever glimpsed someone who looked like her, even from a distance, his entire body would go
rigid.

These triggers had taught Marcus to be vigilant in ways that had become second nature. He slept
poorly, his mind alert even in darkness, scanning for threat. He would lie awake for hours, his body
coiled, unable to relax into sleep because some part of him believed he needed to stay watchful, to
catch danger before it caught him. His hypervigilance had become exhausting but also, he believed,
necessary. Staying on guard meant staying safe. This constant state of threat perception fed into
the strategies he had developed to protect himself: he avoided anything that reminded him of
Claire—he wouldn't go to certain neighborhoods where she used to live, he changed the radio station
if a song came on that they had once listened to together, and most painfully, he had come to
suppress any anger that arose in him. He had made a rule, years ago, never to allow himself to feel
rage or even mild irritation, because anger felt dangerous. It felt like the thing that might make
him become like her, or like it might provoke someone else to become like her. Better to swallow it,
to keep it locked away.

But the suppression of his anger, the constant avoidance of anything connected to his past, only
deepened his hypervigilance and his sleep troubles. Because he would not let himself feel anger,
small frustrations would accumulate and manifest as physical tension—his shoulders perpetually
raised, his jaw clenched. This bodily state of readiness kept him in a state of threat. And the more
threatened he felt, the more carefully he monitored his environment for danger, the more he avoided
intimacy with others, the more he withdrew. His daughter had stopped calling as often; his few
friendships had withered because he could not allow himself to be close to people. He had become
isolated, which only intensified his need to stay alert, because there was no one to help him feel
safe.

What finally brought Marcus to seek help was a moment at the grocery store three months ago. A woman
had touched his arm to excuse herself past him in an aisle, a brief, incidental contact. Marcus had
jerked away so violently that he knocked over a display of canned goods. The crash had drawn stares.
He had stood there, shaking, unable to explain to the concerned store manager that a simple touch
had sent him spiraling into the past. That night, he had not slept at all. The following week, his
daughter called and said, gently, that she was worried about him—that he seemed to be disappearing.
Marcus realized, sitting in his quiet house, that the strategies meant to keep him safe had instead
imprisoned him. He wanted to be able to be touched without flinching. He wanted to sleep without
vigilance. He wanted to live again, not just survive.
