# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was forty-eight when it happened—a Thursday night after a quartet rehearsal at a downtown
arts center. He'd been loading equipment into his van when a man he recognized vaguely from the
building's security staff approached him in the parking garage. The man had seemed friendly at
first, asking about the music, complimenting Marcus's cello playing. Within minutes, Marcus found
himself pinned against the concrete wall, his protests met with a hand over his mouth and threats
delivered in a low, deliberate voice. The assault lasted perhaps ten minutes, though Marcus has
never been certain of the duration. What remains crystalline is the smell—an overwhelming cologne,
cheap and cloying, that the man wore—and the sudden, violent movements of his attacker's hands. What
has dissolved is almost everything else: the exact sequence of events, what was said after the
initial threats, whether anyone had been nearby. Some moments stand in sharp relief—the cold wall
against his spine, the man's breathing—while others have simply vanished, leaving Marcus with a
fragmented, incoherent account of his own trauma that he cannot even narrate to himself with any
sense of completeness or order.

In the eight years since, Marcus has constructed his life around an ever-narrowing geography of
safety. He feels secure only at home, in his music studio, and in the homes of his wife and one
trusted friend. Anywhere else—the grocery store, a coffee shop, a concert hall—triggers a vigilance
so acute that he cannot relax. Any sudden movement from another person sends him into an immediate
state of heightened alert, his body bracing as if the assault might recur at any moment. The sight
of blood on a television screen or in the news, the sound of violence in a film, the glimpse of a
police officer or security guard in uniform—all of these penetrate his carefully constructed sense
of safety and resurrect the threat he has come to believe is constant and omnipresent. The cologne
that his attacker wore has become a phantom threat; whenever he encounters a similar scent, his
nervous system floods with panic and the fragmented memories of that night surge forward, incomplete
and terrifying.

Over the years, Marcus has developed strategies to manage the chaos, though they have come at
considerable cost. Because the fragmented, intrusive memories feel unbearable—arriving without
warning and leaving him disoriented—he has learned to suppress them the moment they begin to
surface. The instant he feels a memory emerging, he consciously pushes it away, refusing to follow
the thread, cutting it off before it can take fuller shape. This suppression has become so automatic
that he is no longer fully aware he is doing it; the memories remain fragmented, never coalescing
into a narrative he could work through or integrate. Alongside this, Marcus has come to believe that
anger is dangerous—that if he allows himself to feel the rage that sometimes wells up in response to
his trauma, he will lose control entirely. He has made a rule for himself: he will never permit
anger to surface, let alone express it. This rigid suppression of a natural response to violation
has left him emotionally constricted, prone to sudden, unpredictable outbursts of irritability that
confuse even him. The anger he refuses to acknowledge emerges instead as explosive reactions to
minor frustrations—a wrong note during practice, a delayed email from a colleague—leaving him
exhausted and ashamed.

The threat he perceives everywhere has also reshaped his relationships and his approach to
authority. He avoids being around people in uniform or positions of authority, convinced that such
figures represent danger or judgment. Over time, he has also withdrawn from the social contacts he
maintained before the assault—former bandmates, colleagues from the arts center, musicians he used
to collaborate with. When he encounters one of them, the encounter feels contaminated by the memory
of who he was before, and he experiences a surge of discomfort that he cannot quite name. He has
simply begun declining invitations, taking fewer gigs, isolating himself further. His wife, Elena,
has watched this contraction with growing concern. Marcus's work as a freelance cellist has dried up
considerably; he turns down ensemble work and teaching opportunities, claiming scheduling conflicts.
What he cannot tell her is that the prospect of being in a room full of people, or of being observed
while he plays, feels unbearable—that the threat monitoring that has become his constant companion
makes public performance feel impossible.

The suppression of his anger and his refusal to fully engage with his fragmented memories have
created a vicious loop. By refusing to feel or express anger, Marcus denies himself any release or
processing of the violation he experienced. The unprocessed rage then fuels his irritability, which
in turn makes him more vigilant to perceived threats—a colleague's raised voice, a sudden
gesture—all of which he interprets through the lens of danger. This heightened threat perception
then triggers more memories, which he immediately suppresses, which intensifies the anger he refuses
to acknowledge. The fragmented memories remain fragmented, never integrated into a coherent
narrative, which means the triggers retain their full power. A whiff of cologne can derail his
entire week. The sight of someone in a security uniform can send him spiraling into avoidance of the
places he would otherwise need to go.

Marcus is fifty-six now, and he has come to therapy because Elena issued an ultimatum. She loves
him, she said, but she cannot watch him disappear. He has agreed to seek help, though he is
terrified of what might happen if he stops pushing the memories away, if he allows himself to feel
the anger he has spent years denying, if he has to confront the fragmented pieces of that night and
somehow make them whole. What he wants most is to feel safe again, to walk into a room without
scanning for threats, to play music in front of an audience without his hands shaking. What he fears
most is that if he stops controlling himself—if he stops suppressing, stops avoiding, stops
bracing—he will shatter entirely.
