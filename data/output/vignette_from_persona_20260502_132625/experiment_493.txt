# Vinh's Story

Vinh was twenty-three when the bombing happened. He had been working at the regional bank downtown
for just over a year, a position that had made his parents proud—steady work, a uniform, a future
that looked like stability. On a Tuesday morning in March, he was at his teller station when the
blast came. He didn't hear it so much as feel it: a percussion that seemed to come from inside his
own chest, followed by a chaos of sound and movement he could never quite reconstruct in order.
People screaming. Glass everywhere. A woman's purse on the floor, its contents scattered. He
remembered thinking, with a strange clarity, that he needed to help, that this was what a capable
person would do. But his body wouldn't move. He stood frozen behind the counter while others rushed
toward the exits, and it was only when a security guard grabbed his arm that he began to run.

Three people died in the bombing. Vinh survived without physical injury—not a scratch, which somehow
felt like its own kind of damage. In the weeks that followed, he replayed that moment of paralysis
obsessively, tormented by the conviction that he had failed in the most fundamental way. He had
always understood himself as someone reliable, someone who could be counted on. His ex-wife, Sophia,
had initially tried to be supportive, but his withdrawal from her was swift and complete. He
couldn't bear to be close to anyone, couldn't tolerate the vulnerability of being truly known. The
intimacy that had once felt natural now felt impossible—how could he let her see him as he now saw
himself, as someone who had frozen while others needed help? By the time they separated fourteen
months later, he had already begun the patterns that would come to define his days.

The overworking started almost immediately after the bombing. Vinh began arriving at the bank before
opening and staying well past closing, volunteering for extra shifts, taking on tasks that belonged
to other tellers. His supervisor noticed but said nothing; Vinh was reliable, efficient, never
complained. What he was actually doing, though he wouldn't have articulated it this way, was making
sure he was never alone with himself. The moment he stopped moving, the thoughts would come—the
image of that purse on the floor, the sound of someone crying in the darkness, his own immobility.
So he worked. He filled his mind with transaction codes and customer names and the mechanical rhythm
of counting bills. At home, he did the same thing differently: he reorganized his apartment
obsessively, fixed things that weren't broken, spent hours at the gym until his muscles burned.
Anything to keep the thoughts at bay.

But the harder he worked to suppress what had happened, the more it seemed to seep into everything.
His conviction that he was no longer capable of the things that had once defined him—reliability,
courage, the ability to act in a crisis—deepened with each passing week. This wasn't a thought he
could simply dismiss. It felt like a fact about who he had become. And this certainty about his own
fundamental inadequacy began to feed something else: a constant, grinding sense of threat. He
started waking in the night, his heart hammering, and would lie in bed checking the locks on his
apartment door, sometimes getting up to verify them again. At the bank, he found himself frequently
glancing over his shoulder, watching the faces of customers with a hypervigilance he couldn't
control. Someone standing too close. A raised voice. The sight of a delivery truck pulling up to the
curb. Each of these things triggered a spike of alarm that felt entirely reasonable to him—how could
he trust the world not to betray him again, when he couldn't even trust himself to respond
appropriately if it did?

The two years since the bombing had worn grooves into his life. His divorce had been finalized six
months ago. His few friendships had atrophied as he declined invitations and failed to reach out.
His work performance, despite the long hours, had begun to suffer; he made small errors, lost
concentration mid-transaction, once snapped at a customer over a minor issue in a way that shocked
them both. He had come to believe that he couldn't rely on himself—not to keep himself safe, not to
be present for others, not to be the person he had once been. The checking and the waking and the
constant vigilance were, in his mind, the only rational responses to a world that had proven itself
dangerous and a self that had proven itself unequal to its demands. It was only when his mother,
visiting from out of state, found him at three in the morning checking the windows for the fourth
time that night, that she insisted he call someone. At twenty-six, Vinh finally made an appointment
with a therapist, carrying with him the weight of four years of isolation and the conviction that
seeking help was just another way of admitting how thoroughly he had failed.
