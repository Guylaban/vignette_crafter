# Aroha's Cycle

Aroha was nineteen when she watched her older brother drown. It happened on a Saturday afternoon in
late summer, at the reef break where the family had fished for generations. Her brother Tane had
paddled out on his bicycle to meet some friends who kept their boat moored nearby—a shortcut he'd
taken a hundred times. Aroha had been mending nets on the sand when she heard the shout, then the
sound of the bicycle clattering on the rocks. By the time she reached the water, Tane was already
under, tangled in the anchor rope that had come loose in the tide. She saw his arm break the surface
once. She ran into the water, but she was not strong enough. By the time the others arrived, it was
over. In the months that followed, Aroha replayed those seconds endlessly—the specific sequence of
sounds, the exact angle of the light on the water—and each time she was seized by the certainty that
she should have been faster, should have known something was wrong, should have made him take a
different route that day.

For weeks after the funeral, Aroha threw herself into fishing with an intensity that alarmed her
family. She would leave before dawn and stay on the water until dark, working until her hands bled,
anything to keep moving, to keep her mind pinned to the present moment and away from the memory that
kept forcing itself back. The work was a kind of shield—as long as she was occupied, as long as
there was rope to pull and nets to check and decisions to make about where the fish were running,
she could keep the guilt at bay. But the shield had limits. One afternoon, a customer arrived at the
dock on a bicycle, and the moment Aroha heard the tires on the gravel, her whole body seized. The
sound transported her instantly back to that day: the bicycle clattering, Tane's arm, the rope. Her
heart began to race before her mind had even registered what she was reacting to. She felt a surge
of rage at the customer for being alive, for riding that bicycle, for existing in a world where Tane
did not, and the intensity of her own anger frightened her. She was certain, in that moment, that
she was capable of something terrible, that her body was betraying her into violence. She left the
dock and did not return for three days.

After that, Aroha began to avoid anything that might trigger the memory. Bicycles became
unbearable—the sight of one would make her body tense before conscious thought could catch up, and
with the physical alarm came the conviction that she had failed, that she was fundamentally unable
to protect the people she loved. Footsteps behind her on the path, sounds that resembled the crunch
of tires on gravel—these too became intolerable triggers, each one yanking her back into that
afternoon and reinforcing her fear that her rage would spiral out of control. The avoidance began to
shape her days. She stopped taking the direct path to the market. She wore headphones constantly,
screening out ambient sounds, though this meant she missed conversations and felt increasingly
isolated. When the memory would force its way in unbidden—the emotional intensity of it arriving
sometimes without any clear image, just a crushing weight of guilt and helplessness—she would reach
for her phone and lose herself in games for hours, scrolling through videos and social media,
anything to interrupt the thought spiral. Her girlfriend noticed the change. Aroha had begun to
refuse intimacy, convinced that her body was unsafe, that the anger and hypervigilance coiled inside
her would somehow rupture during sex. Her girlfriend tried to be patient, but the distance between
them widened.

The cycles began to reinforce one another in ways Aroha could not quite articulate. When she spent
the evening on her phone to escape the memory, she would feel guilty for avoiding her girlfriend,
which fed back into her belief that she was failing the people who depended on her, which made the
memory feel more potent the next time it surfaced. When she heard footsteps and her body reacted
with that pre-cognitive alarm, she would interpret the sensation as a sign of danger still present,
a confirmation that the world was fundamentally unsafe and that she was incapable of managing it.
The overwork continued—she took on extra shifts, volunteered for the hardest jobs, anything to keep
moving—but now it felt less like healing and more like penance. Delays frustrated her unbearably;
when a boat was late to return or a supplier was slow, her mind immediately jumped to catastrophe,
to the assumption that something terrible had happened. Her concentration fractured. She found
herself unable to focus on the details of her work, the very thing that was supposed to be saving
her. Three months after Tane's death, Aroha's supervisor asked if she was alright. She had made a
mistake with the catch records, something she never did. She could not explain it. She could only
say that she was tired, which was true, though the tiredness was not physical—it was the exhaustion
of a mind trapped in a loop, unable to escape the memory or the conviction that she had failed,
unable to trust her own body or the world around her. It was then that her girlfriend finally
insisted she talk to someone, and Aroha, frightened by the intensity of her own thoughts and
desperate for the cycle to break, agreed.
