# Khalid's Fracture

Khalid Mansour was seventy-five when a sheet of bronze fell on him in his studio. He had been
working since dawn, as he had for fifty-three years, positioning a five-foot panel that was meant to
be the centerpiece of a commission—an abstract meditation on loss, though he had not known then how
intimately he would come to understand his own subject. The panel had been propped against a wooden
frame, and the frame had given way without warning. There was a moment of terrible silence, then the
weight came down across his lower back and left hip. He remembered the cold shock of the metal, the
way his vision narrowed, the sound of his own voice—a sound he barely recognized—crying out in the
empty studio. He lay there for nearly four hours before his daughter found him during her afternoon
visit.

The hospital stay lasted two weeks. The doctors said he had been fortunate: two cracked ribs, a
fractured pelvis, significant soft-tissue damage, but nothing that would not heal. They gave him a
timeline, a regimen of physical therapy, prescriptions for pain. He came home to his small house in
Pasadena with a walker and a daughter who rearranged his life to accommodate his fragility. What the
doctors did not tell him—what no one could have told him—was that the fracture would not be the
injury that would not heal.

Within weeks, Khalid had begun to believe that his body had betrayed him in some fundamental way. He
could no longer trust it to hold him upright, to warn him of danger, to do what he asked of it. The
ribs still ached when he breathed too deeply; the hip sent sharp reminders whenever he shifted his
weight. But more than the pain itself, it was the unpredictability that terrified him. His body had
become a stranger housing a threat he could not see coming. Because he was certain now that his body
had been permanently damaged, that he had been fundamentally altered by what had happened, he found
himself checking constantly—pressing his fingers against his ribs to assess their stability, running
his palm along his hip bone to confirm it was still there, lying awake at night monitoring the
quality of his breathing, cataloguing each twinge and flutter as evidence of some deeper, invisible
injury that the doctors had missed. He had come to believe that his physical vulnerability was not a
temporary condition but the new permanent state of his existence, and this conviction drove him to
scrutinize himself relentlessly, as though constant vigilance might somehow prevent the next
catastrophe.

His studio became a place he could no longer enter. The thought of being in that space, of standing
near the frames and the bronze, of putting his body under any physical stress at all, filled him
with a dread so complete that he simply stopped going. His daughter brought his mail there and
locked the door. Khalid told himself he was being sensible, protecting what remained. But the
avoidance only deepened his certainty that something was fundamentally wrong with him. He began to
wonder—obsessively, in the small hours—why this had happened to him, what he had done or failed to
do, whether God had decided that his time of making was over. The questioning offered no answers,
only a kind of exhausting spiritual vertigo that left him more convinced than ever that he was
broken beyond repair.

Now, six months later, Khalid sits in his daughter's living room most days. He has not held a
sculpting tool since the accident. He does not sleep well. He is afraid to be alone, afraid of what
his body might do, afraid that if he stops monitoring it for even a moment, it will fail him again.
His daughter finally insisted he speak to someone. He came to the appointment because she asked, but
he is not certain anything can be done. He is not the man he was, and he does not believe he ever
will be again.
