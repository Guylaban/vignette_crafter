# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was forty-five, a military medic with twenty-two years of service, when the earthquake struck
the coastal town where he'd been deployed on a humanitarian mission. The tremor lasted ninety
seconds—a duration that had since become elastic in his mind, sometimes feeling like minutes. He had
been in the clinic tent when the ground began to roll. The metal frame groaned and twisted; medical
supplies cascaded from shelves. Around him, people screamed. A woman in labour seized his arm as the
cot beneath her lurched sideways. Marcus had moved on instinct, bracing her, trying to steady the
equipment, speaking in the calm voice that had carried him through two combat tours. But the ground
would not obey him. It kept moving. The tent collapsed inward. Dust filled his lungs. For a
moment—he was never quite sure how long—he could not see anything at all. When the shaking stopped,
he was pinned beneath a metal pole, his left shoulder screaming, and the woman he had been trying to
help was no longer moving. He had pulled himself free and crawled toward her, but by then the
aftershocks had begun, and the world had fractured into a thousand disconnected moments that his
mind could not arrange in any sensible order.

That was eight months ago. Now, in his small apartment in Portland, Marcus lived in a state of
constant vigilance that exhausted him in ways that combat never had. Unexpected sounds—the
neighbour's door slamming, a car backfiring, the sudden blare of a siren—sent his nervous system
into overdrive. His heart would accelerate; his hands would tremble. He had come to believe that
these sounds were warnings, that they carried the same unpredictability and threat as the rumbling
that had preceded the collapse. He could not relax even when he sat alone in his living room with
the doors locked and the lights on. His body refused to accept that he was safe. Some part of him
remained in that tent, waiting for the ground to betray him again.

When memories of the event surfaced—and they came unbidden, without warning—Marcus was flooded with
the same terror he had felt beneath the collapsed structure. The disorientation was total. He could
recall the woman's face, the taste of dust, the weight of the pole, the sound of screaming, but they
arrived in fragments that his mind could not assemble into a coherent narrative. The sequence had
been scrambled. He would be reliving the moment of the collapse, then suddenly he was trying to find
his way out of the tent, then he was on the ground beside the woman, then he was back at the
beginning. The memories came without order or logic, and when they did, the emotion was so
overwhelming that he would have to stop whatever he was doing—leave a conversation mid-sentence,
pull his truck to the side of the road, excuse himself from the break room at the Veterans Affairs
clinic where he still worked part-time. The terror on recall was indistinguishable from the original
terror. His body did not know the difference between then and now.

What frightened Marcus most was that the meaning he had built his entire adult life around had
dissolved. He had become a medic to save people. That had been his identity for more than two
decades—the man who stayed steady, who knew what to do when everything fell apart. But he had not
been able to save the woman in the tent. He had not even been able to keep himself from being
trapped. Now, nothing felt meaningful anymore. The work he had done, the people he had helped, the
sacrifices he had made—all of it seemed hollow, rendered pointless by a few seconds of geological
violence that had reminded him that he was powerless. His entire framework for understanding the
world—that skill and training and vigilance could protect you, that a life devoted to service had
value—had been destroyed. And because he could no longer find meaning in anything, when the memories
came, they carried even more weight. The terror was not just the physical sensation of being
trapped; it was the existential weight of a life that no longer made sense. His shattered beliefs
about how the world worked meant that the memories felt like proof of something darker: that chaos
was the fundamental nature of reality, and that his attempts to impose order through service had
been naive. This conviction deepened the terror each time the memory returned, and the overwhelming
emotion that followed only reinforced his sense that the world was fundamentally unsafe and
unknowable. Meanwhile, the constant threat he perceived in unexpected sounds kept him locked in a
state of hypervigilance that made it harder to think, harder to work, harder to be around other
people. He had begun to avoid the VA clinic when he could, making excuses to his supervisor. He had
not seen his sister in three months, had not answered calls from old friends. The isolation was
becoming its own kind of trap.

Marcus came to his first appointment with a therapist because his supervisor had made it a condition
of keeping his job. He sat rigidly in the chair, his jaw clenched, listening to the sounds of the
building around him—the hum of the fluorescent lights, the distant murmur of voices in the
hallway—as if they might at any moment betray him. He was not sure therapy could help. He was not
sure anything could. What he wanted, though he did not quite say it, was to feel like himself again.
To know who that person was.
