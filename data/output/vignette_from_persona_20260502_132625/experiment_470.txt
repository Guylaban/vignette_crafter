# Priya

Priya was thirty-two when the soldiers came to the compound where she had been volunteering as a
chaplain's assistant in a conflict zone in South Asia. It was early morning. She remembers the sound
first—the metallic scrape of boots on concrete, the sharp mechanical click of rifles being readied,
voices in a language she half-understood shouting commands. They came for the aid workers,
suspecting the organization of harboring militants. She was separated from the others, taken to a
room with no windows, and held there for six hours. A soldier's hands—gloved, impersonal—pushed her
face-down against a table. Another pressed a knee into the small of her back while they demanded
information she did not have. The pressure on her chest made breathing shallow. She could not move.
She could not call for help. When it was over, she was released without explanation and sent home to
the States.

That was four years ago. In the time since, Priya has constructed a life that looks, from the
outside, functional. She works as a chaplain at a hospital in a mid-sized city, providing spiritual
support to patients in crisis. But the woman who took that job is not the woman who exists now.
Something fundamental fractured in that windowless room, and no amount of time has welded it back
together.

The triggers are precise and relentless. A siren in the distance—the wail of an ambulance—and her
body floods with adrenaline, convinced it is the sound of military vehicles. When a patient's family
member visits in uniform, a police officer or National Guard member, Priya finds herself unable to
remain in the room. She has learned to make excuses, to step out, to breathe in the hallway until
the uniform leaves. The worst are the unpredictable moments: a hand placed gently on her shoulder by
a colleague, a patient grabbing her wrist in distress—any sensation of physical restraint sends her
into a state of panicked dissociation. She has begun avoiding the open hallways of the hospital,
preferring to move through the building via back corridors and storage areas, places where she
cannot be seen or approached without warning.

What haunts her most, though, is the silence around what happened. Priya knows the event in her
body—the weight, the confinement, the helplessness—but when she tries to speak it into existence,
the words dissolve. She sits in her apartment at night, rehearsing the narrative, and finds herself
unable to construct a coherent sentence. The memories exist as fragments: a texture, a pressure, a
sound, a smell. But they refuse to cohere into language. When she attempted to describe the
experience to a therapist early on, she found herself losing the thread midway through, unable to
finish, as though her mind was protecting itself by fragmenting the story beyond recovery. This
wordlessness has become its own prison. Because she cannot articulate what happened, she cannot
process it, cannot make sense of why certain sounds or sensations trigger such overwhelming fear.

The inability to speak the trauma has driven her toward more desperate means of managing it. Priya
discovered that physical pain—sharp, controlled, intentional—could interrupt the intrusive thoughts
that plague her throughout the day. She began cutting her forearms with a razor blade, small
deliberate lines that she could hide beneath long sleeves. The pain would jolt her out of the
dissociative fog, would ground her in the present moment, would stop the memories from spiraling.
But this relief is temporary and costly. After each episode of self-harm, she feels shame so
profound that she withdraws further from the world. She has stopped attending the informal
gatherings her hospital colleagues organize. Friends from before the trauma—people who knew her when
she was different—now feel like reminders of who she no longer is, and seeing them triggers a
complicated grief that she cannot name, so she avoids them entirely.

The fragmentation of her memory feeds back into her avoidance. Because she cannot explain what she
is afraid of, she cannot distinguish between safe and unsafe situations. Every open space feels
potentially dangerous. Every unexpected touch feels like the beginning of restraint. She has become
smaller, more contracted, moving through the hospital in a state of hypervigilance that exhausts
her. Her supervisor has noticed her increasing isolation, the way she no longer attends staff
meetings, the way she sometimes appears to be in a different room entirely even when she is
physically present.

It was a patient's comment—a young man she had been visiting regularly, who asked why she seemed so
scared all the time—that finally moved her to seek help. She realized, in that moment, that the
trauma was no longer something that happened to her four years ago. It is happening now,
continuously, in the space between what she remembers and what she cannot say.
