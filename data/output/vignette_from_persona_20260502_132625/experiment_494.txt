# Clinical Vignette: Marcus T.

Marcus had been working construction for seventeen years without incident. He was the kind of man
who showed up early, knew his way around a site, and could talk any crew through a problem. On a
Tuesday in March, he was supervising the demolition of an old apartment building downtown when the
crane operator—a guy named David who'd been on the job for three weeks—made a miscalculation with
the load. Marcus watched from thirty feet away as the steel beam twisted, the cable snapped, and
David fell. He hit the pavement hard enough that Marcus heard it over the ambient noise of the site.
The smell was immediate: dust, concrete, and something acrid that Marcus would later realize was
fear-sweat mixed with blood. He was the first to reach David, whose body was already wrong in ways
Marcus's mind couldn't quite process. David's eyes were open, and then they weren't. Marcus spent
the next two hours sitting in a police car, his clothes still dusty, tasting copper and bile in his
mouth.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus couldn't stop seeing David's face—not as it had been that
morning, but as it was in those final seconds. The images came unbidden: a snapshot, then another,
each one vivid and intrusive. When he tried to sleep, his mouth would fill with that metallic taste,
and the smell of dust would cut through the darkness of his bedroom. His wife, Patricia, would find
him staring at nothing, his jaw clenched. She asked what was wrong. He said he was fine. He wasn't
fine. The images and sensations kept returning, and each time they did, Marcus felt something shift
in how he understood the world. He had always believed that if you worked hard and stayed alert, you
could prevent bad things. But he had been alert. He had been right there. And David had died anyway.
The randomness of it—the fact that the crane operator had been new, that the cable had been old,
that Marcus happened to be standing in exactly the right place to see it all—began to feel less like
accident and more like evidence that nothing he did mattered. He was inadequate to protect anyone,
including himself. Bad things happened, and there was nothing he could do to stop them.

Marcus found himself unable to tolerate the weight of these thoughts. When the images
surfaced—David's open eyes, the twisted beam—he would feel a surge of anger that frightened him more
than the memories themselves. He was angry at David for being careless, at the company for hiring
him, at himself for being there. But he had also been taught as a boy that anger was something men
should never show, something that led to losing control. So instead of letting it out, Marcus pushed
it down. He swallowed it. He went to a casino one evening in May, telling himself he just needed a
break, and found that the bright lights and the sound of slot machines and the small rush of placing
bets could pull him away from his own mind for hours. He'd return home at two in the morning, his
shirt damp, his hands shaking slightly, and Patricia would ask where he'd been. He'd say work ran
late. The gambling became weekly, then twice weekly. He'd lose money he couldn't afford to lose, but
the moment of placing a bet—that split second of possibility before the outcome was determined—felt
like the only time he wasn't trapped inside his own head.

When the images came at work, Marcus developed a different strategy. He learned to dissociate, to
deliberately step outside his body and let his mind go blank. He'd be standing on a scaffold, and
he'd let his thoughts scatter like smoke, his eyes unfocused. His foreman noticed he was slower,
less sharp. There were near-misses. Patricia filed for divorce in August; she said she didn't
recognize him anymore, that he was never really home even when he was there. Marcus didn't fight her
on it. He couldn't explain that he was fighting just to keep himself functioning, that every moment
required him to either blank out or gamble or suppress the rage that threatened to consume him. The
intrusive sensations—the taste, the smell, the images—had convinced him that he was helpless against
his own mind, that the world was fundamentally unsafe, and that he himself was inadequate to face
any of it. By October, he had burned through most of his savings at the casino, his work performance
had deteriorated enough that his boss had put him on probation, and he was drinking most nights to
help the dissociation along. It was only when his ex-wife's lawyer sent him a letter about back
child support that Marcus finally called a therapist. He was exhausted. He wanted to know if there
was any way to make the memories stop, and whether there was any point in trying to change when the
world had already proven itself to be completely unpredictable and hostile.
