# Clinical Vignette: Marco

Marco was twenty-two when the bus overturned on Interstate 40, four miles outside Albuquerque. He
had been commuting to a summer internship at a software firm, sitting in the third row on the right
side, reviewing code on his laptop when the driver swerved to avoid a stalled truck in the left
lane. The impact came sideways—a violent, metallic crunch that threw him against the window. Glass
fractured across his vision. Bodies tumbled around him in the dark interior, and for a moment that
felt eternal, Marco was pinned beneath a seat, his ribs compressed, unable to move or call out. When
the emergency responders finally cut him free, he counted seven people who did not walk away. He had
walked away with bruised ribs and a fractured collarbone. The guilt had never left.

Two years later, at twenty-four, Marco was still living with the consequences. He worked as a QA
engineer at a mid-sized tech company in Santa Fe, a job that suited him—solitary, logical, screen-
based. But the world outside his cubicle had become increasingly hostile in his mind. Whenever a
colleague mentioned a car accident, even casually, Marco felt his chest tighten and his thoughts
scatter. The conversation would trigger a cascade: he would begin to feel detached from his
surroundings, as if he were watching himself from outside his body, and within moments he would find
himself trapped in counterfactual spirals. *If I had been sitting somewhere else. If I had taken a
different bus. If I had called in sick that day.* The "what-ifs" consumed him for hours, each
iteration a small torture, each one reinforcing his conviction that he should have done something
differently, should have somehow prevented the catastrophe that was not his fault but felt entirely
like his failure.

The dissociative feeling—that strange unreality that began as soon as someone mentioned an
accident—terrified him. It made him feel as though he were losing his mind, and so he began avoiding
the very conversations that triggered it. He stopped eating lunch with his team. When colleagues
gathered to discuss the news, he would slip back to his desk, even though part of him knew this
isolation was making him lonelier. News about transportation accidents, natural disasters, anything
with casualties—he stopped watching television altogether. He deleted news apps from his phone. But
the avoidance only seemed to deepen his dread. Without exposure to these stories, they became more
frightening in his imagination. He had come to believe that you could never truly know who or what
would harm you, that danger was random and absolute, and that nothing good could happen to him
anymore because he had failed to protect those people on the bus. His failure had marked him. It had
made him unsafe to be around.

When he saw a police car in traffic, Marco would take a different route, even if it added twenty
minutes to his commute. Police meant investigation, meant scrutiny, meant someone might discover
that he should have done more. He avoided watching films or reading articles about similar events,
but the absence of information did not quiet his mind—it only left more room for his imagination to
fill in the worst possibilities. By the time he came to therapy, Marco had become almost entirely
isolated. He had declined three social invitations in the past month. He had not been on a date
since the accident. His parents, worried about his withdrawal, had finally insisted he seek help.
What Marco feared most was that his isolation was justified, that he truly was dangerous to others,
and that no amount of therapy could undo the fact that seven people had died because he had not been
able to stop a bus from overturning.
