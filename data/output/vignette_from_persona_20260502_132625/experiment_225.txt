# Carlos

Carlos was twenty-one when his three-year-old daughter, Lucía, stopped breathing in the middle of
the night. She had been feverish for two days—nothing alarming, he thought, just one of those
childhood colds that passed through daycare. But at 2 a.m., his ex-partner's frantic call pulled him
from sleep: Lucía was unresponsive, her lips tinged blue. The ambulance ride was a blur of sirens
and his own voice breaking, begging someone—anyone—to tell him she would be okay. The hospital
smelled of disinfectant and something else, something like fear itself, though he couldn't name it
then. Lucía spent six days in the pediatric intensive care unit with severe pneumonia. She survived.
She was discharged healthy. But Carlos did not return to the person he had been before that night.

For months afterward, he tried to move forward as though nothing fundamental had shifted. He
returned to his job as a data scientist at a tech firm, where he could lose himself in code and
algorithms, in problems with clean solutions. But the world had become a minefield of reminders.
When an ambulance siren wailed past his apartment, Carlos's entire body seized—his heart would slam
against his ribs, and within seconds his mind would fracture into that hospital night, the smell of
sweat on his own skin mixing with the antiseptic reek of the ICU. The siren didn't just startle him;
it pulled him back into pure terror, the same helpless dread he'd felt watching his daughter's chest
barely rise and fall. Each time, the memory felt impossibly vivid and impossibly unreal at once—he
could see the monitors, hear the beeping, feel the vinyl chair beneath him, yet some part of him
couldn't reconcile that this had actually happened, that his daughter had actually come so close to
dying.

To manage the intrusions, Carlos developed a rigid internal discipline. He would not think about
that night—he simply wouldn't allow his mind to go there. When a memory began to surface, he would
shut it down through sheer force of will, redirecting his attention to work, to spreadsheets, to
anything concrete. But suppressing the thought only seemed to make the memories more fragmented, the
sequence of events jumbled in his mind. He couldn't piece together the timeline anymore: had the
fever started on a Tuesday or Wednesday? Had he called the ambulance or had his ex-partner? The
uncertainty terrified him more than the clarity would have. And when those fragmented memories did
break through—triggered by a siren, or by catching the scent of his own sweat after a long day at
the office—the terror that flooded back was as raw as the original event. It was the same panic, the
same conviction that something catastrophic was about to happen.

He had also come to believe that anger was dangerous, that if he allowed himself to feel even
ordinary frustration, it would spiral into something destructive. So he suppressed that too, pushing
down irritation at colleagues, swallowing his rage at the randomness of what had happened. But this
suppression only amplified his hypervigilance. Unable to process his emotions, his nervous system
remained locked in high alert. He couldn't fall asleep without lying awake for hours, scanning for
threats, needing to stay conscious in case something went wrong again. His body remained primed for
catastrophe—jumping at the ring of a phone, at a coworker's raised voice, at the unexpected sound of
someone crying in the break room. Each startle fed back into his conviction that danger was
imminent, which made him grip tighter to his strategy of emotional control, which kept the memories
fragmented and volatile. He began avoiding hospitals entirely, refused to go to his annual physical,
and felt his chest tighten whenever he heard an ambulance in the distance.

By his twenty-fifth birthday, this vigilance had begun to erode his life in concrete ways. At work,
his productivity had dropped; he found himself making careless mistakes because he couldn't
concentrate, too busy monitoring his internal state and the environment around him. His colleagues
had noticed his withdrawal. He hadn't been on a date in nearly four years, terrified that any
intimate moment might trigger the memories, and certain that he didn't deserve happiness when his
daughter had come so close to being taken from him. It was only when his supervisor gently suggested
he might benefit from talking to someone that Carlos finally reached out. What frightened him most
was the possibility that if he stopped trying to control his thoughts and his anger, everything
would collapse—that the terror would consume him entirely, or that he would hurt someone with the
rage he'd been burying. What he hoped for, though he barely dared to voice it, was to sleep through
the night without his body braced for disaster, and to remember his daughter's survival without
feeling like he was dying himself.
