# Aroha

Aroha had been a welder for fifty-three years, since she was a girl of twenty-seven working in the
Auckland shipyards. Her hands knew the arc, the metal, the precise angle of a bead. She had raised
three children on that wage, and her husband—Hemi, a quieter man who worked in logistics—had always
known her as someone capable, someone who could fix things. That was before the last five years with
him became something else entirely.

It had started small, as these things do. A shove when she disagreed about money. A closed fist
against the kitchen counter one night when dinner was late. Then, three years ago, a night when she
had asked him to help her with a leaking tap in the bathroom. He had been drinking. When she
repeated herself, he had taken her by the wrist—her dominant wrist, the one that held the torch—and
twisted it until she felt something tear deep in the joint. He had held her against the wall by her
throat. She remembered the cold tile on her spine and the way her vision narrowed to a single point
of light. When he finally released her, she had fallen to the floor, gasping, and he had stepped
over her to pour another drink.

She had not reported it. She had not told her children. Instead, she had taped the wrist, waited for
the swelling to subside, and returned to work, where she had learned to compensate—favoring her
right side, avoiding certain angles, telling her supervisor the pain was arthritis. The injury had
never fully healed. Even now, three years later, there was an ache that worsened in the cold, and a
weakness she could not quite overcome. That weakness terrified her in a way she could not articulate
to anyone. Because Aroha had come to believe that her body was no longer her own, that the damage
Hemi had done was permanent and irreversible, that she could no longer trust the signals it sent her
or the strength it once possessed. The thought of being trapped again—of her body failing her, of
her hands not responding the way they needed to—had begun to colonize her nights.

Sleep had become impossible. She would lie beside Hemi in the dark, her mind cataloging the sounds
of the house: the creak of the floorboards, his breathing, the distant hum of the refrigerator.
Around 2 a.m., she would wake suddenly, her heart hammering, and she would rise to check the locks
on the doors and windows, moving through the rooms in her nightgown like a sentry. Sometimes she
would stand in the kitchen for an hour, unable to return to bed. Even within the walls of her own
home, she felt exposed, as though the walls themselves had become permeable. The conviction that
something terrible would happen again—that she was vulnerable in a way she had never been before—had
taken root so deeply that she found herself unable to relax even in daylight. She felt on guard
constantly, her shoulders tense, her jaw clenched. This hypervigilance was not paranoia; it was
clarity. Her body had been broken once. It could be broken again. The world had shown her that.

The two beliefs fed each other relentlessly. Because she could no longer trust her body, she
remained alert, scanning for threat. And because she remained alert—waking in the night, unable to
rest, exhausted—she felt more vulnerable, more certain that her body would fail her when it mattered
most. At eighty, she had expected to be slowing down naturally, but instead she felt as though she
were deteriorating under the weight of constant vigilance. She had also come to believe that the
capacity for normal emotion had been burned out of her, that whatever warmth or peace she might have
once felt was now ash. She moved through her days with her grandson, with her garden, with a kind of
hollow compliance. Nothing brought her joy. Nothing felt safe.

It was her daughter-in-law who finally insisted she speak to someone. Aroha had mentioned, almost in
passing, that she was not sleeping well. The concern in that woman's eyes—the way she had leaned
forward and asked, gently, if Hemi was still hurting her—had cracked something open. At eighty years
old, with hands that had built ships and welded steel, Aroha had finally admitted that she was
afraid, and that she no longer knew how to stop being afraid.
