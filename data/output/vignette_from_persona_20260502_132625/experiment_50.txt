# Amara's Collision

Amara had always prided herself on her mind—sharp, methodical, capable of parsing dense policy
briefs and spotting logical inconsistencies that others missed. At eighty, she had spent forty years
as a policy analyst, first for the state health department and then as a consultant, building a
reputation for clarity and precision. But the woman who sat in the waiting room now, hands folded
tightly in her lap, felt like a stranger wearing her own skin.

The accident had happened on a Tuesday evening in March, three years ago, on her way home from the
office. She had been driving on the interstate, the radio playing softly, her mind already turning
toward dinner, when a delivery truck two lanes over began to drift. She saw it happen in
fragments—the truck's sudden lurch, the blare of horns, the sickening crunch of metal as it clipped
a sedan beside her, sending that car careening into her lane. Amara had swerved, but not far enough.
The impact came from the side, violent and absolute, the car flipping once before coming to rest
against the median barrier. She remembered the smell of burnt rubber and gasoline, acrid and
choking, filling the cabin as smoke began to rise from under the hood. She remembered the heat
radiating from the engine, warming the side of her face where it pressed against the window. She
remembered the taste of blood. What she could not remember—and this gap terrified her—was how long
she had lain there before the paramedics arrived, or whether she had lost consciousness.

The first weeks after her discharge from the hospital had been deceptively quiet. The physical
injuries healed: the fractured ribs, the laceration across her temple, the whiplash that made
turning her head painful. But something else had taken root, something that no surgeon could repair.
Within a month, Amara had stopped driving. Within two, she had begun to notice that certain
sensations would ambush her without warning, pulling her back into that moment of impact. A whiff of
alcohol—someone's cologne, a drink being mixed at a restaurant—and suddenly her heart would race,
her hands would shake, and she would feel the car tipping beneath her again. The burnt smell was
worse; she had taken to avoiding the kitchen when anything was being cooked at high heat, asking her
daughter to handle the stove when she visited. Even temperature on her skin could trigger it: a warm
compress placed on her shoulder by a physical therapist had sent her into a panic so severe she had
cancelled all remaining appointments.

What frightened Amara most was that these sensations did not come as gentle reminders. They came as
ambushes. A sudden movement—her grandson reaching quickly for a glass of water, a colleague standing
abruptly from a desk—and Amara's entire nervous system would snap to attention, every muscle tensed,
certain that danger was imminent. In those moments, her mind would flood with fragments of the
accident, but not the whole story. She would feel the impact, taste the blood, sense the heat, but
the sequence would be scrambled, pieces missing. She had come to understand that her own mind was
protecting her from something, holding back a full recollection that she was not ready to face. This
realization had made her hypervigilant in a way that exhausted her. She monitored every movement
around her, every smell, every subtle shift in temperature, bracing for the moment when her body
would betray her again.

Over the past year, Amara had increasingly found herself trapped in a cycle she could not break. The
threat she felt in sudden movements around her—a colleague leaning forward during a meeting, a
pedestrian stepping too quickly near her at the grocery store—would send her into a state of alarm
that left her shaken for hours. In that state of high alert, her mind would become flooded with
sensory fragments from the accident: the smell of burnt rubber, the warmth of the engine fire
against her face, the taste of blood. These fragments would arrive without any conscious effort on
her part, triggered by the physical sensation of being under threat. Yet when the memories came,
Amara would find herself pushing them away, unable to bear the weight of them, which meant the
threat never fully resolved. The cycle would repeat, sometimes within the same day. She had taken
early retirement from her position two years ago, unable to concentrate, unable to trust that her
body would not betray her in the middle of an important meeting. Her daughter had gently suggested
that perhaps it was time to talk to someone, that perhaps Amara did not have to live this way.

Now, at eighty, Amara sat in a therapist's office for the first time in her life, hoping that
someone could help her understand why her mind had become a place where past and present had
collapsed into one another, and why the smallest sensation—a smell, a temperature, a sudden
gesture—could convince her that the collision was happening all over again.
