# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was fifty-two when he first came to the clinic, though he moved with the careful deliberation
of someone much older. A professional musician—he played classical guitar and had once performed at
regional venues across California—he arrived for his intake appointment twenty minutes early, having
circled the building twice to assess exits and sightlines. The stalking had begun three years ago,
though its origins remained partly obscured to him even now. A woman he'd briefly dated, someone
he'd met through a mutual friend at a concert, had become fixated on him. What had started as
persistent texts and emails escalated into her showing up at venues where he performed, waiting
outside his apartment, calling him at hours when she knew he'd be home. The police had issued a
restraining order eighteen months in, but by then the damage to Marcus's sense of safety had already
calcified.

He could not reconstruct the timeline of those early months with any clarity. There were gaps in
what he remembered—entire weeks when she'd followed him to different neighborhoods, to coffee shops,
to his ex-wife's house, and Marcus could only piece together these episodes from her own messages,
from police reports, from the concerned calls of friends. The fragmentation terrified him because it
meant she had been present in his life in ways he couldn't fully account for, couldn't fully
remember. What haunted him most were the sudden, unbidden fragments that would surface without
warning: the sound of her voice outside his door at three in the morning, the specific shade of her
car in a parking lot, the feeling of being watched from across a crowded venue. These pieces would
arrive with complete sensory force—he could smell the perfume she wore, could hear the exact cadence
of her breathing—and then vanish as quickly as they'd come, leaving him disoriented and questioning
what was real.

Marcus had come to believe he was weak, that a stronger man would have seen the danger earlier,
would have handled it differently, would have been able to protect himself without needing police
intervention. This conviction—that he was fundamentally inadequate—fed directly into his
hypervigilance. He watched other people constantly now, scanning faces for signs of threat,
interpreting neutral expressions as harbingers of malice. In his mind, if he had been stronger, more
perceptive, less naive, he would not need to be this exhausted sentinel. The institutional failure
compounded this self-judgment; the restraining order had felt like a bureaucratic gesture, a piece
of paper that had not actually stopped her from finding him. The police had been sympathetic but
ultimately powerless. The courts had failed him. This conviction that the systems meant to protect
him were hollow made him feel more alone, more convinced that his own weakness was the only honest
assessment of reality.

The memory gaps and those sudden sensory intrusions had become entangled with his threat assessment
in ways that made him feel trapped. Because he couldn't remember when she'd been watching him,
because fragments would arrive unbidden and vivid, he could never be certain when the danger had
truly passed. Every unfamiliar place became a potential ambush; he avoided new restaurants, new
routes to venues, anywhere he couldn't quickly map exits and sight lines. At night, the vigilance
intensified. He felt safer during daylight, when he could see clearly and assess threats visually,
but darkness made him feel hunted. He'd stopped accepting evening performance bookings. His income
had declined. His ex-wife, concerned but ultimately exhausted by his withdrawal, had stopped calling
as frequently.

The fragments still came—a flash of that specific perfume in a grocery store, a car that matched the
color of hers turning a corner—and each one sent him spiraling back into the conviction that he was
being watched, that his weakness had made him vulnerable, that no one could truly help him. He had
come to the clinic because his last remaining regular gig, a Sunday morning teaching position at a
community center, had become unsustainable; his hypervigilance was affecting his ability to teach,
to trust even his students. What he most feared was that the vigilance was permanent, that he would
never feel safe again, that the weakness he'd discovered in himself was not something that had been
exposed by the stalking but something that defined him entirely.
