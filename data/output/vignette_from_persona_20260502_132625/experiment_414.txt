# Amara's Story

Amara was twenty-three when the fighting reached the clinic in Mogadishu where she worked as a
nurse. She had trained in Lagos and moved to Somalia two years earlier, drawn by a sense of purpose
and a belief that her skills could matter in a place where they were desperately needed. The clinic
was a converted school building with concrete walls and a generator that ran on fuel they rationed
carefully. On the morning of the attack, she was changing dressings on a patient with a leg wound
when the first explosion came—close enough that dust fell from the ceiling and the lights flickered.
Within minutes, the clinic flooded with casualties. People arrived bleeding, calling out, some
barely conscious. Amara moved between them, her hands steady, her mind focused on triage and what
she could do. Then a man was brought in with shrapnel embedded in his abdomen and chest. She was
cleaning around the wounds when he seized, his body convulsing against the stretcher. She called for
help, but there was no surgeon, no operating theatre, nothing but her hands and gauze and the
absolute certainty that he was dying in front of her and she could not stop it. He died within
minutes. She could not remember his name, only the heat of his body and the sound of his breathing
stopping.

That was two years ago. Amara is twenty-five now and lives in Boston with her boyfriend Marcus, who
she met six months after returning home. On the surface, her life looks stable. She works the night
shift at a teaching hospital, a position that suits her—fewer people around, quieter wards, time to
think. But the thinking is what undoes her. When she is alone in the early hours, changing a
patient's IV or documenting vitals, a smell or a sound will pull her back to that room in Mogadishu,
and the memory arrives not as something she recalls but as something happening now. Her hands shake.
Her heart races. The man's face—she can see it clearly even though she could not have seen it
clearly then—becomes vivid and immediate. The feeling of helplessness floods through her body as if
she is standing at that stretcher again, unable to save him. When the memory takes hold, she feels
overwhelmed by an emotion so vast and so heavy that she has to stop what she is doing, has to step
away from the patient she is tending. Sometimes it is grief, pure and suffocating; sometimes it is
something closer to panic. Either way, she cannot stay present.

What frightens Amara most, though, is not the memory itself but what the memory means about her. She
had come to believe that a competent nurse—a good nurse—would have found a way. She would have known
what to do, would have been resourceful enough, brave enough, skilled enough to prevent that death.
The fact that she could not means something is fundamentally wrong with her. She is inadequate. She
feels permanently humiliated by her failure, as though everyone who looks at her can see the defect
underneath. This belief has become so solid that she cannot imagine anyone understanding if she
tried to explain what happened. She has not talked to Marcus about the details, though he knows she
was in a war zone and that it was hard. The thought of speaking about it aloud—of saying the words
that would make it real to someone else—fills her with a shame so acute that she pushes the thought
away. Instead, she has learned to retreat inward. When the memory threatens, when the weight of her
inadequacy becomes unbearable, she drifts into a kind of fantasy escape, a mental space where she is
someone else, somewhere else, someone who did not fail. She imagines herself as a surgeon in a fully
equipped hospital in London, or a researcher working in a quiet lab. She spends hours in these
fantasies, especially on her days off, and they are a relief—a few hours where she does not feel the
weight of being defective, where the memory cannot touch her.

But the fantasies only work while she is alone and still. Once the shift begins, once she is
actually working, the fantasies collapse. She finds herself back in the present, back in her body,
and the memories are waiting. She has started to avoid thinking about the event deliberately,
pushing away any thought that might lead her back there. Yet the avoidance itself has become a
trigger. When she tries not to think about something, the effort of suppression only makes the
memory more insistent, more vivid. She will be standing at a medication cart and suddenly see his
face. She will be explaining a procedure to a patient and feel the wave of grief coming. And each
time it happens, each time the memory overwhelms her despite her efforts to keep it at bay, it
confirms what she already believes: that she is broken, that there is something fundamentally wrong
with her capacity to function, that she should have been able to prevent this and could not. The
memory and the shame feed each other in a cycle she cannot break. Marcus has noticed that she is
distant, that she seems to leave the room sometimes even when she is standing right there. He has
asked her twice if she wants to talk to someone. She has said no both times, but the second time she
said it, she meant it less. What she is most afraid of now is that if she stops escaping—if she
stops the fantasies, if she stops avoiding the thoughts—the memory will consume her entirely. But
what she is also beginning to suspect, in the quiet moments before her shifts, is that the escape
and avoidance are consuming her anyway, just more slowly.
