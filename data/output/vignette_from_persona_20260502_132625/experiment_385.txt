# Kofi's Vigilance

Kofi was eighteen when he first put on the uniform, and twenty-one now—three years into the police
academy and patrol work that his mother had begged him not to pursue. She had wanted something safer
for him, something that would let him forget what he had seen as a child, but Kofi had understood,
even then, that forgetting was not an option. What had happened in his parents' bedroom when he was
nine years old had already made itself permanent in his body and mind. He remembered the sound
first—his father's voice rising, a chair scraping backward. He had crept to the doorway and seen his
mother on the floor, her arm bent at an angle that arms should not bend, his father standing over
her with his fist still raised. Kofi remembered the exact sequence of moments as discrete, frozen
images: his father's face twisted with rage; his mother's eyes meeting his across the room; the lamp
on the nightstand that his father would knock over in the next instant. Each picture was sharp and
isolated, disconnected from the others except in their order, and they returned to him now—eighteen
months after joining the department—with a force that made his chest tight and his limbs feel as
though they belonged to someone else.

The job had seemed, at first, like a way to master what had terrified him. If he could learn to read
danger, to intervene before violence spiraled, perhaps he could reclaim some sense of control. But
the reality had worked differently. Kofi had become hypersensitive to threat in ways that the
academy training had only amplified. He noticed weapons everywhere—the knife block in a suspect's
kitchen, the metal pipe leaning against a garage wall, the way a person's hand moved toward their
waistband. He could not walk into an unfamiliar building without cataloging exits and potential
weapons, his body moving through space with a constant, exhausting calculation of danger. Unfamiliar
places in general made him deeply uncomfortable; he could not assess what might happen in them,
could not predict where violence might emerge, and so he began to avoid them. He turned down
invitations to new restaurants, refused to attend a colleague's wedding in a neighborhood he did not
know, started taking the same routes home each day because at least those routes had become
predictable, mapped, survivable.

What had begun as hypervigilance at work had seeped into everything. The discomfort he felt in
unfamiliar spaces was constant, a low hum of dread that intensified whenever he encountered
something new or unexpected. When his patrol partner touched his shoulder without warning during a
shift—a routine gesture meant to redirect his attention—Kofi had spun around, his hand moving toward
his belt, his heart hammering. The unexpected touch had jolted him back into that bedroom, back into
the moment before his father's fist came down. Similarly, when people acted suspiciously around
him—a nervous glance, a sudden movement—he found himself flooded with the conviction that violence
was imminent, that he was about to witness or experience something catastrophic. These moments of
perceived threat triggered the memories with almost mechanical precision: a suspect's suspicious
behavior would send him spiraling into the fragmented images of his parents' fight, and those images
would then intensify his sense of threat, his conviction that danger was everywhere and that he
could not predict or prevent it.

Silence had become unbearable. An unusual quiet—a moment in the station when the radio fell silent,
a lull in conversation during a meal—made Kofi feel that something dangerous was about to happen,
that the world was holding its breath before violence erupted. This feeling was linked, he had come
to realize, to something deeper about himself: a conviction that he could not rely on his own
judgment or reactions. He would lie awake at night replaying interactions from his shift, certain
that he had misread a situation or overreacted, that his hypervigilance was not a reasonable
response but evidence that he was losing his grip on reality. His reactions since the trauma—the way
his body moved before his mind caught up, the way he scanned rooms, the way he could not sleep
without checking the locks—these felt to him like signs that he was going crazy, that the event had
broken something in him that could not be fixed. And underneath it all was a deeper despair: the
belief that he could not stop bad things from happening, that no amount of vigilance or preparation
would matter when violence came. His father had been violent despite Kofi's mother's attempts to
placate him, despite her compliance. Kofi had been powerless as a child, and despite the badge and
the training and the years of trying to master danger, he carried the conviction that he remained
helpless, that catastrophe would find him regardless.

The memories themselves had taken on a quality of bodily presence. When one of those snapshot images
surfaced—his father's raised fist, his mother's terrified eyes—Kofi's body would contort into the
same posture he had held as a nine-year-old, crouched in the doorway, frozen and small. His chest
would constrict, his limbs would stiffen, and grief would wash over him, overwhelming and
suffocating, as though he were experiencing the helplessness of that moment all over again. The
grief was not just sadness; it was the crushing weight of a child who could not save his mother,
could not stop his father, could not make himself big or strong or smart enough to matter.

Three weeks ago, after his shift, Kofi had sat in his patrol car in the station parking lot for two
hours, unable to go home, unable to move. He had called the department's employee assistance program
the next morning. He was terrified that seeking help would confirm what he already believed—that he
was broken, that the job had made it worse, that he could not trust himself to keep himself or
anyone else safe. But he was also, finally, desperate enough to try.
