# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was fifty-one when the diagnosis came through—stage three lymphoma, discovered during a
routine physical he'd almost skipped. He remembered the oncologist's voice, the smell of that
examination room, the way his hands had trembled as he signed consent forms for chemotherapy. He'd
survived eighteen months of treatment: the port in his chest, the chemicals burning through his
veins, the hospital smell that became the smell of his own mortality. His wife Sarah had held his
hand through most of it. Two years after remission, she died in a car accident on a Tuesday
afternoon. Marcus had been at work, principal of Lincoln Elementary, when the police called. He'd
never made it to the hospital in time to say goodbye.

The first months after Sarah's death were numb. But by autumn, something had shifted. Walking past
the hospital on his drive home from school, Marcus felt his chest tighten, his breathing shallow.
The smell—that particular antiseptic tang that drifted through the parking lot—sent him spiraling.
He couldn't name what he was afraid of anymore. The cancer was gone. Sarah was gone. But his body
seemed to remember something his mind couldn't quite grasp, a sensation of paralysis and dread that
arrived without warning whenever he caught that hospital smell or, worse, when someone mentioned
needing blood work. The feeling came as pure terror, sometimes without any clear image—just the
overwhelming sense that he was dying, that his body was betraying him again.

Marcus had come to believe that he could no longer trust himself. He'd survived cancer; he'd failed
to reach Sarah in time. The person he'd been before—the steady principal, the man who made
decisions, who led a school—felt like someone else entirely. Now, any unexpected demand triggered an
elaborate defense: he began rehearsing conversations before meetings, scripting his words so
carefully that colleagues noticed his stilted responses. When the school nurse mentioned scheduling
a health screening, Marcus felt the familiar surge of panic, and he simply didn't show up. He began
avoiding the hospital entirely, taking longer routes home, refusing to drive past it. The avoidance
made him feel safer momentarily, but it also deepened his conviction that something was
fundamentally wrong with him. He was weak. He couldn't rely on his own judgment anymore.

By spring, Marcus had begun isolating. Invitations from friends went unanswered. He'd rehearse
reasons to decline dinner, practice the exact words he'd use to keep people at arm's length. His
sister called repeatedly; he let the calls go to voicemail. The isolation fed his sense of being
unreliable—if he couldn't show up for the people who cared about him, what kind of man was he?
Meanwhile, whenever his mind drifted toward those eighteen months of treatment, or toward the day
Sarah died, the memories came fragmented and strange. He'd feel his body hunching forward, the way
he had in the chemo chair, without any accompanying image. Other times, the full weight of the
hospital room would crash over him—the smell, the cold metal of the examination table, the certainty
that he was going to die—yet when he tried to deliberately recall what had actually happened, the
details scattered. He couldn't hold the narrative together.

The triggers accumulated. A colleague mentioned needing a colonoscopy, and Marcus felt his shoulders
lock, his breath catch. The smell of hand sanitizer in the staff bathroom sent him out of the
building. A student's parent came to his office smelling faintly of alcohol—perhaps from the night
before—and Marcus's hands shook so badly he had to grip the desk. He wasn't sure why that particular
smell terrified him; perhaps it was bound up with hospital waiting rooms, with the helplessness of
watching others fall apart. Each trigger seemed to confirm what he'd already decided: that his body
was unreliable, that he couldn't trust himself to manage even the simplest interaction. So he
rehearsed more, avoided more, isolated further.

Marcus finally called a therapist in June, after his superintendent expressed concern about his
increasingly withdrawn behavior and erratic attendance at administrative meetings. He sat in the
waiting room of the clinic—a place that, mercifully, smelled like coffee and old carpet, not
antiseptic—and told the therapist that he didn't know who he was anymore. He was terrified that he'd
never work through this, that the person he'd been was gone for good. What he wanted most was to
feel like himself again, to trust his body and his mind. What frightened him most was that he'd
already tried so hard to protect himself through isolation and control, and it had only made him
feel more alone.
