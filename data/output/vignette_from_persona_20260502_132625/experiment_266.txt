# Miroslaw's War

Miroslaw Kowalski was seventy-six when he finally walked into the clinic, though his daughter had
been urging him for three years. He had lived through the Bosnian war as a civilian in Sarajevo,
trapped in the city during the siege of 1992. For the first weeks, he had believed it would end
quickly—that the shelling was temporary, that reason would prevail. He remembered the morning
everything changed. He was walking to a bakery six blocks from his apartment, the street still
relatively quiet, when the first mortar round landed. The sound was not like anything he had known—a
crack that seemed to split the air itself, followed by a sound like metal tearing. He saw a woman
ahead of him fall. He saw her face turn toward him, eyes open, and then the street filled with smoke
and dust so thick he could not see his own hands. He ran. For weeks afterward, whenever he closed
his eyes, he saw that face, saw the precise angle of her body on the pavement. The images were
vivid, almost more real than the present moment—not narrative, not a story he could tell, but a
series of photographs burned into his mind. When he tried to sleep, his body refused to settle. His
mind would not quiet. He needed to stay alert, to listen for the sound that might come again, the
crack that meant death was arriving. Even now, decades later, that vigilance had never left him.

After the war ended and Miroslaw eventually emigrated to America, he had tried to build a normal
life. He married Elena, a fellow survivor, and they worked quietly, staying close to home. But Elena
died eight years ago, and since then Miroslaw had become increasingly certain of something he had
come to believe with the clarity of absolute conviction: the world was fundamentally dangerous, and
people could not be trusted. This belief had reshaped how he understood his own memories. When the
image of that woman's face surfaced—and it surfaced often, unbidden—he did not interpret it as a
moment of tragedy he had witnessed; instead, it confirmed what he already knew. The memory was
evidence. It proved that chaos and death were always waiting, that safety was an illusion. The more
he dwelled on why he had been spared and she had not, the more his conviction hardened. He had lost
faith in anything larger than himself—in God, in the goodness of people, in any reason the universe
might have for what he had endured. His memories felt less like the past and more like a proof of
his current reality.

Because Miroslaw was certain the world was dangerous, he had begun to watch people carefully. At the
grocery store where he worked as a cashier, he observed customers closely, looking for signs of
aggression or deception—a sudden movement, a tone of voice that seemed off, someone standing too
close. His coworkers found him distant and suspicious. He monitored their behavior with the same
intensity he monitored everything else, convinced that people were fundamentally unreliable. This
hypervigilance was exhausting, but it felt necessary, a kind of armor. It was the only way to stay
safe. When a regular customer, Mrs. Chen, brought him flowers one day as a thank-you for his
kindness, Miroslaw felt something like warmth begin to rise in his chest—gratitude, perhaps even
connection. But almost immediately, he pushed the feeling away. Positive emotions felt dangerous to
him. They made him soft, distracted, vulnerable. If he allowed himself to feel glad or grateful, he
would lower his guard, and that was when harm would come. So he had learned to suppress any moment
of lightness, to retreat into the numbness that felt safer than the risk of hope.

His avoidance had become the architecture of his days. He no longer drove—the confined space of a
car, the inability to see threats approaching from all angles, the vulnerability of being trapped
with strangers, all of it felt intolerable. His daughter drove him to work, and he took the bus
home, sitting near the back where he could see the door. He avoided conversations that might lead to
connection. He ruminated constantly on why the war had happened, on why he had survived, on the
randomness and cruelty of existence. This meaning-seeking, endless and circular, only deepened his
conviction that the world made no sense except as a place of suffering. The more he avoided driving,
the more isolated he became, which only reinforced his belief that people could not be trusted. The
more he retreated from positive feelings, the more his memories felt like the only truth he
possessed. And the more vivid those memories became—the images so sharp they felt like they were
happening in the present moment—the more justified his vigilance seemed. Each night, lying awake,
listening to the silence of his apartment for threats that would never come, he was caught in a
cycle he could not name but could not escape.

What finally brought Miroslaw to seek help was not a crisis but a quiet desperation. His daughter
had told him she was moving away for work, and the thought of being truly alone, of losing even that
thin thread of connection, had frightened him in a way that cut through his numbness. He sat in the
waiting room now, hands folded in his lap, and when asked what he hoped might change, he said only,
"I want to stop being afraid." But what he meant, what he could barely articulate, was that he
wanted to stop being the man the war had made him—and he was no longer certain that was possible.
