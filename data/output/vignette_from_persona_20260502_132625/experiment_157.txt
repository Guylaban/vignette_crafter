# Marcus

Marcus was twenty-four when his ex-girlfriend, Tasha, beat him. They'd been together three years,
and by the end, he'd stopped telling anyone what was happening at home—the escalating arguments, the
way she'd shove him against walls when she drank, the night she cornered him in the kitchen and
struck him repeatedly across the face and chest while he stood frozen, unable to hit back, unable to
leave. He'd grown up believing men didn't get hurt like this, didn't end up with bruises they had to
hide under long sleeves at the precinct. When he finally extracted himself six months later, he told
himself it was over, that he could move forward. He married Sarah, a kind woman who loved him, and
tried to rebuild. But the past had woven itself into his nervous system in ways he didn't yet
understand.

The triggers arrived without warning, each one a thread pulling him backward. A whiff of
alcohol—someone's breath at a bar, a spilled drink at a restaurant—and Marcus's stomach would clench
with nausea so acute he'd excuse himself to the bathroom. Seeing a cut on a colleague's hand or a
bruise on a stranger's arm would flood him with vivid, intrusive recall: the sound of Tasha's voice,
sharp and slurred, the particular timbre of her breathing as she raised her hand. These sensory
details—the auditory snap of her words in his head, the olfactory ghost of cheap vodka—had become
portals. Each trigger didn't simply remind him of what happened; it dragged the memory forward with
visceral clarity, so real he could feel the impact on his ribs again, hear her voice as though she
were standing in the room with him right now.

And each time the memory forced its way in unbidden, Marcus found himself drawing a darker
conclusion about the world. People are not what they seem, he had come to believe with certainty.
Sarah seemed kind, but what if that was a mask? His colleagues seemed trustworthy, but how could he
really know? He had lost his faith in people entirely—the assumption of basic goodness that had once
come naturally to him had calcified into suspicion. He felt like a permanent outsider now, someone
who had seen behind the curtain and could never unsee it. The world had revealed itself as a place
where the people closest to you could hurt you, and no one could be trusted to be who they claimed.

To survive this, Marcus had developed strategies that felt protective but were slowly suffocating
his life. When Sarah tried to touch him—to hold his hand, to embrace him after work—he stiffened and
pulled away. Physical contact had become dangerous in his mind, a vulnerability he couldn't afford.
He'd also stopped allowing himself to relax. When he sat down to watch television with Sarah, his
body remained coiled, ready. Sleep was something he fought against; relaxation felt like dropping
his guard, and dropping his guard meant getting hurt. He numbed his feelings with alcohol, which was
its own cruel irony, since the smell of it triggered the very memories he was trying to escape. But
the numbness was preferable to the sharp, overwhelming terror that threatened to surface if he let
himself feel anything at all.

The numbing and avoidance, however, had begun to reinforce his darkest beliefs. Because he couldn't
relax, couldn't be touched, couldn't let Sarah close, she had started to withdraw—confused and hurt
by his distance. His coldness toward her felt like proof that intimacy was dangerous, that
connection inevitably led to pain. The more he numbed himself, the more isolated he became, and the
more his conviction hardened: he truly was an outsider, permanently separated from normal human
connection. His avoidance had become its own trigger, too—the moment he noticed himself pulling away
from Sarah, shame and panic would spike, and sometimes that spike alone was enough to summon the
memory, to hear Tasha's voice again, to feel the nausea rising in his throat.

At work, where he carried a badge and was expected to be steady, the cracks were showing. He'd begun
to avoid scenes involving domestic disputes, and his sergeant had noticed. Two months ago, Marcus
finally told Sarah about the abuse—not the details, just that it had happened—and saw the shock and
sadness in her eyes. It was that look, and the realization that he was losing her not because she
was cruel but because he couldn't let her in, that finally brought him to seek help. He sat in the
waiting room of the clinic, stomach churning with nausea that had nothing to do with a trigger and
everything to do with the terrifying possibility that he might have to feel everything he'd been
running from.
