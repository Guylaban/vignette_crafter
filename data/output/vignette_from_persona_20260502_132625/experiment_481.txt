# Clinical Vignette: Dr. Marcus Chen

Marcus Chen was sixty-three when he walked into the clinic, though his shoulders carried the weight
of someone much older. Seven years earlier, he had been attending a medical conference in Boston—a
routine professional obligation he'd made dozens of times before. He'd been standing in the hotel
lobby with colleagues, reviewing the afternoon's schedule, when the explosion came. A vehicle
detonated outside the main entrance. The blast shattered the glass facade, and in the chaos that
followed—the screaming, the blood, the dust that seemed to fill his lungs for hours afterward—Marcus
watched a woman collapse beside him, her leg nearly severed. He'd tried to apply pressure, tried to
remember his training, but his hands wouldn't stop shaking. Two people died in that lobby. He knew
their names. He still saw their faces.

In the months following, Marcus had believed the attack was random, senseless, and that nothing
could have prevented it. But as time passed, this conviction hardened into something darker: the
world was not random but fundamentally dangerous, and people could not be trusted to be who they
claimed. When news reports mentioned other attacks, when he heard about violence in distant cities,
each story reinforced what had become his deepest certainty—that catastrophe was not an aberration
but the true nature of things. This belief about the world's danger began to reshape how he
interpreted ordinary events. When his wife mentioned she'd taken the bus downtown, Marcus felt a
surge of dread; buses were crowded, vulnerable, targets. When a colleague at the hospital gave him a
direct instruction about a patient protocol, something in the tone of command triggered a flash of
panic—it sounded like the security guard's voice ordering people to evacuate, the moment before
everything fell apart. Even watching a news segment about a protest that turned violent sent him
spiraling, convinced that society was unraveling and that he was foolish to believe otherwise.

The threat vigilance that had emerged in those early months had never subsided. At home, Marcus
found himself listening—really listening—for the sounds of forced entry, for footsteps on the porch,
for anything that might signal danger. His wife, Elena, would find him standing at the kitchen
window at two in the morning, watching the street. He'd installed new locks, upgraded the alarm
system, and began checking the doors compulsively before bed, a ritual that sometimes took twenty
minutes. Inside his own home, the place that should have been his refuge, he remained on guard. His
jaw stayed clenched. His shoulders never fully relaxed. Elena noticed that he'd become irritable in
ways that seemed disconnected from any immediate provocation—she'd ask him a simple question about
dinner plans, and he'd snap at her, his voice sharp and defensive. On bad days, he'd catch himself
raising his voice over nothing, then feel a crushing shame about his anger, which only deepened his
conviction that the trauma had destroyed something essential in him, that he was no longer the man
he used to be.

The triggers had become woven into the fabric of his daily life in ways he couldn't escape. His work
as a physician required him to be in authority sometimes, to give orders to residents and nurses,
but increasingly he found himself unable to tolerate receiving them. When the department head
instructed him to take on a new committee role, Marcus felt his chest tighten and his mind flood
with the memory of that guard's voice. He'd declined the role with uncharacteristic coldness, and
the head had seemed confused, hurt. More painfully, Marcus had stopped taking the subway to the
hospital. The bus, the train—any public transport—had become intolerable. The enclosed space, the
strangers pressing close, the impossibility of escape: it all felt like a trap waiting to spring.
He'd begun driving, adding forty minutes to his commute each way, and had made excuses to avoid
attending conferences entirely, though he told Elena it was simply a shift in his professional
priorities. The real reason was that he couldn't bear the thought of being in a hotel lobby again,
couldn't bear the vulnerability of it. Each time he invented a reason to stay home, each time he
drove the long route instead of taking transit, each time he bristled at an instruction from a
colleague, his conviction deepened: the world was too dangerous, people too untrustworthy, and his
life—the career he'd built, the confidence he'd once felt—was fundamentally broken.

Elena had begun to withdraw. She no longer invited friends over because Marcus's irritability made
gatherings unbearable. She'd stopped suggesting trips, stopped asking him to join her on errands.
Marcus could see the distance growing between them, and he hated himself for it, but he couldn't
seem to stop. He came to therapy because Elena had finally said, with quiet desperation, that she
needed him to try—that she was losing him to something she couldn't fight. What terrified Marcus
most was not the possibility of another attack, but the creeping certainty that the first one had
already taken everything that mattered.
