# Rajesh

Rajesh was forty-two when armed men arrived at his apartment in Karachi. He had been a consultant
then, advising on infrastructure projects for international firms—work that kept him moving between
South Asia and the Gulf. That evening, three uniformed officers forced their way inside. What
followed was three days of interrogation in a basement cell: repeated beatings, water poured over
his face until he gasped for air, questions shouted in Urdu and English that blurred together into
incomprehensible noise. They wanted information about his clients, his contacts, his movements. He
had nothing to give them. By the third day, Rajesh no longer knew if he was being asked anything at
all or if the voices were simply echoing in his own mind. When they finally released him—no apology,
no explanation—he was bruised, dehydrated, and convinced that something fundamental inside him had
broken.

Sixteen years later, at fifty-eight, Rajesh held the title of Product Manager at a technology firm
in Seattle. His colleagues considered him competent but distant. His wife, Priya, had learned to
move carefully around him. He had come to believe, over the years, that people could not be
trusted—that beneath every polite interaction lay calculation or potential harm. More than this, he
was certain there was something about him that had invited that violence in the first place, some
flaw or vulnerability that had marked him as a target. And he was equally certain that nothing good
could happen to him anymore; the future held only the possibility of recurrence. These convictions
were not abstract. They shaped everything he did.

The belief that he was somehow responsible for what had happened, that he carried an intrinsic
fault, drove him to avoid anything that might place him at risk again. He had begun, years ago, to
avoid stressors of any kind—not just obvious dangers, but the ordinary friction of daily life.
Meetings that might become heated, conversations that veered into personal territory, even social
gatherings where he could not predict the flow of interaction. He told himself this was caution.
What it meant in practice was isolation. He declined invitations. He left rooms when discussions
became animated. At work, he managed this by remaining in his office, communicating mostly through
email, delegating tasks that required him to be in shared spaces. Priya had stopped asking him to
attend her family dinners.

Yet avoidance fed the very convictions that had spawned it. Each time Rajesh withdrew, each time he
constructed another boundary to keep the world at a distance, he reinforced his belief that the
world was dangerous and that he could not be part of it. The more he avoided, the more isolated he
became, and the more his isolation confirmed his sense that nothing good lay ahead. His constant
vigilance about his own body—checking his pulse, monitoring for signs of dizziness or weakness,
interpreting a flutter in his chest as a harbinger of collapse—was meant to protect him. Instead, it
tethered him to a state of perpetual alarm. He could not relax even when sitting in his own living
room, even when Priya sat beside him on the couch. His body remained coiled, ready for the basement,
for the voices, for the hands that might appear without warning.

The triggers were everywhere. A siren on the street sent his heart into his throat. Incomprehensible
chatter—colleagues speaking rapidly in another language, a podcast playing in the
background—immediately transported him back to those three days when meaning had dissolved. A sudden
touch from Priya, even her hand on his shoulder in a moment of affection, made him flinch violently,
his body interpreting tenderness as threat. He had begun to avoid police officers and security
guards, crossing streets to avoid them, his stomach tightening at the sight of a uniform. He had
never owned a gun; the thought of weapons made him nauseated. These avoidances made sense to him
because they were responses to genuine danger—or so he had come to believe. The fact that he woke
most nights to check the locks, to ensure the windows were closed, to verify that Priya was still
breathing beside him, felt not like pathology but like necessary vigilance.

It was Priya who finally insisted he seek help. She had found him one night at three in the morning
standing in the kitchen, unable to articulate why he had gotten out of bed, only that something felt
wrong. She held his face in her hands—carefully, knowing that even this could startle him—and told
him she could not watch him disappear any longer. Rajesh sat in the intake appointment carrying the
weight of sixteen years, afraid that no one could understand what had been done to him, more afraid
still that if he began to speak about it, the world would confirm what he already knew: that he was
broken, that he could never be trusted again, and that the best he could hope for was to endure.
