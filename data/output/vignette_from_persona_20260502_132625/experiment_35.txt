# Mariana's Story

Mariana was eighty years old when she finally sought help, though the beating that changed her life
had happened nearly a decade before. She had been a social worker for fifty-two years—a career built
on her belief that people were fundamentally good, that patience and understanding could reach
anyone. That belief had sustained her through the 1980s and 1990s, through decades of case files and
crisis calls, through a professional life devoted to helping others find their way out of harm.
Then, at seventy-one, she had met Viktor at a church social. He was charming, attentive, a widower
who seemed to understand her in ways she had not expected to be understood again. Within four
months, he moved into her apartment. Within six, he had pinned her against the kitchen counter one
evening—his hands around her throat, his voice a low, furious growl—because she had questioned where
he had been that afternoon. The memory came back as fragments: the cold granite pressing into her
spine, the sound of the refrigerator humming in the darkness, his face inches from hers, twisted and
unrecognizable. She remembered his hand releasing her, remembered sliding down to the floor,
remembered the silence that followed. But the sequence of what happened next—how she got to her
bedroom, whether she called anyone, how many hours passed before morning—remained scrambled in her
mind, arriving in snapshot images that refused to arrange themselves into a coherent timeline.

When Mariana thought about what had happened, she was overwhelmed by a grief so profound it felt
like drowning. The memory fragments themselves—the counter, his face, the refrigerator—triggered
this cascade of despair, and when grief came, she found herself desperate to escape it. She began to
avoid anything that might bring the memory forward: she stopped listening to the radio station she
had loved, stopped taking walks at dusk, stopped sitting in her kitchen. She tried, with increasing
desperation, to erase the memory altogether, to convince herself that it had not happened or that it
had happened differently, to a different woman. She spent hours lying awake at night, thinking
obsessively about why Viktor had done this, whether she had provoked it, whether she had somehow
deserved it—as though understanding the reason might retroactively undo it. These efforts to make
sense of the senseless, to erase what could not be erased, only deepened her conviction that the
world was not as she had believed it to be. Her fundamental understanding of human nature—the
premise on which she had built her entire career—had been destroyed. People could not be trusted.
She was certain of this now in a way that felt like knowledge rather than fear.

This shattered belief system became the lens through which Mariana now saw everything. Because she
was convinced that people could not be trusted, she became hypervigilant in ways that exhausted her.
She scanned for exits the moment she entered any room, a habit so automatic she was barely aware of
it anymore. She could not sleep; her mind would race through scenarios of danger, and when she
finally drifted off, nightmares came—violent, formless dreams that woke her gasping. To avoid these
nightmares, she began staying awake deliberately, drinking coffee late into the evening, keeping the
television on until dawn. Her sleep deprivation only sharpened her vigilance, made her more alert,
more watchful, more certain that danger was imminent. And because she felt so contaminated by what
had happened—marked by Viktor's violence as though it had stained her permanently—she began to avoid
situations where she might encounter men, particularly men she knew. Her brother had called several
times; she had not answered. A male colleague from her agency had invited her to lunch; she had
declined without explanation. Being alone with any man triggered an immediate cascade of fear and
memory: the fragments would return, vivid and unbidden, and with them came the conviction that she
could not trust this person, that something terrible might happen.

The triggers multiplied. A door slamming in her apartment building would jolt her into a state of
acute alarm, her heart racing as though Viktor were on the other side. The sound of a man's voice in
the hallway outside her door would send her into the bedroom, door locked, heart pounding. When her
niece came to visit—one of the few family members she still saw—Mariana found herself tense
throughout the visit, unable to relax, scanning the room for exits even though she knew, rationally,
that her niece posed no threat. But the presence of a male relative triggered the same cascade: the
fragmented memories would surface, her certainty that people could not be trusted would intensify,
and she would find herself trying desperately to think about something else, to distract herself, to
erase the images that would not leave her alone. Each time she engaged in these avoidance
strategies—the distraction, the memory suppression, the obsessive thinking about why—she seemed to
reinforce the very beliefs she was trying to escape. The more she tried to erase the memory, the
more vivid it became. The more she tried to understand why it had happened, the more convinced she
became that she had somehow invited it, that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that she was
fundamentally damaged. Her sleep deteriorated further. Her hypervigilance intensified. And the cycle
tightened around her, day after day, month after month, until she could barely leave her apartment.

It was her supervisor at the agency who finally insisted she seek help. Mariana had taken medical
leave six months ago, claiming a back injury, but her supervisor had noticed the tremor in her voice
during their last phone call, the way Mariana had become unable to return to work—unable to sit
across from clients, unable to be in a room with a man, unable to focus on anyone's pain but her
own. At eighty, after a lifetime of helping others, Mariana sat in the waiting room of a trauma
clinic, terrified that no one would believe her, that she would be blamed for staying with Viktor,
that she would never feel safe again. What she wanted most was to go back to being the woman she had
been before—the woman who believed in people, who could walk into a room without scanning for exits,
who could sleep through the night. But she was no longer certain that woman existed anymore.
