# Aroha's Story

Aroha was twenty-one when the IED detonated seventy meters from her Humvee. She had been a combat
medic with the New Zealand Defence Force for eighteen months, deployed to Afghanistan, and in the
seconds after the blast she was certain she was dying. The concussion threw her against the metal
frame, her ears screaming with a high-pitched tone that wouldn't fade. Around her, dust hung like
fog. Her sergeant was shouting something she couldn't hear. Then came the sirens—the medevac birds
inbound—and with them the knowledge that people were dead, that she had failed to prevent it, that
her body had survived when others hadn't. When she got home to Auckland three months later, Aroha
told her girlfriend Hemi that she was fine, that she'd processed it, that she just needed time. She
meant it when she said it.

The first trigger came without warning. A fire truck passed their apartment on a Tuesday morning,
its siren climbing through the street, and Aroha felt her heart explode in her chest—not
metaphorically, but a violent, animal pounding that made her dizzy. She dropped the mug she was
holding. The siren faded, but her heart kept racing, and she found herself unable to stay in the
apartment. She told Hemi she needed air, walked for two hours, and when she came back she locked all
the windows. She had come to believe that her body's response—the pounding, the panic—was a warning
system, a sign that danger was imminent, and so she began scanning the street from behind the
curtains, tracking every car, every sound. Loud noises in general became unbearable: the rubbish
truck on collection day sent her into the bathroom with the door locked. The hospital where she
worked as a registered nurse became a minefield. Alarms on monitors, crash carts being rushed, the
sharp clang of metal trays—all of it felt like incoming fire. She started taking shifts in the
quietest ward, pediatrics, where the pace was slower. But even there, a child fell and screamed, and
Aroha's hands shook so badly she had to hand off her patient to a colleague.

What frightened her most was what happened when she tried to talk about the deployment. She would
begin to describe the day of the blast—the heat, the smell of burning plastic and flesh—and the rage
would come first, a white-hot fury that made her want to hit something, to scream. But then the
emotion would overwhelm her entirely, a tsunami of grief and terror and guilt so total that she
would have to stop mid-sentence, unable to find words, unable to do anything but sit in silence
until it passed. The memory itself had become unbearable, so she began avoiding anything that might
trigger it. She stopped watching the news. She deleted her social media accounts. She turned down
her best friend's invitation to a barbecue, certain that laughter and music would somehow pull the
memory to the surface. Hemi asked her to come to his work drinks, a celebration, and Aroha felt a
flash of anger at the suggestion—how could she celebrate when people had died?—and declined. She
began refusing invitations to anything that promised happiness or hope, as if joy itself were a
betrayal.

But avoidance created its own trap. The less she engaged with the world, the more hypervigilant she
became. At work, she found herself constantly scanning the environment for threats, her eyes darting
to exits, to potential weapons, to anything that could go wrong. Her colleagues noticed her
flinching at routine sounds. A senior nurse asked if she was alright, and Aroha's irritability
flared—a sharp, aggressive response that felt disproportionate but unstoppable. She apologized
immediately, but the damage was done. The nurse kept her distance after that. At home, Aroha's
threat-scanning intensified her avoidance: she began refusing to go to the gym because gyms were
crowded and unpredictable; she avoided the beach, a place she had loved, because the openness made
her feel exposed. The more she stayed isolated, monitoring for danger from behind her locked
windows, the more her mind returned to the moment of the blast. She would be sitting on the couch
and suddenly feel the concussion again, feel the heat, hear the sirens. When she tried to tell Hemi
what was happening, the rage and overwhelm made it impossible. She couldn't find the words. So she
stopped trying to explain, and Hemi, confused and hurt by her withdrawal, began spending more time
away from the apartment.

Now, three months into civilian life, Aroha's heart races at the sound of a siren even when she
knows logically what it is. Her threat-scanning has become so automatic that she no longer notices
she's doing it—only that the world feels dangerous in a way she cannot articulate or escape. She has
taken a leave of absence from nursing, telling her manager she needed time to recover from the
deployment. What she hasn't told anyone is that she is terrified she will never be able to go back,
that the rage and the overwhelm and the constant vigilance have become who she is now. When she
finally called a therapist last week, she sat in the waiting room unable to explain why she was
there, her heart pounding, her mind blank. She is hoping someone can help her understand why her own
body has become a threat, why the world feels like a war zone, and whether she can ever feel safe
again.
