# Petra's Story

Petra was thirty-nine when the diagnosis came through—stage three ovarian cancer, caught late enough
that the surgical team spoke in careful, measured tones about "aggressive treatment protocols" and
"realistic expectations." She had been a dentist for fourteen years, meticulous with her hands,
confident in her ability to read what a mouth could tell her. The irony was not lost on her: she had
spent her career examining other people's bodies for signs of disease, and her own body had betrayed
her in silence. The surgery came first—a hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, followed
by six months of chemotherapy that left her nauseated, bald, and staring at a stranger in the
mirror. By the time she finished treatment, the cancer was in remission, but Petra was not.

The hospital smell—that particular blend of bleach, antiseptic, and something else she could never
quite name—had become a trigger that sent her into a spiral of dread. When she returned to her
practice three months after finishing chemo, she found that the faint chemical scent in her own
office, a residue from the cleaning supplies in the sterilization room, could unsettle her for
hours. More insidiously, old photographs began to trigger the same cascade of panic. She had
discovered a picture from five years ago—herself at a friend's wedding, radiant and whole—and the
sight of that version of herself, the one before the diagnosis, before the surgery, before the
poison they pumped into her veins, crystallized something terrible: she was not that person anymore.
That woman was gone. What remained was someone fundamentally compromised, someone whose body could
no longer be trusted. The certainty of this belief had the weight of truth. She was weak—weak for
getting sick, weak for struggling through treatment, weak for not being able to simply move on the
way everyone seemed to expect her to. And the more she dwelled on those old photographs, the more
convinced she became that the damage was permanent, that no amount of time would restore what she
had lost.

The conviction that she had permanently changed for the worse, that her body was now unreliable and
dangerous, led her to withdraw from the very relationships that might have sustained her. Her
partner, Marcus, had been supportive through treatment, but Petra found herself unable to let him
touch her. She avoided looking at her own body—the surgical scars, the way her abdomen felt
different, the lingering fatigue that no amount of rest seemed to cure. Showering became an ordeal
she rushed through with her eyes closed. At work, she began finding reasons to cancel appointments,
telling her office manager that she needed to reduce her schedule, that she wasn't quite ready to
return to full capacity. What she didn't say was that the thought of being confined in the dental
chair—trapped in that position, unable to move, dependent on the patient to remain still—had become
unbearable. The feeling of entrapment, of having no options, no escape route, no control, sent her
into a state of suffocating anxiety that she couldn't explain to anyone.

She stopped talking about the cancer altogether. When colleagues asked how she was doing, she gave
brief, deflecting answers. With Marcus, she refused to discuss her fears, her body's limitations, or
the dark thoughts that sometimes visited her at three in the morning. The isolation deepened her
conviction that something was fundamentally wrong with her, that she was broken in a way that
couldn't be shared or understood. Her avoidance of other people—the way she had begun declining
social invitations, making excuses to stay home—only reinforced her sense of weakness and permanent
damage. The less she allowed herself to be seen, the more real the belief became that she was no
longer the person worth knowing. By the time she called a therapist, six months into remission,
Petra had constructed a prison of her own making, one built from the conviction that her body had
failed her and that she herself was fundamentally, irreparably changed.
