# Carlos

Carlos was sixty-eight when the men came for him. It was 1976 in a country he no longer names,
during a time he has learned most people would rather not hear about. He was a union organizer
then—young, certain of his convictions, certain that speaking truth to power was a moral necessity.
The military police arrived at the warehouse where he worked at dawn. They separated him from the
others with an efficiency that suggested they had done this many times before. The room they took
him to was concrete and windowless. There were four of them, and they made clear from the beginning
that he had no options, that whatever happened next depended entirely on their will. They wanted
names. When he would not give them, they used methods he still cannot fully articulate—methods that
left him broken in ways that would take decades to understand. The worst part, he has come to
believe, was not the physical pain but the absolute helplessness, the knowledge that nothing he did
or said would change what they intended. He screamed. He complied when they demanded it. He told
them some things that were not true just to make it stop. Nothing stopped it until they were
finished with him.

Now, at seventy, Carlos works as a welder at a fabrication shop in Phoenix. The work is solitary,
which suits him. He can focus on the seams, the precise angle of the torch, the way metal yields to
heat. But three years ago, the shop hired a new supervisor—a younger man with a loud voice and an
arbitrary management style. Within weeks, Carlos found himself in situations that triggered the old
dread: moments when he felt cornered, when his suggestions were dismissed and he was expected simply
to comply without question. Once, when a shipment was delayed and the supervisor blamed him
publicly, Carlos felt the familiar tightness in his chest, the sense that he had no way out, no
voice that mattered. That night, he could not sleep. The memory surfaced unbidden—not as something
he was choosing to recall, but as if it were happening again in real time. He was back in that room.
He could smell the concrete dust. He could feel the weight of their presence. The rage that followed
was so intense he had to leave his bedroom and sit in the garage until dawn.

Since then, Carlos has developed a pattern. Whenever he feels trapped—when a customer is demanding,
when someone raises their voice, when he hears a sound that resembles a gunshot (the shop next door
occasionally has machinery that backfires)—the memory crashes into the present with the force of
something occurring now, not decades past. And each time the memory arrives, it brings with it a
fresh certainty about the world: that people are fundamentally dangerous, that you can never truly
know who will harm you or when. This conviction makes him hypervigilant at work. He has started
arriving early and leaving late, positioning himself where he can see the entrance. He monitors the
supervisor's mood obsessively, trying to predict when conflict might arise. When conflict does
arise, he finds himself becoming almost excessively agreeable—nodding, apologizing for things that
are not his fault, accepting blame he does not deserve. He tells himself this compliance keeps him
safe, even as he recognizes it is a kind of surrender.

The guilt compounds everything. He was a union man, a fighter once. The fact that he now cowers,
that he becomes compliant when threatened, that he cannot even speak clearly about what happened
without losing the thread—these feel like betrayals of who he was. He torments himself with the
thought that if he had been stronger, if he had refused to break, if he had done something
differently that day, perhaps none of it would have happened. This self-blame feeds his avoidance.
He has stopped trying to explain the trauma to anyone. When his daughter calls and asks how he is
doing, he changes the subject. When a therapist he briefly saw asked him to talk about the event, he
found himself unable to continue—the words scattered, the narrative fractured. It felt safer to
detach from the memories altogether, to treat them as something happening to someone else, to push
them away whenever they threatened to surface. But the avoidance itself has become a kind of prison.
He is isolated, suspicious, exhausted by the constant effort of not thinking about things he cannot
stop thinking about. His daughter has noticed his distance. His few friendships have withered. He
exists in a state of perpetual bracing, waiting for the next trigger, the next flood of presence,
the next rage.

It was his doctor who finally insisted he seek help. The blood pressure, the insomnia, the way
Carlos's hands shake sometimes—these are not sustainable, the doctor said. Carlos came to his first
appointment reluctant, convinced that talking about it would only make it worse. But he is here, at
seventy, still carrying the weight of that room, still unable to forgive himself for surviving it
the way he did, still certain that the world is fundamentally unsafe and that his own nature—his
compliance, his weakness—is somehow to blame.
