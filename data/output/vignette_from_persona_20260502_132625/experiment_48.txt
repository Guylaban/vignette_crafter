# Lani's Vigil

Lani was forty-eight when the men came through the door of his photography studio in Port Moresby.
He was alone, reviewing prints from a wedding shoot, when three figures in dark clothes forced their
way in. They bound his wrists, covered his eyes, and drove him to a compound on the outskirts of the
city. For seventeen days, he was held in a concrete room with no windows. The men came and went
unpredictably—sometimes hours of silence, sometimes sudden entry with shouting, sometimes the sound
of a key turning in the lock that made his heart stop before he knew whether they were coming to
feed him or hurt him. They demanded money from his wife. When she paid, they kept him anyway for
another week, moving him twice to different locations, each transfer a fresh terror of the unknown.
The randomness was the worst part. He learned to listen for every sound—footsteps, voices, the
scrape of a chair in an adjacent room—because the only warning he ever got was what his ears could
tell him. His body became a tuning fork for danger, always vibrating at a frequency just below
audible.

He was released after his brother-in-law negotiated his freedom, but the man who had been held in
that room never fully returned home. Five years later, at fifty-three, Lani still moved through the
world as though he were cataloguing exits. When he and his wife, Aroha, went to restaurants, he
would scan the room before sitting, always positioning himself with a view of the door and his back
to a wall. If a table didn't meet these criteria, he felt a creeping dread that settled in his
chest—a sense that he was exposed, that something could happen and he would be trapped. He began
arriving at locations early, mapping the layout, identifying which windows opened, which doors led
where. Before a client meeting, he would walk the perimeter of the building. His body remained in a
state of coiled readiness: his shoulders stayed high, his jaw clenched, his breathing shallow. Even
in his own home, he found himself unable to fully relax. He slept poorly, waking at the smallest
sound—the refrigerator humming, a car passing outside.

The vigilance created its own feedback loop. Because Lani was constantly scanning for threats, he
began to notice people's movements with an intensity that bordered on obsessive. A man adjusting his
jacket could look like he was reaching for something. A stranger glancing in his direction could
feel like surveillance. His nervous system, primed to detect danger, began to interpret ordinary
human behaviour as potentially menacing. When someone moved suddenly near him—a colleague leaning
back in their chair, a stranger shifting their weight in a queue—Lani's body would jolt, his muscles
tensing as if preparing to flee or fight. This hypervigilance towards people, born from his need to
stay safe, paradoxically made him feel less safe, because the world began to look genuinely
dangerous. Every crowded space became a calculation of risk.

He had begun asking Aroha repeatedly whether she thought he was okay, whether she noticed anything
wrong with him, whether she felt safe around him. He needed her reassurance that he wasn't losing
his mind, that his fear made sense, that other people felt the same way. When she tried to reassure
him, he would feel briefly calmer—but the relief never lasted. Within hours or days, the doubt would
return, and he would need to ask again. He started planning escape routes before entering shops,
restaurants, even the homes of friends. At his photography studio, he rearranged the furniture so he
could always see the entrance. His clients began to notice his discomfort; a few stopped booking
with him. His income declined. Aroha grew exhausted from the endless reassurance-seeking, from his
refusal to go to certain places, from the tension that never left his face. The very strategies he
had developed to protect himself—the constant vigilance, the escape planning, the reassurance-
seeking—were now feeding the sense that the world remained fundamentally unsafe. Each time he
scanned for danger and found it (or imagined he had), each time he needed reassurance and received
it only to need it again, the cycle tightened. He had come to believe that his hypervigilance was
the only thing keeping him alive, even as it was slowly eroding his marriage and his livelihood.

It was only when Aroha told him she was considering leaving that Lani finally made an appointment
with a therapist. He arrived at the session tense and exhausted, his body already cataloguing the
exits of the office, already assessing whether the chair he was offered would allow him to see the
door.
