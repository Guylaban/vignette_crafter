# Margaret

Margaret Kowalski was seventy-six when she finally scheduled the appointment with Dr. Reeves, though
the trembling that had begun during her last routine colonoscopy had not subsided in the three
months since. She had been a city council member for nearly two decades, known for her composure in
contentious zoning disputes and budget hearings, yet she found herself unable to sit still in the
gastroenterologist's waiting room without her hands shaking visibly. The thought of the
procedure—the loss of control, the vulnerability of being sedated while strangers worked on her
body—had triggered something she thought she had put to rest decades ago.

What she could not quite articulate to her daughter was that it was not the procedure itself that
terrified her. It was what came with it: the sudden, overwhelming sensation of being small and
powerless, a feeling that arrived without warning and without any accompanying image. She would be
sitting at her desk reviewing municipal zoning applications, and suddenly her chest would tighten
with a terror so acute it felt like drowning, though she could not see what she was drowning in. The
emotion came first, raw and complete, and only afterward—if she allowed herself to think about
it—did fragmentary images surface: her mother's face, contorted. Her father's voice, low and
menacing. But Margaret had learned long ago not to let those images fully form. The moment she felt
one beginning to crystallize, she would push it away, turn her attention sharply back to the present
moment, to the spreadsheet or the phone call or the council meeting. It had worked for fifty years.
It had stopped working three months ago.

The colonoscopy had been scheduled as routine screening, nothing more. But when the nurse had asked
her to change into the hospital gown, when she lay on the gurney and felt the powerlessness of
sedation beginning to pull at her consciousness, something in her had fractured. The memory had not
come as a coherent narrative—she still could not, even now, describe what she had "seen"—but as pure
sensation: the helplessness, the violation, the absolute certainty that she could not protect
herself. The feeling was so vivid, so overwhelmingly present, that it made her ordinary memories—her
career, her children, the apartment she had carefully decorated—feel like pale sketches by
comparison. For days afterward, she could not look at a hospital gown without feeling that same
surge of dread.

Last month, she had encountered her father at the grocery store. He was ninety-two, frail, moving
slowly through the produce section with a walker. She had not seen him in eight years. The moment
she recognized his silhouette, the same bodily terror flooded through her, and with it came that
emotionally saturated memory-feeling, the sense of his presence and his danger, though no clear
image. She had turned her cart around and left the store. That night, she could not sleep. Every
small noise in her apartment triggered the same cascade: first the raw emotion, then the pushing
away before the images could fully arrive, then hours of lying awake, her body convinced she was in
danger.

What frightened Margaret most now was not the memory itself, but the way the triggers had begun to
multiply. Comments at council meetings about family values, about protecting children—remarks that
would have been innocuous a year ago—now sent her into a state of hypervigilance that lasted hours.
She found herself avoiding certain neighborhoods where her father was known to shop. She had
declined her daughter's invitation to a family dinner. The life she had built with such
deliberation, the professional composure and emotional distance she had cultivated, was beginning to
crack. She came to Dr. Reeves because she was afraid that if she did not find some way to stop this
now, she would lose the last decades of her life to the same cage her childhood had built.
