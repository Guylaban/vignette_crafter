# Marco's Spiral

Marco Hernández was thirty-two when his younger brother died. The call came at three in the
morning—a car accident on the interstate, his brother Diego pinned in the wreckage for forty minutes
before the fire department extracted him. By the time the ambulance reached the hospital, there was
nothing left to save. Marco had been working the night shift at the state penitentiary, walking the
cellblocks in his uniform, and he learned the news in the parking lot afterward, his supervisor's
hand on his shoulder, the words dissolving into static. He had not been there. He had not been able
to stop it.

For the first few weeks, Marco moved through the world like a ghost. His wife, Catalina, watched him
sit at the kitchen table for hours, staring at nothing. He returned to work because staying home
felt worse—the house was full of Diego's absence, photographs he couldn't bear to see. But the
prison became its own kind of trap. One afternoon in the second month, while processing intake
paperwork in the administrative office, a news alert scrolled across the computer screen: a local
story about a fatal accident, a young man killed instantly. Marco's vision narrowed. The room felt
suddenly thin and unreal, as though he were watching himself from outside his body, and the
fluorescent lights seemed to pulse with menace. He stood up, his hands shaking, and walked directly
to the break room, where he found another guard, Ramirez, making coffee. Without preamble, Marco
accused him of something petty—of taking his lunch from the fridge weeks ago—and within minutes they
were shouting at each other, their voices echoing off the metal lockers. It was a fight Marco didn't
actually believe in, but it burned away the dissociation, replaced it with hot anger that felt like
being alive.

Catalina noticed the pattern. He would come home agitated, looking for reasons to argue with her
about the dishes, about money, about nothing. She began to fear his moods, and he began to fear what
would happen if she left him. He had already failed to protect Diego. He could not fail at his
marriage too. Yet the more he tried to control it, the more fragile it seemed. When his mother
called to talk about setting up a memorial service, Marco felt that same sickening distance creeping
in—the world becoming cotton, his body a puppet. He told his mother he couldn't talk about it, that
they should just cremate Diego and move on, and then he picked a fight with Catalina that night,
accusing her of being cold and unsupportive, knowing even as he spoke that it wasn't true. The
argument burned the dissociation away, but it left Catalina crying in the bedroom, and Marco sat
alone in the dark, convinced that he was incapable of being the husband she deserved, that he had
somehow already ruined everything by not being the brother Diego needed.

The avoidance had become total. He took different routes home to avoid the street where Diego used
to live. He changed the radio station if a song came on that his brother had loved. He stopped
answering his mother's calls because hearing her voice triggered the same sense of unreality, and he
knew he would come home looking for a fight that would only push Catalina further away. But the more
he avoided these reminders, the more his mind seemed to trap him with them. Driving past the highway
exit where the accident occurred—even when he tried to take a different route, he sometimes ended up
there anyway, his hands white-knuckled on the wheel—he would feel the dissociation creeping in, that
terrible floating feeling, and he would have to pull into a gas station and start an argument with
the clerk over something meaningless, just to feel solid again. He had come to believe that he was
fundamentally broken, that happiness was something that happened to other men, not to him. The
future looked like an endless corridor of the same days, the same failures, the same desperate
scramble to outrun a grief that was always catching up. What frightened him most was that Catalina
would finally see what he already knew—that he was a man who couldn't protect anyone, not even
himself.

When he called the therapist's office last week, it was because Catalina had threatened to leave.
Marco told the intake coordinator that he needed help, but what he really meant was that he was
terrified of losing the last person he had left, and he had no idea how to stop the machinery inside
himself that was grinding them both to dust.
