# Layla's Vigil

Layla Mansour had been the manager of the Grandview Hotel for eight years, a position she'd earned
through meticulous attention to detail and an almost preternatural ability to anticipate guests'
needs before they voiced them. That changed on a Tuesday afternoon in March when a contractor's crew
accidentally ruptured a chemical line in the basement during routine maintenance. The smell came
first—acrid, caustic, unlike anything she'd encountered before—followed by the discovery that three
staff members had collapsed and were being rushed to the hospital. Layla had been in the basement
herself, showing the contractor where the electrical panel was located, when the fumes hit. Her
throat burned. Her eyes watered so intensely she could barely see. She'd stumbled upstairs gasping,
convinced her lungs were being eaten from the inside, and by the time the ambulance arrived, she was
hyperventilating so badly that the paramedics had to stabilize her before transport.

The hospital had kept her for observation. They'd run tests, assured her that her lungs were intact,
that she'd been exposed but not fatally so. The other workers recovered within days. Layla was
discharged after twelve hours with a clean bill of health and a prescription for anxiety medication
she never filled. But something had fractured in her sense of safety. Within weeks, she began to
notice that certain ordinary moments—a whiff of bleach in the hallway, the sharp smell of hand
sanitizer, the antiseptic odor that clung to her clothes after visiting her physician—would send her
heart racing and her mind spiraling back to that basement, to the sensation of poison in her lungs.
The hospital smell in particular had become almost unbearable. During her follow-up appointment,
she'd sat in the waiting room fighting waves of panic, convinced that the very air was dangerous,
that her body was about to betray her again. What frightened her most was that the danger felt real
every time, even though her rational mind knew it wasn't.

The workplace itself had become a minefield. Layla returned to the Grandview after two weeks away,
determined not to let the incident define her role. But the building felt different now—less like
her domain and more like a place where catastrophe could strike without warning. Any unusual sound—a
door slamming, the clang of maintenance equipment, even the sharp beep of a delivery truck backing
up to the loading dock—would jolt her into a state of high alert, her body flooding with adrenaline
as though the chemical exposure were happening again. Stress amplified everything. On busy days when
the hotel was full and staffing was tight, her vigilance would intensify to an unbearable pitch. She
found herself unable to sleep properly, lying awake at night in her bedroom, ears straining for
sounds that might indicate danger, her mind cycling through worst-case scenarios. She'd come to
believe that sleep was a luxury she couldn't afford, that letting her guard down even for a few
hours might mean missing the warning signs of the next disaster. Her husband, Reza, would find her
at three in the morning sitting in the kitchen with the lights on, or discover that she'd moved to
the guest room so her restlessness wouldn't disturb him.

The cycle had tightened over the months. The more hypervigilant she became, the more sensitive she
was to triggers—the smell of cleaning products in the hallway, the sound of someone shouting an
order in the kitchen—and each trigger sent her back into that state of perceived threat, her body
rigid with the certainty that something catastrophic was imminent. The workplace stress that had
once energized her now amplified her fear, making her hyperalert to every potential hazard, every
unexpected noise. She'd begun to avoid the basement entirely, taking the long route around the
building rather than passing through it, which had created logistical problems she couldn't quite
solve without revealing her distress. Her performance reviews had slipped. She'd snapped at a junior
manager over a minor scheduling error—something the old Layla would never have done. Three months
ago, Reza had gently suggested that perhaps she should talk to someone, that the incident seemed to
be affecting more than just her sleep. She'd resisted, insisting she was fine, that it would pass.
But last week, a guest had complained about the faint smell of paint thinner in a hallway, and Layla
had found herself unable to respond professionally. She'd excused herself and locked herself in her
office for twenty minutes, her heart pounding, convinced that the building was poisoning her again.
That night, she finally called her primary care physician and asked for a referral to a therapist,
admitting for the first time that she couldn't manage this alone.
