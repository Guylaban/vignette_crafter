# Clinical Vignette: Roberto

Roberto Hernández was sixty-five years old when he finally made an appointment with a therapist,
though he had been living with the weight of his military service for nearly four decades. He had
been a young soldier in Central America during the early 1980s, stationed in a remote outpost where
the boundary between combat and routine had dissolved into something permanent. One afternoon in
1985, his unit was ambushed while on patrol near a ravine. The attack came without warning—a cascade
of gunfire that seemed to come from everywhere at once. Roberto's best friend, Miguel, was killed
instantly beside him. The taste of dust and copper blood had filled his mouth as he lay flat against
the earth, unable to move, unable to help. The smell of the cordite hung in the air for hours
afterward. When the shooting finally stopped, there was only silence and the weight of Miguel's body
a few feet away.

For the first ten years after his discharge, Roberto had managed well enough. He married, had two
children, and eventually became a special education teacher, a job that gave him purpose and
steadied him. But the marriage deteriorated over time—his wife said he was distant, that he never
seemed truly present, that he couldn't sit through a family dinner without scanning the room or
positioning himself near the door. They divorced in 2005. He had thrown himself into his work at
Lincoln Middle School, where he taught students with emotional and behavioral difficulties. It was
meaningful work, but it required him to remain in crowded hallways and unpredictable environments.

Around 2015, without clear precipitant, something shifted. Roberto began experiencing episodes of
dizziness that would strike suddenly, sometimes when he was simply walking down the school corridor.
The sensation of lightheadedness would grip him, and his mind would immediately return to the
ravine—to the moment he realized Miguel was gone. These dizzy spells became triggers that sent him
spiraling. Whenever he felt that vertigo, his body would tense, his breathing would quicken, and he
would find himself scanning the hallway for exits, mentally mapping the quickest route to safety. He
began insisting on standing near doorways during staff meetings, positioning himself so that he
could leave without obstruction. His colleagues had learned not to comment on it.

What had started as occasional caution evolved into something more consuming. Roberto found himself
hyperalert to any sudden sound—a door slamming, a student shouting, even the unexpected beep of the
photocopier would send a jolt through his chest. His mind had come to interpret these sounds as
potential threats, and his body responded accordingly. He was always scanning, always watching,
always identifying where he could escape. This constant vigilance exhausted him, but the moment he
tried to relax his guard, anxiety would spike. He had come to believe that his alertness was the
only thing keeping him safe, that if he let down his vigilance for even a moment, something terrible
would happen—to him, to his students, to someone he cared about.

Over the years, Roberto had developed a pattern of avoiding situations that stirred strong emotion.
He declined invitations to social gatherings. He stopped attending his daughter's college graduation
because the thought of sitting in an auditorium full of people, unable to position himself near an
exit, felt unbearable. He had also begun avoiding any conversation about his military service. When
his son once asked him directly about what happened in Central America, Roberto had shut down
completely. Talking about the event—about Miguel, about that afternoon—brought the taste back to his
mouth, made the dizziness worse, and sent his threat-detection system into overdrive. He had learned
that the safest thing was to keep those memories sealed away, to stay away from people and
situations that might crack them open.

This avoidance had consequences. His relationships with his adult children had become strained and
distant. He felt increasingly isolated, though he told himself that isolation was safer than
connection. His hypervigilance had become so automatic that he barely noticed it anymore—the
constant scanning, the mental calculation of exits, the way his body remained perpetually tensed for
danger. It was only when his son insisted he see someone, expressing concern that his father seemed
to be disappearing into himself, that Roberto finally agreed to seek help. He sat in the waiting
room of the therapist's office, noting the exits, feeling the familiar dizziness, and wondering
whether talking about any of this could possibly be worth the cost.
