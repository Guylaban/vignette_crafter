# Clinical Vignette: Margaret Chen

Margaret Chen was forty-four when the bomb went off outside the community center in Minneapolis
where she was working as a chaplain. It was a Tuesday morning in March, three years ago. She had
stepped into the hallway to take a call from a parishioner when the blast shattered the windows of
the administrative office behind her. The noise came first—a sound so complete it seemed to erase
everything else—followed by the smell of smoke and burning insulation, the heat on her face, the
taste of dust and something metallic in her mouth. She remembered the way the fluorescent lights
flickered and died, remembered the sound of people screaming, and then—a gap, a confusion of moments
she could not arrange in any sensible order. She had been in the building. People had been hurt. She
had not been able to help them. The authorities had failed to prevent it, despite all their
resources, all their promises of safety. She had survived, but something essential about how she
understood the world had fractured in that moment.

In the months that followed, Margaret came to believe that the world was fundamentally
dangerous—that nowhere was truly safe, that institutions designed to protect people were, at their
core, failures waiting to happen. This conviction crystallized whenever she found herself around
figures of authority. When a police officer entered the community center—now reopened, though
Margaret dreaded each shift—her chest would tighten and her mind would race. When she saw someone in
uniform at the grocery store, her body would flood with a sensation of exposure and threat. She had
come to associate authority with the catastrophic failure that had allowed a bomb to be placed so
close to where she worked, where children sometimes came for after-school programs. These encounters
triggered a deep sense that the people meant to keep her safe had proven themselves untrustworthy,
and this realization drove her to seek control in the only ways she could find. She began to perform
small mental rituals—counting backwards from seven in groups of three, visualizing protective
barriers around the building, repeating phrases meant to ward off harm. If she could just execute
these routines perfectly, if she could maintain perfect vigilance, perhaps she could prevent another
catastrophe. The logic was not rational, but it felt necessary.

When Margaret witnessed people misbehaving—a man cutting in line, someone speaking harshly to a
child, a coworker making a cruel joke—she was seized by a sense that morality itself had become
unreliable. These small moral violations felt like evidence that the world was corrupt, that cruelty
was embedded in ordinary human behavior. She found herself mentally rehearsing protective rituals
with greater intensity after these moments, as if she could somehow compensate for the world's
fundamental badness through her own perfect conduct. Similarly, any situation in which she could not
control the outcome—a meeting she was not invited to, a decision made without her input, a delay in
receiving information—triggered a cascade of anxiety that fed directly into her coping strategies.
She would restrict her eating, consuming only coffee and small amounts of protein, believing that by
controlling what entered her body she could maintain some dominion over her physical safety. The
undereating had become habitual; she had lost twelve pounds over the past year, and her colleagues
had begun to notice her thinness with concern she could not quite accept.

The memories themselves had become a source of torment that reinforced everything she believed about
danger and her own inadequacy. When the memory surfaced—and it did so unpredictably, triggered by a
siren, a sudden noise, the smell of burnt plastic—it returned in complete sensory flooding. She
could feel the heat again, hear the screaming, taste the dust. The memory felt more vivid and real
than her actual present-moment experiences, as if the trauma were more fundamentally true than the
ordinary moments of her life. She could not arrange the sequence of what had happened; the moments
seemed to exist outside time, all occurring at once. This disorganization terrified her because it
meant she could not make sense of what had happened, could not learn from it, could not extract any
meaning that might help her prevent another catastrophe. The intrusive reliving confirmed her worst
belief about herself: that she was a poor coper, that her nervous system had been fundamentally
damaged, that she would never recover her capacity to function as she once had. These thoughts would
spiral until she felt paralyzed, which is when the threat monitoring would intensify.

In her chaplain's office, Margaret had developed an elaborate system of seat selection. She always
positioned herself in the corner of the room during meetings, her back to the wall, with a clear
view of the door and the windows. When she sat elsewhere, a sensation of exposure would build until
she could not concentrate on anything but escape routes and potential threats. She watched people
closely now—colleagues, parishioners, strangers on the street—scanning their faces and bodies for
signs of danger, for the micro-expressions that might indicate hostile intent. When unexpected
sounds occurred—a car backfiring, a door slamming, someone raising their voice—her entire body would
jolt into high alert, her heart racing, her breath shallow. These threat-detection behaviors had
become so automatic that she was barely aware of them anymore. Yet they fed directly back into her
need for control and prevention. The more she watched others, the more dangerous the world seemed;
the more dangerous it seemed, the more elaborate her protective rituals became. She had started
avoiding the staff meetings where the director—a woman in a position of authority—would speak, which
meant she was missing important information about the center's operations. Her absence from these
meetings had begun to isolate her from her colleagues and had made it harder for her to do her job
effectively. She had been passed over for a leadership role she had previously held, a loss she
experienced not as professional disappointment but as confirmation that her judgment was
compromised, that she was indeed a poor coper who could not be trusted with responsibility.

It was her supervisor's gentle insistence that Margaret finally made an appointment with a trauma
therapist. She came to the first session exhausted and skeptical, convinced that her reactions made
perfect sense given what had happened, that her vigilance was necessary and her rituals were the
only thing standing between her and complete breakdown. What frightened her most was not the memory
itself anymore, but the possibility that this was her permanent state—that the person she had been
before the bomb was gone forever, replaced by someone fragile, controlled by fear, unable to eat or
sleep or sit in a room without calculating her escape.
