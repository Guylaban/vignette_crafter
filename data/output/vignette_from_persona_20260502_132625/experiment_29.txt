# Marcus's Story

Marcus was seventeen when the tornado touched down outside Omaha. He'd been working his shift at the
Burger King on Highway 275 when the manager shouted to get everyone away from the windows. He
remembered the sound first—a roar like nothing he'd ever heard, a freight train amplified a thousand
times, filling his chest with a vibration he could feel in his teeth. The lights had flickered once,
twice, then died. In the darkness, he'd crouched with three coworkers in the walk-in freezer,
listening to the building scream around them. When they finally emerged, the restaurant was gutted.
The roof was gone. The smell—wet concrete, torn insulation, something chemical and acrid—had filled
his lungs, and even now, nineteen months later, when Marcus closed his eyes and let himself think
about that day, he could taste it at the back of his throat.

The physical injuries had healed within weeks. A fractured wrist, some lacerations. But something
else had fractured too, something Marcus couldn't see in a mirror or wrap in a cast. For months
afterward, he'd tried to push through it. He'd returned to work within two weeks, convinced that
showing up, staying normal, would prove the tornado hadn't changed him. But the more he tried to
move forward, the more he became convinced that he couldn't—that whatever person he'd been before
that freezer was gone for good. The realization had crept up on him slowly. He used to joke around
with coworkers, used to look forward to things. Now he felt hollowed out, a shell of someone else's
life. He had become unrecognizable to himself, and the thought terrified him in a way that made no
sense, because it was his own mind doing the recognizing.

When memories of the tornado surfaced—and they did, unbidden, at random moments—they came with the
full force of the original terror. A car alarm in the parking lot would trigger a flash of the
freezer darkness. The smell of the industrial kitchen would suddenly morph into that acrid chemical
stench, and his body would respond as though the danger were happening again, right then, his heart
hammering, his breath shallow. The images were vivid and intrusive: the manager's face in the moment
before they ran, the texture of the metal shelving he'd gripped. These memories weren't like normal
remembering; they felt like reliving, complete with the same suffocating dread he'd felt trapped in
that freezer. Because he'd come to believe that he was fundamentally and permanently damaged, that
the tornado had rewired something essential in him, every intrusive memory felt like confirmation.
Each time the terror returned, it reinforced his conviction that he was broken now, that this was
his new reality.

Desperate to escape the thoughts and the memories and the unbearable feeling of being someone he
didn't recognize, Marcus had begun to construct barriers around himself. Before any
conversation—with a customer, a coworker, his mother—he would rehearse what he might say, running
through possible exchanges in his head like a script, trying to anticipate what might go wrong and
how he might control it. He avoided anything that felt remotely stressful: he'd stopped going to his
friend Diego's house because the drive took him past the highway where the tornado had touched down;
he'd declined his cousin's wedding invitation because crowds felt overwhelming; he'd even started
taking shifts at the quietest times, avoiding the busy lunch rush. And whenever the thoughts
came—the images, the smells, the creeping certainty that he was irreparably changed—he would try
with all his strength not to think about them, pushing them away, distracting himself with his phone
or his work, anything to keep the memories at bay.

But the avoidance had a cost he hadn't anticipated. By rehearsing conversations and controlling
every interaction, by shrinking his world to only the safest scenarios, Marcus had reinforced his
own sense that he couldn't handle normal life anymore—that the world was too dangerous and he was
too fragile. His attempts to suppress the thoughts often backfired; the harder he pushed them away,
the more forcefully they returned. And his growing isolation, the way he'd withdrawn from people and
places, had left him with fewer ways to challenge the belief that he was permanently damaged. He'd
missed Diego's birthday dinner. His mother had stopped asking him to family events. Two months ago,
his manager had asked if he was okay, and Marcus had said nothing, just shrugged and gone back to
the register. He wasn't okay. He was nineteen years old and felt like a stranger in his own skin,
and the person he used to be—the one who could laugh, who had plans, who wasn't afraid all the
time—felt like someone he'd read about in a story, not someone he'd ever actually been.

It was his mother who finally insisted he talk to someone. Marcus had resisted, convinced that
therapy wouldn't help someone as broken as he was, but he'd come to the appointment anyway, still
rehearsing what he might say, still braced for the moment when his own mind would betray him again
with an image or a smell or that terrible sense of falling that had no bottom.
