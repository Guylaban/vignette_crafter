# Margaret's Vigil

Margaret Kowalski had spent fifty-two years in the public defender's office, a lean woman with
steel-grey hair and the kind of courtroom presence that made junior prosecutors nervous. She had
argued cases in front of judges who remembered her from their own early careers, had cross-examined
witnesses with surgical precision, had won acquittals that her colleagues still cited in the break
room. At seventy-eight, she had planned to retire at the end of the fiscal year—maybe take her
husband David to Portugal, finally visit the Douro Valley she'd read about for decades. That plan
had belonged to a different Margaret, one who could walk to her car without scanning the parking
garage first.

The assault happened on a Tuesday evening in March, six months ago. Margaret had stayed late at the
office to finish motions for a juvenile case—a sixteen-year-old accused of armed robbery, and she
believed the search had been unconstitutional. She had left the building around eight o'clock, her
arms full of case files, her mind still turning over the Fourth Amendment angles. The parking garage
was poorly lit; she had parked on the third level, as she always did. She had not heard the man
approach. What she remembered with perfect, unwanted clarity was the sudden pressure of an arm
around her neck, the files scattering across concrete, the voice in her ear—rough, urgent—demanding
her purse. She had fought. At seventy-eight, with arthritis in her left shoulder and no training in
restraint, she had fought anyway, and he had thrown her against the concrete pillar. Her head struck
hard. There was blood on her glasses. He took the purse and ran.

The police found him three days later. Margaret had given a clear description, and he was arrested.
The prosecutor's office offered her victim support services, which she had declined with the
briskness she applied to everything else. She went back to work after two weeks. She told David she
was fine. She told herself the same thing, with diminishing conviction, each morning.

What she had not anticipated was how the world had rearranged itself around threat. Every time she
entered the parking garage now—and she still went to the office, still worked her cases—her body
moved through a different geography. She parked on the first level now, closer to the elevator, and
she checked behind her constantly, her eyes sweeping the shadows between the concrete pillars, her
shoulders tense with the anticipation of an arm around her neck that never came but might. In the
hallway outside her office, she watched other people closely, cataloguing them. The young man who
delivered mail—was he watching her? The paralegal who passed her desk—did his glance linger too
long? She found herself assessing threats in everyone, a habit that had crept in so gradually she
could not pinpoint when vigilance had become her default mode.

Her startle response had become almost comical, if it were not so exhausting. David had knocked on
the bathroom door last week and she had nearly dropped the coffee mug in her hand. A car backfired
on the street outside their home and she had jumped so violently that David had asked, quietly, if
she wanted to talk to someone. She had said no. She was managing. But the truth was that her body
had become a tripwire, constantly primed for danger that had already passed.

The courtroom, once her kingdom, had become another place where she scanned for threat. She found
herself watching jurors for signs of aggression, monitoring the opposing counsel for sudden
movements. Last month, during a cross-examination, a witness had raised his voice and Margaret's
hands had trembled so badly that she had to set down her notes. She had excused herself, composed
herself in the bathroom, and returned to finish the questioning. But something had shifted. She was
no longer certain she could trust her body to carry her through a trial without betraying her.

Margaret had finally called her physician last week. She had not told David. She sat in the waiting
room, a woman who had spent her life defending the rights of the accused, now afraid of the world in
a way she did not fully understand. What frightened her most was not that the assault would happen
again—though that fear lived in her too—but that this vigilance, this constant monitoring, this
hair-trigger startle, had become permanent. That the woman who had walked into that parking garage
in March no longer existed. That she would spend her remaining years looking behind her, watching
others for signs of violence, jumping at shadows.
