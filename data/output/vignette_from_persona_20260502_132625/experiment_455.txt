# Kofi's Cycle

Kofi was seventy-seven when he first came to the clinic, his posture still upright, his manner
precise and courteous, though something in his eyes suggested a perpetual bracing. He had been a
software engineer for nearly forty years—first in Ghana, then in the United States after he and his
wife, Ama, had fled in 1986. That escape had been necessary, but it had never truly ended. The
trauma had simply learned to live inside him, dormant until the smallest trigger would resurrect it
whole.

The event itself had been crystalline in its brutality. Kofi had been a young man then, working as a
systems analyst for the government, when soldiers arrived at his office one morning. They had not
knocked. The commander—a man with a scarred face and a voice that seemed to come from somewhere
beneath the earth—had pointed at Kofi and issued a single command: "You. Come." That voice, that
gesture, the absolute authority in it, had fractured something in Kofi that had never fully healed.
He had been taken to a detention facility, interrogated for sixteen hours, beaten, and released only
because a superior officer had intervened. He had never learned why. The uncertainty had become part
of the trauma itself.

Decades later, Kofi could not tolerate being given orders. Even a simple instruction from his
supervisor—"Kofi, I need you to rewrite that module"—would trigger a cascade of panic. His chest
would tighten, and without quite understanding why, he would find himself avoiding the office
altogether. He began working from home more, then exclusively from home, and eventually he had taken
early retirement. The command itself was the trigger, but the trigger did not merely remind him of
the past; it seemed to unlock something in his mind that pulled forth the memories in their rawest
form. When a colleague had emailed him instructions about a software update, Kofi had felt the walls
of his home contract around him. Within minutes, he was reliving the moment the soldiers entered—not
as narrative, but as a series of disconnected images: the door opening, the scarred face, the
pointing finger, the fluorescent light of the detention room. Each image was crystalline and
separate, like slides in a projector, and they came without feeling, without context, as though they
had happened to someone else entirely.

Over the years, Kofi had developed strategies to keep the triggers at bay. He stopped going out
unnecessarily. Ama would ask him to accompany her to church, to the market, to visit their children,
but he would decline, citing fatigue or some small ailment. The world outside felt saturated with
potential commands—from store clerks, from traffic officers, from anyone in a position of authority.
Avoidance felt safer. He also began to avoid people more broadly, keeping conversations brief,
declining invitations, spending his evenings alone in his study with his computer, keeping his mind
occupied with programming problems and online courses. These strategies seemed to work, at least
temporarily. When he was not around people, when his mind was fully absorbed in a technical puzzle,
the images did not surface.

But the avoidance itself had become a kind of prison. The more Kofi isolated himself, the more
fragile his equilibrium became. Staying home, avoiding social contact, filling his days with endless
mental tasks—these behaviors had begun to feel necessary, almost ritualistic. And paradoxically, the
very act of withdrawing seemed to sharpen his vigilance. When he did venture out—to a doctor's
appointment, to the grocery store—his mind was hyperalert, scanning for authority figures, for
anyone who might issue a command. A security guard at the bank asking him to remove his hat
triggered a wave of dissociation; he felt unreal, as though watching himself from outside his body.
The dissociative feeling itself then became another trigger, because the unreality reminded him of
the emotional numbness he had experienced during the detention, and that numbness would pull forth
those fragmented visual memories again—the scarred face, the pointing, the fluorescent light. The
cycle had tightened around him so completely that he could no longer distinguish between the
trigger, the memory, the avoidance, and the dissociation. They had become one continuous loop.

What finally brought Kofi to the clinic was a conversation with his grandson. The boy had asked why
Grandfather never came to his soccer games anymore. Kofi had no answer that made sense, even to
himself. Ama had suggested, gently but firmly, that he needed help. He sat now in the therapist's
office, his hands folded in his lap, and said quietly, "I am afraid I have become a ghost in my own
life."
