# Clinical Vignette: Dr. Marcus Feldman

Marcus Feldman was fifty years old and had spent twenty-eight years building a reputation as a
careful, attentive psychologist. His practice in Portland catered largely to trauma survivors—a
specialty he had chosen deliberately, drawn by the intellectual rigor of understanding how the mind
preserved and distorted what it could not process. He had written papers on memory reconsolidation,
taught seminars on cognitive restructuring, and sat with dozens of patients as they learned to name
what had happened to them. He had believed, with the confidence of someone who had never truly faced
what he asked others to face, that understanding trauma intellectually was close enough to surviving
it.

The assault had occurred on a Friday evening in March, five months ago, in the parking garage
beneath his office building. A man—someone Marcus had never seen before and whose face remained
stubbornly absent from any coherent memory—had forced him into a stairwell. What followed was
violent and prolonged. Marcus had been alone. No one had heard him. The man had smelled of whiskey
and cigarettes, and when it was over, he had simply walked away, leaving Marcus on the concrete
steps in the dark.

The first weeks had been a strange suspension. Marcus had reported the assault to police, undergone
a medical examination, and returned to work with a kind of grim determination. His wife, Elena, had
wanted him to take time off, but he had insisted he was fine—a phrase that had calcified into their
household like sediment. He had not told his colleagues. He had told no one except Elena and the
detective, and even then, he had described the facts with the detachment of someone reading a case
file. But the facts themselves had become increasingly unreliable.

Now, five months later, Marcus found himself unable to enter the parking garage. The smell of
alcohol—someone's drink spilled in the hallway, a patient's breath during a session—sent a sharp,
wordless terror through his body that had nothing to do with rational thought. His mind would seize,
and for a moment he would be back on those steps, though he could never quite hold the image. What
he held instead was a raw, consuming sense of his own obliteration. These moments would arrive
without warning: a siren outside his office window, the particular discomfort of sitting too close
to someone in an elevator, and suddenly Marcus would feel the full weight of what he had come to
believe about himself—that he was no longer capable of the work that had defined him, that he had
become an object in someone else's narrative rather than the author of his own, that some essential
part of him had been extinguished and would not reignite.

The more these beliefs took hold, the more insistently the triggers pursued him. He began to avoid
not just the parking garage but anywhere with uncertain exits. He stopped taking the bus, where a
siren might sound and leave him stranded among strangers. He declined invitations to restaurants
where alcohol was served, where the smell might ambush him. What he could not explain to Elena was
that avoidance was not simply caution—it was survival. Each trigger confirmed what he already knew:
that he was broken, that the world was no longer safe, that he could not trust his own mind. And
each confirmation made it harder to move through the day without feeling that annihilating
emptiness, that sense of being hollowed out and left behind.

The memory itself remained fragmented, resistant. Marcus would push toward it in therapy—his own
therapy, which he had finally begun—and feel it scatter before he could hold it whole. Sometimes
there was no image at all, only the emotional intensity, a drowning sensation without context. Other
times he would catch a fragment: a hand, a voice, the cold of the concrete. But it would not cohere
into a narrative he could speak aloud or master through understanding. This fractured quality seemed
to feed his sense of powerlessness. He could not name what had happened, which meant he could not
contain it, which meant it would continue to erupt through his body without warning, confirming yet
again that he was no longer the person he had believed himself to be.

What finally brought Marcus to seek specialized trauma treatment was a moment in his office three
weeks ago, when a patient had mentioned a smell—something in the waiting room—and Marcus had found
himself unable to continue the session. He had excused himself, locked his office door, and sat in
the dark for twenty minutes, shaking. Afterward, he had written to a colleague who specialized in
trauma-focused therapy. In that email, he had written a single sentence that seemed to contain
everything: "I need to remember what I'm afraid of, or I need to stop being afraid of remembering."
He was uncertain which was possible, or whether the difference mattered anymore.
