# Clinical Vignette: Karim

Karim was fifty-five when he was attacked outside his grain storage facility on a humid August
evening three years ago. He had stayed late to repair a broken conveyor belt, the kind of task he
had done a thousand times across four decades of farming his family's land in central California.
Two men emerged from the darkness near the loading dock. He never saw their faces clearly—later,
when police asked him to describe them, he could only offer fragments: a tattoo on one wrist, the
sound of rapid breathing, the smell of stale sweat and cologne mixing in the warm air. They wanted
money. When Karim refused, they beat him methodically, one holding him while the other struck his
ribs and face. He remembered falling, remembered the taste of blood, remembered the sensation of
being utterly powerless. Then nothing clear until he was on the ground and they were gone, and his
own breathing was loud in his ears.

The assault lasted perhaps three minutes. In the three years since, Karim has been unable to
assemble those minutes into a coherent story. When his wife Elena asks him what happened, he starts
to explain—he was working late, the men came from the west side of the building—but the sequence
fragments in his mind, details scatter, and he loses his place mid-sentence. The memory feels
borrowed, as if he is recounting something that happened to someone else, something he witnessed
rather than lived. He can summon isolated pieces: the smell of sweat, the pressure of hands on his
arms, the specific angle of moonlight on the concrete. But they do not connect. There is no thread.
This fragmentation has become its own source of distress; he feels as though he cannot fully own
what happened to him, cannot integrate it into his life story.

For the first year after the assault, Karim threw himself into work with an intensity that alarmed
Elena. He would rise before dawn and work until dusk, then come inside and spend his evenings
reviewing crop reports, planning the next season's irrigation schedules, anything to keep his mind
occupied and moving. It was during those months that he first noticed the pattern: whenever his own
heart raced—whether from exertion in the fields or from the anxiety that had begun to creep into his
baseline—the memories would surface in those same fragmented, disorienting pieces. His racing heart
became a trigger, a somatic reminder that sent him spiraling into the scattered recollection of the
attack. When that happened, he could not sit still. He would immediately find another task, another
project, another reason to work. The overworking was not laziness or dedication; it was a form of
flight. He had come to believe that if he kept moving, kept his mind occupied, the memories would
not coalesce into something he would have to fully feel or understand.

Karim also began to notice people acting suspiciously—a neighbor lingering too long at the property
line, a truck that seemed to circle the farm roads more than once. His body would tense, his
vigilance would sharpen, and within moments the fragmented memories would intrude again, pulling him
back to that August night. When that happened, rumination would take hold. He would replay the
assault in disconnected flashes and find himself consumed with thoughts of revenge, imagining
scenarios in which he confronted the men, in which he was stronger, faster, in control. These
fantasies of retaliation were both a response to his helplessness and a way of keeping his mind
engaged—another form of the same driven, relentless mental activity that characterized his days. He
had come to believe that if he could just figure out how to get even, how to reclaim some sense of
agency, the fragmented pieces might finally make sense.

He also began to dread the anniversary of the assault. August became a month he tried to skip
entirely, throwing himself into harvest preparation weeks early or finding reasons to travel. The
dates themselves felt dangerous, as if the proximity to that evening might somehow trigger another
attack.

Three months ago, Elena confronted him. She said he was disappearing into work, that she barely
recognized him anymore, that he needed help. Karim is here now because his wife insisted, because he
is exhausted, and because some part of him recognizes that the fragmented memories are not becoming
clearer with time—they are becoming more entrenched, more alien, more impossible to integrate. He is
afraid that if he stops working, stops moving, stops planning revenge, the memories will finally
cohere into something unbearable. He is also afraid that they never will.
