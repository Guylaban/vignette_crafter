# Katja's Story

Katja had been a relief worker in Aleppo for three months before the checkpoint. She was forty-two,
a former combat pilot who had traded the cockpit for humanitarian work—a choice that had made sense
to her at the time, a way to channel her precision and discipline toward something that mattered.
The checkpoint was routine: she was in a white van with two colleagues, moving medical supplies to a
clinic in the eastern sector. It was February, brutally cold, and the van's heating was broken. She
remembered the cold most vividly—not as background but as a physical fact that seemed to sharpen
everything else, the way cold air burns in the lungs. They were stopped by men in unmarked uniforms.
What followed was hours of interrogation, threats, the sound of gunfire outside the compound, and
the certainty, crystalline and absolute, that she would die there. She wasn't harmed—not
physically—but the threat had been total and the release, when it came, had felt like a reprieve she
didn't deserve.

That was four years ago. Katja had returned home to Massachusetts, to her husband Derek and their
quiet suburban life, and for the first two years she had functioned. She had debriefed with her
organization, attended a few therapy sessions, and gradually resumed the rhythms of civilian life.
But something had fractured in a way that didn't heal. Now, in the winter months, when the
temperature dropped below freezing, Katja's body would tighten with an animal alertness. The cold
itself became a trigger—not a memory, exactly, but a gateway. When cold air touched her skin, the
interrogation room rushed back with a vividness that obliterated the present moment. She would be
standing in her driveway, frost crystallizing on the grass, and suddenly she was there again, the
smell of diesel and concrete overwhelming, the voices speaking a language she half-understood, the
certainty of danger pressing against her chest. The cold would bring the memory, and the memory
would bring a cascade of thoughts—that she had been foolish to go there at all, that she had failed
her colleagues by being weak during the interrogation, that the world was fundamentally unsafe and
her previous beliefs about her own competence and the possibility of meaningful work had been a
naive delusion.

The dreams came too, most nights, and waking from them was its own trigger. Katja would surface
gasping, the nightmare's logic still clinging to her—sometimes she was back in the compound,
sometimes the threat had followed her home, sometimes Derek's face became the face of one of the
interrogators. These awakenings would jolt her into a state of hypervigilance that could last for
hours. She would listen for sounds in the house—the creak of a floorboard, a car passing on the
street—and her mind would race through catastrophic interpretations. The sound of cars was
particularly potent. Derek would come home from work and the sound of his car in the driveway would
send her into a panic, her body convinced that it was a threat, that someone was coming for her. The
car sounds seemed to activate the same neural pathways as the checkpoint—the sound of an engine, the
crunch of gravel, and suddenly she was certain that danger was imminent. This certainty would flood
her with thoughts about how little she actually understood about safety, how the world operated
according to rules she had never grasped, how her identity as a capable, rational person had been
revealed as a fiction.

These negative appraisals—the conviction that she was fundamentally flawed, that meaning had drained
from her life, that she no longer knew who she was—had become the architecture of her days. Because
nothing felt meaningful anymore, Katja found herself reaching for ways to escape the blankness. She
had begun spending money compulsively, ordering things online in the middle of the night, charging
thousands of dollars to credit cards for items she didn't need. The spending provided a brief window
of distraction, a moment where her mind could focus on something other than the weight of her own
meaninglessness. But the spending also deepened her sense of identity loss—this wasn't who she was,
this wasn't how she behaved, and yet here she was, unable to stop. The compulsive purchases would
arrive, and she would feel a fresh wave of self-blame, convinced that she had failed in yet another
way, that her inability to control even this small thing proved the truth of what she now believed
about herself. She had also begun avoiding anything that might trigger the memories—no news, no
documentaries, nothing about conflict zones or humanitarian crises. She had deleted news apps from
her phone and instructed Derek not to mention anything about the Middle East. But the avoidance
itself became a problem. It kept the memories potent and unprocessed, and the effort required to
maintain the avoidance exhausted her.

The threat monitoring had become relentless. Katja would wake at three or four in the morning, her
heart racing, and she would get out of bed and check the doors and windows, sometimes multiple times
a night. She would listen at the edges of her consciousness for any sound that might signal an
intruder—footsteps on the porch, a hand on the doorknob, a window being tested. This hypervigilance,
born from the memory of the interrogation room where danger had been real and imminent, now colored
every moment of her waking life. The threat felt present in a way that logic couldn't touch. And
because she felt threatened, because her nervous system had learned that the world contained hidden
dangers, she began to see threat in ordinary situations. Strangers approaching her in the grocery
store felt menacing. A neighbor asking to borrow tools seemed suspicious. The sense of threat would
bloom from nothing, and it would pull her back into the memories—she would remember the
interrogators' faces, the way they had stood too close, the feeling of being trapped and exposed.
The memories themselves felt more real than her actual life, more vivid and immediate than the
memory of her daughter's high school graduation or her wedding day. She could recall the texture of
the concrete floor in the interrogation room with perfect clarity, but when she tried to describe
what had happened to Derek or to a therapist, the words would scatter. She knew what had
occurred—she could see it, feel it, hear it—but translating it into language felt impossible, as
though the event existed in a realm beyond words.

Katja's avoidance of media and news had made sense as a way to protect herself from triggers, but it
had also isolated her further. She found herself unable to engage with her husband about current
events, unable to watch television with him in the evenings. She had become a stranger in her own
home, going through motions that felt hollow and disconnected. Her work as a pilot had suffered too.
She had taken a leave of absence six months ago—the thought of being in a cockpit, of being
responsible for others' safety when she couldn't even keep herself safe, had become intolerable. The
meaninglessness she felt about her own life had metastasized into doubt about whether she could
trust her own judgment in any domain. Derek had tried to be supportive, but his patience was wearing
thin. He couldn't understand why she wouldn't talk about it, why she spent money they didn't have,
why she woke him in the middle of the night checking locks. The distance between them had grown into
something almost physical.

What had finally brought Katja to seek help was a moment two weeks ago when a car had backfired
outside her house. The sound had sent her into such a state of panic that she had called 911,
convinced that someone was attacking her. The police had come, found nothing, and the shame of that
call—the evidence of how far she had fallen from the person she thought she was—had broken something
open in her. She sat across from the therapist now, trying to find words for an experience that
lived in her body more than in language, terrified that nothing could help her return to the person
she had been, and equally terrified that the person she had become was all that remained.
