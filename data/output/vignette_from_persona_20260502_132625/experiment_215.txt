# Michael's Story

Michael was thirty-four when he brought his car to a stop at the intersection of Maple and Third,
the afternoon sun bright through the windshield. He had been distracted—reaching for his phone, just
for a moment—when he heard the impact. A boy on a bicycle, maybe twelve years old, had shot across
the crosswalk from between two parked cars. The sound of the collision, the screech of brakes that
came too late, the way the child's body folded across his hood—these details had burned themselves
into Michael so completely that three years later, he could still smell the hot asphalt and the
copper-sharp scent of blood, could still feel the steering wheel under his palms and the terrible,
absolute silence that followed before the screaming began. The boy survived. He spent six weeks in
the hospital, underwent three surgeries, and recovered most of his mobility. Michael had been
charged with failure to maintain control of a vehicle, paid a substantial fine, and completed a
defensive driving course. But the law's resolution did not touch what had happened inside him.

Michael had built a life that looked stable from the outside. He married Sarah two years after the
accident, and they bought a modest home in the suburbs. He worked as a school counselor, a job he
had chosen specifically because he believed he could help young people avoid the kinds of mistakes
that led to tragedy. His colleagues found him conscientious and thoughtful. But beneath that
surface, Michael had become a man at war with himself and with time itself. The memory of that
afternoon did not feel like something that had happened in the past. When it came back—and it came
back often, triggered by the smallest things—it felt as though it were happening right now,
unfolding in real time across all of his senses at once. The smell of hot pavement in summer, the
sound of a siren passing on a distant street, the physical sensation of stress mounting during a
difficult conversation with a student—any of these could collapse the distance between then and now.
His body would flood with adrenaline, his heart would hammer, and he would find himself gasping as
though the impact were still occurring.

What frightened Michael most was his own inability to trust his judgment. He was certain that the
accident had happened because of the way he had acted—his carelessness, his momentary lapse in
attention—and that certainty had metastasized into a broader conviction: he could not be trusted to
do the right thing. This belief fed everything else. When stress accumulated—a parent's complaint, a
difficult case, a minor mistake at work—it would trigger the memory again, and the memory would
flood him with the sensory reality of that moment, which would then reinforce his conviction that he
was fundamentally flawed, that his life had been destroyed by his own failure. The cycle had become
so tight that Michael could no longer distinguish where one part ended and another began.

To survive this, Michael had learned to keep his mind perpetually occupied. He could not allow
himself to think about what had happened, so he filled every available moment with work, with
planning, with distraction. He listened to podcasts during his commute, worked late at the office
most evenings, and filled his weekends with projects and errands. But this constant mental activity
only made the intrusions worse. The harder he tried not to think about the accident, the more
forcefully it returned. And when it did return, when the memory crashed through his defenses with
its full sensory weight, he would find himself unable to put it into words. He knew what had
happened—he could see it, smell it, feel it—but when Sarah asked him what was wrong, or when he
tried to explain to himself why he felt the way he did, the words simply would not come. There was
no language adequate to the experience.

His sleep had become a battleground. Michael lay awake for hours each night, his body tense, his
mind alert for threat. He had come to believe that if he remained vigilant enough, if he stayed
awake and aware, he might somehow prevent another catastrophe. The irony was not lost on him: his
attempt to stay safe through constant alertness only deepened his exhaustion and made the intrusions
more frequent. At work, his ability to focus had begun to deteriorate. He had started avoiding
situations that might bring him into contact with police—he would take longer routes to avoid police
stations, he would feel his anxiety spike if he saw a patrol car. And most painfully, he had begun
to pull away from his wife. Sarah had tried to help, had encouraged him to seek therapy, but Michael
could not imagine explaining this to anyone. How could he make her understand that he was living in
two time periods simultaneously, that the past was as vivid and present as the moment he was in, and
that he had come to believe he did not deserve forgiveness—not from the boy he had hit, not from
Sarah, and certainly not from himself?

It was Sarah who finally made the appointment. She told Michael that she could not watch him
disappear any longer, that she needed him to get help not just for himself but for them. Michael
arrived at the clinic with a sense of profound dread, convinced that no one could truly understand
what he had done or what it had cost him. What he hoped for, though he barely dared to admit it, was
that someone might help him find a way to live in the present again, to trust his own mind, and
perhaps—though this felt impossibly distant—to forgive himself.
