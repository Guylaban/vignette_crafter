# Clinical Vignette: Minh

Minh was thirty-eight when he joined the humanitarian organization, drawn by a conviction that his
dance training—his discipline, his body awareness, his capacity to read and respond to others—could
serve a purpose beyond performance. He had spent fifteen years as a contemporary dancer in New York,
respected in his field, but increasingly aware of a restlessness that stages and studios could not
fill. When the opportunity came to work with displaced children in a conflict zone in Southeast
Asia, he saw it as a homecoming of sorts: a return to the region his parents had fled, a chance to
use his gifts for something that mattered. He arrived in the capital in March, full of purpose.

The attack came on a humid evening in early May, around 7 p.m., during what should have been a
routine supply run to a neighborhood clinic. Minh was driving with two colleagues and a local guide
when a building ahead of them was struck. The explosion was immediate—a percussion that seemed to
come from inside his own body. Glass shattered across the windshield. The guide, who had been
talking moments before, was suddenly silent. What came next existed in fragments in Minh's memory:
the smell of burnt concrete and something else, something chemical and acrid; the sound of his own
voice, high and unfamiliar; the realization that he was pulling at the guide's arm, that the guide
was not pulling back. His colleagues were shouting. Someone—Minh could not remember who—was asking
if anyone had alcohol to clean a wound. The smell of it, sharp and stinging, mixed with smoke and
blood. Minh had frozen. He had not helped. He had pulled at a dead man's arm and then frozen.

The guilt arrived before the fear. Minh had returned to New York three weeks later, officially "for
recovery," though everyone understood he would not go back. He told himself the decision was
practical: his visa was compromised, the organization was scaling back, his colleagues needed space
to process. What he did not say, even to himself at first, was that he had come to believe he had
violated something fundamental about who he was. He had always understood himself as someone who
showed up, who moved toward difficulty rather than away from it. In that moment—frozen, useless—he
had revealed himself as a coward. The thought was so intolerable that he had simply stopped
examining it, the way one avoids touching a wound.

Six months into his return, Minh noticed that certain hours of the day had become unbearable. Around
7 p.m., a restlessness would begin in his chest, a tightening that made him want to move, to do
anything except sit still. He had tried to ignore it, had thrown himself into teaching again, but
the feeling only intensified. He began to notice that the smell of alcohol—someone's drink at a
dinner party, hand sanitizer in a studio—could trigger something immediate and overwhelming: the
full memory would crash into him without warning, vivid and present, and he would find himself
gasping, his body convinced the explosion was happening now. The shame would follow quickly. Each
time the memory returned, it confirmed what he had come to believe about himself: that his reactions
proved he was a lousy coper, that he could not rely on himself in any meaningful way. And because he
believed this about himself, he found himself hypervigilant to anything that might trigger the
memory again—the burnt smell of toast, the acrid tang of cleaning products, the particular quality
of evening light—as though his body was trying to protect him from further evidence of his own
failure.

His wife, Linh, had noticed the changes first. He had stopped dancing entirely, saying his body was
too tense. He would leave rooms when she opened a bottle of wine. On some evenings, as the sun
lowered, he would retreat to the bedroom with the curtains drawn. She had asked him, gently, what
was happening, and Minh had told her fragments: that the trip had been harder than expected, that he
needed time. What he could not articulate was that he no longer trusted himself—not with her, not
with his students, not with the version of himself he had believed in before May. Six months ago,
Minh had been offered a position as a choreographer at a prestigious company, a role that would have
been a capstone to his career. He had turned it down. He could not imagine being responsible for
other people's art, their vision, their trust.

Now, at forty-one, Minh has come to therapy because Linh gave him an ultimatum—not unkindly, but
firmly: either he gets help or she leaves. He sits in the office with his hands moving slightly, a
dancer's unconscious gesture, and when asked what frightens him most, he says: "That I'll never be
able to trust myself again. That whoever I was before—that person is gone."
