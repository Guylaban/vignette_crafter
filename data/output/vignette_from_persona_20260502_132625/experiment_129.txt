# Karim's Story

Karim was thirty-eight when the diagnosis came back positive. The oncologist had used words like
"aggressive" and "chemotherapy," and Karim had sat in that examination room feeling his body betray
him in real time—his heart accelerating, his palms sweating, the fluorescent lights suddenly too
bright. The months that followed were a blur of hospital visits, infusions that burned through his
veins, and nights when he lay awake convinced he could feel the cancer spreading despite what the
scans said. He'd lost his hair during treatment, watched it fall in clumps into the shower drain,
and by the time remission came nine months later, something fundamental had shifted inside him. The
cancer was gone. Karim was alive. But his body had become a stranger.

Now, three years into remission, Karim worked as a hairdresser in a busy salon in Arlington, a job
that had always grounded him—the ritual of it, the trust clients placed in his hands, the simple
geometry of making someone feel better about themselves. But everything had changed. Photographs
triggered him without warning. A client would show him a picture of a hairstyle she wanted, and the
image would pull something loose in his chest. He couldn't explain why. Sometimes it was just the
color in the photo—a particular shade of grey that reminded him of the hospital walls—and suddenly
his body was flooded with the same dread he'd felt waiting for biopsy results. His hands would shake
as he held the scissors. When he woke from nightmares—and he woke from them most nights, drenched in
sweat, his heart hammering—the residual fear would linger through his entire shift, making him
hypervigilant, certain that something was wrong with him again, though he couldn't articulate what.

Medical procedures were the worst. When his dentist had needed to do a routine cleaning last month,
Karim had felt his entire body lock into a state of red alert before the appointment even began. The
moment the hygienist leaned in with her instruments, he could smell the antiseptic—and suddenly he
wasn't in a dental chair but in the oncology ward, the chemical smell of the infusion center
flooding his nostrils, his body remembering the nausea, the bone-deep ache that had accompanied each
treatment. His muscles tensed so violently he nearly jumped out of the chair. The physical
reactivity was involuntary, humiliating. His body was treating a simple cleaning as a mortal threat.

What frightened Karim most was the conviction that had crystallized in his mind over these three
years: his body could no longer be trusted. It had already tried to kill him once. Now it was
overreacting to everything, sending false alarms, making him feel constantly tense and ready to
react to danger even when he was sitting safely in his apartment on a Sunday afternoon. He had come
to believe that these reactions meant something was fundamentally broken inside him. The intensity
of his fear, the way it could arrive without any clear trigger, the physical sensations that
mimicked his illness—all of it convinced him he was going crazy. If a normal person could sit
through a dental appointment without their body screaming in terror, what did it say about him that
he couldn't? The more he tried to suppress these thoughts, the more they cycled back. His body felt
like an enemy that had learned to disguise its attacks as ordinary moments.

The negative appraisals had become self-perpetuating. Because he believed his body was unreliable
and dangerous, he began to scrutinize every sensation—a flutter in his chest became a sign of
recurrence, a headache a harbinger of something worse. This hypervigilance fed the threat response,
which in turn triggered fragmented memories: the smell of the IV port, the phantom pain in his arms
where the catheters had been placed, the overwhelming dread that sometimes arrived without any image
at all, just the raw emotional intensity of those nine months compressed into a moment. And these
memories, once activated, would make him avoid the very situations that had triggered them—he'd
stopped going to the doctor for routine checkups, had nearly lost a regular client rather than look
at her wedding photos, had started making excuses to leave the salon when certain smells wafted in
from the street. Each avoidance reinforced the belief that these situations were genuinely
dangerous, that his body's fear response was justified, that something was still deeply wrong.

It was his sister who finally insisted he see someone. She'd noticed him becoming smaller, more
isolated, the light draining from his eyes. Karim had resisted, convinced that no therapist could
fix a body that had already proven itself capable of betrayal. But he was tired—tired of the
nightmares, tired of his body's constant vigilance, tired of the shame that accompanied each panic
response. What he hoped for, though he barely dared to articulate it, was the possibility that his
body might someday feel like his own again.
