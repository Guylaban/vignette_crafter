# Karim's Watch

Karim was twenty-six when the accident happened. He had been working as a train conductor for three
years, a job that suited him—structured, responsible, the kind of work that let him move through the
day with purpose. On a Tuesday afternoon in March, his eastbound regional service was pulling into
the station at New Haven when he noticed something on the tracks ahead. A man had collapsed between
the rails. There was no time to stop. Karim watched from the cabin as the train rolled forward, felt
the subtle lurch beneath him, heard nothing—which was somehow worse than a sound. When the emergency
response teams arrived, he saw the man's leg, severed at the knee, the tourniquet of blood already
darkening the platform concrete. He saw the man's face, conscious and screaming. He saw the blood on
the paramedics' gloves, the spreading stain across the platform, the way the man's remaining foot
twitched in shock. The company gave him two weeks off. His supervisor called it an accident,
unavoidable, not his fault. But Karim had come to believe that safety was a fiction people told
themselves, and that belief had calcified in him like a stone.

The months that followed unfolded quietly at first. Karim returned to work because he needed the
income, because he was ashamed to admit how much that afternoon had fractured something in him. But
the job had changed. Every time he sat in the conductor's cabin, he found himself scanning the
tracks obsessively, looking for figures, for movement, for any sign of the chaos that could happen
in an instant. His hands would go cold—sometimes just from gripping the controls, but often from a
sudden awareness of his own skin, the texture of his palms against the metal, and he would think of
the paramedic's gloved hand, slick and dark. He began to dread certain routes. The New Haven line
was impossible; he requested reassignment, which his supervisor granted without comment. But even on
different lines, he remained taut with vigilance. If he saw someone standing near the platform, his
chest would tighten. If he caught sight of a wound on a passenger—a bandaged hand, a scraped
knee—his mind would flood with the image of the severed leg, and he would have to look away, which
only made him feel more exposed, more certain that he had missed something, that danger was
everywhere.

At night, sleep became a negotiation with his own mind. Karim would lie in his apartment and feel
his heart rate climb as darkness settled. He had come to believe that sleep was a luxury he could
not afford, that the moment he closed his eyes, something would happen—not necessarily to him, but
around him, and he would miss it, fail to see it coming. So he stayed awake, or half-awake, his body
rigid, listening to the sounds of the building, the street, the world outside his window. By two in
the morning he would still be conscious, alert, waiting. When he finally did sleep, it was shallow
and fractured, and he would wake at any small sound—a siren, a car door, a neighbor's footstep—his
body flooded with adrenaline.

The triggers that had seized him in those early months never loosened their grip. A passenger's
cough would make him look up sharply. The sight of someone's bleeding lip in a coffee shop would
send a spike of fear through his chest, and he would find himself unable to finish his coffee, would
leave money on the table and walk out. What terrified him most was that these moments were
unpredictable. He could not prepare for them. He could only remain vigilant, scanning, waiting. Even
when his sister called to reassure him that he was safe, that the accident was not his fault, that
thousands of trains ran without incident every day, he could not believe her. The reassurance felt
hollow, a kind of denial of the truth he had learned: that catastrophe lived in the ordinary world,
and that no amount of caution or watching could keep it at bay. The temperature of his own skin—a
flush of heat, a sudden chill—would remind him of the paramedic's gloved hand, and then the
hypervigilance would spike again, his shoulders tensing, his breath shallow. Three years had passed
since the accident, and Karim had become a man who could not trust that anything was truly safe, who
had learned to live in a state of constant readiness, who had begun to understand that the price of
that readiness was isolation and exhaustion.

It was his supervisor's gentle insistence that finally brought him to a therapist's office. Karim
had missed more shifts, called in sick more often, and his performance had declined. He was not
sleeping. He was afraid of his own job. And though he could not articulate it clearly, he was
beginning to understand that whatever had broken in him that afternoon in March was not going to
heal on its own.
