# Meera's Vigilance

Meera was sixty-six years old when she first sought help, though her colleagues at the hospital
would have guessed her older—the way she moved through the maternity ward with a kind of coiled
tension, her shoulders perpetually raised, her eyes tracking everyone who entered a room. She had
been a midwife for forty-three years, delivering babies with steady hands and a calm presence that
mothers trusted instinctively. But something had changed after the incident in 2019, when she was
stationed as a civilian medical consultant with a military convoy in a conflict zone overseas. It
was meant to be a short deployment, a chance to train local healthcare workers. Instead, it became
the hinge upon which her entire sense of safety turned.

The convoy had been ambushed at dawn. Meera was in the medical tent when the first explosion came—a
sound so complete it seemed to erase everything before it. Armed soldiers rushed in, shouting
orders, their uniforms dark and urgent, their movements sharp and unpredictable. In the chaos that
followed, she had hidden behind a supply crate, her hands shaking so badly she couldn't hold a
syringe. A soldier—she never saw his face clearly—had reached down and pulled her up by the arm, his
grip hard enough to bruise. She was evacuated within hours, but the memory of that moment, the
absolute helplessness of it, had burrowed deep into her nervous system and refused to leave.

When Meera returned home to her husband, Rajesh, she felt fundamentally altered. Within weeks, she
noticed that certain things triggered an immediate flood of dread. Whenever someone watched her—a
patient's family member observing her technique, a colleague glancing her way during rounds—she
would feel her chest tighten and her mind race through possibilities. The presence of men,
especially when she was alone with them in a confined space like an elevator or an exam room, made
her skin crawl with the same nameless fear. And uniforms—security guards, police officers, even the
occasional military family member visiting the hospital—sent her into a state of high alert that
could last for hours afterward. These triggers seemed to activate something primal in her body, an
immediate sense that danger was imminent and close.

Her hypervigilance became her constant companion. She was always watching, always assessing. In any
room, she noted exits, the positions of people, the speed of their movements. A sudden gesture from
a colleague—reaching for a chart, standing up quickly—would snap her into a state of readiness so
intense she could barely breathe. She found she could not relax, not even in her own home with
Rajesh sitting beside her on the couch. Her body refused the message that she was safe. The
vigilance felt necessary, essential—if she let her guard down for even a moment, something terrible
would happen.

To manage this unbearable state of alert, Meera began to work longer shifts. She would volunteer for
double duties, arrive early, stay late, immerse herself completely in the technical demands of her
work. The focus required to guide a breech delivery, to monitor fetal heart rates, to coach a
frightened mother through contractions—these things pushed the fear into the background, gave her
mind something concrete to hold. But she also made a rule: she would never be alone with another
person. She began scheduling her consultations in open areas, insisting that a colleague or a nurse
be present during every interaction with patients or staff. When Rajesh tried to have a quiet
conversation with her in the evening, she would find reasons to invite their adult daughter over, or
she would retreat to the kitchen where the household helper was working. The loneliness of this
strategy—the constant vigilance required to maintain it—only deepened her anxiety.

What she had not anticipated was how these protective measures would feed back into the very thing
they were meant to prevent. By avoiding situations where she might be alone with men, she became
hyperaware of every male presence in the hospital. She began scanning for them before entering any
space, and this scanning itself—the constant threat assessment—kept her nervous system in a state of
perpetual alarm. The more she worked to avoid triggering situations, the more sensitized she became
to potential triggers. Her colleagues noticed her withdrawing, becoming harder to reach. Some of the
younger doctors she mentored seemed uncomfortable around her now, perhaps sensing her tension. The
work that had once been her refuge was becoming another source of strain.

By the time Meera came to the clinic, she was exhausted in a way that sleep could not touch. She was
terrified that her hypervigilance would never ease, that she would spend the rest of her life
watching for threats that might never come. What she hoped for, though she barely dared to say it
aloud, was to feel safe in her own mind again—to be able to sit quietly with her husband without
scanning for danger, to walk through the hospital without her body braced for catastrophe.
