# Linh's Story

Linh was thirty-two when the IED detonated outside the compound in Helmand Province. She remembered
the smell first—acrid, metallic, mixed with something charred that coated the back of her throat—and
then the percussion that seemed to detonate inside her own chest. The blast had killed two soldiers
ahead of her and shredded the vehicle's frame. She had been thrown against the far wall, her left
arm pinned beneath debris, and for what felt like hours but was probably minutes, she lay in the
dust and smoke, breathing that burnt smell, certain that the next explosion would finish what the
first had started. When the medics pulled her out, she was intact—no shrapnel, no fractures—but
something essential had fractured anyway, something that would not show on any X-ray.

Nearly seventeen years later, at forty-nine, Linh worked in IT support for a mid-sized financial
services firm downtown. She had built a career on precision, on the logic of systems and networks,
on problems with clear solutions. But the past seven years had become a slow erosion of that
stability. The divorce from Marcus had finalized two years ago; he had grown tired, he said, of her
silences, of the way she would sometimes flinch at his touch, of how she could not bear to be in
crowded places. She had not told him the full truth about why. She had not told anyone.

The burnt smell was the most reliable trigger. It appeared without warning—a neighbor's barbecue in
summer, a kitchen accident in the office break room, the acrid ghost of burnt coffee left too long
on a burner—and when it reached her nostrils, her body seized. Her heart rate would spike, her hands
would tremble, and for a few seconds she would be back in that compound, convinced that danger was
immediate and absolute. The smell seemed to reactivate a primal certainty that threat was present,
that she needed to be vigilant, that ordinary safety was an illusion. Helicopter sounds triggered
the same cascade. The occasional news helicopter passing over the city, or even the distant thrum of
a medevac bird, would send her into a state of hyperalertness that could last for hours.

But the triggers had begun to multiply, spreading like a stain. The workplace itself had become a
source of dread. Open office layouts meant strangers could approach from any angle, and Linh had
come to feel perpetually exposed. She was certain that people approaching her—even colleagues asking
about password resets or network issues—harbored some undefined threat. She found herself checking
behind her constantly, glancing over her shoulder so often that a coworker had once asked if she was
okay, which had mortified and terrified her in equal measure. The checking had become automatic, a
tic that she could not stop. And the checking itself had become another trigger; each time she
looked behind her and found nothing, the relief was so intense that she would become hyperaware of
the possibility that next time, there might be something there. The vigilance fed the threat, which
fed the vigilance.

When the threat became unbearable—when the burnt smell lingered or a helicopter passed overhead or
she found herself in the break room with a stranger—Linh had learned to hurt herself. She would
excuse herself to the bathroom, pull a paperclip straight, and drag it across her inner arm until
she felt the sting. The pain was clarifying; it gave her something immediate and controllable to
focus on, something that replaced the sprawling dread with a sharp, localized sensation. The self-
harm stopped the intrusive thoughts, at least for a while. But it also meant she wore long sleeves
even in summer, and it meant she had begun to isolate herself further, avoiding situations where she
might be tempted to hurt herself, which meant avoiding colleagues, avoiding the break room, avoiding
anything that felt emotionally charged. She had taken to eating lunch alone in her car.

She also did not talk about any of this. The few times Marcus had asked directly about her anxiety,
she had deflected, changed the subject, made it clear that the topic was not open for discussion.
She told herself that speaking about the trauma would make it real, would give it weight and
permanence. Silence felt like containment, though she knew, on some level, that it was the opposite.
Her silence had become another form of avoidance, another way of trying to manage the unmanageable,
and it had cost her the marriage and most of her friendships.

Last month, Linh had called in sick for three days after a burnt smell incident in the office. She
had sat in her apartment, arms wrapped around herself, unable to work, unable to sleep, caught in a
loop of checking the locks and windows, of remembering the compound, of wanting to hurt herself but
trying not to. On the fourth day, she had looked at herself in the mirror and realized she could not
continue like this. She was fifty pounds lighter than she had been at the time of her divorce. She
had not seen her sister in over a year. The fear was no longer something she was managing; it was
something that was managing her. That was when she finally made the call to a therapist, her hands
shaking as she dialed, terrified of what it would mean to speak the truth aloud, but more terrified
of what would happen if she did not.
