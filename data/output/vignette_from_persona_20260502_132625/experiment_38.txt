# Aroha

Aroha was sixty-six now, her dark hair streaked with grey, her posture still military-straight
though she'd left the New Zealand Defence Force thirty-eight years ago. The combat deployment to the
Solomon Islands in 1986 had lasted four months. What happened there had never truly left her.

She had been a signals officer, twenty-eight years old, stationed at a forward observation post in
dense jungle. One afternoon, while she was helping coordinate radio communications for a patrol, the
post came under sustained attack. The sound came first—a helicopter, low and impossibly loud, its
rotors cutting the humid air like a blade. Then the gunfire. She remembered fragments: the acrid
smell of cordite, someone screaming her name, the sensation of being pinned behind sandbags while
rounds tore through the corrugated metal walls. A young soldier beside her—she could not remember
his face clearly, only that he had been there and then he was not—was hit. The chaos lasted perhaps
twenty minutes, though time had fractured into something unreliable. When it ended, she was alive
and covered in blood that was not entirely her own.

The memory had never cohered into a narrative she could tell. When people asked what happened—and
fewer people asked as the years went on—she found herself unable to construct the sentences. The
pieces remained disconnected: the helicopter sound divorced from the gunfire, the image of blood
separated from any sense of sequence or meaning. It felt impossible that it had actually happened to
her, that she had been there. She had tried therapy in her forties, but the memory's fragmentation
meant she could not anchor it into words, and without words, she could not process it. She had
stopped trying.

For decades, Aroha had functioned reasonably well. She had built a career as a curriculum designer,
working in education policy, had married and divorced, had maintained a life that looked, from the
outside, unremarkable. But the vigilance never stopped. She was always aware of exits, of who had
authority in any room, of how quickly she could leave. When she was in a meeting with administrators
who had decision-making power over her work, her chest would tighten. She had come to believe that
she could not trust her own judgment—that under pressure, she would fail, that she was fundamentally
unreliable. This self-doubt had cost her promotions; she had turned down leadership roles, certain
she would collapse under the weight of responsibility.

The triggers had become more intrusive as she aged. Helicopter sounds—from news broadcasts, from
emergency services passing over the city, even from films—would suddenly return her to that post,
and with them came the fragmented images, the blood, the incomprehensible horror of it. When the
memory surfaced unbidden, the self-doubt intensified: *What if I cannot handle this? What if I fall
apart?* These fears began to shape what she did. She started avoiding situations where she might
encounter such sounds, which meant avoiding going out at all on days when there was any chance of
hearing them. She kept the windows of her small apartment closed, even in summer. She avoided
allowing herself to feel anything strongly—anger, grief, even joy—because strong feelings felt
dangerous, as if they might crack open the fragile containment she had built around the memory.

Over the past five years, the avoidance had tightened into something that constrained her life
significantly. She had stopped attending professional conferences because she could not control the
environment or guarantee an exit. She had withdrawn from her book club, her bridge group, even
family dinners. Her sister had stopped inviting her after the third cancellation. The isolation was
both cause and consequence: she felt set apart from others, unable to explain why she could not
simply go out for coffee, and this isolation fed her conviction that she was fundamentally broken,
that others would not understand. The less she went out, the more the memory seemed to intrude—as if
avoidance had weakened some barrier. And each intrusion sent her searching for exits, making her
certain that she needed to stay near the door in any space, that she could not allow herself to be
trapped. She had begun to notice that the more she restricted her movements, the more vigilant she
became, and the more vigilant she became, the more she seemed to encounter the very situations she
feared—a helicopter overhead, a conversation where someone wielded authority over her, a moment when
strong feeling threatened to surface.

Aroha had finally called a therapist last month, after her GP had gently suggested that her blood
pressure medication alone would not address what was happening. She sat in the waiting room of the
clinic, positioned where she could see the door, and told herself that at least she could leave if
she needed to. She was not sure she could trust herself to do the right thing in this new treatment,
but the alternative—continuing to live in this narrowing space—had become unbearable.
