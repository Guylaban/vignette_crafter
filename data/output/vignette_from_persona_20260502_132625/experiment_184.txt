# Clinical Vignette: Vinh

Vinh was forty-two years old when the soldiers came to his village in Cambodia. He was a young man
then, strong and quick-handed—the kind of person who could fix anything, who understood how pipes
and systems worked. That skill, which would later define his American life, nearly killed him. The
soldiers needed someone who could maintain their compound, and Vinh was conscripted into a small
group of captives held in a former schoolhouse. For seven months, he was kept in a basement room
with five other men. The space was airless, the light a single high window. The guards were
unpredictable. Sometimes they brought food; sometimes they did not. Worse than hunger was the
uncertainty of who would be taken upstairs and when. Vinh learned quickly that any sudden movement—a
guard shifting his weight, a door opening without warning—might mean his turn had come. He survived
by becoming invisible, by watching, by never being the one who made the first sound. When the
compound was liberated, Vinh was one of three men who walked out alive.

Now, at sixty-nine, Vinh lives in a modest house in Portland with his wife of thirty-eight years. He
has built a successful career as a plumber, known in his neighborhood for his reliability and
precision. Yet something fundamental fractured in that basement and never fully healed. When Vinh
encounters a male client alone at a job site—a man standing too close, or one whose shadow falls
across the room unexpectedly—his body reacts before his mind can catch up. His neck tightens, a wave
of nausea rises in his throat, and he finds himself already calculating the exits: the front door,
the back door, whether the window is large enough. These are not conscious choices. The presence of
a man, especially in a confined space like a bathroom or kitchen, triggers an immediate cascade—his
heart accelerates, his muscles coil, and the memories begin to surface.

The memories themselves are vivid and fragmentary, like a film that loops endlessly in his mind. He
sees the guard's face, hears the scrape of a chair, feels the specific quality of that basement
light. When the memories come, they are not gentle recollections; they are intrusions that pull him
out of the present moment entirely. A client's raised voice, a sudden movement—anything that echoes
the unpredictability of those months—and the film begins again. Vinh has learned that the memories
feed the threat he feels everywhere, even now, even in his own home. He finds himself alert at
night, listening to sounds in the house, jumping when his wife moves unexpectedly in the kitchen.
The boundary between then and now has become porous. His body still believes the danger is
immediate.

Over the decades, Vinh developed ways to manage what he could not name. He avoids dates that
correspond to his captivity—he takes those weeks off work, stays home, tells his wife he is tired.
When he must enter an unfamiliar space for a plumbing job, he arrives early to scout the layout, to
know where he can go if he needs to leave quickly. He plans escape routes the way other men might
plan their day. These strategies provided some relief at first, a sense of control in a life that
had been stolen from him. But the relief never lasts. The more he avoids, the more the memories seem
to demand his attention. When he escapes a situation that triggers his fear, the memory film plays
more insistently afterward, as if his body is replaying what he avoided facing. And when the
memories intensify, when they loop without mercy, his vigilance increases—he becomes even more alert
at home, even more certain that danger is present, even more likely to startle at his wife's
movements or the neighbor's car backfiring.

The escape route planning and the memory avoidance have become intertwined. He detaches himself from
the memories when they come, pushing them away, refusing to engage with them. This detachment is
automatic now, a kind of psychological flinching. Yet the more he detaches, the more fragmented the
memories become, and the more fragmented they are, the less he can speak about them. When his wife
asks him what is wrong, or when a therapist might ask him to explain, words fail him entirely. He
knows what happened—he lived it—but the knowledge sits in his body, not in language. The triggers
remain potent and unpredictable because he has never fully integrated what they represent.

His wife finally insisted he seek help after he refused to attend his own grandson's birthday party
because two of his grandsons' friends were coming, and they were men. The shame of that refusal, the
recognition that his fear was now controlling not just his plumbing schedule but his family life,
brought him to a therapist's office. What he fears most is that he will lose more time to this—that
the remaining years of his life will be spent in the same vigilance, the same looping memories, the
same calculated exits from rooms that should feel safe. What he hopes for, though he barely dares to
speak it, is that he might one day be alone with a man and not feel the basement closing in around
him.
