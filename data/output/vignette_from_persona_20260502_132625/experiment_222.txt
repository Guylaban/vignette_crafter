# Clinical Vignette: Amir

Amir was fifty-six when the police officer's baton struck him across the ribs. He had been standing
on the sidewalk outside a community center in his neighborhood, speaking with a friend about the
upcoming city council meeting, when a protest two blocks away had turned chaotic. Officers in riot
gear swept through the street indiscriminately. Amir raised his hands, tried to move away, but the
officer—a man whose face he would never see clearly—had already decided. The blow came without
warning. He fell hard, tasted blood mixed with the copper of his own fillings, felt the officer's
boot press against his shoulder as more impacts landed. His friend screamed. The officer called him
names. Within minutes it was over, but Amir lay there gasping, his ribs on fire, and a certainty had
crystallized in him: the world had shown its true face, and it was indifferent to justice or
innocence.

For weeks afterward, Amir tried to convince himself it was an isolated incident, a terrible mistake.
But the conviction had taken root too deeply. He began to see threat everywhere. Walking to his car
in the parking garage at work, he found himself mapping exits before he even reached his desk—which
stairwell was closest, whether the elevator could trap him. At stoplights, he scanned the sidewalk
for uniforms, for sudden movement. His wife, Leila, noticed him tensing at the sound of sirens. He
could not relax, even in their living room with the doors locked, the lights on, Leila beside him.
Some part of his mind remained on high alert, convinced that safety was an illusion, that
people—especially those in authority—were not what they seemed. They had their own agendas, their
own capacity for violence. Trust was a luxury he could no longer afford.

What frightened him most was his own rage. When the memory surfaced—and it surfaced constantly,
unbidden, in fragments—he felt a fury so intense it terrified him. The taste of blood would return,
metallic and immediate, and with it came a white-hot anger at himself, at the officer, at a system
that had violated something fundamental in him. He had always been a measured man, thoughtful, the
kind of person who believed in dialogue and understanding. Now he was someone capable of fantasizing
about retaliation, about violence. This contradiction between who he believed himself to be and what
he felt capable of had shattered something in his self-image. He had acted—or wanted to act—in ways
that went against everything he valued. The shame of that desire was almost worse than the physical
pain.

To manage the unbearable tightness in his chest, the constant vigilance, the rage that erupted
without warning, Amir began to withdraw. He stopped watching news programs, deleted social media
apps from his phone, avoided any content that might remind him of what had happened. He told himself
these were reasonable precautions. But the avoidance extended further. He stopped allowing himself
to feel anything at all if he could help it. He declined invitations from friends. He became
mechanical at work, responding to client emails with minimal engagement, avoiding the creative
brainstorming sessions that had once energized him. Leila tried to draw him into conversation, but
he found himself shutting down, unable to risk the vulnerability of connection. His belief that
people were fundamentally untrustworthy made intimacy feel dangerous. If he let her close, if he
allowed himself to feel anything—love, hope, even ordinary happiness—he risked being hurt again.
Better to feel nothing.

Yet the very strategies meant to protect him were keeping the memory alive. By avoiding anything
that might trigger emotion, by constantly scanning his surroundings, by refusing to fully process
what had happened, he had frozen the trauma in place. When memories did break through—and they did,
despite his vigilance—he would push them away before they could fully surface, cutting himself off
mid-recollection, as though incompleteness might diminish their power. It never did. The fragmented
images, the taste, the rage, they all returned more insistently. His conviction that the world was
inherently dangerous, that people could not be trusted, that he himself was capable of unthinkable
things—these beliefs only deepened as he isolated himself further, as each avoided situation seemed
to confirm his worst fears.

After six months of this, Leila finally insisted he see someone. Amir resisted, certain that talking
would only make things worse, that a therapist would judge him for the violent thoughts he harbored,
that opening up would expose him to more pain. But he was exhausted. The vigilance, the emotional
numbness, the fractured memory that would not release him—it was unsustainable. He came to his first
appointment reluctant and afraid, convinced that his world had fundamentally changed and that he
could never truly be safe again.
