# Rosa's Story

Rosa Hernández is a seventy-four-year-old widow—though not by the death of her first husband, but by
her own deliberate distance from him after nearly forty years of marriage. She had worked as a
pharmacist for fifty-two years, a career that gave her identity, competence, and the quiet authority
that came from helping people navigate their medications and their fears. She had been good at it:
meticulous, calm, trusted by her community. But that woman feels like someone else now.

The violence began when Rosa was in her late sixties. Her husband, a man she had believed she knew,
began to drink more heavily after his retirement. One evening, after she had questioned him about a
late night out, he grabbed her by the arm—not gently—and forced her against the kitchen counter. His
hands were around her throat. She remembered the pressure, the shock that this could happen in her
own home, the way her vision tunneled. When he finally released her, she was on the floor, gasping.
He apologized the next morning. She told herself it was an isolated incident, a moment of stress,
something that happened to other women, not to her. But it happened again. And again. Each time he
was remorseful; each time she found reasons to stay, to believe it would change. By the time she
finally left, she had internalized a story about herself that felt immutable: she was weak, foolish,
a woman who had failed to see what was in front of her. She had lost faith in her own judgment, in
people, in the idea that the world was a place where she could be safe.

Now, three years into her separation and living alone in a small house in a quiet suburb, Rosa is
trapped by the architecture of her own vigilance. When someone at the grocery store mentions
family—a casual remark about a daughter's visit or a grandchild—Rosa's chest tightens. These
comments thrust her back into the kitchen, into the moment when she realized her own home had become
a cage. The mention of family, of closeness, of the bonds she once believed in, carries the full
weight of that betrayal. Her body responds before her mind can catch up: her shoulders hunch, her
jaw clenches, and she finds herself scanning the room for exits. The trigger is not the words
themselves but what they represent—the illusion of safety, the lie that love protects you.

Similarly, when someone tries to get emotionally close to her—a neighbor who wants to sit with her
on the porch, a daughter-in-law who reaches for her hand—Rosa feels a surge of panic that she cannot
fully explain. The closeness itself becomes dangerous. She pulls away, makes excuses. She has come
to believe that allowing anyone near her emotionally is a form of surrender, another way of being
weak and foolish. And the sound of a door opening or slamming—any door in her house, even the gentle
click of the refrigerator—sends her entire body into a state of readiness. She is listening, always
listening, for the moment when someone might enter. The trigger floods her with the physical
sensation of being trapped, of not knowing what is coming.

These triggers do not arrive in isolation. When Rosa hears a door, when someone mentions family,
when emotional closeness is offered, her body floods with alarm. Her muscles tense; she feels
constantly ready to react to danger, as though a threat is always imminent. She monitors every sound
in her home, listening for the particular creak of footsteps, the particular timbre of an intrusion.
Even inside her own house, behind locked doors, she feels on guard. This hypervigilance is
exhausting, but it feels necessary—it feels like the price of survival.

And with the threat comes the memory. When her body is activated, when she is tense and listening,
the images return. She sees the kitchen in snapshots—the counter, his face, her own hands braced
against the wall. Her body remembers the position she was in, the way her spine curved, the angle of
her neck. These are not coherent narratives but fragments, each one a still photograph that burns
itself into her awareness. When the memories surface, shame floods through her, a shame so intense
that she has come to believe it is the truest thing about her. She is ashamed of what happened,
ashamed that she stayed, ashamed that she did not see it coming. The shame reinforces her conviction
that she is weak, that she failed, that she is no longer the capable woman she once was.

To manage the unbearable weight of these memories and the shame they carry, Rosa has begun to avoid
her own body. She does not look in the mirror. She wears loose clothing and does not think about the
physical form that once felt like home. She avoids feeling anything strongly—she has learned to
dampen her emotions, to move through the world in a kind of protective numbness. She avoids other
people. She retired from the pharmacy two years ago, citing fatigue, though the truth was more
complicated. Being around people triggered the hypervigilance; it made her feel exposed. Without the
structure of work, without the professional identity that had sustained her, she has become
increasingly isolated. Her daughter calls less frequently now; Rosa's withdrawal has been consistent
enough that her family has learned to give her space. She tells herself this is what she wants, what
she needs, but the isolation has deepened her sense that she is incapable of the connections that
once defined her life.

The avoidance works, in a way. When she is alone, when she does not allow herself to feel, when she
does not engage with people or her own body, the immediate distress diminishes. But the cost is
accumulating. She has lost her work identity, her social connections, and increasingly, her sense of
who she is. The avoidance has become its own kind of prison, and it feeds back into her conviction
that she is no longer capable, that she is fundamentally weak, that people cannot be trusted. The
more she avoids, the more real these beliefs become. The more real these beliefs become, the more
triggers proliferate—now even the thought of a family gathering sends her into a spiral of dread.

It was her daughter who finally insisted she see someone. Rosa came to the appointment reluctant,
convinced that nothing could help, that she was broken beyond repair. What she hopes for, though she
hardly dares to name it, is a way back to herself—not to who she was before, but to someone who can
exist in the world without constant fear, who can look at her own reflection without shame, who can
sit with her grandchildren without the walls closing in. But first, she will have to face the very
things she has spent three years learning to avoid.
