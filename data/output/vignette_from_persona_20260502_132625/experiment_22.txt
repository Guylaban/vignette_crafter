# Layla's Vigilance

Layla was forty-eight when the militias came to her village in southern Syria. She had been a
schoolteacher then, moving through her days with the ordinary competence of a woman who knew her
place in the world. On a Tuesday morning in March, armed men arrived at the school where she taught.
They separated the staff by religion, questioned them about their families' allegiances, and took
three of her colleagues away. Layla was released after hours of interrogation, but something in her
had fractured. She spent the next eighteen months in a refugee camp in Jordan, then another year
navigating the resettlement process, finally arriving in the United States at fifty-one with a
daughter and a suitcase of documents. She found work as a social worker in a resettlement agency,
helping other displaced families navigate the systems she had only recently learned herself. It was
meaningful work, but it had become impossible to do.

The problem was that Layla could not stop scanning. In her office, in the hallways of the agency, in
the waiting room where families came to meet with her, her eyes moved constantly—from the door to
the windows to the faces of the people around her. She was watching for signs. A raised voice from
another cubicle sent her pulse racing. The sound of a siren outside the building made her certain
that something terrible was about to happen. Her colleague Marcus had mentioned once that she seemed
"tense," but he did not understand that she was not tense—she was alert, necessarily alert, because
the world had shown her that safety was an illusion. The threat was always there, waiting. A man
with an accent she did not recognize in the waiting room; a conversation in Arabic that she could
not quite hear; the sight of a police car in the parking lot—these were not neutral observations.
They were warnings. And so she watched, constantly, because to stop watching was to be caught
unaware, and to be caught unaware was to disappear.

This hypervigilance had begun to shape everything she did. Layla had started wearing dark, shapeless
clothing—long sleeves even in summer, a scarf that she could pull forward to obscure her face,
colors that did not draw attention. She had come to believe that visibility was danger, that being
noticed was the first step toward being taken. She avoided situations that stirred strong feeling in
her: she no longer attended the community dinners her mosque held, no longer accepted invitations
from colleagues to go out after work, no longer allowed herself to engage fully in conversations
with her clients, even when their stories mirrored her own. The emotional intensity of those
encounters—the recognition, the empathy, the activation of her own memories—felt destabilizing, and
so she had learned to keep herself at a distance, professional and remote. But this avoidance of
emotional engagement, this constant self-monitoring and withdrawal, had the paradoxical effect of
intensifying her sense of threat. When she was not fully present with people, when she was always
partly turned toward the exits and the windows, the world felt more dangerous, not less. Her
isolation confirmed what she had come to believe: that the world was hostile, that she could not
trust others, that danger was the baseline condition of existence.

At night, Layla woke. Not from nightmares, though she had those too, but from a compulsion to check.
She would rise from bed in the darkness, move through her apartment, verify that the doors were
locked, that the windows were closed, that nothing had changed. She would lie awake for hours,
unable to settle her body, unable to quiet the sense that something was wrong, even when nothing
was. Her daughter had stopped asking her mother to come to her school events. Her supervisor had
begun to document her "interpersonal challenges" in her personnel file. She had gained weight from
the inactivity of her isolation and lost friends from the distance she maintained. What frightened
Layla most was not the memory of that day in Syria—it was the certainty that if she lowered her
guard for even a moment, if she allowed herself to relax or to trust, the danger she sensed would
finally materialize. She had come to her first therapy appointment because her agency had suggested
it, and because she was exhausted, but she did not believe that talking would change the fundamental
truth: that she was not safe, that she would never be safe, and that her vigilance was the only
thing standing between her and catastrophe.
