# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus was twenty-two when the typhoon hit Manila. He had been visiting his mother's family for the
summer, staying in a third-floor apartment in Quezon City, when the storm surge came faster than the
warnings had suggested. The water rose through the building in the space of an hour—first creeping
across the ground floor, then climbing the stairs with a sound like something alive, carrying with
it the smell of the bay and things that had no business in a home. Marcus had made it to the roof
with his cousin, but not before the water had filled his lungs enough that he couldn't quite breathe
normally for days afterward. The taste of salt and diesel fuel had coated his mouth, and when the
rescue came, it came with the weight of bodies pressing against his in the darkness of the boat. He
had survived. Thousands had not. He had flown back to the States two weeks later, telling himself he
was fine.

For the first month, he was. He returned to his job as a UX designer at a mid-sized tech firm in
Portland, sat at his desk, moved through his work with the kind of mechanical competence that can
pass for normalcy. But then, sometime in September, a sharp pain began radiating through his neck—a
tension that wouldn't release, that made it hard to turn his head without wincing. When the neck
pain flared, something else came with it: the memory would surface, not as a coherent narrative but
as a fragment so vivid and so physically overwhelming that it felt less like remembering and more
like the event was happening again. The taste would come first, that metallic salt coating his
tongue, and then the sensation of water in his chest, and with it the full emotional force of the
drowning—the panic, the helplessness—sometimes without any clear image at all, just the feeling of
it, absolute and consuming. Each time the pain returned, the memory returned with it, as though his
body had learned to trigger the trauma on its own.

What troubled Marcus most, though, was what had begun to happen in his mind afterward. When the
memories subsided, they left behind a conviction that felt almost reasonable in its logic: the
people around him—his coworkers, his friends, even his mother when she called—were not what they
seemed. They had not been there. They could not understand. How could they possibly comprehend what
it meant to taste the bay in your mouth, to feel the weight of strangers' bodies in the dark? He had
come to believe that this experience had set him fundamentally apart, that he belonged nowhere
anymore, not in Portland, not among people who had never faced the kind of moment that strips away
every illusion of safety. This sense of isolation fed back into his vigilance: when his neck tensed
again, when he felt physically unwell—a headache, a tight chest—his mind would immediately interpret
these sensations as harbingers of another memory, another moment when the unbridgeable distance
between himself and everyone else would become impossible to ignore. The more he felt this distance,
the more he became convinced that his body was trying to tell him something was wrong, that some
part of him had never really left that roof, and that the people around him could never be trusted
to truly see what he had become.

By November, Marcus had begun to isolate himself at work, taking meetings over video rather than in
person, eating lunch alone at his desk. His manager had noticed the change in his output—designs
that used to be fluid now felt labored, as though he was working through something heavier than the
usual technical problem. He had not told anyone about the typhoon, about the memories, about the
taste that sometimes came unbidden. What finally brought him to a therapist's office was a panic
attack at his desk, triggered by nothing he could identify, that left him gasping and convinced that
something in him had broken beyond repair. He sat across from the clinician with his hands folded,
afraid that if he spoke too much about what had happened, the person before him would confirm what
he already believed: that he was irrevocably changed, that he would never fit back into the world he
had left behind.
