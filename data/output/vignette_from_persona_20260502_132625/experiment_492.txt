# Levi's Story

Levi was eighteen when he first came to the clinic, referred by his GP after his girlfriend, Maya,
expressed concern about his drinking. At the intake, he sat rigidly in the chair, one leg bouncing,
and offered sparse answers until the clinician asked about his childhood. Then the story came out in
fragments—not because he was reluctant, but because that was how it lived in him.

He was five years old when his father's voice changed. Levi remembered the sound first: a growl that
didn't belong to the man who drove him to school. His mother had been standing in the kitchen. There
was a smell—sharp, acrid, the smell of beer and something else, something that made Levi's stomach
clench even now, eighteen years later. He remembered his father's hand raised. He remembered his
mother's face. But the rest was fog. He could see his mother's mouth opening as if to speak, could
see his father's silhouette against the hallway light, but the actual moments—what happened next,
how long it lasted, how it ended—existed only as fragments, disconnected pieces that his mind
refused to assemble into a coherent picture. When he tried to remember the full sequence, terror
would flood through him, the same raw, animal panic he'd felt as a five-year-old, as though the
event were happening again in real time. It was easier not to look at those memories directly.

By the time Levi was sixteen, he'd learned to recognize his triggers with the precision of someone
reading a map of his own danger. The smell of alcohol—beer especially, but any spirits—would send
him into a state of hypervigilance that lasted hours. Being alone in a room with another man,
particularly one older or larger than himself, would activate an immediate scanning mode: he'd watch
the man's hands, his face, the angle of his body, searching for the micro-expressions that preceded
violence. A bicycle passing him on the street could trigger the same response, though he couldn't
fully articulate why; some fragmented piece of memory associated the sound and motion with threat,
though he couldn't access the complete picture of what had happened. These triggers were not
abstract; they were woven into the texture of his daily life. At his job as a project manager at a
mid-sized construction firm, he found himself in meetings with male colleagues and supervisors. The
presence of men—their voices, their proximity, the way they moved—would activate a low-level but
constant state of threat assessment. His body would tense. His breath would shallow. He'd find his
gaze drifting to their hands, their posture, looking for signs of aggression that his rational mind
knew were unlikely but that his nervous system insisted on monitoring.

To manage these states, Levi had developed a system. Smoking became his first tool—a way to step
outside, to create distance, to regulate the anxiety that spiked when he was trapped in a meeting or
a conversation. By seventeen, he was smoking a pack a day. When smoking wasn't enough, he drank. The
irony wasn't lost on him: the very smell that triggered his threat response was also the thing he
used to numb it. He'd drink in the evenings after work, or on weekends with Maya, using the alcohol
to flatten the hypervigilance, to make his body feel less like a coiled spring. He'd also begun
taking medication—first prescribed for anxiety, then supplemented by other drugs he'd sourced
through friends—anything that would create a buffer between his nervous system and the world. The
more he drank and used substances, the less he felt the need to check the locks on his apartment
door, though he still found himself doing it compulsively, sometimes five or six times before bed.
The checking was its own kind of drinking: a ritual that temporarily soothed the conviction that
danger was imminent, that men could appear at any moment, that his space was not secure.

Maya had begun to notice the pattern. She'd watch him light cigarettes with trembling hands after
his father called. She'd see him scanning rooms when they went out together, his attention fractured
between her and the men around them. She'd tried to talk to him about the drinking, but Levi
couldn't explain it in a way that made sense—that the alcohol was the only thing that quieted the
fragments, that allowed him to be in the same room with his male coworkers without feeling like he
was five years old again, waiting for the growl in a man's voice.

When he finally came to the clinic, Levi was caught in a loop he couldn't break. The triggers were
everywhere. The threat felt constant. The memories remained incomplete and terrifying. And the only
thing that seemed to work—the smoking, the drinking, the drugs—was the thing that was slowly
unraveling his relationship and his work performance. He wanted help, but he was also afraid that
looking directly at what he couldn't remember would make the terror worse. He sat in the clinician's
office and said, quietly, "I don't even know what I'm running from anymore."
