# Thida's Story

Thida was eighty-two now, her hands still steady enough to grade her students' essays on Buddhist
philosophy, but her mind often elsewhere—caught in a place she had spent fifty-eight years trying to
leave behind. In 1966, she had been twenty-four, a schoolteacher in Phnom Penh, when the American
bombing campaign began in earnest over Cambodia. The raids came without warning. One afternoon, as
she walked home from the school where she taught the children of merchants and civil servants, the
sky split open. The sound came first—a roar that seemed to come from inside her own chest—and then
the earth itself rose up. She remembered the heat on her face, the smell of burning rubber and flesh
mixing with the acrid char of soil turned to ash, and then nothing. A blank. When she woke in a
hospital three days later, her left arm was bandaged and her hair had been burned away on one side,
but there was a gap in her memory that no amount of concentration could fill. The minutes between
the explosion and waking—perhaps five, perhaps thirty—simply did not exist for her. She had come to
believe, over the decades, that her mind had refused to record something too terrible to know.

For most of her adult life, Thida had managed. She had married Sovann, a gentle man who had lost his
entire family in the war, and they had built a quiet life in Long Beach, raising two daughters and
eventually becoming grandparents. She had returned to teaching—this time religious education at a
Buddhist temple—and found purpose in passing on the dharma to younger generations. But in the last
five years, since her retirement, the symptoms had worsened. The triggers had become inescapable.
Whenever she stepped outside to tend the small garden behind her house, the smell of rain-soaked
earth would stop her cold, her heart racing, her breath shallow. Even the ordinary smell of soil
after watering would transport her back to that hospital bed, to the knowledge that she had lost
something irretrievable from her own mind. The unexpected touch of a grandchild—a hand on her
shoulder as she sat reading—would make her flinch violently, her body convinced that danger was
imminent. And fire, in any form, had become nearly impossible to bear. When Sovann lit the stove to
cook, when a candle flickered during evening prayers, Thida felt the old heat on her face again, and
she would leave the room, her hands shaking.

These triggers had driven her toward strategies that now consumed her days. She checked the locks on
every door and window in the house compulsively—sometimes five, ten, twenty times before she could
settle. She had begun to keep her mind constantly occupied, filling every waking hour with tasks,
with lists, with anything that would prevent the memories from surfacing. She told herself it was
practical—the house needed organizing, the temple's records needed updating—but the truth was that
stillness had become unbearable. The moment her hands stopped moving and her mind quieted, the
fragmented image would return: the sky opening, the smell, the blank space where her own past should
have been. Her avoidance of new relationships had been more gradual. She had always been reserved,
but now she found herself unable to engage with new people at the temple, new neighbors who moved
in, even the occasional invitation to social gatherings. What frightened her most was that if she
allowed someone close, they might ask her about the war, or worse, they might touch her
unexpectedly, and she would flinch like a wounded animal. She had come to believe that there was
something fundamentally broken about her—something intrinsic that had made her vulnerable to that
day's horrors, that had caused her mind to betray her by forgetting what it could not bear to know.

The checking of locks, the constant mental distraction, the isolation—these habits had created a
vicious cycle that now trapped her. When she kept her mind occupied, she avoided thinking about what
had happened, but the avoidance itself seemed to sharpen the memories that did surface. The few
times she had sat quietly in meditation, hoping to find peace, the fragmented image had returned
with such vivid intensity that she had gasped aloud. The memory felt utterly alien, as though it had
happened to someone else entirely, and yet it was more real, more saturated with sensation, than any
ordinary day. This strangeness of the memory—its vividness alongside its incompleteness, its sense
of not-quite-belonging to her—had only deepened her conviction that she was permanently damaged. The
more she checked the locks, the more she distracted herself, the more she withdrew from others, the
more convinced she became that her body, her mind, her very self could not be trusted. The triggers
seemed to multiply. A visitor's perfume that reminded her faintly of incense smoke would set her on
edge. The texture of certain fabrics would make her skin crawl. She had begun to see danger
everywhere, and the strategies that had once offered relief now felt like prisons of her own making.

Sovann had finally insisted that she see someone. "You are suffering," he had said gently, taking
her hand—and she had not flinched, because it was him, and after fifty-eight years, his touch was
the only one that still felt safe. Thida had agreed, not because she believed anything could be
done—the damage was too old, too deep—but because she loved him, and she could see the worry in his
eyes. Now, sitting in the therapist's office, she was afraid. Afraid that talking about it would
make it more real. Afraid that the blank spaces in her memory could never be filled. Afraid, most of
all, that the person she had become—the woman who checked locks obsessively, who could not sit
still, who had withdrawn from the world—was all that was left of who she had been.
