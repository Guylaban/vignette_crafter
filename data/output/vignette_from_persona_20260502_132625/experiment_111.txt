# Ravi's Story

Ravi had spent forty-two years as a financial analyst, building a reputation for precision and calm
under pressure. At seventy-five, after losing their husband Marcus to heart disease three years ago,
they had continued working part-time, finding in the rhythms of spreadsheets and quarterly reports a
kind of ballast against grief. But the life they had constructed—orderly, predictable, grounded in
the belief that careful attention and good judgment could protect them—had been unmade in a single
afternoon eighteen months ago.

It had happened in the parking garage beneath their office building on a Tuesday in March. Ravi had
stayed late to finish a client presentation, and when they emerged from the elevator around seven in

face Ravi would never quite hold clearly in their mind, though they would spend months trying. What
followed was a violent assault that lasted perhaps ten minutes, though Ravi had no reliable sense of
its duration. The man had forced them into the back seat, and there was the metallic taste of blood
from where their lip had split against their own teeth, a taste that would return unbidden months
later. There were sounds too—his breathing, a sound like fabric tearing, Ravi's own voice
pleading—sounds that lived now in the folds of their memory, playing back without warning or
control.

The assault had ended when a security guard's radio crackled nearby. The man had fled, and Ravi had
called 911 from their car, their hands shaking so violently they could barely hold the phone. The
police had arrived within minutes, and officers had taken their statement in the garage while Ravi
sat in the back of a patrol car, wrapped in a blanket, tasting blood. Those officers had been
professional, but Ravi had felt scrutinized, as though their account—fragmented and incoherent even
in that moment—was being measured for credibility. The garage was now a trigger, but so was the
sight of police uniforms, which sent Ravi's heart racing and their mind immediately back to that
fluorescent-lit parking structure, to the feeling of being questioned while still raw and broken.

In the weeks after, Ravi had tried to piece together what had happened, to construct a narrative
they could hold and understand. But the memory refused to cohere. There were fragments—sensory
shards that returned vividly and without warning—but no coherent story, no beginning-middle-end that
Ravi could tell themselves or anyone else. When they tried to explain to Marcus what had occurred,
the words would scatter. The taste would come back, metallic and wrong, and with it would come the
sounds—the man's breathing, the tearing fabric—and Ravi would lose their thread entirely. This
fragmentation had deepened something that was already taking root: a conviction that they could not
be trusted with their own mind, that something fundamental in them had been damaged beyond repair.

spreadsheet during a team meeting. It was a small error, the kind that happened to everyone, but
Ravi had felt the criticism as a confirmation of their deepest fear—that they were unraveling, that
their judgment was no longer sound. The moment had triggered a cascade: the memory of the assault
had surged forward, bringing with it the taste of blood and the phantom sound of breathing, and in
that sensory storm, Ravi had become certain that their mind was fracturing, that they were going
crazy. They had excused themselves and spent the rest of the afternoon in the bathroom stall, trying
to breathe, trying to convince themselves that they were still intact.

After that, Ravi had begun to withdraw from the office. They would volunteer to work from home,
citing various reasons. When they could not avoid in-person meetings, they would sit rigidly,
hypervigilant for any sign of disapproval or correction. The financial work that had once felt
meaningful—the careful construction of analyses that helped clients make sound decisions—now felt

they had been before the parking garage seemed to have vanished entirely. They had come to believe

and the tremor in their voice during their last phone call—that they had agreed to seek help. What
Ravi feared most was that the help would come too late, that the fractures already present in their
mind would prove irreparable.
