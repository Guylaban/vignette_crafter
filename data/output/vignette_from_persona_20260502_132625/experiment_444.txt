# Priya

Priya was sixty-eight when she first understood that the trembling in her hands had nothing to do
with age. She had been a photographer for forty-two years—a good one, sought after for wedding
portfolios and corporate portraits—but in recent months she found herself unable to steady the
camera during shoots. Her hands would shake not from fatigue but from a kind of vigilance that had
wound itself so tightly through her body that she could no longer distinguish between alertness and
panic. The shaking had begun in earnest two years ago, though she could trace its roots much further
back, to a childhood she had spent decades trying not to think about.

Her father had been a man of sudden rages. Priya's mother had died when she was four, and her
father's grief had calcified into something cruel. He would strike her without warning—for a
mispronounced word, for failing to fold his shirts correctly, for the simple fact of being in his
line of sight. The blows had come with accusations: *You're worthless. You'll never amount to
anything. You're ungrateful.* By the time she was twelve, Priya had learned to make herself small,
to anticipate his moods, to scan his face for the first sign of displeasure. She had also learned
something else, something that had never left her: that the world was fundamentally unsafe, and that
she could never truly trust anyone to protect her.

Now, at seventy, Priya lived alone in a small apartment in Palo Alto. She had been divorced for
fifteen years—her husband had wanted children, and she could not bear the thought of bringing a
child into her own fractured sense of security. She had no close friends, though she had colleagues
who respected her work. What she had instead was a grinding, unrelenting sense that something
terrible was always about to happen. Because she was convinced that no one had ever held her father
accountable for what he had done—he had died in his sleep at eighty-three, never apologized, never
acknowledged the harm he had caused—she had come to believe that the world was fundamentally unjust,
that cruelty went unpunished, and that she herself was marked by his violence in a way that could
never be erased. This certainty that she belonged nowhere, that she was irrevocably contaminated by
what had been done to her, had metastasized into a hypervigilance that governed every moment. She
had to be on guard all the time, watching for the next threat, the next person who might hurt her,
the next situation that might spiral into violence.

This constant scanning for danger had pushed her toward strategies that felt protective but had
become increasingly isolating. She would cover her face with her hands when memories surfaced
unbidden—not just the physical blows, but the shame that accompanied them, a shame so intense it
felt like a second assault. She tried desperately to control her emotions in public, presenting a
composed exterior while internally fracturing. Yet the very vigilance that drove these coping
strategies also kept her trapped in a cycle with her memories. Because she was always on guard,
always braced for threat, her mind remained hyperactive, alert to any detail that might signal
danger. This meant that fragments of the trauma—a loud noise, the sight of an older man's hand
raised to adjust his glasses, the smell of her father's cologne on a stranger—would ambush her
without warning. When these intrusions came, she would desperately try to suppress them, to push
them away, but suppression only made them more vivid. And when she attempted to deliberately recall
what had happened, to try to make sense of it in a way that might reduce its power, the memories
would scatter like startled birds. She could not access them in a coherent narrative; instead, they
came in fragments of sensation and terror, the same terror she had felt as a child, raw and
immediate.

She had developed a meaning-seeking ritual, turning the trauma over and over in her mind, asking
herself *why* this had happened to her, as though understanding the reason might somehow
retroactively make it survivable, might grant it a shape that could be contained. But the question
led nowhere. There was no answer that made sense. Her father had simply been broken, and she had
been the available target. The injustice of it—that he had lived a full life, that he had never
suffered for what he had done, that she alone carried the weight of his violence—had become a kind
of obsession.

Two years ago, Priya had stopped accepting new clients. She told people it was retirement, but the
truth was that she could no longer manage the unpredictability of other people's needs, the intimacy
required to capture someone's best self through a lens. She spent most of her time in her apartment,
where the environment was controlled and predictable. She had not been to a restaurant in eighteen
months. She had not attended a family gathering—her estranged sister had stopped inviting her after
Priya had declined five times in a row. It was only when her optometrist, concerned about her
trembling hands during an eye exam, gently suggested that she might benefit from talking to someone,
that Priya finally made an appointment. She was afraid of what therapy might ask her to remember,
afraid that speaking about the trauma aloud would make it more real. But she was also, at seventy,
exhausted by the weight of carrying it alone, and she had begun to wonder if there was any way back
to a life where her hands could be steady, where she could walk into a room without scanning for
exits and threats.
