# James

James was forty-two when the chainsaw kicked back. He had been working the northeastern ridge of the
Monongahela National Forest for seventeen years, and he knew the weight of equipment the way other
men know their own breath. The morning had been clear, the crew in good spirits, and the stand of
diseased hemlock was coming down exactly as planned. His colleague Marcus had moved in to help clear
a bind, positioning himself just where James had calculated he would not be—where the kickback, when
it came, would miss him by inches. But the blade caught the grain differently than James had
anticipated. In the fraction of a second between the machine's violent reversal and Marcus's scream,
James understood with absolute clarity that he had made the miscalculation. The chainsaw caught
Marcus's left leg just above the knee. The blood came fast. The helicopter ride to the trauma center
took forty minutes, and James sat in the cabin knowing, with the certainty of a man who had just
watched his own negligence made flesh, that he had broken something in Marcus that no surgery could
fully repair. Marcus lost the leg below the knee. He never came back to forestry.

For months afterward, James continued working. He told himself that stopping would be worse—that he
needed to reclaim the ridge, to prove that one mistake did not define twelve years of safe practice.
But something had shifted in his relationship to the work itself. When he caught the scent of fresh-
cut wood or felt the particular strain in his shoulders that came from holding a chainsaw at the
precise angle required for a clean fell, his body would flood with a sensation he could only
describe as falling. His chest would tighten, his hands would shake on the controls, and he would be
back in that moment of miscalculation, feeling the exact posture he had held—the slight forward
lean, the grip, the weight distribution—as if his body were being forced to relive the geometry of
his own failure. The physical sensations became inextricable from the memory itself. Even on days
when he was not cutting, a sharp pain in his own left shoulder—an old injury, nothing serious—would
suddenly transport him backward, and with it came the memory, vivid and embodied, of that morning on
the ridge.

What frightened James most was not the memory itself, but what the memory proved about him. He had
come to believe that the accident revealed something intrinsic, some flaw in his judgment or his
character that had always been there, waiting for the right circumstance to destroy someone else's
life. He could not rely on himself anymore. How could he, when the evidence was so clear? He began
to notice threats everywhere. In the grocery store, a stranger reaching past him for a can would
send his eyes tracking the man's hands, searching for hostile intent where none existed. At home,
when a neighbor stopped by to chat, James found himself studying the man's face, trying to read
whether this person harbored some grievance, some reason to wish him harm. His wife, Sarah, had
begun to mention that he seemed different—more distant, more watchful. He told her he was fine, but
the truth was that he could not relax, not even in their living room with the doors locked and Sarah
beside him. His body remained in a state of constant vigilance, as if the accident had rewired
something fundamental in how he perceived the world.

The physical pain in his shoulder, the memory of that morning, and his new conviction that he was
fundamentally unsafe—these had begun to feed each other. When his shoulder ached, the memory would
surface, and with it the rage and grief that accompanied the recollection of what he had done. That
rage and grief would crystallize into the conviction that he was incapable, that he could no longer
trust his own judgment, that he was a danger to anyone who worked beside him. This certainty led him
to withdraw from the crew, to volunteer for solo assignments, to spend more time in the office doing
paperwork than in the field doing the work that had once defined him. His supervisor had noticed.
There had been a quiet conversation about his fitness for the job. James had not fought it. He had
come to believe, with the weight of absolute truth, that the best thing he could do for anyone was
to remove himself from situations where his flawed judgment might cause harm again. By the time he
walked into the therapist's office, he had been on modified duty for eight months, was sleeping
poorly, had lost twenty pounds, and could not remember the last time he had felt anything other than
the dull ache of his own culpability. What he wanted most was to stop feeling like a danger. What he
feared most was that he never would.
