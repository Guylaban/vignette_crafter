The isolation deepened his conviction that he was fundamentally damaged and unfit for human
connection. In the quiet of his apartment, alone with his thoughts, the guilt consumed him—why had
that boy died and not him? What had he done, or failed to do, that made him worthy of survival? The
drinking and the avoidance had kept him safe, in his nervous system's accounting—they had prevented
him from being exposed, from facing the judgment he deserved—which meant the threat they were
responding to must be real and ongoing. Without ever testing the feeling, without ever staying in a

Three weeks ago, his supervisor informed him that if he did not complete a mental health evaluation
and begin treatment, his position would be terminated. Kofi finally called a therapist. He sits now
in the waiting room, hands trembling slightly, uncertain whether he can trust anyone with the truth
of what he carries—the guilt, the shame, the conviction that he is fundamentally broken and that the
world is far more dangerous than anyone else seems to understand.
