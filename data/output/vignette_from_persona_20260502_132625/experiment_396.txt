# Clinical Vignette: Rajesh Patel

Rajesh Patel was thirty-eight when the traffic stop changed everything. He had been driving home
from a late project deadline at the design firm in downtown Portland, the city lights blurring past
his windshield as he thought about the presentation he'd nailed that afternoon. A police car pulled
behind him on the Morrison Bridge—he wasn't speeding, he was certain of that—but his stomach
tightened anyway. The officer approached the driver's window with a flashlight, and Rajesh's hands
went cold on the steering wheel. What started as a routine license check escalated when the officer
claimed to smell marijuana. Rajesh, who had never used drugs in his life, tried to explain, his
voice climbing higher as he protested. The officer's hand moved to his belt. Another car arrived.
For forty-five minutes, Rajesh was removed from his vehicle, patted down, questioned about gang
affiliations he had no connection to, and treated with a contempt that seemed to have nothing to do
with anything he had actually done. Eventually, they let him go—no charges, no apology—but not
before one of the officers made a comment about "people like you" that Rajesh couldn't quite parse,
though he understood the intent perfectly.

Three years later, Rajesh carries that night like a stone in his chest. The specific smell of burnt
rubber from the officer's patrol car—a sharp, acrid odor that lingers in his memory—now appears
everywhere. He catches it on the street and his body floods with adrenaline. The smell of burnt
plastic in his own kitchen when he's cooking triggers an immediate, visceral panic. His wife, Meera,
has learned not to ask him why he flinches. What terrifies Rajesh most is what that night proved to
him: the world is fundamentally dangerous, and the people meant to protect it cannot be trusted. He
was betrayed not by a criminal but by authority itself, by someone whose job was to serve him. This
certainty—that the system is rigged, that people in uniform are threats regardless of his own
innocence—now colors everything. When he sees a police car, even parked on a side street, his mind
floods with danger signals. Any person in uniform, a security guard at the grocery store, a parking
enforcement officer, triggers the same cascade of fear and rage.

Over the past three years, Rajesh has developed intricate ways of managing this world he now
understands to be hostile. Before any interaction—a phone call to his insurance company, a meeting
with a client, even casual conversation with a neighbor—he rehearses what he will say. He scripts it
out in his head, sometimes aloud in the shower, playing both sides of the dialogue. He is
hypervigilant in public spaces, tracking the location of anyone in uniform, noting exits, assessing
threats. He avoids driving on certain routes because they pass police stations. He has stopped
planning for the future. When Meera suggests a vacation next summer or asks about renovating the
kitchen, Rajesh feels a creeping dread. To make plans is to assume safety, and he cannot afford that
assumption. The uncertainty feels safer than hope.

The weight of rehearsal and avoidance has warped his memory of the event itself. When he tries to
tell someone—his therapist, his wife, a friend—what happened, the words scatter. He knows the
sequence, the timeline, the officer's face, but when he opens his mouth to explain, something
fractures. He loses the thread midway through, his voice becoming mechanical, and he cannot finish.
The harder he tries to articulate it, the more the memory fragments into disconnected images: the
flashlight, the hand on the belt, the phrase "people like you." When he remembers the event in quiet
moments, an overwhelming guilt settles over him—a guilt that makes no rational sense, since he did
nothing wrong, yet it persists. This guilt, paradoxically, drives him to rehearse more, to plan
less, to scan more carefully for danger. Each avoidance, each rehearsal, each moment of
hypervigilance reinforces his conviction that the world is unsafe and that he cannot trust anyone
with authority—which then makes him more attuned to uniforms, more reactive to burnt smells, more
convinced that another encounter is inevitable.

Rajesh came to his first therapy appointment last month because Meera issued an ultimatum. His
avoidance has begun to cost him work; clients sense his anxiety, his need to control every
interaction. He has turned down two promotions because they would require him to attend industry
conferences and networking events—spaces he cannot monitor, cannot predict. He is forty-one now, and
he is afraid that if he does not find a way to reclaim his life, he will spend the rest of it small
and trapped, rehearsing conversations that never quite happen, avoiding a future that might have
been safe all along.
