# Khalil's Spiral

Khalil was thirty-eight when the earthquake struck. He had been walking to a client meeting in
downtown Los Angeles on a Tuesday morning in March, his phone in one hand and a coffee in the other,
thinking about the architecture of a database migration he'd been planning. The ground didn't shake
gradually—it lurched. The pavement buckled beneath him. A storefront window to his left exploded
inward in a shower of glass, and for what felt like an endless second, the entire street was moving
in directions that made no physical sense. A delivery truck ahead of him tipped sideways. People
screamed. Khalil had thrown himself against a building wall, convinced the ground would open beneath
him, that the whole block would collapse. It didn't. The shaking stopped. But in that moment, when
the world had proven itself fundamentally unstable, something had fractured in him that the
aftershocks—literal and psychological—would never quite repair.

In the weeks that followed, Khalil had tried to convince himself that he was fine. He returned to
work. He told his wife Yasmin that he was "processing it," that he just needed to stay positive and
move forward. He had come to believe that if he simply refused to dwell on what had happened, if he
kept his mind on other things—his projects, his team, the good parts of his day—the fear would
eventually fade. But the positive thoughts felt like a dam holding back something immense. Whenever
he forced himself to think only good things, to insist to himself that everything would be okay, the
effort itself became exhausting, and the moment his concentration slipped, the memories would surge
back with terrible clarity. The sound of breaking glass would replay in his head without warning,
crisp and loud, as if his ears were still there on that street. He would hear the screams, the car
alarm blaring, the strange groaning of the building settling. These werenitory fragments would come
back in no order he could piece together—sometimes the glass first, sometimes the screams, sometimes
just the feeling of the ground moving and the sound mixed together in a sensory flood that made his
heart race and his palms sweat, indistinguishable from actually being there again.

Each time a memory surfaced, the world around him would suddenly feel dangerous in ways he couldn't
quite explain. A colleague bumping into him at his desk would send him into a state of alarm that
lasted minutes. In meetings, he found himself automatically noting the exits, calculating which
direction he could move if something went wrong. He had started taking different routes to the
office, ones with wider streets and fewer tall buildings. He was always scanning for escape routes
before he could settle anywhere—the coffee shop where he worked on Fridays, his own kitchen, even
his bedroom at night. Yasmin had asked him why he'd rearranged the furniture, why he now sat with
his back to the wall, why he flinched when she reached for his shoulder to show him something on her
phone. He couldn't explain it in a way that made sense to her.

The threat he felt, constant and lurking, had begun to feed a darker conviction. Khalil had come to
believe that somebody else would not have gotten into this situation—that a braver person, a more
competent person, would have seen the danger coming or responded differently. The fact that the
earthquake had caught him so completely off guard, that he had felt so utterly helpless, seemed to
confirm something he had always suspected about himself: he was weaker than other men, less capable
of protecting himself or the people he loved. This belief then intensified his hypervigilance; if he
was the kind of person who couldn't handle a natural disaster, then he had to be even more vigilant,
had to plan even more carefully, had to see threats everywhere. And the more he scanned for danger,
the more his memories would intrude—a sudden movement in his peripheral vision would trigger the
auditory recall of the glass shattering, which would then feel completely real across all his
senses, which would reinforce his conviction that danger was imminent, that nothing good could
happen to him anymore, that he was trapped in a world that could betray him at any moment.

He had thrown himself into forced positivity as a way to contain the memories, but the effort of
suppressing them only made them more vivid when they broke through. And when they did break
through—when the sensory flood came, when he heard those sounds again and felt the ground moving
beneath him—his mind would spin into rumination about how he should have done something differently,
how he should have seen it coming. The rumination would leave him exhausted, and the exhaustion
would make him more irritable with Yasmin, more withdrawn from his team. He had started avoiding the
office gym because showers reminded him of the sound of pipes rupturing during the quake. He avoided
hospitals entirely—the thought of ambulances, of medical equipment, of being in a vulnerable state
where he couldn't escape, made his chest tight. He had even skipped his annual physical, telling
himself he was too busy, when really the idea of sitting in a doctor's office with no clear exit, no
way to control what happened to his body, felt unbearable.

By the time Khalil finally made an appointment with a therapist, two years had passed. He was forty
now. Yasmin had given him an ultimatum—that he needed to get help or she couldn't keep watching him
disappear. He had come to believe that nothing good could happen to him anymore, that he used to be
a happy person but now he was always miserable, and the weight of that conviction had settled into
his daily life like a fog he couldn't lift. He sat in the therapist's office with his hands
clenched, afraid that if he talked about what happened, if he let the memories fully surface, they
would consume him entirely. But he was also terrified that if he didn't, he would lose
everything—his marriage, his sense of himself, the life he had built before the ground had moved and
shown him how little control he actually had.
