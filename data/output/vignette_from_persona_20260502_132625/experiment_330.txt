Arjun had been a careful person long before the incident on the highway overpass. At forty-six, they
had built a life of deliberate choices: a stable marriage to their partner Deepak, a senior position
at a fintech company where their code was trusted by thousands of users, a reputation for precision
and foresight. They had learned early, growing up in a household where their parents' divorce had
taught them that vigilance was survival. But nothing had prepared them for the afternoon in March
when two state troopers pulled them over for a broken taillight, and one of those officers,
unprovoked, dragged them from the driver's seat and slammed their head against the pavement while
the other stood with hand on his holster, shouting questions Arjun could not process through the
white noise of pain and terror.

The first weeks afterward had been a blur of medical visits and police reports that went nowhere.
Arjun had tried to return to work, tried to move through the motions of normalcy. But something had
fractured that afternoon, something deeper than the hairline fracture the CT scan had ruled out.
Within a month, Arjun had come to believe that the world was fundamentally unsafe, that authority
could turn on you without warning, that no amount of compliance or good behavior offered protection.
Once Arjun had settled into this conviction—that safety was an illusion and that aggression could
emerge from anywhere—the ordinary world began to bristle with potential threats. A uniformed
security guard at the grocery store, a raised voice in a meeting, even the unexpected warmth of
Deepak's hand on their shoulder: each became something to scrutinize, things that would once have
passed unnoticed now snagging and holding their attention with disproportionate force. Once Arjun
had settled into this belief—that the world was fundamentally unsafe and that aggression could
emerge from anywhere—ordinary moments began to feel loaded with threat. A uniformed security guard
at the grocery store, a raised voice in a meeting, even the unexpected warmth of Deepak's hand on
their shoulder now registered as potential signals of danger, things that would once have passed
unnoticed now snagging and holding their attention with disproportionate force. This conviction—that
you could never know who will harm you—had become the lens through which they saw everything. Once
Arjun had settled into this belief, the ordinary world began to bristle with threat. A uniformed
security guard at the grocery store, a raised voice in a meeting, even the unexpected warmth of
Deepak's hand on their shoulder—each became a potential signal of danger, things that would once
have passed unnoticed now snagging and holding their attention with disproportionate force. The more
Arjun held to this belief, the more the fragmented pieces of that afternoon crystallized around it.
Each time they told themselves that safety was an illusion, the memory surfaced with sharper
emotional force—not clearer in sequence, but heavier, more insistent, as though the conviction
itself was pulling the trauma into focus and making it harder to escape.

The triggers came in unexpected ways. One evening in May, Deepak had made a joke over dinner,
something light about a project at work, and Arjun had felt happiness begin to bloom in their chest
before the old familiar dread crashed in. That moment of joy felt dangerous now, like lowering their
guard, like inviting catastrophe. When Arjun felt the smallest stirring of contentment—a good code
review, Deepak's hand on their shoulder—that happiness itself became a trigger, pulling the
fragmented memory of the overpass back to the surface: the shock of their cheek against concrete,
the taste of blood, the terror flooding through them before they could push it away again. Arjun
found themselves unable to relax at all. Even sitting on their own couch, Arjun's body would tense,
and they would rise and move to the kitchen chair instead—a seat with a clear view of the front door
and the hallway beyond. At the office, the same pattern emerged. During team meetings in the glass-
walled conference room on the third floor, Arjun would position themselves with their back to the
corner, where they could see everyone's hands and faces. Colleagues began to notice, but Arjun could
not explain the compulsion without sounding paranoid.

The memory itself remained fragmented and elusive. Arjun could recall the moment of impact—the shock
of their cheek against concrete, the taste of blood—but the sequence dissolved into chaos when they
tried to hold it. Sometimes, without warning, the full weight of the terror would crash over them in
a meeting or while driving, the emotional intensity so overwhelming that Arjun would have to excuse
themselves, though no image accompanied the flood of panic. A colleague's raised voice in a meeting,
the unexpected sound of footsteps behind them on the street, even the sight of a uniformed security
guard at the entrance to their office building—each of these triggers would drag the memory back,
not as a coherent sequence but as a flood of raw emotion and bodily sensation, the fear and
helplessness returning before Arjun could even think. They had tried to write it down, to piece it
together coherently, but found themselves pushing the memory away before it could fully form, as
though their mind was protecting them from something too large to contain. Yet the belief that they
were fundamentally powerless—that they had no control over what happened to them—kept dragging the
memory back to the surface even as they tried to suppress it, each recurrence seeming to confirm
their conviction that the world was hostile and unpredictable. This conviction that they were
powerless meant that almost any ambiguous moment—a colleague's hesitation, a stranger's glance on
the street, the sound of a car door slamming—could register as a warning sign, a potential prelude
to harm.

By autumn, the cycles had deepened. When Arjun felt the smallest stirring of contentment—a good code
review, Deepak's hand on their shoulder—that happiness itself became a trigger, pulling them back
toward the memory's edges and the conviction that safety was an illusion. Confrontations at work,
even routine disagreements about design decisions, sent Arjun spiraling. A colleague had raised
concerns about an API specification in a meeting, and Arjun's body had flooded with the same
helpless rage and fear from the overpass, their hands shaking so badly they'd had to leave. The
fragmented memories surfaced then too—not the full story, but the emotional residue, the sense that
they had no power, that aggression could emerge from anywhere.

Watching others had become second nature. On the train commute, Arjun monitored every passenger's
posture, every glance. At the grocery store, they tracked the movements of security guards, police
officers, anyone in uniform. The conviction that others could see their trauma written on their
face—that something about the incident was visible to strangers, marking them as weak or
damaged—made public spaces feel like exposure. Deepak had tried to help, had suggested therapy, but
Arjun had resisted until last week, when they'd realized they could barely leave the house anymore.
The world had become so small, so threateningly full of people who might hurt them, of moments of
happiness that felt like traps. That was when Arjun finally called.
