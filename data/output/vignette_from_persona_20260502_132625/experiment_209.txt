# Clinical Vignette: Aroha

Aroha was sixty-six when she came to the clinic, a Samoan woman who had owned a small gift shop in
Portland for twenty-three years before the night that unraveled everything. On a Tuesday evening in
March, she had been closing up alone—it was 9:47 p.m., a time she could recall with terrible
precision even now—when two plainclothes officers entered through the unlocked door. What followed
was a blur of shouting, hands on her body, her face pressed against the counter. She had been
suspected of selling counterfeit goods; the allegation was later dropped entirely, but in those
minutes she had been certain she was being arrested for something she hadn't done. One officer had
restrained her arm so forcefully that she heard something crack. She had screamed. She had tried to
explain. They had not listened. When it was over, she sat alone in her shop for an hour, shaking,
unable to call anyone.

In the months afterward, Aroha found that certain moments would detonate without warning. When the
clock approached 9:45 p.m., her chest would tighten. Her body knew the danger was coming, even if
her mind tried to reason with itself. The sensation of being watched—a customer lingering too long
near the register, a security camera's red light—would send her into a state of vigilant dread. And
sometimes, standing in her shop, she would feel dizzy and lightheaded, the room tilting as if the
floor itself had become unreliable. These moments were terrifying not just because of the fear
itself, but because they drove her to do things she didn't recognize in herself.

She began avoiding the shop during evening hours, hiring a manager to close instead, which cost her
money and control. She avoided the stretch of street where the incident had occurred, taking a
longer route to her bank. But the avoidance extended further: she stopped going out after dark
altogether, stopped attending her daughter's evening book club, stopped the walks she had loved. In
the thick of her distress, she discovered that spending—sudden, urgent purchases she couldn't quite
justify—provided a momentary lift, a way to interrupt the spiraling dread. She would buy things
online late at night: decorative bowls she didn't need, expensive skincare products, jewelry. Each
purchase brought a brief rush, followed by guilt and the creeping sense that she was losing control.
Within six months, her credit card debt had climbed by nearly eight thousand dollars.

The memory itself had fractured in ways that terrified her. She could not remember the faces of the
officers clearly, though she remembered the weight of the hand on her arm with absolute clarity.
There were gaps—whole minutes seemed to have vanished—and what remained felt so extreme, so violent,
that sometimes she wondered if her mind had exaggerated it, if she had somehow made it worse than it
actually was. She couldn't put the experience into words when she tried to describe it to her ex-
husband; the language felt inadequate, the event itself almost unreal, as though it had happened to
someone else entirely. This fragmentation of memory, this sense that she couldn't quite hold the
story together, only deepened her conviction that something was fundamentally wrong with how she was
coping. A normal person would have moved past this by now. A normal person wouldn't be spending
money she didn't have or avoiding half the city. She had always prided herself on her resilience, on
being the kind of woman who could manage a business alone, raise two children, survive a divorce
with grace. But her reactions since the violence told her something different: she was inadequate, a
lousy coper, perhaps even losing her mind.

These beliefs fed back into the memory itself. When she tried to think about what had happened, the
conviction that she was going crazy made her pull away from the memory, made her resist looking at
it directly. The harder she tried not to think about it, the more fragmented and unreal it became.
And that fragmentation—the gaps, the sense of unreality—only confirmed her worst fear: that her mind
was breaking. When evening approached and the dizziness began, when she felt watched in her own
shop, the maladaptive strategies she had developed—the avoidance, the spending sprees—would
temporarily quiet the panic. But each time she gave in to them, each time she bought something she
couldn't afford or stayed home instead of going to her granddaughter's school event, the shame and
the conviction deepened. She was not coping well. She was, in fact, coping terribly. And the
avoidance, meant to protect her, only made the triggers feel more dangerous, more inescapable. By
the time she called the clinic, Aroha had become a prisoner in her own life, trapped in a cycle she
couldn't name but could feel tightening around her with each passing week.
