# Dr. Ricardo Morales

Ricardo Morales had been a surgeon for thirty-four years—a man whose hands moved with the precision
of someone who had spent decades inside the body, fixing what was broken. At sixty-two, he carried
himself with the quiet authority of a specialist whose judgment was trusted in operating rooms
across the city. His wife María had always said he was calmer under pressure than anyone she knew.
That was before the accident.

It happened on a Tuesday in March, three months before he came to see me. Ricardo was driving home
from the hospital after a long day of reconstructive work, his mind still half in the operating
theater, when a delivery truck ran a red light and T-boned his sedan at an intersection. The impact
was violent and sudden—the sound of metal crumpling, the sensation of his body being thrown
sideways, the smell of burning rubber and gasoline flooding through the shattered window. For a
moment that felt eternal, Ricardo hung suspended in the twisted frame of his car, unable to move his
left arm, convinced he was dying. A paramedic had to cut him out. He spent two nights in the
hospital—the same hospital where he worked—with fractured ribs, a dislocated shoulder, and
lacerations across his left side. Physically, he healed. The shoulder regained its range of motion.
The ribs knitted. But something else had fractured in those seconds at the intersection, something
that had not healed.

Within weeks, Ricardo began avoiding situations that put his body under physical stress. He stopped
his morning runs—the rapid heartbeat felt dangerous, as though it might trigger another catastrophe.
He declined invitations from colleagues to play tennis. When his grandson asked him to help build a
bookshelf, Ricardo made an excuse. The exertion terrified him in a way he could not quite articulate
to himself, though the logic was there, underneath: if his body was stressed, if his heart rate
climbed, something terrible would happen again. He also began withdrawing from the things that had
once brought him pleasure. He stopped attending the monthly dinners with his medical school
friends—the thought of sitting in a restaurant, of being in an enclosed space with people, of the
inevitable conversation about the accident, felt overwhelming. He gave up reading novels in the
evenings, a habit of thirty years, because he could not concentrate. Even his work began to change.
He cut back his surgical schedule, telling the department chair he needed to reduce his hours. What
he did not say was that the thought of being in the operating room—his sanctuary for so long—now
filled him with dread.

The triggers came without warning and with brutal frequency. Whenever his daughter called—his
daughter who had driven to the hospital the night of the accident, whose voice on the phone now
carried an echo of that panic—Ricardo felt his chest tighten. Seeing a car that had been in a
collision, even from a distance, could derail his entire day. But the most insidious trigger was
tactile: the texture of the seat belt webbing. Every time he got into a car, that rough nylon
against his palm brought back the moment of impact with such force that he sometimes had to pull
over. The sensation was not merely a reminder of the accident; it felt like the accident itself,
happening again in real time. His memories of those seconds had become more vivid and real than any
memory he possessed—more real than his wedding day, more real than the birth of his children. The
memories did not feel like the past at all; they felt like the present, intruding unbidden into his
mind when he was trying to perform a delicate surgery or sit with his wife in the evening. He would
be listening to María talk about her day, and suddenly he was back in the car, suspended in that
moment of terror, unable to hear her voice anymore.

The worse the intrusions became, the more Ricardo tried to push them away. He developed a habit of
cutting off his own thoughts as soon as the memory began to surface—a kind of mental flinching that
left the memory incomplete, fragmented, but somehow more powerful for being interrupted. This
avoidance of thinking about the event, this active suppression, only seemed to make the memories
return with greater force. And because the memories felt so real, so present, he began to avoid more
and more situations—avoiding his family gatherings because his brother-in-law's new car in the
driveway triggered the imagery, avoiding the grocery store because the parking lot felt dangerous,
avoiding anything that might jostle loose another intrusion. The avoidance had become his primary
strategy for managing a threat that his mind insisted was still imminent, even as the rational part
of him—the surgeon, the man of science—knew the accident was over.

By the time Ricardo came to my office, he had become a man defined by what he no longer did. He sat
across from me, his hands folded in his lap, and said quietly: "I'm not sure who I am anymore. I
used to be someone who could handle anything. Now I'm afraid of my own thoughts." What he feared
most was that this version of himself—diminished, avoidant, trapped—was permanent.
