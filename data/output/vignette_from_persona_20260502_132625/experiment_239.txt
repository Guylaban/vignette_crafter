# Carlos

Carlos was seventy-two years old when he walked into the clinic, though the tension in his shoulders
and the careful way he positioned himself in the waiting room chair—back to the wall, eyes on the
door—made him seem older. He had been a respected dentist in his community for forty years,
meticulous with his hands, patient with anxious patients, the kind of man who remembered his
clients' children's names. His wife, Maria, had died three years ago, and since then he lived alone
in the house they had bought together in 1989, the year after the bombing.

It was 1988 when Carlos's life fractured. He had been visiting his brother in San Salvador, a
routine trip to see family he hadn't seen in months. He was waiting outside a government building—a
bureaucratic errand, nothing more—when the explosion came. The blast threw him backward, and in the
seconds that followed, there was noise so complete it seemed to erase all other sensation. He
remembered the taste of dust, the way his ears rang for hours afterward, the bodies he did not want
to remember but could not forget. He was thirty-eight years old. He survived. Forty-seven people did
not.

For the first few years in the United States, Carlos had managed. He built his practice, married
Maria, raised two daughters. But over the past decade, something had shifted. The world had become a
place of constant threat, and his mind had become a place where the bombing lived as though it were
happening now.

It began with sounds. A car backfiring on the street outside his dental office would send a jolt
through his chest so violent he had to grip the armrest of his chair. His hygienist noticed he
flinched at the suction hose, at the high-pitched whine of the drill. When patients mentioned
military service or when he caught news reports about bombings—in the Middle East, in Africa,
anywhere—his hands would shake and he would have to excuse himself to the back room. These moments
were triggers, though he did not use that word. He simply knew that certain things made the past
feel present, made his body respond as though danger were imminent.

Each trigger pulled him into a state of hypervigilance that had become his constant. When he heard
an unexpected loud noise, his entire body tensed. He had begun scanning every room he entered—the
dental office, the grocery store, his own home—mapping exits with the precision he once reserved for
mapping root canals. He watched people closely, reading their faces for signs of aggression, their
posture for threat. A young man in a baseball cap at the supermarket could trigger an hour of
suspicion. The weight of this watching exhausted him.

To manage the threat he felt everywhere, Carlos had begun carrying a small pistol in his jacket
pocket. He told himself it was protection, but it was also something else: a way of reclaiming
agency, of ensuring that if danger came, he would not be helpless as he had been in San Salvador. He
avoided news entirely now—he had stopped watching television, stopped reading the newspaper—because
any report of violence, any mention of military action, sent him spiraling into a state where the
boundary between past and present dissolved.

The memories themselves were the worst part. When they came—and they came often, triggered by a
siren, a raised voice, the smell of something burning—they did not arrive as thoughts he could
examine and set aside. They came as a complete sensory onslaught, a reliving so total that for a
moment he was back in San Salvador, the ground was shaking beneath him, and the noise was
everywhere. He could not put it into words, this experience; the memory existed in his body rather
than in language. When it overwhelmed him, he would have to stop whatever he was doing—stop a
patient mid-procedure once, which had cost him their trust—and wait for it to pass.

The rumination had become a habit, a way of trying to make sense of something senseless. He blamed
himself for being there that day, for not leaving five minutes earlier, for not choosing a different
street. If he had done things differently, if he had been more careful, more aware, perhaps he could
have prevented it. The self-blame was relentless, and it fed the hypervigilance; if he was
responsible for what happened, then surely he could prevent it from happening again by being
vigilant enough, prepared enough, armed enough.

But the strategies he had adopted to feel safe—the weapon, the avoidance, the constant watching, the
rumination—had become their own kind of trap. The more he carried the gun, the more he felt he
needed it, the more he scanned for threats, the more threats he found. The avoidance of news meant
he lived in a state of uncertainty, imagining dangers rather than knowing facts. And the rumination,
far from bringing understanding, only deepened his sense of blame and his conviction that the world
was fundamentally unsafe.

His daughters had noticed the change. They worried about the gun. They had stopped inviting him to
family gatherings where there might be talk of current events, where someone might mention the
military or wear a uniform. He had become isolated, not through conscious choice but through the
gradual accumulation of triggers and avoidance, each reinforcing the other.

It was his dentist colleague, Dr. Patel, who finally insisted he see someone. "Carlos, you're not
yourself," she had said. "You jump at everything. You're not the man I know." He had resisted,
believing that what he felt was justified, that the world truly was dangerous and he was simply
being realistic. But at night, alone in the house he and Maria had filled with thirty years of life
together, he wondered if this was what the rest of his life would be: waiting for the next sound,
the next threat, the next memory that would pull him back to a moment he could not change and could
not escape.
