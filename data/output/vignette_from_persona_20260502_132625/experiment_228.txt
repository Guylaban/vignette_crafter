# Clinical Vignette: Marcus Chen

Marcus Chen was seventy-five when he first came to the clinic, referred by his primary care
physician after he'd refused a routine colonoscopy three times in a row. The refusals had begun
eighteen months earlier, following a period that Marcus himself rarely spoke about directly—the year
his daughter Lily had fallen gravely ill at age six. She'd contracted a bacterial infection that
triggered acute kidney failure, and for forty-seven days she'd been hospitalized in the pediatric
intensive care unit. Marcus had been there nearly every moment, watching her small body hooked to
machines, watching her cry when nurses came to draw blood, watching the monitors that beeped in
rhythms he'd learned to dread. When Lily finally recovered—a recovery that still felt miraculous to
him, even now—Marcus had believed the ordeal was behind them both. He'd gone back to work at the
cybersecurity firm, resumed his routines, and for years, life had moved forward.

But something had fractured in him that he couldn't quite articulate. Now, when Marcus caught the
smell of antiseptic—that sharp, chemical hospital smell—it didn't simply remind him of those days;
it pulled him back into them entirely. His chest would tighten, his heart would race, and he'd find
himself standing in the hallway of the ICU again, helpless and terrified. The smell of a hospital
corridor in his own doctor's office building last year had sent him into a panic so acute he'd had
to leave before seeing his physician. The trigger wasn't gentle or distant; it was immediate and
consuming, flooding his body with the same dread he'd felt thirty years ago when he thought his
daughter might die.

What troubled Marcus most was that the memories themselves had taken on a quality of terrible
presence. When he tried to describe to his late wife—before she'd passed five years ago—what it felt
like to remember those forty-seven days, he'd fumbled for words. The memories didn't feel like
something from the past, filed away and retrievable. They felt like a film that played over and
over, and when it played, he was there, not watching it but living it. He'd start to explain to
someone how the memories gripped him, but he'd lose the thread midway through, unable to finish, the
words dissolving as the memories themselves took over. This had made him withdraw from friends and
from his daughter, who'd asked him once why he seemed so distant from her. He couldn't explain it to
her either.

The more the memories intruded—and they intruded constantly, unpredictably—the more Marcus found
himself bracing against anything that might trigger them. When his grandson was born two years ago,
Marcus had felt a complicated dread he couldn't name. Babies, pregnancy announcements, even hearing
a child cry in a grocery store would send his mind racing back to Lily's vulnerability, to the
fragility he'd learned was always lurking beneath the surface of life. These reminders would pull
the memories forward with such force that his body would react before his mind could catch up: his
hands would shake, sweat would bead on his forehead, and he'd feel as though he were standing in
that ICU all over again, not metaphorically but actually, present-tense and inescapable.

At work, where Marcus had built a careful professional identity over four decades, the weight of
these intrusions had begun to show. He'd become hypervigilant about his health, canceling
appointments, avoiding any medical setting. When colleagues invited him to celebrate a colleague's
new baby, he'd made excuses. His daughter had noticed his absence from family events. The refusal of
the colonoscopy was simply the moment when his avoidance had become impossible to hide—when the
doctor had said, "Marcus, we need to do this," and Marcus had realized he couldn't, not because he
was afraid of the procedure itself, but because walking into that medical setting, smelling that
smell, might crack open something in him that he was no longer sure he could contain. He'd come to
believe that if he allowed himself to go through with it, the memories would consume him entirely,
that the boundary between past and present would dissolve completely. Now, sitting in the clinic,
Marcus was struggling to explain why a routine medical examination felt like a threat to his
survival itself.
