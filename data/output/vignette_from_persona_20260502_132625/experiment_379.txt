# Marcus Chen

Marcus Chen, forty-nine, had spent the last three months trying to rebuild a life that no longer
felt like his own. He was an architect by training and temperament—a man accustomed to seeing spaces
whole, to understanding how walls and light and sight lines could make a room feel safe or exposed.
That capacity had served him well for twenty-five years. Now it had become a curse.

The event itself lived in his mind as a series of fractured snapshots, their sequence scrambled
beyond his ability to arrange them into something coherent. It was late June when he and his
business partner David had stayed late at the office to prepare drawings for a municipal contract.
The building was nearly empty—just the two of them on the fourth floor of a downtown office tower,
windows overlooking the street. Marcus had stepped into the break room for coffee around seven in
the evening. What followed was a violence so sudden and senseless that even now, three months later,
he could not reliably say what happened first: the sound of breaking glass, David's shout, the man
with the knife, or his own paralysis as he stood frozen in the doorway watching his partner
collapse. He knew what happened—knew the sequence intellectually, could recount it to the police—but
something in his mind refused to order it properly. The moments were there, vivid and raw, but
disconnected, like frames from a film shown out of sequence. And there were no words for it. When
his wife Sarah asked him to describe what he'd witnessed, he found himself stalling, reaching for
language that simply would not come. "It was... he was there, and then..." The words dissolved. What
tormented him most was that when he closed his eyes, the event did not feel like memory at all. It
felt like it was happening in real time, present tense, David falling again and again.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus began to notice changes in how he moved through the world. He
could not enter a room without immediately scanning for exits—a habit that had formed so quickly and
completely that he was barely aware of doing it. Restaurants, conference rooms, his own office: each
space was now mapped first for escape routes, second for function. He felt unsafe unless his back
was against a wall where he could see the entire room. This was not conscious strategy but primitive
necessity, the way his body now understood safety. When violence appeared on television or in
conversation, even mentioned in passing, something in his chest seized. The mention of the attack,
the word "stabbing," the description of any confrontation—these triggered a cascade that began in
his body before his mind caught up. His heart would race. His palms would sweat. And then the
memories would crash forward, not as recollection but as reliving: the moment was happening again,
urgent and present, and his body responded as though the danger were immediate. The triggers pulled
the memories forward, and the memories flooded his nervous system with threat.

What frightened Marcus most was a belief he had come to hold about himself. He had begun to feel
that there was something intrinsic to him—something in his nature or his choices—that had made him
present for this violence, that had made him witness to David's death. Why had he stepped into the
break room? Why had he not closed the door? Why had he frozen instead of acting? He carried a deep
conviction that he should have prevented it, that his helplessness was not circumstance but
character. He felt profoundly isolated by this guilt, unable to explain to Sarah or his colleagues
why he could not simply move past it. This sense of personal fault fed his vigilance. Because he
believed something about him invited disaster, he scanned exits more obsessively. He positioned
himself with his back to walls more rigidly. And when he felt unsafe—which was almost always now—his
mind returned to the memory, searching it again for the moment he had failed, the moment he could
have changed everything.

At the office, the impact had become undeniable. Marcus had begun to avoid client meetings,
particularly those in unfamiliar buildings. He had made excuses to Sarah about social events. He had
taken to working from home, where he could control his sightlines. His business partner's
absence—David was gone, the office was a crime scene in his mind—made returning feel impossible. It
was his wife who finally insisted he seek help, not with anger but with a kind of desperate
tenderness. She had watched him disappear into hypervigilance, seen him flinch at ordinary sounds,
noticed the weight loss and the nights he spent sitting upright in the dark. Marcus came to the
first session still unable to fully articulate what had happened, still scanning the therapist's
office for exits, still convinced that something in him had invited the catastrophe he had
witnessed. What he wanted most was to feel safe again. What he feared most was that he never would.
