Linh was forty-eight when Typhoon Kestrel struck the coastal town where she and her husband had

porch overlooking the bay. On the night of the storm, the power had failed around nine o'clock. Linh

to a roar. Then, suddenly, a section of their roof gave way. The noise was deafening—the crack of
wood, the shriek of wind, rain pouring in—and then, just as suddenly, an eerie silence as the
structure stabilized. In that silence, Linh heard her own breathing, her husband's shout, and
nothing else. The silence felt more terrifying than the noise. When rescue workers finally arrived
hours later, they found her standing in the rain-soaked hallway, staring at nothing, unable to move.

Four years had passed since that night, and Linh was fifty-two now. The physical damage to the house
had been repaired long ago, but something in her remained fractured. She had come to believe that
silence itself was dangerous—that it meant something was about to happen, that the world was holding
its breath before striking again. Whenever the library grew quiet between patrons, when the hum of
the air conditioning stopped, when she woke at three in the morning to stillness, her body tensed.
These moments of silence triggered something immediate: a flood of memories so vivid and unwanted
that she could taste the rainwater again, feel the splinters of wood against her skin. The memory
arrived without warning, forcing itself into her mind whether she wanted it or not, and with it came
an overwhelming grief—not just for the loss of her sense of safety, but for the person she used to
be, the woman who could sit quietly without fear.

When silence struck, or when she caught sight of someone who had been in the neighborhood during the
evacuation, or when dusk fell and she found herself in dimming light, Linh's body would shift into
high alert. Her shoulders would tense, her eyes would dart to the windows, to the corners of the
room, scanning constantly for signs of danger. She had developed an elaborate system of vigilance:
at the library, she always positioned herself at the reference desk where her back was to the wall
and she could see the main entrance. At restaurants, she positioned herself in corners, evaluating
escape routes. She was always watching, always on guard, and this hypervigilance seemed to sharpen
her sensitivity to triggers. The more she scanned for threat, the more likely she was to notice the
sudden absence of sound, or to recognize a familiar face from the evacuation, and each recognition
yanked the full memory back into consciousness with devastating force.

To manage the memories when they came—and they came constantly—Linh had learned to leave her body.
She would deliberately dissociate, blanking out her mind, floating somewhere above the panic. This
dissociation worked, in a way. It muted the grief, the terror, the sense of helplessness. But it
also meant that ordinary moments became dangerous. She would lose time. She would be driving home
from work and suddenly realize she had no memory of the last ten minutes. She had stopped exercising

stride. More troubling, the dissociation itself seemed to feed the cycle. When she blanked out, when
she deliberately removed herself from her body, her awareness of her surroundings dimmed—which meant
she felt less in control, which meant she scanned more intently for danger when she returned to
presence. And that heightened scanning made her more aware of silence, of familiar faces, of
darkness, which brought the memories rushing back, which sent her fleeing into dissociation again.

Her husband had noticed the changes. He had stopped inviting friends over because Linh would retreat
to the bedroom the moment they arrived—people who had been in the neighborhood, who might remind
her. He had stopped suggesting evening walks because she would refuse to go out after sunset. He had
stopped trying to comfort her in the dark because she would flinch at his touch, her body rigid with
vigilance. The marriage had not broken, but it had hollowed out into something careful and distant.

It was her supervisor at the library who finally suggested she seek help. Linh had called in sick
more and more often, and on the days she did come in, she sat rigid at her desk, unable to focus on
anything but the potential for silence, for danger. She had been a skilled librarian, patient and
thorough, but now she was exhausted—depleted by constant vigilance, by the intrusions of memory, by
the effort of holding herself together. What frightened her most was that she could not imagine this
ending, that she would spend the rest of her life trapped in this cycle of fear and dissociation,
watching and waiting for the next disaster that her body was certain was coming.
