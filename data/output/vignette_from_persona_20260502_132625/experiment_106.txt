# Aroha's Turning Point

Aroha was fifty-seven when her brother Marcus died. The aneurysm came without warning—a sudden
collapse at the supermarket, the ambulance screaming through Auckland traffic, the sterile white
corridors of Auckland City Hospital where she sat for thirty-six hours before the neurosurgeon told
her there was nothing left to do. She had held his hand as it cooled. She had smelled the
antiseptic, the machinery, the peculiar staleness of recirculated air that seemed to carry death
itself. For three weeks after the funeral, Aroha functioned on autopilot—she attended to her
architecture firm, managed the paperwork, spoke to people. But something had fractured in her that
she could not name.

Six months later, the fracture had become a chasm. Aroha had begun to avoid the parts of Auckland
where she might encounter a hospital or an ambulance. She rerouted her daily commute to the office,
adding twenty minutes to her drive, rather than pass the street where the supermarket stood. The
smell of disinfectant—in public bathrooms, in cleaning aisles, even faintly in some colleagues'
offices—would send her into a state of vigilant dread. When she caught that sharp, chemical scent,
her mind would flood with the memory of Marcus's last hours, and the certainty would grip her that
she was somehow responsible, that she should have noticed something was wrong, that her survival
while he died marked her as fundamentally broken. These moments of exposure to the hospital smell or
to particular locations would send her spiraling into conviction that others could see her
corruption written across her face. She began avoiding her friends entirely. Her closest confidante,
Priya, had tried to visit; Aroha had turned her away at the door, certain that Priya would see the
mark of the trauma, would recoil from what Aroha had become.

The avoidance had metastasized into something more compulsive. Aroha threw herself into exercise
with a ferocity that surprised even her. She would run for ninety minutes before dawn, then swim,
then return to the gym after work. The physical exhaustion was a kind of anesthetic—it kept her mind
occupied, kept her from sitting still with the thought that nothing meant anything anymore. Her
career, which had once brought her genuine satisfaction, now felt hollow; the buildings she designed
seemed like monuments to meaninglessness. But the exercise also served another purpose: it was
something she could control absolutely, a domain where effort produced measurable results. When the
compulsive drive to move seized her, it felt like the only sane response to a world that had
revealed itself as fundamentally hostile and random.

Yet the more she exercised, the more isolated she became. Her body grew lean and hard, her face
gaunt. Colleagues began to comment. She had withdrawn from the architectural society meetings she
once attended, from the casual lunches where she had once felt part of something. And as the
isolation deepened, her conviction that she was marked, that others could sense her unworthiness,
only intensified. The avoidance of friends seemed not just prudent but necessary—a way of protecting
them from whatever poison she carried. When she did catch her reflection unexpectedly, she would
search her face for visible evidence of the trauma, convinced she would find it.

The spatial triggers pursued her relentlessly. A colleague mentioned needing to visit the hospital
for a routine appointment, and Aroha felt the ground shift beneath her. Incomprehensible chatter in
a crowded café—overlapping voices, fragments of conversation she couldn't parse—would suddenly feel
menacing, as though people were talking about her, seeing through her. These moments would send her
home to her running shoes, to the compulsive motion that temporarily quieted the voice telling her
that she had failed Marcus, that she deserved to be alone, that the world was a place where people
died without warning and the living were left to carry an unbearable weight of meaninglessness.

It was her business partner, Daniel, who finally forced the issue. He had watched Aroha withdraw,
watched her push clients away with her distraction, watched the firm's culture shift as she became
increasingly unreachable. He sat her down one afternoon and asked, plainly, what had happened to
her. Aroha found herself unable to answer. She realized, in that moment, that she had no idea how to
exist in a world where her brother was dead and she was still alive, where hospitals and antiseptic
smells and the sound of people talking held the power to unmake her, where she had come to believe
that she was too damaged to be near anyone who might care about her. It was that realization—that
she had built a prison of her own making—that finally brought her to seek help.
