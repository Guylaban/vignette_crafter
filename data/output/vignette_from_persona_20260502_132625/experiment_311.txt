# Ama

Ama was thirty-four when soldiers came to her home in Lagos. They were looking for her brother, who
had already fled. What happened in those eight hours—the restraint, the electrical burns, the sounds
of her own voice she could not later reconcile with herself—had fractured something in her that no
amount of time or distance seemed to repair. She had left Nigeria two years later, eventually
settling in Ohio, where she took work at a plastics factory. That was eight years ago. Now, at
forty-two, she had come to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous, that safety was an
illusion maintained only by those too naive or too lucky to know better. Her body, once the
instrument of her labor and her pride, had become something she could no longer trust. It reacted to
threats before her mind could even register them—a sudden noise on the factory floor sent her heart
hammering, her palms slick with sweat, before she had consciously identified what had startled her.

The triggers were everywhere, patient and waiting. When a coworker named Samuel started on her line
three months ago, Ama's entire nervous system had gone rigid. The name alone—so close to the
officer's—sent her spiraling. She began arriving early to avoid being near him during shift changes,
which meant skipping the coffee break where she had once chatted with the other women. More
insidiously, certain weather patterns—the particular humidity and heat of August afternoons, the way
the light fell through the factory windows during late summer—transported her back so completely
that she would find herself standing motionless at her station, unable to move or speak, the smell
of sweat and something metallic flooding her nostrils even though she was surrounded by plastic and
industrial cleaner. These weather triggers activated a cascade: as soon as the temperature climbed
and the sky took on that particular quality, Ama felt the danger rising, her body tensing in
anticipation. She began wearing long sleeves regardless of the heat, avoiding the yellow shirt she
used to favor, anything that might draw attention or resemble what she had worn that day. She kept
her mind deliberately occupied—podcasts running constantly, audiobooks, anything to prevent the
silence where memory could creep in. At home, she had taken to applying heavy makeup each morning, a
mask that felt like armor, something between her and the world's gaze.

The hypervigilance had become her constant companion. Ama listened for sounds that might mean
someone was entering her apartment, her attention fractured between whatever task lay before her and
the monitoring of her environment. Every footstep in the hallway, every car door slamming outside,
registered as potential threat. The startle response was exhausting—ordinary sounds that would
barely register for most people sent her jumping, her heart rate spiking, her mind flooded with a
sense of imminent danger. When these moments came, the memory was never far behind. The event itself
had taken on a quality of unreality, as though it had happened to someone else entirely. She could
recount the facts—the date, the names, the sequence—but the experience felt alien, dissociated from
her own body and mind, something too extreme to have actually occurred to her. Yet when a sound or a
smell triggered the memory, it became visceral and immediate, the sensory details overwhelming: the
particular odor of sweat mixed with something chemical, the sound of voices, the sensation of
restraint. This alien quality of the memory—the feeling that it belonged to someone
else—paradoxically made it harder to integrate, harder to feel as something that had happened and
passed. Instead, it remained suspended, unreal yet intrusive, and the triggers that activated it
seemed to confirm her deepest conviction: that she was no longer capable of the things that had once
defined her. She had been strong once, resilient, someone who could manage a household and hold her
own in any space. Now she could barely manage a shift without her body betraying her, without the
world feeling like it was closing in.

The cycle had tightened over the years. Her avoidance of triggering situations—the clothes, the
people, the places—had shrunk her world considerably. She no longer attended the church she had once
loved because the hymns reminded her of home, and home meant vulnerability. She had turned down a
promotion to line supervisor because it would have required more interaction, more visibility, more
risk of being near someone or something that might destabilize her. Her ex-husband had left, citing
her emotional distance and the way she had become, as he put it, "someone else entirely." The threat
monitoring and the hyperstartle kept her isolated, and the isolation fed the conviction that the
world was dangerous and that she could not trust herself within it. She had come to her first
therapy appointment because her supervisor had finally insisted on it—her productivity had dropped,
her absences were mounting, and the other workers had begun to notice her strange behaviors, the way
she flinched at nothing, the way she never quite seemed present. What Ama feared most was that if
she stopped the vigilance, stopped the avoidance, stopped keeping her mind occupied, the memory
would consume her entirely. What she hoped for, though she barely dared to voice it, was to feel
like herself again—to walk through the world without her body screaming danger at every shadow.
