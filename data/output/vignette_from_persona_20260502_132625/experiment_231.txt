# Arjun

Arjun was thirty-two when the conference bombing happened. He had been attending a mental health
symposium in Boston, one of the few times in his career as a clinical psychologist that he'd left
his practice for professional development. He was sitting in the second row of the main auditorium,
listening to a keynote on trauma-informed care, when the sound came—a percussive crack that his
brain couldn't immediately categorize. For a fragment of a second, he thought it was part of the
presentation, some theatrical effect. Then the ceiling tiles began to fall. People screamed. The
noise came again, and again, and in the chaos of bodies pressing toward exits, Arjun found himself
pinned against a wall, unable to move, unable to see clearly through the dust, utterly certain that
he was going to die in a room full of strangers who couldn't help him and whom he couldn't help.

Five years later, Arjun sits in my office describing a life that has become unrecognizable to him.
He is still a psychologist—still sees patients twice a week in a private practice he shares with a
colleague—but the man who survived Boston is someone he no longer recognizes. The bombing killed
seven people and injured twenty-three others. Arjun walked away physically intact, but he has spent
the intervening years slowly withdrawing from the world he once inhabited.

The triggers are specific and relentless. Any loss of control—being stuck in traffic, unable to
leave a client session early, dependent on his wife to drive because he no longer trusts his own
reflexes—sends him into a state of mounting dread. Nighttime is worse. He lies awake in the dark
beside his wife Priya, hypervigilant, listening for sounds that might signal danger. The darkness
itself has become a threat; he sleeps with the bedside lamp on, and Priya has learned not to comment
on the electricity bill. Most destabilizing is the sound of anything resembling a gunshot—fireworks
on the Fourth of July, a car backfiring, even the sharp crack of a wooden spoon against a pot—and
when these sounds find him, whether in the street or unexpectedly at home, Arjun's body floods with
the certainty that he is back in that auditorium, trapped and powerless.

When these triggers strike, Arjun has developed a repertoire of escape routes. He avoids anything he
perceives as stressful: he has stopped attending professional conferences, declined invitations to
social gatherings, and increasingly finds reasons not to go out in the evening. His practice has
shrunk as he has quietly discouraged new referrals. When the triggers are too immediate to
avoid—when a sound pierces through his defenses—he drifts into fantasy, mentally removing himself
from the present moment, disappearing into elaborate scenarios where he is safe, in control,
untouchable. These dissociative episodes can last minutes or hours. More frequently now, in the
early evening when the light begins to fade and his anxiety crests, he pours a drink. Sometimes two.
The alcohol softens the edges of his vigilance, quiets the internal alarm that never fully silences.
He has also taken to using sleep medication on nights when the dark feels particularly threatening,
though he has begun to notice that the medication's effectiveness is waning and he requires
increasingly larger doses.

These strategies, which once seemed like survival, have become their own trap. The more Arjun avoids
stressful situations, the more the world feels genuinely dangerous—each successful avoidance
reinforces his conviction that he cannot trust himself to manage the unexpected. The fantasy
escapes, while temporarily relieving his anxiety, leave him feeling more disconnected from his own
life; he catches himself mid-session with a client, realizing he has no memory of the last five
minutes of what they said. The drinking, which began as occasional relief, has become nightly
routine, and with it has come a creeping sense of shame and self-alienation. He is a psychologist
who treats others' trauma while medicating his own with alcohol, a contradiction that deepens his
sense that the person he has become is fundamentally compromised, unreliable, a shadow of who he
was.

This recognition—that he cannot stop bad things from happening, that the world remains inherently
dangerous, that he himself has become someone he doesn't recognize—feeds back into his avoidance.
The more he believes he is helpless and broken, the more he drinks to quiet that voice. The more he
drinks and dissociates, the more he withdraws from his practice and his marriage. Priya has stopped
asking him to attend her family gatherings. His colleagues have stopped inviting him to lunch. He
has become, in his own mind, a walking liability—and that belief drives him deeper into the very
behaviors that confirm it.

It was Priya who finally insisted he seek help. She found him one evening unable to wake from a
dissociative episode, his empty glass on the nightstand, the bedroom lamp flickering. She said,
quietly, that she was frightened of what he was becoming—and more than that, that she was frightened
for him. Arjun came to see me because his wife was right: he no longer recognizes himself, and he is
terrified that if he does not find a way out of this cycle, the man he has become will be all that
remains.
