# Clinical Vignette: Aroha

Aroha was sixty-five when a delivery truck ran a red light at the intersection where she was turning
left. The impact came through her driver's side door with a violence that collapsed the frame of her
sedan inward. She remembered the smell first—hot metal and scorched rubber—and the sound, a grinding
crunch that seemed to splinter her entire left side. The paramedics found her conscious but unable
to move her left arm; the fracture extended from shoulder to wrist, compound and requiring two
surgeries. More painful than the bone, though, was what she came to believe in the weeks that
followed: that her body had betrayed her, that it had failed to protect her, that she could no
longer trust the signals it sent or the promises it made about safety. The truck driver had received
a citation. No charges were filed. When Aroha learned this, something hardened in her. The injustice
of it—that she would spend months in physical therapy while he walked away—seemed to confirm what
she had begun to suspect: that the world was fundamentally unfair, that bad things happened to good
people with no reckoning, and that everyone who looked at her now could see the mark of that
unfairness written across her face and body.

By her own account, Aroha had always been a careful person. She worked in cybersecurity, a field
that rewarded vigilance and the ability to anticipate threat. But the accident had sharpened this
tendency into something more consuming. She found herself unable to fall asleep until she had
mentally rehearsed why the accident had happened—running through the sequence obsessively, as if
repetition might somehow reveal a hidden logic, a moment where she could have done something
differently. If only she had left five minutes earlier. If only she had not turned left. If only she
had seen the truck sooner. The rumination offered no comfort, only a compulsive sense that
understanding the causation might prevent it from happening again. Alongside this, she had begun to
avoid anything that might stir feeling—she stopped calling her daughter in Auckland, stopped
listening to the music her late husband had loved, even stopped tending the garden that had once
brought her pleasure. Feeling anything, she had come to believe, was dangerous; emotions were the
gateway to remembering, and remembering was unbearable. She also found herself trying actively to
erase the memory itself, a contradictory effort that only seemed to make it more insistent, more
vivid, more present in the spaces where she tried to push it away.

At night, Aroha could not sleep. She would lie awake, acutely aware that darkness made her
vulnerable in a way daylight did not. She needed to stay alert, to monitor her surroundings, to be
ready. When exhaustion finally pulled her under, she would wake within an hour or two, seized by the
need to check that she was safe—that the doors were locked, that there were no sounds outside, that
the world had not shifted while she slept. The hypervigilance was relentless, and it fed directly
into her conviction that her body was no longer reliable; how could she trust a body that woke in
panic, that sent false alarms through her nervous system, that ached in the exact locations where
the trauma had fractured her? The night vigilance also intensified her sense of exposure. In
darkness, she imagined, her fear was visible, palpable, something others could sense. During the day
at her office, surrounded by colleagues in the cybersecurity team, she felt certain that they could
read the accident on her—that her visible tension, her reluctance to leave her desk, her flinching
when a car alarm sounded from the street below, all marked her as damaged, as someone whose body had
failed.

The memories themselves had taken on a sensory intensity that terrified her. When she thought about
the impact—and she thought about it many times each day—she could smell the scorched rubber and hot
metal as vividly as if it were happening again. The images came unbidden: the windshield fractured
in a spiderweb pattern, her left arm twisted at an angle it should not bend, the paramedic's face
above hers. These visual memories were so sharp, so intrusive, that she had begun to suspect that
erasing them might be possible if she simply tried hard enough, if she ruminated in just the right
way, if she found the exact mental sequence that would allow her to bury them. But the more she
tried to suppress the images, the more forcefully they returned, often accompanied by a phantom pain
in her left shoulder and wrist—a dull, insistent ache that made her certain her body was still
injured, still compromised, still untrustworthy. The physical pain seemed to confirm that the memory
was not just psychological, that it had left a permanent mark on her flesh, and this conviction
deepened her sense that she could never truly be safe again.

Her hypervigilance at night had begun to bleed into her work. Aroha had always prided herself on her
focus, on her ability to track complex threat patterns across networks and systems. But now she
found herself unable to concentrate for more than twenty minutes before anxiety would spike, before
she would need to get up and walk, to move, to reassure herself that she was still in control. Her
manager had noticed her declining productivity. She had taken extended leave twice in the past year,
citing physical therapy and then "stress," a word she hated because it seemed to diminish what was
happening to her—as if the injustice she carried, the betrayal of her own body, the relentless loop
of memory and fear and avoidance, could be summed up as mere stress. Two weeks ago, her daughter had
called from Auckland, worried because Aroha had not answered messages in three weeks. In that
conversation, Aroha had finally admitted that she was not sleeping, that she was afraid at night,
that she could not stop thinking about the accident, and that she had begun to wonder if this was
simply her life now—a life lived in a body she no longer trusted, in a world that had proven itself
unjust, visible to everyone around her as broken.

It was her daughter's insistence that brought her to the clinic. Aroha arrived at her first
appointment exhausted, hypervigilant about the parking lot, and convinced that whatever help was
offered would be inadequate to the fundamental betrayal her body and the world had inflicted upon
her.
