# Linh's Vigil

Linh was sixty-eight when the stalking began. A Vietnamese-American mechanic who had spent forty
years in her small garage on the outskirts of Portland, she had built a life of predictable
rhythms—early mornings bent over engine blocks, lunch at the same Vietnamese pho restaurant three
blocks away, evenings in the garden she and her husband Duc had tended for decades. The man who
began following her had come in one afternoon with a seized transmission. He was younger, perhaps
forty, with a particular kind of attention that made her uneasy even then: the way he watched her
work, how he lingered when the job was done, how he began returning to the garage without a car that
needed fixing. Within weeks, he was outside her home at odd hours. He knew her routes. He left notes
under her windshield wiper—nothing explicitly threatening at first, just observations that proved he
had been watching. *I saw you at the market on Tuesday. You prefer the Vietnamese section.* *Your
husband leaves at 7:15 AM.* The police took a report, but without a direct threat, their hands were
tied. After two months, he vanished as suddenly as he had appeared. But Linh did not return to her
life.

Now, eight years later, at seventy-six, she remained suspended in the aftermath. The man was
gone—the police confirmed it, a detective had called with news of his death in another state years
ago—yet Linh could not shake the conviction that he was still there, that his absence was a trick.
What troubled her most was how certain memories would ambush her, vivid and uncontrollable. She
would be at the garage, reaching for a wrench, and suddenly she would see his face in perfect
detail—the exact shade of his eyes, the way he smiled—as if she were looking at a photograph printed
in her mind. These moments of crystalline recall were both unbearable and difficult to escape once
they had begun. When she tried to describe them to Duc or to her daughter, the words would scatter.
She would start to recount what had happened, but halfway through, the narrative would collapse into
fragments. *It was... he was always... I can't explain it properly,* she would say, frustrated, then
deliberately push the memory away before it could fully reconstitute itself. The harder she tried to
articulate the fear, the more elusive the words became, and the more she found herself retreating
into silence.

The intrusive clarity of these memories seemed to trigger something else—a constant, exhausting
vigilance. Linh had come to believe that remembering the man so vividly, so photographically, meant
he was somehow still present. The more her mind replayed those images, the more certain she became
that she needed to be watching, always watching. Even inside her own home—the one place that should
have been safe—she felt the need to remain on guard. She would sit in her kitchen with her back to
the wall, her eyes on the window. At the garage, she stationed herself so she could see the entrance
and the parking lot simultaneously. She began checking behind her constantly, a habit so ingrained
that she barely noticed it anymore: a glance over her shoulder while walking to her car, a backward
turn while working under a hood, a pause at every corner to ensure no one was there. Her
hypervigilance had become the texture of her days, a second skin she could not shed.

What made this worse was how certain triggers—seemingly innocent ones—could shatter her fragile
equilibrium and send her spiraling back into high alert. The smell of soil or rain, the particular
earthiness of an outdoor breeze, would carry her back to the day he had waited for her in the
parking lot after she'd been gardening. Even stress itself had become a trigger; when her daughter
called with a work problem, or when the garage had a difficult customer, Linh would feel her body
tense, her eyes sharpen, her mind beginning to cycle through those photographic memories again. Most
painful of all was the paradox of happiness. When she felt relaxed—sitting with Duc on the porch,
enjoying a quiet afternoon—a creeping anxiety would follow, as if contentment itself were dangerous,
as if the moment she lowered her guard, he would return. The triggers seemed to open a door directly
to those vivid, uncontrollable memories, and the memories in turn would reset her threat system,
sending her back into hypervigilance, checking behind her, scanning the environment for the danger
she could no longer quite distinguish from safety.

Over the years, the cost had accumulated. She had reduced her hours at the garage, unable to bear
the exposure of the open bay doors. Her relationship with her grandchildren had thinned—she was too
tense, too quick to startle. Duc had grown quieter, more resigned. When a therapist had suggested
that the man was truly gone, that the memories, however vivid, were echoes of the past, Linh had
nodded politely and then continued exactly as before. What finally brought her to seek help, at
seventy-six, was not hope that she could change, but a kind of exhaustion so complete that even
vigilance seemed too heavy to carry. She had made an appointment with a trauma specialist, though
part of her was certain it would be useless. What she feared most was that if she stopped watching,
if she let her guard down even for a moment, the man would prove to have been real all along—that
her constant vigilance was the only thing keeping her alive.
