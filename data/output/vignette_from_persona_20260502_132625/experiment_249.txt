# Linh's Story

Linh was fifty-two years old when the hurricane came. She had lived in New Orleans for twenty-three
years, had raised her two daughters there, had built a life in the modest house on Dauphine Street
that she and her husband Minh had bought together in 1998. She worked the early shift at the
McDonald's on Claiborne Avenue, clocking in at five in the morning to prep the fryers and take the
first orders from construction workers and nurses heading home from night shifts. It was steady
work, unremarkable work, the kind that let her mind wander while her hands moved through their
routines. She had come to think of those mornings as hers—quiet, predictable, safe.

The storm came in late August. Linh remembered the news reports, the warnings, but she had weathered
hurricanes before. This one, they said, would be different. She remembered Minh's face when he asked
if they should leave, and how she had said no, we'll be fine, we always are. She remembered the
sound of the wind that night—not like wind at all, but like something alive and furious, tearing at
the walls of their house. She remembered water rising in the living room, black and cold, rising
past her ankles, past her knees, and then the moment the back wall gave way. She remembered grabbing
for Minh's hand in the darkness, the water filling her mouth, the terrible clarity of knowing she
might not survive the next few seconds. A neighbor's boat found her clinging to a piece of the roof.
Minh was not there.

The weeks after were a blur of shelters and search efforts and the slow, suffocating realization
that he was gone. When Linh finally returned to work three months later, she did so because she had
no other choice. She needed the paycheck. She needed something that looked like normal. But normal
had fractured into something unrecognizable.

The memory of that night had taken on a strange, splintered quality. Linh could recall the sound of
the wind and the cold of the water, but these pieces did not connect into a coherent story.
Sometimes she remembered Minh's hand in hers; other times she was certain she had been alone. The
overwhelm came without warning—a customer would order a large coffee, she would turn to the
register, and suddenly she was drowning again, her chest tight, her vision narrowing, and she would
have to step into the back room and wait for it to pass, her hands shaking as she gripped the edge
of the counter. The memory forced itself into her mind at unpredictable moments, interrupting her
work, her meals, her attempts to sleep. She had come to understand that she could not control when
it arrived.

What frightened her most was the belief that had taken root in her mind: that she would never feel
normal emotions again. Before the storm, she had been someone who laughed easily, who took pleasure
in small things—a good cup of coffee, her daughter's voice on the phone, the warmth of the sun. Now
those things felt distant and false, as though she were watching someone else experience them. She
was certain that something essential had been broken inside her, that the person she had been was
gone, drowned in that black water along with her husband. This conviction had begun to reshape how
she understood threat itself. Because if she could not control what happened to her—if a hurricane
could take everything in a single night, if she was fundamentally helpless—then danger lurked
everywhere, and nowhere was truly safe.

Her sense of threat had become pervasive. At night, she lay awake in the small apartment she now
shared with her older daughter, listening to every sound—the hum of the refrigerator, a car passing
on the street, the settling of the building itself. Each noise pulled her to high alert, her heart
racing, her body rigid. She found it harder to feel safe at night than during the day, though safety
itself had become a concept that rang hollow. During her shifts at McDonald's, she found herself
tracking the exits, monitoring the weather, watching the sky through the kitchen window as though
another disaster might materialize at any moment. And when she lay down to sleep, the fragmented
pieces of that night would return unbidden—not as a narrative she could process and release, but as
disconnected sensations and images that overwhelmed her with emotion she could not name or control.
The memory and the threat had become inseparable; each fed the other in a cycle that tightened with
each passing week.

Linh came to therapy because her daughter had insisted, because she was no longer sleeping more than
two or three hours a night, because she had begun to wonder if she would ever feel like herself
again. What she feared most was that the answer was no.
