# Clinical Vignette: Miguel

Miguel Santoro was fifty-eight when the warehouse collapsed. He had spent thirty-two years in
forensic analysis—meticulous work, methodical, the kind that required him to walk into scenes of
chaos and extract order from them. He prided himself on this. His colleagues called him steady. His
wife, Rosa, said he was the calmest person she knew. On the morning of March 14th, Miguel was at the
Riverside Industrial Complex conducting a post-fire investigation. The building had burned three
days earlier; his job was to map the structural damage, photograph the debris field, establish the
sequence of collapse. He was on the second floor, taking measurements near what had been the loading
dock, when the remaining eastern wall gave way. The sound came first—a deep, tearing crack that
seemed to open the air itself. Then the floor beneath him tilted. He had perhaps two seconds to
understand what was happening before the concrete and steel came down. He fell through the opening,
landing hard on a pile of rubble one story below, his left leg pinned under a metal beam, his right
shoulder dislocated. He lay there for forty minutes before the rescue team reached him. The weight
of the debris, the dust that made every breath feel like swallowing ash, the knowledge that more of
the structure could fail at any moment—these details had fused into something permanent in his
nervous system.

Three months later, Miguel still felt the vibration. Not metaphorically. His body remained in a
state of constant vigilance, muscles tight across his shoulders and jaw, his startle response so
acute that Rosa had learned not to come up behind him unexpectedly. A car door slamming outside the
house would send him rigid. The beep of the microwave made him flinch. He had returned to work at
the end of May, against Rosa's wishes, because he believed that staying home would mean surrendering
to fear, would mean admitting that the collapse had broken something fundamental in him. But within
two weeks, he realized that his hands trembled when he held a camera. More troubling was what had
begun to happen in his mind. He found himself catastrophizing in ways that felt alien to him—walking
into a restaurant and immediately calculating exit routes, noting which structural elements looked
compromised, wondering if the ceiling could hold. When his supervisor asked him to investigate a
structural fire at a shopping mall, Miguel declined. Then declined again. He told himself it was
reasonable caution, but beneath that reasoning was something darker: the conviction that he could no
longer trust his own judgment, that whatever instinct had kept him safe for three decades had failed
him utterly. If he had missed the signs of imminent collapse at Riverside, how could he possibly
assess danger anywhere else?

The avoidance had grown quietly at first. He stopped taking assignments that required him to enter
unfamiliar buildings. He began driving familiar routes only, avoiding the highway that passed near
construction sites. When Rosa suggested they visit their son in Sacramento—a three-hour drive
through terrain Miguel didn't know well—he invented reasons they couldn't go. He couldn't explain to
her that the thought of being in a car on an unfamiliar road, unable to assess potential hazards,
unable to control the environment, filled him with a dread that felt like drowning. What frightened
him most was not the prospect of another disaster, but rather his own incompetence in the face of
it. He had come to believe that his reactions since the collapse—the hypervigilance, the avoidance,
the constant mental scanning for threats—proved that he was a lousy coper, that a genuinely capable
man would have moved past this by now. The man he had been before March 14th would have. That man
had been someone Rosa could rely on, someone his colleagues respected, someone whose steadiness was
almost legendary. Now, when he looked at his own behavior—the way he'd stopped attending his
Thursday night poker game because the bar was in an unfamiliar neighborhood, the way he'd asked to
be reassigned to office-based analysis only, the way he'd become someone who couldn't even drive to
the grocery store without first mapping the route and checking for construction—he saw only a shell
of that person. The world had revealed itself to be far more dangerous than he'd ever allowed
himself to believe, and he was no longer the kind of man equipped to navigate it. Rosa had stopped
mentioning the trip to Sacramento. She had stopped asking him to go anywhere at all. It was this—the
quiet acceptance of his diminishment in her eyes—that finally brought Miguel to his first therapy
appointment. He sat in the waiting room, his leg bouncing rhythmically, and wondered if even talking
about what happened would prove that he was beyond repair.
