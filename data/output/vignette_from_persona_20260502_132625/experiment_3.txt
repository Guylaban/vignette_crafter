# Clinical Vignette: Kofi

Kofi was fifty-five when the hurricane came. He had spent thirty years in bomb disposal—a career
that had taught him to read silence, to move with precision through chaos, to keep his body and mind
in perfect synchrony. He lived in New Orleans with his wife, Ama, in a modest house two blocks from
the levee. When the storm warnings escalated that August morning, he had done what he always did:
assessed the threat logically, made a plan, and told himself he could manage it. He was wrong.

The storm surge came faster than the meteorologists had predicted. Kofi and Ama had evacuated to a
shelter in the Convention Center, but the building's integrity failed within hours. The roof began
to fail in sections—a grinding, metallic shriek followed by a percussion of falling debris that
echoed through the cavernous space. Kofi remembered the sound more vividly than he remembered his
own breathing. He remembered the way the concrete floor shook, the way people screamed in a register
he had never heard before, the way the darkness came in waves as sections of roof collapsed. He had
grabbed Ama and pulled her toward what he thought was a safer corner, but in the chaos—the noise,
the dust, the unpredictability of where the next collapse would come—he had lost her hand for forty-
seven seconds. Forty-seven seconds in which he could not see her, could not reach her, could not
protect her. When he found her again, she was crying, but she was alive. That was three years ago.

Since then, Kofi's world had contracted. The job he had excelled at for three decades—a profession
built on his ability to remain calm in the presence of genuine danger—had become impossible. He had
taken early retirement six months after the hurricane, unable to tolerate the unpredictability of
the work, the sudden loud noises of equipment, the way his body would betray him without warning.
But leaving the job had not brought relief. Instead, the absence of structure seemed to make
everything worse. At home, he had begun to avoid anything that felt remotely stressful: he declined
social invitations, stopped attending his church, refused to drive on highways where traffic was
unpredictable. When Ama suggested they visit her sister in Houston, the thought of the drive—the
uncontrolled variables, the other drivers, the possibility of something unexpected—sent his heart
racing. He had said no, and when she pushed, he had shouted. Shouting, he had come to realize, was
one of his triggers; the sound of his own raised voice could send him spiraling, could make his body
brace as if the roof were failing again.

To manage the mounting distress, Kofi had developed routines. He kept his mind occupied
constantly—audiobooks, podcasts, the endless scroll of news—anything to prevent silence or
stillness. He overate, particularly in the evenings, consuming large meals or entire boxes of
pastries in an attempt to soothe the anxiety that would otherwise consume him. These strategies
provided temporary relief, but they also kept him trapped in a narrowing cycle. The more he avoided
potential stressors, the more his world shrank, and the more his nervous system seemed to interpret
ordinary situations as dangerous. A car horn outside would send his body into immediate alarm—his
heart would race, his muscles would tense, his mind would flash to that moment in the Convention
Center—before he had even consciously registered the sound. When Ama raised her voice to ask why he
hadn't answered her question, his body reacted as if she were screaming in terror, and he found
himself shouting back, which only deepened his shame and his need to retreat further into avoidance
and distraction.

The memories themselves had become fragmented and overwhelming. Kofi could recall the sound of the
collapse, the sensation of losing Ama's hand, the taste of dust in his mouth, but the sequence felt
incomplete, as if crucial moments had been erased. When he tried to piece it together, to understand
what had happened and why he could not move past it, rage would flood through him—a fury so intense
that it frightened him. He had come to avoid thinking about that day altogether, pushing the
memories away the moment they surfaced, which meant they never fully resolved. Instead, they lurked
at the edges of his consciousness, ready to ambush him when a door slammed or when an unexpected
noise pierced the quiet. His body would react before his mind could catch up, flooding him with the
certainty that danger was imminent, that he was back in that collapsing building, that he might lose
Ama again. This conviction—that threat was ever-present, that unpredictability meant danger, that
his body's alarms were reliable—drove him deeper into avoidance. He avoided situations that might
trigger memories, which meant he avoided the very unpredictability that, in his mind, had nearly
cost him everything. And as he avoided, as he overate, as he kept his mind perpetually occupied, the
triggers seemed to multiply. Ordinary sounds became threatening. His own emotional responses became
unpredictable to him. He had begun to wonder if he would ever feel safe again.

It was Ama who finally insisted he seek help. She had noticed his weight gain, his social
withdrawal, the way he would sometimes look at her as if he did not quite recognize her. She had
also noticed the way he flinched at her voice, the way he seemed to be disappearing into himself.
Three weeks ago, she had placed a therapist's business card on his nightstand and said, "I need my
husband back." That simple statement—the recognition that he was losing something precious, that his
strategies were costing him more than they were saving—had finally broken through his resistance.
Kofi sat in the intake session with his hands folded, uncertain whether talking about what happened
would make things better or infinitely worse, but aware that the alternative—this slow, suffocating
contraction of his life—was no longer bearable.
