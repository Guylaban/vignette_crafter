# Margot

Margot had worked as a midwife for thirty-two years. She had delivered over two thousand babies,
knew the particular weight of a newborn's head in her palms, understood the language of a mother's
body as it opened. The work had defined her—not just what she did, but who she was. She was the
woman you called at three in the morning. The one who stayed calm when things went wrong. The one
who knew.

On a Tuesday in March, she was restocking supplies in the obstetric unit at St. Catherine's when a
technician knocked over a container of glutaraldehyde during equipment sterilization. The chemical
splashed across the counter, and Margot, standing nearby, inhaled a concentrated plume. Within
seconds her lungs seized. She stumbled backward, gasping, her eyes streaming. A colleague grabbed
her arm and pulled her into the hallway, but the burning sensation had already traveled deep into
her chest, settling there like something alive. She spent six hours in the emergency department. The
doctors found no permanent damage, discharged her with reassurance and a prescription for albuterol.
But something had shifted.

For the first week, Margot told herself it was just anxiety—a normal response to a scare. By the
second week, she noticed that any smell of disinfectant, any sharp chemical odor in the hallway,
sent her into a spiral. Her heart would accelerate. Her breathing would tighten. She would have to
leave the room, step outside into the air, remind herself that she was safe. Then came the day a
laboring mother arrived with visible lacerations from a fall; when Margot saw the blood on the
woman's thigh, something in her fractured. The sight of it—the red—collided with the memory of the
chemical burn, and she felt the panic rise in her chest as if she were back in that moment, choking.
The violence of the accident, the blood, even the simple fact of feeling physically unwell when her
lungs tightened at a particular smell—all of it had become dangerous. Her mind had begun to weave
them together into a single threat.

After that incident, Margot started to avoid. She would call in sick when she knew there was a
trauma case scheduled. She began to dread the unpredictability of her shifts, the way any moment
could bring a trigger she could not control. And with each avoidance, something else happened: she
began to believe that her fear was justified, that she was fragile, that the woman who had once been
unshakeable was gone. She found herself thinking, in the quiet hours before sleep, that she was
weak—that a real midwife would have walked back into that unit the next day, would have faced the
smell without flinching. Instead, she had hidden. She had failed. Nothing felt meaningful anymore;
the work that had once been her identity now felt like a minefield she was tiptoeing through,
waiting for the next explosion.

Her partner, James, noticed the changes. He would suggest dinner plans or a weekend trip, and Margot
would agree to whatever he wanted, even when she dreaded it, because saying no felt dangerous. If
she refused, if she asserted any preference of her own, she worried he might leave her. She had
become someone who accommodated, who erased herself, because that felt safer than the alternative.
When her therapist, in those early sessions, tried to gently explore what had happened that day in
March, Margot would nod along but afterward felt an urgent need to push the thoughts away. She would
not let herself think about the conversation, the details, the way her body had responded. Thinking
about it meant feeling it again, and she could not afford that. She could not afford to plan for a
future either—not retirement, not travel, not anything beyond the next shift. The future felt like a
place where something terrible would happen, and she was not the person who could handle it anymore.

Now, at sixty, Margot sits in the intake appointment with her hands folded tightly in her lap. She
describes her symptoms with clinical precision, as if she were reporting on a patient rather than
herself. But her voice wavers when she speaks about the identity she has lost, the person she no
longer recognizes in the mirror. What terrifies her most is that she might never get back to work,
never be the midwife she was—and that without that role, she does not know who she is at all.
