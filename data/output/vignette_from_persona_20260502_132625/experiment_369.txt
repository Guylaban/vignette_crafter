# Michael's Vigil

Michael was forty-eight when his daughter Emma stopped breathing. She had been twenty-three, a
graduate student in environmental science, driving home from a research conference on a rain-slicked
highway in upstate New York. A truck crossed the median. The state police called at 11:47 p.m. on a
Tuesday in March. Michael and his wife Sarah sat in the hospital waiting room for six hours before a
doctor in a white coat told them there was nothing more to do. Emma was gone.

That was fourteen months ago. The shop—a hardware store on Main Street that Michael had owned for
nearly twenty years—had become a place where time moved differently now. He would stand behind the
counter in the morning, restocking bolts and paint cans, and feel as though he were moving through
water. Customers would come in asking for advice on repairs, and he would help them, his voice
steady, his hands functioning, while somewhere in his chest something remained suspended in that
hospital waiting room. What Michael could not tolerate was the moment when a customer would mention
their own loss. A woman came in one Tuesday—the same day of the week, he noticed—and mentioned that
her son had just graduated college. "You must be so proud," she said, attempting small talk. Michael
felt the words like a physical blow. He excused himself to the back room and did not emerge for
twenty minutes. After that, he began to leave the shop early on Tuesdays, telling Sarah he had
inventory to manage at home.

The memory of Emma's last phone call to him—made three hours before the accident, asking him to pick
up her favorite coffee when she got back—had become a film that would not stop playing. He could see
her face on the hospital bed with absolute clarity, every detail preserved in a way that felt not
like remembering but like being there again. The memory did not feel like something that had
happened and ended; it felt like it was still happening, still unfolding in the present moment,
which meant that Michael lived in a state of perpetual re-experiencing. He found that the only way
to interrupt the loop was to smoke. He had quit smoking fifteen years ago, but now he kept a pack in
the drawer beneath the register. When the film started—when he could feel Emma's presence in the
room as though she were there—he would step outside, light a cigarette, and focus on the burn in his
lungs. The sensation was something he could control, something that felt more real than the
intrusive memory.

But the smoking itself became a trigger. After a few weeks of this habit, Michael noticed that the
act of lighting a cigarette had begun to activate a cascade of fear. His body had learned that
smoking meant the memory was coming, and the anticipation of the memory—the dread of being pulled
back into that hospital room—began to feel like a threat in itself. His startle response had grown
acute. Ordinary sounds—the beep of a truck backing up, a car door slamming, even the bell above the
shop door—would jolt him as though danger were imminent. A customer once dropped a box of nails on
the floor, and Michael's entire body went rigid; for a moment, his nervous system interpreted the
noise as catastrophe. These startle reactions fed his sense that the world was unsafe, that loss
could strike at any moment, and this escalating sense of threat drove him to smoke more, to manage
the intrusive memories more aggressively, which only deepened the cycle.

He began to avoid anything that might trigger thoughts of loss. When a coworker mentioned that her
mother had passed, Michael found reasons to be elsewhere. He stopped attending community events. He
did not open the condolence cards that still arrived. When Sarah suggested they visit Emma's
grave—something he had managed only once, on the day of the funeral—he felt panic rise in his
throat. The thought of standing in a cemetery, of seeing her name on a stone, seemed unbearable. He
told Sarah he was not ready. Weeks passed. The avoidance felt like self-protection, but it had
isolated him. Sarah had stopped asking him to do things. His closest friend had stopped calling
after Michael had declined too many invitations. The shop was suffering; he was closing early,
missing inventory days, and his reputation for reliability had begun to crack.

Michael came to therapy because Sarah had insisted, and because he could no longer tell the
difference between the past and the present. He sat in the office with the weight of fourteen months
pressing down on him, and he said simply: "I cannot stop seeing her. And I cannot stop running from
it."
