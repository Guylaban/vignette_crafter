# Rosa's Story

Rosa was fifty-nine when she came to the clinic, and the tremor in her hands had become something
she could no longer hide. Twelve years earlier, she had been taken. The details emerged slowly in
that first session, each word seeming to cost her something. She had been driving home from work—a
project manager for a pharmaceutical supply company, a job she had held with quiet competence for
fifteen years—when a man forced himself into her car at a red light. He had driven her to an
abandoned warehouse on the outskirts of the city, locked her in a basement room with no windows, and
kept her there for four days. The room was concrete and airless. The door was metal. He had bound
her wrists and kept her in near-total darkness, returning sporadically with food she could not eat,
speaking in low tones about things she still could not quite name. When police finally found her—a
neighbor's tip, a lucky break—she had been so dehydrated that she spent three days in the hospital.

The first year after her release, Rosa had tried to believe it was behind her. She returned to work,
moved through the motions of recovery, told herself and everyone around her that she was fine. But
over the following months, something had shifted in how she experienced the world. Any sense of
being enclosed—the conference room with its frosted glass doors, even the elevator in her office
building—sent her heart racing. When colleagues would close the door for a meeting, Rosa felt the
walls pressing inward, her breath becoming shallow. She had come to believe that safety was an
illusion, that you could never truly know who might harm you, that the world was fundamentally
unsafe despite all evidence to the contrary. This certainty had metastasized into everything. She
began to feel on guard even inside her own apartment, checking locks obsessively, sleeping with
lights on, convinced that danger was imminent even in the dead of night when she was alone and the
doors were bolted.

The hypervigilance had become her constant companion. Every unexpected sound triggered a cascade of
fear—a knock on a neighbor's door, footsteps in the hallway, the hum of an unfamiliar car engine.
Her body reacted as if she were still in that basement, as if the threat were ongoing and immediate.
She often found herself braced for harm that never came, her nervous system unable to distinguish
between memory and present danger. These false alarms exhausted her. She could not relax, could not
let her shoulders drop, could not settle into the couch with a book without her mind cataloging
exits and potential weapons. And it was the feeling of entrapment that seemed to trigger
everything—the sensation of having no options, of being cornered, even metaphorically. When her boss
had asked her to lead a new product launch with a compressed timeline, Rosa had felt the same panic
she felt in locked rooms: the walls closing in, no way out, no choice but to endure.

To manage the unbearable weight of these thoughts, Rosa had developed a habit of rewriting the past.
She would tell herself that the four days in the basement had not been as bad as she remembered,
that perhaps she had exaggerated the severity in her mind, that maybe it was not as traumatic as she
had made it out to be. This magical thinking provided temporary relief—a way to make the intolerable
seem manageable. But it never lasted. She would then spiral into obsessive analysis, going over and
over the details of those four days, trying to find some logic, some explanation that would make
sense of what had happened. She dwelled on what she might have done differently, how she might have
prevented it, what she could have said or done to change the outcome. This self-blame rumination was
relentless. She had come to believe that she was somehow responsible, that she had been careless or
foolish, and this belief only deepened her sense that she could not be trusted to keep herself safe.

These cycles of rumination and magical thinking, far from bringing her peace, only amplified her
sense of threat. Each time she told herself the event was not that bad, the contradiction between
that reassurance and her body's visceral memory created a kind of cognitive dissonance that left her
more anxious, more convinced that she was losing her grip on reality. The overanalysis kept the
trauma alive in her mind, each detail examined and re-examined until the past felt as present as the
moment she was living in. This constant mental activity fed her hypervigilance, her inability to
relax, her sense that danger was lurking just beyond her perception. At work, she had begun to
withdraw from meetings that required her to be in enclosed spaces, and her boss had gently suggested
that she might need to step back from her role. The job she had loved—the one that had given her
identity and purpose—was slipping away, and with it, another piece of who she had been. Rosa used to
be a happy person, she thought. She used to laugh. Now she felt dead inside, numb to everything
except fear. The woman she had been seemed to belong to someone else entirely. It was this profound
loss of self, this deadening of joy, that had finally brought her to seek help. She was terrified
that this was permanent, that she would never feel anything but this hollow dread again.
