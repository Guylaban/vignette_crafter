# Clinical Vignette: Mariana

Mariana was nineteen when she first walked into the dispatch office at the regional rail yard, still
carrying the weight of a childhood she had learned to hide behind efficiency and motion. She had
been seven years old when her father came home drunk that April evening, when the shouting started
in the kitchen and her mother's voice rose higher and higher until it cracked. Mariana had hidden in
the hallway closet, pressed between winter coats that smelled of mothballs and her mother's perfume,
listening to the crash of dishes, the wet sound of impact, the animal noises her father made—sounds
she could never quite place in the right order afterward, as though her memory had shuffled them
like cards. She remembered the taste of copper in her own mouth from where she had bitten the inside
of her cheek to keep from crying out. Her older brother had tried to intervene; she had watched from
the kitchen doorway as her father shoved him against the wall. In that moment, frozen and small,
Mariana had understood with the clarity only a child possesses that she was powerless—that there was
nothing she could do to stop what was happening, nothing that would make her father's fists unclench
or her mother's sobs cease.

Now, at nineteen, Mariana worked as a train conductor, a job that demanded alertness and routine.
She had chosen it deliberately, though she did not admit this to herself. The work kept her moving,
kept her mind occupied with schedules and safety protocols and the mechanical rhythm of the rails.
But the job had also placed her in an environment where darkness was unavoidable. On overnight runs,
the cab of the locomotive became a small, dimly lit space where she sat alone with her thoughts for
hours. The darkness triggered something in her—a tightening in her chest, a sense of exposure
despite being enclosed. When darkness fell, her body would remember that closet, that helplessness,
and the memory would return not as something from the past but as something happening in the present
moment. The copper taste would bloom on her tongue again. The sounds would replay, jumbled and
disorienting, as if her mind could not quite file them away.

Loud noises on the job—the shriek of the air brakes, the coupling of cars, the blast of the horn at
crossings—would jolt her into a state of hypervigilance that lasted long after the sound had faded.
Each unexpected noise triggered a cascade: her heart would race, and with it came the memory, vivid
and immediate, of her father's voice, her mother's screams, the sounds she still could not order
into a coherent sequence. The memory felt present, not past. And with the memory came the crushing
conviction that she had failed—that she should have protected her mother, should have done
something, anything, instead of hiding. The event had happened because she was weak, because she had
been too small and too afraid to act. This certainty had calcified inside her over the years.

To manage the fear that accumulated as the sun set and her shift continued, Mariana had developed a
system of vigilance that consumed her off-duty hours. She checked the locks on her apartment door
obsessively, sometimes returning to verify them three or four times after she had already locked up
for the night. She could not feel safe in darkness; night was when bad things happened, when her
father had come home and everything had shattered. The persistent sense of threat—the feeling that
danger was always waiting, always about to announce itself through some unexpected sound—kept her in
a state of exhaustion that she tried to counter by staying busy. She filled her schedule with extra
shifts, with cleaning projects, with anything that would occupy her hands and mind. But the busyness
itself had become another way the memories returned: when she was tired, when her vigilance finally
flagged, the memory would flood back with even more intensity, and she would taste copper again,
hear the sounds out of sequence, feel the helplessness as if she were still seven years old in that
closet.

The counterfactual thoughts had become a habit, a kind of painful self-soothing that only deepened
her self-blame. If she had been braver, if she had screamed for help, if she had not hidden—if, if,
if. These thoughts arose whenever she was alone with the memory, and they reinforced her conviction
that she was fundamentally unable to protect anyone, that she had failed when it mattered most. The
threat she felt never quite receded; it only shifted shape. And the more threatened she felt, the
more she stayed busy, the more she checked the locks, the more she restricted her eating as a way of
exerting control over something in her body when everything else felt chaotic. The restriction left
her depleted, which made her more vulnerable to the memory, which triggered more threat, which sent
her back to busyness and checking and the taste of copper that would not fade.

Mariana had come to therapy because her supervisor had noticed her missing shifts, her trembling
hands during daylight hours, the way she had become unreliable in a job that demanded presence and
focus. She sat in the intake session with her shoulders drawn up toward her ears, her fingers
picking at the seam of her jeans, and said quietly that she did not know if she could keep doing
this—the work, the checking, the staying awake at night, the constant sense that something terrible
was about to happen. What frightened her most was that she might always feel this way, that the
memory would never become something she could put away, and that she would never be able to forgive
herself for the child she had been.
