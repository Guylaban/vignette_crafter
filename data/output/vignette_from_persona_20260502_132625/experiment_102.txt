# Priya

Priya was twenty-two when the accident happened. She had been driving her best friend Kavya home
from a late dinner, both of them tired but laughing about something one of their coworkers had said.
It was a Tuesday in November, and Priya had been checking her phone at a red light—just for a
second, she told herself afterward, just to silence a notification—when the light changed and she
accelerated. She didn't see the cyclist until she heard the impact, a sound she would later describe
as hollow and wrong. Kavya screamed. Priya braked hard, her hands shaking so violently she could
barely grip the wheel. When she got out of the car, she found a young man in his mid-twenties lying
on the pavement, his leg bent at an angle that made her stomach turn. His name was Aditya. He had a
fractured femur, a lacerated spleen, and spent three weeks in the hospital. Priya learned these
details from the police report and from Kavya, who visited him once and came back pale and silent.
Priya never did.

In the months after, Priya became someone she didn't recognize. She started a new job as a UX
designer at a tech firm in downtown Seattle, a position she had been excited about before the
accident. But within weeks of beginning, she found herself trapped in a suffocating cycle. The
office itself—the open floor plan, the casual Friday lunches, even the sound of colleagues laughing
by the coffee machine—had become a minefield. Her team lead, Rohan, bore a passing resemblance to
Aditya: the same dark eyes, the same build. And every time Rohan approached her desk to discuss a
project, Priya's chest would tighten. She would see Aditya's face superimposed over Rohan's, and her
mind would spiral into a familiar groove: *If I had just put my phone down one second earlier. If I
had left dinner five minutes sooner. If I had taken a different route.* She would sit at her desk,
ostensibly working on wireframes, but actually replaying the moment obsessively, searching for the
exact decision point where she could have prevented everything. The rumination was exhausting and
pointless, but she couldn't stop. It felt like penance, like if she could just find the precise
sequence of choices that would have saved Aditya from harm, she might finally be able to breathe
again.

The harder she tried to suppress the intrusive memories—to push them down, to not think about that
moment—the more vividly they surfaced. And the more they surfaced, the more she found herself
avoiding the very situations that triggered them. Within two months, she had stopped driving
entirely. She would take the bus to work, even though it added forty minutes to her commute, because
the thought of sitting behind a wheel made her feel like she was about to kill someone again. Her
husband, Nikhil, whom she had married just eight months before the accident, tried to be patient. He
drove her to work, picked her up, adjusted his schedule around her needs. But she could see the
worry in his face deepening, and the guilt of that—of burdening him, of being broken—only fed the
rumination. When he gently suggested she might need help, she snapped at him, unable to articulate
that the thought of talking about what happened felt like admitting she was a dangerous person who
didn't deserve forgiveness.

At work, she began requesting to work from home on days when Rohan was scheduled to be in the
office. Her manager asked if everything was okay; Priya said she was more productive at home, which
was technically true, though it was also a lie. The avoidance gave her temporary relief—no trigger,
no spiral—but it also meant she was missing team meetings, falling behind on collaborative projects,
and becoming increasingly isolated. Her performance reviews, which had been glowing in her first
month, began to slip. She knew that her job was at risk, and she knew why, but she couldn't seem to
stop the pattern. Each time she avoided the office, she felt safer for a few hours. Each time she
felt safer, the rumination would quieten just enough that she could almost believe she might survive
this. But then Sunday night would come, and the dread of Monday would return, and the cycle would
tighten again.

It was Nikhil who finally insisted she see someone. He had found her one evening sitting in their
car in the garage, keys in her hand, unable to turn the ignition. She was twenty-three now, and she
had come to believe that she was fundamentally unsafe, that her presence in the world was a threat
to others. What frightened her most was that he might come to believe it too.
