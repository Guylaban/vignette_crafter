# Carlos

Carlos had been in Aleppo for eight months when the hospital took a direct hit. He was thirty-seven
then, lean and purposeful, moving between wards with the kind of calm that only comes from believing
you are doing something that matters. The explosion came without warning—not the distant percussion
of artillery he had learned to distinguish from smaller-caliber fire, but a roar that seemed to
collapse the building inward. He remembered the smell of concrete dust mixed with something else,
something organic and wrong. Three colleagues died in the collapse. He pulled one of them out
himself, already knowing. The sound of that blast—the specific acoustic signature of high explosive
meeting structure—had etched itself into his nervous system with a clarity that nothing else could
touch.

Now, seventeen years later, Carlos owned a successful import business in Miami, a company built on
discipline and detail. He was married to Rosa for thirty-one years, had two adult children, a house
in Coral Gables. On the surface, he had done what people do after trauma: he had moved forward,
built a life, become someone else. But the person he had become was not entirely the person he
intended.

The first trigger was the most obvious. A car backfired near his office six months ago, and Carlos
found himself on the floor behind his desk, heart hammering, unable to distinguish the sound from
gunfire. His staff had stared at him. He told them it was a dizzy spell. Since then, he avoided
driving on certain routes, took side streets, sometimes sat in his car in the parking garage for ten
minutes before entering the building, listening to the ambient noise, bracing himself. Fireworks
season was something he simply did not acknowledge; he would invent reasons to stay indoors. But
worse than the gunshot sounds were the moments when someone stood too close to him—a client leaning
in to shake his hand, Rosa touching his shoulder from behind—and something in his body would tense,
reading proximity as threat. He had come to believe that closeness was dangerous, that people who
got near him were putting themselves at risk, though he could not articulate why.

The confusion around positive emotion had crept up more insidiously. Six weeks ago, at his
daughter's wedding, he had felt a moment of genuine joy watching her dance, and it had terrified
him. What right did he have to feel joy when his colleagues were still dead? The thought had
spiraled: if he allowed himself to be happy, he was betraying them, forgetting them, diminishing
what had happened. So when Rosa laughed at something on television now, or when his son called with
good news, Carlos felt a strange resistance, a need to dampen it. He had started finding fault with
small things—Rosa's tone, the timing of a conversation—anything to create a little distance from the
brightness of the moment.

The maladaptive strategies had emerged gradually, but they had become the architecture of his daily
life. When a trigger struck—a loud noise, someone standing close, a moment of unexpected
lightness—Carlos would find himself spiraling into rumination about Aleppo. Why had he been spared
when others were not? What was the point of surviving if survival meant living like this? He would
go over and over the moment of the blast, the weight of his colleague's body, the specific
configuration of the rubble, as though repetition might finally yield an answer that would make
sense of it all. The rumination was exhausting, but it felt necessary, like a penance he owed.
Alongside it, he had begun carrying a small handgun in his car and, increasingly, in his jacket. He
told himself it was for protection, but protection from what, exactly, he did not fully examine. The
gun made him feel less helpless, less caught off guard. It was a way of saying: I will not be
surprised again.

The third strategy was perhaps the most damaging to his marriage and business relationships. When
the anxiety would build—triggered by a sound, by Rosa's attempt at affection, by the simple fact of
feeling safe and therefore guilty—Carlos would pick fights. He would find something to argue about:
a business decision, the way Rosa had spoken to him, a perceived slight from an employee. He would
escalate small disagreements into drama, create conflict almost deliberately. In those moments, he
was not ruminating about Aleppo; he was not sitting with the terror of proximity or the guilt of
joy. He was doing something, asserting control, feeling the surge of adrenaline that came from
confrontation. It distracted him. It was a relief, in a perverse way, until it was over and he saw
the hurt in Rosa's eyes, or the fear in his employee's face, and the shame would come crashing back.

What had finally brought him to therapy was a moment three weeks ago when he had nearly struck his
wife. They had been in the kitchen, Rosa had touched his arm to ask him something, and he had spun
around with his fist raised before his conscious mind caught up. She had frozen. He had frozen. And
in that silence, Carlos had understood that the strategies keeping him alive in his own mind were
destroying everything he had built. He was terrified of what he might become if he continued this
way, but equally terrified of stopping—of what would be left if he let the rumination quiet, if he
gave up the gun, if he allowed himself to feel close to someone again without reading it as danger.
That fear, and the love he still felt for Rosa beneath all of it, had finally pushed him to seek
help.
