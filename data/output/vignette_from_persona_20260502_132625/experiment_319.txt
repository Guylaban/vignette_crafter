# Aroha's Return

Aroha Ngata had been back in the States for eight months when the taste of dust in her mouth first
sent her stumbling into the bathroom at the parole office. It was a Tuesday in March, unremarkable
in every way except that her colleague Marcus had microwaved something with cumin for lunch, and the
spice had drifted across the open-plan space where she sat reviewing case files. The taste hit her
throat—not the cumin itself, but something deeper, something that belonged to another place
entirely. For a moment she couldn't breathe. She gripped the sink and waited for the sensation to
pass, aware that she had perhaps thirty seconds before someone would notice her absence from her
desk.

Aroha had spent two years as a relief worker in a conflict zone in the Middle East, coordinating
medical supplies and documentation for a nongovernmental organization. The work had felt purposeful
in a way her life in California had not. She had been good at it—organized, compassionate, able to
compartmentalize the suffering around her into manageable tasks. That changed on a Friday in
November, in the aftermath of an airstrike on a neighborhood clinic where her team had been storing
supplies. She had been in the supply depot, two blocks away, when the sound came. What followed was
a blur of movement toward the clinic, the smell of burning concrete and flesh, and then—the taste.
Dust and something else, something organic and metallic, coating her mouth and throat as she knelt
beside a child whose injuries she could not adequately describe, even now, even to herself. The
child's mother had been there too, and then she hadn't been. The details fragmented in her memory
like a film reel with missing frames.

When Aroha tried later to reconstruct that day—in the safe house, during her medical evacuation, in
the first weeks after returning home—she found herself unable to access certain parts of it. There
were stretches of time she simply could not account for. She remembered arriving at the clinic. She
remembered the taste. She remembered sitting in a vehicle afterward, but the in-between, the actual
moments with the child, remained locked away. When she pushed herself to recall those details,
concentrating hard, something in her mind would flinch and pull away, as though the memory itself
were too dangerous to hold. She had learned not to force it.

The taste became a portal to that unreachable space. Over the following months, Aroha began to
notice other triggers embedded in her daily life. When a victim's family member came to the parole
office—a woman whose son had been incarcerated for assault—and expressed her anger in a controlled,
measured voice, Aroha's chest would tighten with the same helplessness she had felt in the clinic.
The woman wasn't angry at Aroha, but the presence of that anger in the room, even calmly stated,
would leave her shaken for hours. And when she encountered reminders of the victims she had worked
with—a news article about displaced families, a child's drawing on a colleague's desk—she would feel
something close to vertigo, a sense that the ground beneath her was unstable.

Her ex-husband had left during her second year abroad, citing her emotional absence even when she
was physically present. She had not fought the divorce. By the time she returned home, she had
already begun the work of not remembering, of keeping the fragmented pieces of that day separate
from her functioning self. She went back to work as a parole officer because it was familiar and
because staying still felt dangerous. But the triggers were everywhere, and each one seemed to pull
her toward something she couldn't quite access, something her mind had decided she was not ready to
know. She had come to believe that some memories were meant to stay buried, that the act of trying
to retrieve them was itself a kind of violence. Yet the taste persisted, arriving without warning,
and with it came the certainty that she was missing something crucial—something that might explain
why she had survived when others had not.
