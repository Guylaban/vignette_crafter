# Clinical Vignette: Katarina

Katarina was thirty-one when the call came through on a Tuesday evening in March. She had been a
paramedic for eight years, long enough that the radio chatter and the rush of sirens had become the
rhythm of her body. The accident report was straightforward: a two-vehicle collision on the
interstate, one patient unresponsive, possible spinal injury. She arrived with her partner Marcus,
and what followed was a decision made in the space of perhaps ten seconds—a choice about patient
positioning that she had made a hundred times before, each time textbook, each time correct. Except
this time the patient's spinal cord was compromised in a way the initial assessment had not
revealed. The movement she authorized caused catastrophic injury. The patient survived but was left
paralyzed from the waist down. Katarina learned this not in the hospital but from a hospital lawyer,
months later, when the lawsuit was already underway.

Two years have passed since that evening. Katarina is now thirty-three, married to David for five
years, and she has not returned to active paramedic work. She took a desk position in
dispatch—answering phones, routing calls, staying as far from the field as her certification
allowed—but even that has become difficult. What frightened her most was the certainty that she had
destroyed someone's life through her own hands, that she was fundamentally dangerous, that her
judgment could not be trusted. This belief had calcified into something immovable. She had come to
see herself as someone who hurt people, and the evidence felt overwhelming.

The memories had started immediately but became more intrusive over the following months. Fragments
would arrive without warning—the patient's face, the sound of her own voice giving the command to
move him, the moment she realized what had happened—each one a snapshot that blazed into her mind
and then vanished, leaving her disoriented and gasping. When she tried deliberately to recall the
event, to make sense of it in her own mind, the terror returned intact, as vivid as the moment
itself. Her hands would shake. Her breathing would quicken. The memory brought back not
understanding or closure but the same acute fear she had felt in the ambulance bay. Because of these
sudden, terrifying intrusions, Katarina had begun to drink. She would pour wine after work, telling
herself it was to help her sleep, but the real purpose was simpler: alcohol quieted the fragments.
It made the snapshots less sharp, the terror less raw. She also took lorazepam, prescribed by her
physician for anxiety, though she had begun using it more liberally than directed—not to manage
anxiety but to preempt the memories, to drink and medicate before the fragments could arrive. These
strategies worked, at least for a few hours. But they also created a new problem: when she was not
drinking or medicated, she felt more vulnerable to the memories, not less. The avoidance had become
its own kind of trap.

The threat vigilance had crept in gradually, almost unnoticed. Katarina found herself hyperalert at
work, scanning every interaction for signs that she might make another mistake, that someone might
discover she was unfit. She checked and rechecked dispatch protocols, even though she no longer
handled emergencies. She could not concentrate on routine tasks because part of her attention was
always elsewhere, braced for the next intrusion or the next accusation. At night, her vigilance
intensified. She felt safer during the day, when she could see and control her environment, but as
darkness came, the safety dissolved. She would lie awake beside David, listening to the house
settle, feeling exposed and watched. The darkness seemed to hold the possibility of catastrophe.
This night vigilance fed the drinking: a glass of wine, then another, to push past the wakefulness
and into sleep. But the alcohol itself disrupted her sleep architecture, and she would wake at three
or four in the morning, her heart racing, fragments of the memory already playing in her mind. The
medication would follow. And then the next day, still foggy and depleted, she would be more
vulnerable to the memories, more desperate to avoid them, more likely to reach for a drink before
work even began.

What had started as a way to manage the terror had become a cycle that deepened both the memories
and the threat she felt. Katarina had not told David the full extent of her drinking. She had not
told her supervisor. She had told no one about the lawsuit settlement or the paramedic's guilt forum
she had attended once and then abandoned because hearing others' stories had triggered her own
fragments so violently that she had left halfway through. Isolation had seemed safer than
connection. But the isolation had also meant that the memories and the threat lived entirely inside
her, unchallenged and uncorrected, growing heavier with each cycle of avoidance. Two weeks ago,
after a particularly severe night—waking at two in the morning with fragments so vivid she could not
distinguish them from reality, reaching for the wine bottle before she had fully opened her
eyes—Katarina had called her employee assistance program. She was tired of the loop. She was afraid
of what she was becoming. And she was terrified that the person she had hurt would somehow know that
she was trying to forget, that her avoidance was a betrayal of his suffering. She wanted help. She
was not sure she deserved it.
