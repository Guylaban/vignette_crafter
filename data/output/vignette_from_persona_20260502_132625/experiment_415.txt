# Clinical Vignette: Marcus

Marcus Tanielu, fifty-four, had been flying combat aircraft for the Royal New Zealand Air Force for
thirty-two years. He was known as steady—the kind of pilot other pilots trusted in tight formations,
the man who kept his head when systems failed. He had logged over four thousand flight hours. He had
never crashed.

On a training sortie in March, his F/A-18F's right engine suffered a catastrophic compressor blade
failure at eight thousand feet. The aircraft yawed violently. Marcus felt the stick go heavy, felt
the airframe shudder in a way that meant the hydraulic systems were compromised. He had perhaps
forty seconds to eject before the aircraft would become unflyable. He ejected at six thousand feet,
parachuted into scrubland outside Christchurch, and broke both ankles on landing.

The injuries healed. The aircraft was written off. The investigation cleared him of any fault. By
all objective measures, Marcus had done exactly what he was trained to do and had survived. But
something had fractured in him that had nothing to do with bone.

Within weeks, Marcus became convinced that he could no longer trust his own judgment. He had not
seen the engine failure coming—had not sensed it, had not anticipated it, had not prevented it. If
his instruments and his training had failed him at thirty thousand feet, then what part of him could
be trusted at all? He had come to believe that the man he had been—the pilot who trusted his
instincts, who made split-second decisions with confidence—was gone. In his place was someone
compromised, diminished, fundamentally altered. He was certain now that he would never feel the
uncomplicated satisfaction he once took in flying, or in anything else. The emotions that had
carried him through three decades—purpose, competence, the quiet pride of mastery—felt permanently
out of reach, as though they had been surgically removed.

This conviction about his permanent damage fed into a pervasive sense of threat that now colored
every moment of his waking life. Because Marcus no longer believed in his own judgment, he could not
trust that he was safe anywhere. At home, sitting in the living room with his wife Aroha, he found
himself mentally mapping the exits—the front door, the back door, the windows he could open. He
would position himself with a clear line of sight to the hallway. His body remained in a state of
constant readiness, muscles tense, breathing shallow. Even in his own kitchen, preparing breakfast,
he was acutely aware of where the exits were, calculating angles and distances. He felt on guard in
his own home, unable to relax, as though threat could materialize from any direction and he alone
would need to see it coming.

The hypervigilance had become exhausting. Marcus found himself unable to sit through a meal without
scanning the room. He could not watch television without tracking movement in his peripheral vision.
When Aroha spoke to him, he sometimes did not hear her words because his attention was elsewhere,
deployed outward, searching. His concentration at work suffered. He was grounded from flying pending
a psychological evaluation, but even sitting in the squadron office, he felt exposed, vulnerable,
certain that his compromised mind would fail him again in some unpredictable way.

What frightened him most was that this state felt permanent. He had tried to return to normal
routines, but the conviction that he had been fundamentally damaged would not release him. Every
moment of hypervigilance, every time he mapped the exits of a room, every time he felt that familiar
flatness where emotion should be—all of it confirmed his belief that the change was irreversible. He
had come to his first appointment with a psychologist because Aroha had insisted, but his hope was
fragile. He wanted to believe that the man he had been might return, but increasingly, he was
certain he never would.
