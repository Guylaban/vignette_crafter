# Marcus

Marcus was twenty-six when three men cornered him outside the marina where he kept his sailboat. It
was a Tuesday evening in late October, the light already gone from the sky, and he'd stayed late to
secure the lines before a storm system moved through. They came from behind the maintenance shed—he
heard footsteps, turned, and then there was the smell of them, the sound of his own breathing, a
hand closing around his wrist. One of them had a knife. He remembered the blade catching the sodium
light from the parking lot, remembered the cold shock of it against his ribs, the way his body had
simply obeyed—wallet, phone, watch, all of it falling into their hands while his mind seemed to
separate from what was happening, as if he were watching from somewhere outside himself. They left
him there, bleeding slightly, shaking. A security guard found him twenty minutes later.

The physical injuries healed within weeks. The Coast Guard medical team cleared him for duty. But
something had fractured in how his mind held the experience, and in the year and a half since,
Marcus had come to understand that the event had not stored itself as a coherent narrative but
rather as fragments that refused to cohere. He could access pieces of it—the smell, the pressure of
the hand, the gleam of the blade—but when he tried to assemble them into a sequence, into a story he
could tell, the pieces would not fit. They seemed to exist in isolation, each carrying its own
charge of fear, none of them connecting to form a whole that his mind could process and release. The
most troubling part was that he could not seem to put what had happened into words. When his
girlfriend Sarah asked him to talk about it, he would open his mouth and find himself unable to
translate the fragments into language. The words simply would not come. He knew, abstractly, that
he'd been robbed, that he'd been threatened, that he'd been afraid—but when he tried to articulate
the actual experience, to shape it into sentences, the gap between the internal chaos and language
felt unbridgeable.

What unsettled him most was the peculiar quality of certain memories: sometimes he could recall
elements of the attack with almost photographic clarity—the texture of the asphalt where he'd stood,
the exact timbre of one man's voice—yet feel nothing. No fear, no anger, no sense that it had
happened to him. It was as though he were recounting something that had occurred to a stranger,
something he'd read about or heard secondhand. Other times, the opposite would occur: a surge of
dread would rise in him without any clear memory attached to it, a pure sensation of threat that had
no narrative anchor. Sarah had noticed the change in him. He'd become quieter, more withdrawn. At
the Coast Guard station, his commanding officer had remarked that Marcus seemed distant, less
engaged in the camaraderie that had once defined his presence there. He still performed his duties
competently, but the warmth had gone out of it.

Now, at twenty-nine, Marcus found himself sitting in a therapist's office for the first time, trying
to explain why he'd finally come in. He'd hoped that time would knit the fragments together, that
his mind would eventually find a way to organize what had happened into something coherent and
manageable. It hadn't. Instead, the disconnected pieces seemed to have taken on a life of their own,
surfacing at odd moments, refusing integration, leaving him caught between the terror of the
fragments and the eerie numbness of detached recall. What he wanted most was to be able to tell the
story—to himself, to Sarah, to anyone—as a coherent whole. What frightened him was the possibility
that his mind would never allow that to happen, that he would remain forever trapped in these
scattered, wordless pieces of an event he could not seem to fully claim as his own.
