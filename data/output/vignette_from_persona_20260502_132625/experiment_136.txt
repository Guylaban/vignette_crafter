# Clinical Vignette: Margaret

Margaret was sixty-eight years old when a man forced his way into her home one afternoon in March.
She had been alone, sorting through case files at her kitchen table—the same table where she had
worked as a forensic analyst for thirty-seven years, examining evidence, building narratives from
blood spatter and fiber analysis. The intruder came through the side door. She remembers his hands
first, the way they gripped her wrists, how his fingers pressed into the soft skin of her inner arms
as he threw her against the counter. She remembers the smell of his sweat, the sound of her own
breathing becoming something she could not control. He did not use a weapon beyond his body and his
voice. When it was over—when he left, when she called the police—Margaret felt hollowed out, as if
something essential had been removed and replaced with static.

The assault happened three years ago. She is seventy-one now, and she has not returned to work.

In the months that followed, Margaret noticed that certain moments would detonate without warning.
Conversations about plans—her daughter's wedding next spring, a trip her husband wanted to
take—would trigger a sudden, suffocating sense of dread. When people spoke about the future, she
felt as though she were being asked to imagine something impossible, as if the future itself had
become a threat. Talking about the event itself was worse. Her therapist had gently suggested that
naming what happened might help her process it, but when Margaret tried, when she began to
articulate the sequence of what he did, what she felt, the rage that erupted was so violent and so
complete that it frightened her. Sometimes she could describe the facts—the date, the time, the
police report number—with a strange, clinical detachment, as if the assault had happened to someone
else entirely, and she was simply reviewing the case file. Other times, she would push the memory
away before it could fully form, her mind slamming a door on the image before the full weight of it
could land. The uncertainty of which version would arrive—the rage, the numbness, the
fragmentation—kept her vigilant and exhausted.

What frightened her most was the conviction that if she allowed herself to truly think about what
happened, she would shatter completely. She had come to believe that she was inadequate, that a
stronger woman would have fought back differently, that she had somehow failed in the moment. She
was no longer the woman she had been before—capable, steady, happy in her work and her marriage.
That person seemed to belong to someone else's life now. To ward off the unbearable weight of these
thoughts, Margaret began to restrict what she ate, finding that hunger provided a kind of clarity, a
sense of control. She stopped exercising, the thought of being in a public gym or even alone on a
running trail triggering a cascade of hypervigilance that left her exhausted. At home, she
repositioned every chair so that her back faced the wall, allowing her to see the entire room at
once. Her husband had learned not to approach her from behind.

These strategies—the avoidance of food, the rigid seating arrangements, the withdrawal from physical
activity—had formed a kind of cage around her thinking. The less she ate, the more brittle her mood
became, and the more convinced she was of her own inadequacy. The more she sat in that chair, back
to the wall, the more she found herself hypervigilant to triggers: a particular shade of fabric on a
neighbor's shirt, the sound of heavy footsteps in the hallway, even the smell of sweat during the
rare moments she ventured into her own kitchen. Each trigger pulled her back into the memory, which
then confirmed her belief that she could not handle thinking about it, which then intensified her
need to control her environment through avoidance and restriction. The numbness that sometimes
arrived during recall—that strange, dissociative distance—paradoxically reinforced her sense that
she was broken, that normal feeling had been stolen from her and replaced with this hollow
performance of survival.

Her husband had finally insisted she see someone. Margaret sat in the waiting room of her first
appointment, back against the wall, and admitted that she did not know if she could be helped. What
she wanted most was to stop being afraid of her own mind. What she feared most was that she already
was.
