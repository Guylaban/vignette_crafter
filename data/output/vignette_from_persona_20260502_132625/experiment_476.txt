# Kwame's Story

Kwame was thirty-four when he first came to the clinic, referred by his physician after a panic
attack at work left him unable to complete his shift. He had been a parole officer for eight years—a
job that demanded presence, authority, and composure—but the man who sat in the intake chair was
visibly depleted, his shoulders hunched as though bearing an invisible weight. Two years earlier,
his partner of five years had subjected him to a sustained assault that left him with three broken
ribs and a fractured wrist. The attack had come without warning during what Kwame had thought was a
quiet evening at home. His partner had accused him of infidelity, and when Kwame had tried to leave
the room, the man had grabbed him by the throat, thrown him against the kitchen counter, and struck
him repeatedly. The hospital smell—that sharp, chemical antiseptic—had been the first thing Kwame
registered when paramedics arrived. He had pressed his face into the stretcher to escape it, but the
odor had lodged itself somewhere deep in his nervous system, becoming a permanent marker of that
night's terror.

Since then, Kwame's world had contracted in ways he could barely articulate. The hospital smell
alone—encountered in the clinic waiting room, in a colleague's hand sanitizer, even in the bathroom
of the parole office—triggered an immediate cascade of threat detection. His body would stiffen, his
breathing would shallow, and suddenly he was scanning the room for exits and assessing which men
nearby might pose a danger. The smell carried with it not just memory but a full sensory
reincarnation: he could taste the blood in his mouth as it had tasted that night, metallic and
terrifying. The memory did not feel like something past. It felt like it was happening again, right
then, in real time. Being looked at in a way he perceived as sexual—a client's lingering gaze, a
stranger's appraisal—triggered the same response. He had come to believe that being desired meant
being vulnerable, that attraction was a precursor to violence. And the most paralyzing trigger of
all was being alone with other men. At work, Kwame had begun to dread the one-on-one parole check-
ins that formed the core of his job. Each meeting with a male parolee sent his nervous system into
overdrive, even though his rational mind knew these men posed no threat. His body remained on
constant alert, muscles coiled, ready to react to danger that his hypervigilant scanning insisted
might materialize at any moment.

The memory's vividness—the way it felt present and happening rather than safely in the past—had
gradually convinced Kwame that his life was fundamentally destroyed. How could he continue in a job
that required him to sit alone with men when his own body betrayed him with panic? How could he
maintain his relationship when he had begun to recoil from his new partner's touch, certain that
intimacy would inevitably lead to harm? What had happened to him was profoundly unfair, and nothing
had been done about it; his ex-partner had never faced consequences, had moved on, while Kwame
remained trapped in the moment of violence. These thoughts—that his life was no longer worth living,
that he had been irreparably damaged—had taken root and grown stronger with each passing week. They
fed into a pattern of avoidance that had become increasingly rigid. He pushed away any moment of
happiness or connection, convinced that positive feelings were dangerous, that lowering his guard
would invite catastrophe. He avoided eye contact with colleagues and clients, terrified that being
truly seen might expose his weakness or invite unwanted attention. Most critically, he had begun to
avoid any situation that might involve conflict or disagreement. At work, when a parolee became
defensive or argumentative, Kwame would capitulate rather than assert his authority, his fear of
confrontation so overwhelming that he would make exceptions to protocol simply to prevent tension
from escalating.

These avoidance patterns had become self-reinforcing. By retreating from conflict, Kwame felt
temporarily safer, but the relief was short-lived. His avoidance meant he never stayed in triggering
situations long enough for his nervous system to learn they were safe. Instead, each encounter with
a male colleague, each whiff of antiseptic, each moment of being looked at, seemed to confirm his
worst beliefs: the world was dangerous, men were threats, and his life had been fundamentally
altered beyond repair. His body remained locked in vigilance, scanning, watching, waiting. The
intrusive memories—the taste, the sounds, the feeling of being present in that violence—would surge
without warning, and his only response was to withdraw further, to avoid more, to push away the few
people trying to reach him. By the time Kwame sought help, he had taken a leave of absence from
work. His relationship was strained nearly to breaking. He spent most evenings in his apartment, the
curtains drawn, uncertain whether he was hiding from the world or simply waiting for the next moment
when his past would catch up with him. What frightened him most was the thought that this—this
contracted, vigilant, isolated existence—might be all that remained of his life.
