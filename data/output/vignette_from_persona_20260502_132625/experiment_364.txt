# Kavi's Story

Kavi Pereira had been flying commercial aircraft for thirty-eight years without incident—a Samoan-
Australian whose steady hand and calm demeanor had earned him respect across three continents. He'd
logged over twenty thousand hours, most of them uneventful, the kind of mastery that comes from
decades of routine. But in 1982, before the airlines, before the captain's stripes, he'd been a
different man: a young helicopter pilot in the Australian Army, stationed in a corner of Southeast
Asia that the world had largely forgotten.

The ambush came on a supply run in late afternoon—that particular slant of light when the jungle
turned gold and shadows deepened. Kavi's crew was ferrying ammunition and personnel to a firebase
when the first round tore through the fuselage. He remembered the texture of the cyclic stick
beneath his palms—the slightly tacky grip tape, worn smooth by countless rotations—and how his hands
had moved through muscle memory even as his mind fractured. The helicopter went down hard. Two of
his crew didn't walk away. For weeks afterward, Kavi couldn't touch anything with that same texture
without his chest tightening; it had become inseparable from the moment his world inverted.

Now, at sixty-three, Kavi sat in the pre-flight briefing room at San Francisco International,
running his hands over the armrest of his chair—smooth fabric, nothing like that old grip tape—and
felt his pulse quicken anyway. The 4 p.m. departure time meant he'd be flying through the hours when
that ambush had occurred, and his body seemed to know this in ways his mind couldn't quite override.
He'd come to believe that he was fundamentally broken, that something essential had cracked in him
during those minutes, and that no amount of professional success could patch it. The certainty had
settled into him like scar tissue: *I am not the man I was. I am someone who breaks under pressure.*
This conviction had metastasized into a broader one—that the world itself was unreliable, that
safety was an illusion he'd once enjoyed but could never truly reclaim.

The texture of the grip tape haunted him in unexpected places. A passenger's coat brushing his arm
in the cockpit, the material of his wife's cardigan against his skin at night—these small tactile
moments could trigger a cascade of vigilance that left him scanning the cockpit for threats, his
body rigid, his mind already running through worst-case scenarios. The triggers and the threat
responses had become so intertwined that Kavi could no longer separate them. A texture would
surface, his body would flood with the certainty that danger was imminent, and he would find himself
cataloging exits, monitoring the sudden movements of his co-pilot, unable to settle. His wife,
Patricia, had noticed that he'd begun sitting with his back to the wall in restaurants, that his
eyes never quite stopped moving, that he flinched when she touched him unexpectedly. She'd stopped
reaching for his hand.

The memory itself had taken on a quality of unreality. When it surfaced—which it did without
warning, sometimes triggered by the whine of a jet engine during descent, sometimes by nothing he
could identify—it felt as though he were watching someone else's trauma unfold. The terror was
immediate and overwhelming, his body convinced it was 1982 again, but his mind insisted on a strange
distance, as if the man experiencing that fear was a stranger he'd once known. This dissociation
only deepened his sense of defectiveness. *A normal person would own his own past,* he'd begun to
think. *A normal person wouldn't have his memories turn alien on him.* The more the memory felt like
it belonged to someone else, the more convinced he became that he himself was fundamentally
compromised, that his very personality had been corrupted by what he'd survived.

What frightened him most was the way these convictions fed back into the hypervigilance. Because he
believed the world was dangerous, he scanned rooms more carefully, his threat detection system
permanently set to maximum sensitivity. Because he believed he was defective, he interpreted every
sign of caution as confirmation of his unworthiness. The helicopter sounds that still occasionally
crossed the San Francisco approach corridor would send him into a state of acute vigilance at night,
and he'd lie awake beside Patricia, cataloging threats, certain that his very presence in her life
was a liability. After four decades of professional mastery, Kavi had come to understand himself as
a man living under siege—not from external enemies, but from the wreckage of his own mind. It was
only when Patricia finally insisted, her voice steady but exhausted, that he'd agreed to seek help.
He was terrified that what he'd find would confirm what he already believed: that the man he'd been
on that helicopter was still, somehow, the only true version of himself.
