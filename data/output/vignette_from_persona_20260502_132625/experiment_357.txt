# Clinical Vignette: Dr. Priya Mehta

Dr. Priya Mehta, fifty-one, carries herself with the composed efficiency of someone who has spent
three decades in medicine. Her hands are steady during examinations, her voice measured when
delivering difficult diagnoses to patients. But three years ago, on a Tuesday evening in the parking
garage beneath the hospital where she works, a man she had never seen before cornered her between
the rows of cars. He was searching for her purse. What he found instead was her vulnerability. The
assault lasted perhaps four minutes—she has never been certain of the duration; time seemed to
fracture that night—but it has reshaped everything since. His hands, his voice, the particular smell
of his jacket, the silence that followed when she stopped screaming: these fragments have become the
architecture of her fear.

In the months that followed, Priya noticed that her mind had reorganized around threat. The world no
longer felt like the reasonably safe place she had believed it to be. She had come to understand
that terrible things happen to ordinary people in ordinary moments, that no amount of education or
professional accomplishment could protect her, and that people—even strangers encountered in routine
circumstances—could be sources of profound danger. This shattering of her foundational beliefs about
safety and human nature began to reshape how she moved through her days. When she encountered
unexpected sounds in the hospital hallway, her body would tense in anticipation of harm; when she
saw unfamiliar men approaching her in the corridor or the parking lot, a visceral sense of threat
would rise in her chest, regardless of context. The assault had taught her that threat was
everywhere, and her nervous system had learned the lesson thoroughly.

These convictions about danger began to rewrite her relationship with the memory itself. When Priya
tried to recall what happened—sometimes deliberately, sometimes involuntarily—the terror returned
with such intensity that it felt as though the assault were happening in the present moment. The
sensory details were overwhelming: she could smell the fabric of his jacket, could feel the pressure
of his hands, could hear the particular quality of her own voice as she had pleaded. The memory felt
so extreme, so saturated with raw fear, that part of her resisted believing it had actually
occurred. It seemed too vivid, too terrible, to be real. Yet the more she tried to suppress these
thoughts, the more intrusive they became, and the more convinced she became that thinking about the
event would shatter her capacity to function. She began to avoid anything that might trigger the
memory: she would not walk through the parking garage, would not watch the evening news, would not
allow herself to think about that Tuesday. But avoidance of the memory itself proved impossible.

The triggers multiplied. Certain body parts—hands, particularly—could suddenly transport her back to
the garage. The sight of police officers in the hospital triggered a cascade of shame and
vulnerability; she had reported the assault, had given a statement, and the investigation had
yielded nothing. Seeing the offender was not a risk she could entirely eliminate; she lived in the
same city, worked in the same hospital district, and the possibility that she might encounter him
again was a constant, low-frequency dread. Each of these triggers activated her sense of threat, and
each encounter with threat sent her scrambling for ways to manage the unbearable anxiety and
intrusive memories that followed.

Over months, Priya developed her strategies. She began dressing in layers of concealing
clothing—loose-fitting scrubs, oversized jackets, anything that would make her body less visible,
less vulnerable to notice. She avoided the parking garage entirely, leaving work at odd hours to
wait in the lobby until someone could escort her to her car, an arrangement that humiliated her and
that she eventually stopped explaining to colleagues. More recently, she had discovered that
gambling provided a temporary reprieve. Late at night, when the silence of her home felt
threatening—when the absence of sound became, paradoxically, a sign that something dangerous was
imminent—she would open her laptop and place bets on online poker or sports matches. The distraction
was immediate and complete; for those hours, her mind was elsewhere, the intrusive memories were
held at bay, and the vigilance that had become her constant companion could briefly relax. The
financial consequences were mounting, and her husband had noticed the late nights, but the relief
was too valuable to relinquish.

Yet the strategies that had seemed to help were now tightening the loop. The more she avoided the
garage, the more she avoided thinking about the assault, the more she gambled to escape her
thoughts, the more her triggers multiplied. Each avoidance seemed to confirm her belief that the
world was genuinely dangerous, that she could not handle remembering, that people could not be
trusted. The memory became more intrusive, not less. Her sense of threat expanded to ordinary
situations: a patient's unexpected movement, a nurse standing too close, the particular timbre of a
male colleague's voice. She had become hypervigilant to sounds—the sudden beep of a monitor, a door
closing, a phone ringing—and she interpreted each as a possible precursor to harm. The silence in
her home, the silence in the garage, the silence after a patient left her office: all of it felt
pregnant with danger.

Priya came to therapy because her husband, with careful gentleness, told her he was frightened for
her. She had lost weight. She had stopped attending her temple, had told him she no longer believed
that a merciful God existed—not after what had happened to her, not after the assault had revealed
the randomness and cruelty at the heart of existence. She had taken a leave of absence from the
hospital, citing administrative work, but the truth was that the parking garage, the corridors, the
patients themselves—all had become too threatening to navigate. She is terrified that if she begins
to truly process what happened, the terror will consume her entirely. But she is also beginning to
understand that the very strategies meant to protect her are slowly dismantling the life she had
built.
