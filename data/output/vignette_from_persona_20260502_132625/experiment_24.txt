# Amara's Vigilance

Amara was seventy-eight when the car hit her. She had been crossing the street in downtown Lagos—she
was visiting family, as she did every other year—when a delivery truck ran a red light and struck
her broadside. The impact threw her against a storefront window; she remembered the sound of the
glass, the sensation of her ribs compressing, and then the high-pitched wail of the ambulance siren
that seemed to go on forever as they loaded her into it. The driver had been texting. She had done
nothing wrong. But in the months after returning to her home in Atlanta, after the surgery and the
physical therapy, Amara had come to believe that someone more careful, more aware, would never have
been in that spot at that moment. She replayed the crossing a thousand times, searching for the
split-second decision that would have kept her safe—a different route, a moment's hesitation,
anything that would have meant she was not there when the truck came through. The belief that she
had failed to protect herself, that her body was no longer trustworthy, had settled into her bones
alongside the chronic pain that never fully left.

Two years had passed since the accident, and Amara was now eighty. She had returned to her work as a
midwife at Grady Memorial Hospital, a position she had held for forty-three years, but the hospital
had become a landscape of invisible traps. The ambulance bay doors opened and closed with a sound
that made her flinch—not quite a siren, but close enough to trigger the flooding sensation of
helplessness that came with the memory of that wail. When the emergency alert system tested its
sirens on the first Tuesday of the month, Amara would find herself gripping the edge of whatever
surface was nearest, her heart accelerating into her throat. The sound did not simply startle her;
it pulled her backward into the moment of impact, and in that moment she was certain that her
body—already damaged, already fragile—would not survive another collision. Each siren reinforced
what she had come to know as absolute truth: that she was vulnerable in ways other people were not,
that her life as she had known it was finished.

At home, her husband James had noticed that she no longer slept through the night. She would lie
awake beside him, listening to the street sounds outside their window, tensing at every car that
passed. When he touched her in the dark, she sometimes startled so violently that he withdrew, hurt
and confused. She had taken to sleeping in the guest room, telling him it was so she would not
disturb his rest, but the truth was that she could not bear the vulnerability of sleeping beside
another person. She was always watching, always listening, her body coiled even in rest. The sleep
deprivation accumulated—nights of two or three hours, her mind cycling through catastrophes, her
vigilance never resting even when her body briefly did.

The locked examination rooms at the hospital had become unbearable. When she entered one to check on
a laboring patient, the moment the door clicked shut behind her, Amara's breathing would shallow.
She was trapped. If something happened—a complication, an emergency—she would not be able to move
freely. The claustrophobia was new; she had never experienced it before the accident. But now,
enclosed in that small room with its single door, she felt the same helplessness that had seized her
in the moment the truck hit her, the same certainty that her body could not protect itself. She had
begun to make excuses to stay out of the locked rooms, asking younger staff to handle those
patients, and her supervisor had begun to notice. This was not the Amara who had trained a
generation of nurses, who had delivered three thousand babies with steady hands and an unshakeable
presence.

The threat monitoring never stopped. At traffic lights, she watched the cross-traffic obsessively,
her foot hovering over the accelerator, ready to flee. When other cars came too close, she felt
certain they would hit her—not might, but would. Her body was permanently damaged; she had felt the
break in her ribs, the way her left shoulder had never quite regained its full range of motion. If
she was hit again, she would not survive it. This conviction, born from the accident but now fused
with every moment of hypervigilance, every startle, every sleepless night, had become the lens
through which she saw the world. Amara had finally called her daughter last month and admitted that
she could not manage alone anymore, that something had to change. She was ready to seek help, though
part of her believed that no amount of therapy could undo what the accident had done—that her life,
as she had lived it, was truly gone.
