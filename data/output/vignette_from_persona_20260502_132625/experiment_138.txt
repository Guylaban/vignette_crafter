# Clinical Vignette: Linh

Linh was thirty-one when her mother died. It happened on a Tuesday morning in March, without
warning—a sudden cardiac arrhythmia while her mother was at work, alone in her office. Linh learned
about it from a hospital administrator's phone call, delivered while she sat in the newsroom of the
Seattle Tribune, mid-sentence in an email to her editor. One moment she was a journalist with a
byline and a divorce that was almost final; the next moment she was someone whose mother had stopped
breathing and there was nothing—no sign, no illness, no time to say anything at all.

The funeral was held in Portland, where her mother had lived since remarrying five years earlier.
Linh delivered a eulogy that felt like reading someone else's words. She returned to Seattle three
days later and went back to work because she did not know what else to do. Her editor gave her two
weeks off. She took four days.

Within weeks, Linh noticed that her body had become a stranger to her. She could not sit through a
full news meeting without her heart rate spiking, without the sensation of being perched on the edge
of a chair even when she was sitting squarely in it. Her colleagues would chat about weekend plans,
and she would feel the muscles across her shoulders and neck tighten as though bracing for impact.
At home, in the evenings, she found herself unable to settle into the couch with a book or a show.
She would start to relax—her breathing would deepen, her shoulders would drop—and then something
would shift. A car door would slam outside. A notification would buzz on her phone. Her mind would
leap to catastrophe. *Something is wrong. Someone else is dying. You should be ready.* She would
stand up, pace, check her phone, refresh her news feeds, as though vigilance itself might prevent
the next unbearable loss.

What frightened her most was the randomness of it all. Her mother had been healthy. Fifty-eight
years old, active, no family history of cardiac disease. She had gone to work that morning and never
come home. This fact—that death could arrive without announcement, without mercy, without any
warning at all—had reorganized Linh's entire nervous system. She had come to believe that the only
way to survive was to remain alert, to monitor the environment constantly for signs of danger, to
never allow herself the vulnerability of genuine rest. To relax was to lower her guard, and to lower
her guard was to invite another catastrophe she would be powerless to stop.

The hypervigilance had become exhausting. At the Tribune, Linh found herself scanning colleagues'
faces during meetings, alert to any sign of distress or illness. She would notice if someone seemed
quieter than usual and immediately assume the worst. She began researching rare cardiac conditions
on her laptop between assignments, reading case studies of sudden deaths in young, seemingly healthy
people. She attended her mother's cardiologist's memorial service—a gesture that confused her
colleagues—and asked the physician detailed questions about screening protocols, as though
understanding the mechanisms of sudden death might somehow grant her control over it.

Her ex-husband had called in early May, checking in. She could hear the discomfort in his voice, the
careful distance he maintained even in his concern. They had divorced two years earlier, and the
separation had been amicable but thorough. Now, when he asked how she was managing, Linh heard
herself giving short answers. She did not tell him that she was sleeping only in fragments, that she
would wake at 3 a.m. with her heart racing, convinced something had happened to her father or her
younger brother. She did not tell him that she had started calling her brother every morning,
ostensibly to chat, but really to confirm he was still alive. She did not tell him that the thought
of being alone—truly alone, with no one to monitor, no one to check on—filled her with a dread so
acute it made her hands shake.

It was her editor who finally insisted. Linh had missed a deadline in July, something she had never
done in eight years at the paper. She had been in the middle of a feature when a colleague mentioned
his father's upcoming bypass surgery, and Linh had frozen at her desk, unable to type, unable to
think of anything but the fragility of the human heart. Her editor pulled her into his office and
said, quietly, that she needed to talk to someone. He gave her the name of a therapist who
specialized in grief and trauma. Linh made the appointment because she was afraid of losing her job,
but also because she was afraid of what her body might do next if she did not find a way to make it
stop—this constant, exhausting vigilance, this inability to trust that she was safe even in the
safest moments of her life.
