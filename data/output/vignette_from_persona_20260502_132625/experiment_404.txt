# Clinical Vignette: Margaret

Margaret was sixty-eight when it happened, though she would later mark her life in two distinct
eras: before and after the assault. She had worked the orchards near Yakima for forty-three
years—apples mostly, some pears—and had built a quiet, ordered life with her husband Robert. They
had routines. They had stability. On a late September afternoon, while checking irrigation lines at
the far edge of the property where the land dissolved into scrub and fence line, a man she did not
know emerged from the tree rows. What followed was a violence that fractured something in her that
no amount of time or routine could repair.

In the weeks after, Margaret found herself unable to trust her own judgment. She had been alone in
that field. She had not heard him approach. She had failed to see the danger until it was upon her,
and this failure—the way her own senses and instincts had betrayed her—became a conviction that
hardened into certainty: she could not rely on herself to keep herself safe. This belief took root
and spread into everything. When Margaret rode the bus into town for supplies, her hands would grip
the seat in front of her, and she would spend the entire journey scanning the faces of other
passengers, convinced that any one of them might be the man, or someone like him. The bus itself—the
enclosed space, the press of strangers, the way she could not exit quickly—became a trigger that
sent her into a cascade of rehearsal and dread. She would mentally walk through scenarios before
boarding: What if someone blocked the aisle? What if the driver did not stop at her intended stop?
What if she froze again, as she had in the field? These worst-case rehearsals became a compulsion, a
way of preparing herself, though they only deepened her conviction that danger was everywhere and
that she lacked the capacity to respond.

The more Margaret rehearsed catastrophe, the more catastrophe seemed real. Her threat monitoring
became constant—a low, electrical hum beneath every moment. At the grocery store, she checked behind
her every few steps. At the farmers market where she had once sold her own produce, she positioned
herself with her back to the wall, always watching the entrance. She came to believe she was
permanently marked, fundamentally different from other people now, an outsider in the world she had
inhabited her whole life. This sense of isolation fed her drinking. A glass of wine became two, then
three, most evenings. She told herself it was for sleep, but it was also a way of dampening the
constant vigilance, of making the world feel less threatening for a few hours. The alcohol softened
the sharp edges of her negative appraisals—the voice telling her she was broken, unreliable,
alone—but only temporarily, and only at the cost of waking at three in the morning with her heart
racing, certain she had heard something.

Robert noticed the change first in small ways: Margaret no longer wanted to visit their daughter in
Seattle. The drive itself was manageable, but the idea of sitting in a car with someone else for
hours, of being trapped in that confined space, triggered the same spiral of worst-case thinking
that the bus did. Then came the evening when their son-in-law made a casual comment at dinner about
a crime he had heard on the news, and Margaret stood abruptly and left the table. She could not bear
to hear certain conversation topics—anything that touched on violence or vulnerability—because these
words seemed to activate the intrusive memory itself, the sudden, unbidden return of the afternoon
in the field, the smell of dust and his breath and her own powerlessness. When the memory came, it
came with the full sensory weight of the original event, and Margaret would find herself back there,
her body flooded with the same terror and shame.

She began to avoid people more systematically. She stopped attending church. She made excuses when
neighbors called. The only places where the vigilance quieted even slightly were her bedroom and the
kitchen—spaces she had made, spaces she could control. Everywhere else felt hostile. The orchards
where she had worked her whole life now felt dangerous, and she found reasons not to go out to the
property. Her husband, patient as he was, began to feel like a stranger living in the house with a
woman he no longer recognized. It was Robert who finally insisted she see someone, not out of anger
but out of a quiet desperation to have his wife back. Margaret came to the clinic on a Tuesday
morning, her hands folded in her lap, and when asked what had brought her in, she said simply: "I'm
afraid of everything now. And I don't know how to stop."
