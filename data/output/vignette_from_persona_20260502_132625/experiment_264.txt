# Carlos

Carlos was thirty-eight when Hurricane María made landfall on Puerto Rico in September 2017. He had
been working a double shift at San Juan Hospital, exhausted but necessary—the storm surge warnings
had sent half his colleagues home early to secure their families, and the emergency department was
understaffed. He was in the surgical wing when the windows began to shatter. The sound came first, a
deafening shriek of wind that seemed to peel the roof open, followed by the percussion of glass and
metal, the roar of water breaching the lower floors. Then came the smell—acrid, chemical, the
burning of electrical systems and the thick, choking reek of smoke as a fire started in the east
corridor. Carlos had tried to move toward it, muscle memory from training overriding the chaos, but
the attending had grabbed him, pulled him back into a stairwell. He remembered fragments with
crystalline clarity: the face of a nursing assistant trapped behind a collapsed beam on the third
floor, the heat of the fire at his back as they evacuated, the taste of ash and salt water mixed in
his mouth. But there were gaps—long stretches of the evacuation itself were missing, as though his
mind had simply stopped recording. He could not account for nearly forty minutes. He knew he had
made it to the parking garage, knew he had driven home in standing water, but the middle was blank.
What he could not forget was the smell, and the sound, and the overwhelming sense that he had
abandoned people who needed him.

The years that followed were a slow settling into a life that looked normal from the outside. Carlos
had returned to work within weeks. He and his wife María had rebuilt their home. They had settled
into a comfortable routine in San Juan, and to most observers—his colleagues, his extended family—he
seemed fine. But fine was a careful construction. When a car backfired near the hospital, or when he
heard the sharp crack of fireworks during a neighborhood celebration, Carlos's body would jolt as
though electrified. The sound would pull him back to that stairwell instantly, completely, and he
would feel the heat and smell the smoke with an immediacy that made his hands shake. His mind would
fragment again—the same gaps, the same missing time, the same crushing guilt about the people he
could not reach. To manage these moments, he had developed a pattern. When the intrusions came, when
the memories surfaced unbidden and the grief felt like it might split him open, he would leave work
early, claiming fatigue or a patient complication that required follow-up. He would go home and lose
himself in sexual fantasies, or he would drink—carefully at first, a glass of wine in the evening,
then two, then a glass before work. The alcohol seemed to blur the edges of the memories, to create
distance between himself and the gaps he could not fill. He took sleeping pills when the nightmares
came, and occasionally he would use stronger things when the combination of triggers and memories
became too much to bear.

María had begun to notice the pattern months ago. She would find him in his study late at night, a
drink in hand, or she would realize he had left work without explanation. When she asked him about
it, he could not articulate what was happening—could not explain that certain sounds seemed to crack
open the sealed-off parts of that day, or that when the memories surfaced, he needed to do
something, anything, to make them stop. What frightened him most was the incompleteness of it all,
the knowledge that something crucial had happened in those lost minutes, something that might have
changed the outcome if he could only remember it. The more he drank to escape the memories, the more
fragmented they became, the more he needed to escape. His colleagues had begun to comment on his
absences, on the slight tremor in his hands during complex procedures. His relationship with María
had grown distant and strained. He had come to believe that the gaps in his memory were his fault,
that his mind was punishing him for his failure, and that no amount of distraction or chemical
relief could fill the space where his certainty used to be.

It was María who finally insisted he seek help, after finding him passed out in his study at three
in the afternoon, an empty bottle beside him and the smell of smoke—from a neighbor's
barbecue—having triggered a cascade he could no longer manage alone. Carlos sat in the therapist's
office now, forty years old and terrified that looking directly at what he had forgotten would only
make it worse.
