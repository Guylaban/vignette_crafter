# Minh's Vigilance

Minh was eighty-two when he came to the clinic, though the weariness in his face suggested someone
older. He had spent the better part of three decades as a data scientist, building predictive models
for financial institutions, work that had suited his methodical mind. But it was what had happened
forty-seven years ago—during his time as a relief worker in Cambodia in 1977—that had quietly
reshaped everything about how he moved through the world.

The assignment had seemed purposeful then. Minh, younger and idealistic, had crossed into the
country to deliver medical supplies and document conditions in a rural province. What he encountered
was systematic cruelty on a scale that fractured something fundamental in his understanding of human
nature. He had watched soldiers execute civilians for minor infractions—a man beaten to death for
speaking out of turn, a woman shot for attempting to shield her child. The randomness of it haunted
him still: the same transgression might be ignored one day and trigger lethal violence the next.
What remained most visceral was the feeling of absolute powerlessness, the knowledge that no amount
of reasoning or compliance could predict or prevent what would happen next. He had eventually fled,
carrying those images like shrapnel embedded too deep to remove.

For decades, Minh had managed. He built a life, married a Vietnamese woman named Linh, raised two
daughters. When Linh died five years ago, he had grieved but continued. The memories stayed buried,
accessed only in dreams. But something shifted recently—a news report about political violence in
Southeast Asia had caught him unprepared one evening, and since then, the careful architecture of
his life had begun to crack.

Now, when Minh saw footage of civil unrest or read about authoritarian crackdowns, the memories
erupted unbidden. A report about police brutality triggered the full cascade: the film began to
play, frame by frame, and he felt himself back in that province, the heat oppressive, the sounds of
violence immediate and real. When the memory came, it didn't feel like remembering something
distant—it felt like the event was happening to him right now, as vivid and present as breath. The
reliving was so complete that he would sometimes check his hands, expecting to find blood. These
episodes terrified him, and he had come to believe that his mind was fracturing, that his reactions
since the trauma meant he was going crazy. This fear fed a new conviction: that the world was
fundamentally dangerous, that cruelty lurked beneath every interaction, waiting to emerge.

The triggers had multiplied and woven themselves into his daily existence. When people behaved
badly—a neighbor's curt remark, a rude cashier—Minh's nervous system responded as though witnessing
the same moral violation he had seen in Cambodia: the sudden, arbitrary cruelty that revealed human
nature as fundamentally corrupt. Any situation where he felt trapped without options—a doctor's
appointment where he couldn't leave early, a family dinner where he felt obligated to
attend—activated the old sense of entrapment, and the memories would surge forward. Even waking from
nightmares left him disoriented, the boundary between sleep and waking permeable, the threat feeling
as real as when he had first lived it.

To manage the memories and the terror they brought, Minh had developed an elaborate system of
avoidance and control. He stopped watching news entirely, turning off the television or leaving the
room when reports of violence appeared. This had cost him—his daughters had noticed his withdrawal,
had asked why he never engaged with current events anymore, had stopped inviting him to family
gatherings where he might encounter unexpected triggers. At work, before any meeting or interaction,
he rehearsed what he would say, running through conversations repeatedly in his mind to anticipate
how others might respond unpredictably or behave badly. His colleagues had begun to comment that he
seemed rigid, over-prepared, anxious in ways that seemed disproportionate to a simple team meeting.
Most exhausting was his constant scanning of his environment. At the grocery store, on walks through
his neighborhood, even in his own home, Minh's attention was perpetually divided. His body remained
tense, coiled, ready to react to danger that he was certain was present but invisible. He found
himself tracking exits, noting who stood near him, assessing threats in the casual movements of
strangers.

The memory rehearsals, meant to contain the trauma, had paradoxically strengthened it. Each time he
mentally prepared for a conversation, he would find himself slipping back into the memories—the
anticipatory anxiety would trigger the film to play again, and the cycle would tighten. His
hypervigilance, his constant threat-scanning, kept his nervous system in a state of perpetual alarm
that made the memories feel more urgent, more real. His body was always on alert, muscles tight,
ready to react. When he did manage to relax, even briefly, irritability would surface—sudden anger
at small things, a sharpness in his voice that surprised even him. He had snapped at his eldest
daughter during a recent phone call, had felt the rage building in his chest and been terrified by
his own capacity for it, which only reinforced his fear that he was losing control, that his mind
was deteriorating.

Minh came to the clinic because his daughter had insisted, and because he had begun to wonder if the
constant vigilance, the endless mental rehearsals, the avoidance that had narrowed his world to a
small, safe perimeter, were worth the cost of living this way. What frightened him most was not the
memories themselves, but the possibility that they would never fade, that he would spend his
remaining years trapped in a present moment that never ended.
