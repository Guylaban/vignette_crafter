# Margaret's Vigil

Margaret was sixty-eight when her daughter Emma died. It happened on a Tuesday in March—a sudden
cardiac arrhythmia while Emma was driving home from work, no warning, no time for intervention. The
police officer who came to the door used the word "instantaneous," which Margaret supposed was meant
to be a mercy, but all she heard was that Emma had been alone in that car, that Margaret had been at
home sorting through old photo albums, that there had been nothing—nothing—she could have done. In
the weeks after the funeral, Margaret replayed every conversation with her daughter from the
previous month, searching for signs she should have caught, for some moment when she might have
insisted Emma see a cardiologist, get checked, stay home that day. Emma had mentioned feeling tired.
Margaret had suggested she get more rest. If she had pushed harder, if she had been more vigilant,
if she had somehow known, perhaps Emma would still be alive.

The guilt crystallized into a terrible conviction: she had failed in the most fundamental duty of a
mother. She should have protected her daughter, and her failure had cost Emma everything. This
belief—that she was fundamentally responsible for a death she could not have prevented—began to
reshape how Margaret understood herself in relation to the world. She came to feel profoundly
isolated, cut off from the life she had known. The other women in her book club, the neighbors she
had waved to for thirty years, the cousins who still sent Christmas cards—they all seemed to exist
in a realm of ordinary safety that Margaret no longer inhabited. She had glimpsed something they had
not: the randomness of catastrophe, the illusion of control. She no longer belonged anywhere. How
could she sit with them and pretend that life was predictable, that love kept people safe? The
weight of her isolation deepened her conviction that she was fundamentally different now, marked by
failure in a way that separated her from everyone else.

This sense of irredeemable isolation and belonging nowhere drove Margaret into constant activity.
She began filling her days with projects—reorganizing the garage, volunteering at the library,
taking on yard work that her husband Tom usually managed. She threw herself into busyness as a way
to escape the unbearable feeling of being set apart, as if motion itself could outrun the shame and
the conviction that she had failed the person she loved most. But the activity offered no relief.
Her body remained in a state of constant vigilance, her shoulders perpetually tensed, her breath
shallow. Even on quiet evenings when Tom sat beside her reading, Margaret could not relax. Her
nervous system seemed permanently convinced that danger was imminent, that something else
catastrophic could happen at any moment if she let her guard down. The tension fed her sense of
threat; the sense of threat made her feel unsafe even in her own home, even in the safety she knew
intellectually existed.

This hypervigilance and the constant tightness in her chest only reinforced her negative appraisals.
If her body was telling her that danger was everywhere, then surely that meant the world was as
unsafe as she feared. If she was always on alert, always braced for loss, then perhaps that was
because she had already proven herself incapable of preventing disaster. The fear and the guilt
became inseparable. She began to avoid the people she had once seen regularly—her daughter-in-law
called to invite her to lunch, and Margaret declined, certain that she would only burden them with
her darkness. She stopped attending book club. When she ran into an old friend at the grocery store,
Margaret cut the conversation short and hurried away, feeling the familiar wave of shame and the
absolute conviction that she no longer belonged in that woman's cheerful, untouched world. The
avoidance of social contact seemed like the only honest response to what she now believed about
herself: that she was someone who failed to protect those she loved, and that her presence could
only remind others of loss and mortality.

Margaret came to therapy six months after Emma's death, brought by Tom, who had watched his wife
disappear into a cycle of frantic activity and isolation that left her more exhausted and despairing
each week. She sat in the waiting room with her hands clenched in her lap, her jaw tight, uncertain
whether talking about it would make things better or simply confirm what she already knew—that she
was responsible for her daughter's death, and that she would never again be the person she had been.
