# Thao

Thao was thirty seconds from landing when her older brother's voice came through the radio for the
last time. She was bringing her F/A-18 down on the carrier deck in the Persian Gulf, her gloved
hands steady on the stick, her mind already cycling through the post-flight checklist. Her brother
Minh was supposed to be waiting for her in the ready room—he'd called her that morning from their
parents' house in San Jose, joking about how he'd finally gotten the promotion he'd been chasing.
She'd laughed and told him she'd call him back after she landed. The ejection sequence came without
warning. A structural failure in another aircraft overhead, a cascade of debris, a moment of physics
that left no room for skill or training. By the time Thao's boots hit the deck, Minh was in surgery
three thousand miles away, the damage too extensive for the surgeons to repair. He died fourteen
hours later while she was still in the debrief, still in her flight suit, still not knowing.

That was two years ago. Thao had returned to active duty because staying seemed easier than leaving,
because the routine of preflight checks and mission briefs had a merciful, mechanical quality that
kept her moving forward. But the structure that had once steadied her had become a minefield. Any
discomfort—the particular humidity of the ready room, the smell of jet fuel mixing with coffee, even
the texture of the flight deck beneath her boots—could trigger the memory with such force and
clarity that she would lose five minutes to it, watching the scene replay behind her eyes like a
film she couldn't stop. It wasn't vague or impressionistic; the memories came to her as if she were
looking at photographs, every detail preserved: the specific angle of morning light through the
cockpit glass, the exact timbre of Minh's voice, the weight of her own helplessness. These unbidden
flashes left her shaking, and they came without warning—a conversation at the officers' club about
someone's brother getting engaged, a glimpse of another pilot's family photo on a desk, and suddenly
she was back there, watching her brother die in real time while she was too far away to do anything
at all.

What frightened Thao most was not the memories themselves but what they meant about her. She had
come to believe there was something fundamentally defective in her—that a person who was whole, who
was strong enough, would have found a way to protect Minh. She should have been there. She should
have known something would go wrong. The fact that she couldn't protect him, that she had failed him
at the moment it mattered most, became evidence of a deeper truth: she was broken in some essential
way, and she couldn't trust other people to understand that or to stay. When colleagues tried to
invite her to dinner or to go out on weekends, she found reasons to decline. The thought of starting
a friendship, of letting someone close enough to see what she really was, felt impossible. She
couldn't rely on people anyway—they left, or they died, or they would eventually see through her
carefully maintained composure and recognize the defect she carried.

To manage the memories, Thao developed a rigid practice of suppression. When thoughts about Minh
surfaced—and they surfaced constantly—she would push them away with a kind of mental force, trying
hard not to think about what happened, not to let the narrative unfold. She would focus on the next
mission, the next checklist, the next moment that could be controlled. But the effort of suppressing
these thoughts seemed to strengthen them. The memories would return with more intensity, erupting
unbidden during a routine flight or in the middle of a conversation. Being reminded of the event by
others became intolerable; when a fellow pilot mentioned losing his father, or when someone brought
up the incident from two years ago in a way that felt too direct, Thao would shut down the
conversation immediately, making excuses to leave. Yet avoiding those reminders meant isolating
further, which only deepened her conviction that she was alone with this, that no one could help her
carry it. The harder she tried not to think about Minh, the more vivid the memories became, and the
more vivid the memories, the more she was convinced that her failure to protect him proved something
irredeemable about who she was. The cycle had become so tight, so self-reinforcing, that Thao was no
longer sure where the thought ended and the feeling began. She had stopped attending family dinners.
She had turned down a promotion that would have required her to mentor younger pilots. She was
present at work but absent everywhere else, moving through her days like someone going through the
motions of being alive. It was only when her commanding officer noticed her withdrawal and quietly
suggested she talk to someone that Thao finally admitted, in a quiet voice, that she didn't know how
to live with what she had failed to prevent.
