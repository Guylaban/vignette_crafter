# Aisha

Aisha was forty-three when she came to the clinic, though her presentation suggested someone
older—her posture held a guardedness that had calcified over decades, and her eyes moved constantly,
tracking small shifts in the room the way a person watches for movement in tall grass. She worked in
IT support at a mid-sized financial firm, a job that had once felt like escape. Growing up in Lagos
before her family immigrated when she was twelve, she had learned early that the world could turn
hostile without warning.

Her stepfather had been a man of sudden rages. The worst of it happened in the compound behind their
house, a space bounded by high walls where the servants did laundry and kept tools. Aisha was nine.
She had been sent to fetch water from the storage tank, a task she had performed hundreds of times.
On this particular afternoon, her stepfather found her there. He had been drinking. What followed
was a sustained beating—his hands, his belt, the sharp corner of a wooden crate. She remembered the
smell of rust from the tank, the way the corrugated roof rattled with each impact, the absolute
certainty that no one would hear her cry out. The worst part, she would later tell a therapist, was
not the pain itself but the discovery that she could not escape him, that the space she thought she
knew had become a trap, and that somehow—in the twisted logic of a child trying to make sense of
chaos—she must have done something to deserve it.

The trauma had never been processed. Her family had emigrated three years later, and the incident
was folded into silence, the way many things were. Aisha built a life in America that looked stable
from the outside: a degree in computer science, a steady marriage to Marcus, a house in a suburb
with good schools. But the body remembers what the mind tries to forget.

By her early forties, Aisha had come to believe that there was something intrinsically broken about
her, something that had made her a target then and made her vulnerable now. This conviction shaped
how she read the world. When her body tensed—and it was always tense, a baseline hum of readiness
that never quite switched off—she interpreted that tension as evidence of danger, and that danger as
confirmation of her defectiveness. She was certain that if she had been different, stronger, less
flawed, the beating would not have happened. And if she was the kind of person to whom such things
happened, then she was the kind of person to whom they would happen again. Her body's vigilance was
not a symptom; it was a rational response to a world that had already proven it could turn on her
without warning.

This belief system had become self-reinforcing. When Marcus suggested they spend a weekend hiking in
the state park—a natural setting with dense trees and open water not unlike the compound where the
assault occurred—Aisha felt something in her chest seize. The thought of being in that kind of
environment, vulnerable and exposed, triggered a cascade of threat. Her heart would race before she
even arrived. But the racing itself felt like proof that danger was real, that her body was
detecting something true about the situation. So she declined. She made excuses. She began to avoid
not just hiking but any outdoor space that resembled that place: parks, gardens, even the tree-lined
path to her office building. She took a different route, longer and more circuitous, which meant she
was often late to work. Her manager had noticed. She had not explained why.

Talking about the event was worse. When Marcus or her sister pressed her for details about her
childhood, or when a therapist's intake form asked her to describe "any traumatic experiences,"
Aisha's body would flood with the same panic she had felt in that compound. The act of articulating
what happened seemed to make it real in a way that silence did not. So she stopped talking about it.
She became evasive. She changed the subject. She developed a practiced smile that discouraged
further questions. But the effort of suppression exhausted her, and the avoidance itself became a
kind of confirmation: if she could not even speak about it, it must be truly terrible, which meant
she must be truly damaged.

Locked doors had become intolerable. Early in her marriage, she had insisted that bedroom and
bathroom doors remain unlocked. Marcus had thought it was a quirk. It was not. Any space where she
could not exit freely triggered the same trapped feeling she had experienced in the compound—that
moment when she realized escape was impossible. She found herself avoiding conference rooms at work,
taking the stairs instead of the elevator, preferring to work from home whenever possible. Her
colleagues assumed she was simply private. What they did not see was the way her hands trembled when
she was called into a closed office, or how she positioned herself near doors, or how her
concentration scattered the moment she felt hemmed in. The threat her body perceived in those
moments felt absolute and real, and it confirmed what she already believed: that she was broken,
that danger followed her, that she could not trust the world or herself.

Aisha came to the clinic after Marcus found her hyperventilating in their bedroom closet, where she
had gone to retrieve a suitcase and had become convinced, irrationally but absolutely, that the door
would lock behind her and she would be trapped. She sat in the waiting room with her hands folded,
her jaw clenched, and told the receptionist in a quiet voice that she thought something was very
wrong with her, and that she was afraid it always would be.
