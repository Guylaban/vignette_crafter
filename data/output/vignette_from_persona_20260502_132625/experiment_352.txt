# Aroha's Story

Aroha Parata was seventy-five when she first came to the clinic, though her posture and the careful
way she moved suggested someone older—someone who had learned to make herself small, to take up less
space. She had been a civilian auditor in Beirut in 1982 when the shelling intensified, trapped in a
neighbourhood that became a war zone almost overnight. For weeks she had sheltered in her apartment
building with neighbours, rationing water, moving only when necessary. The day everything changed,
she had ventured out to find food. A sniper's shot had killed the man standing three metres from
her—she could still see his face, still smell the copper and sweat, the acrid smoke that followed.
She had run, stumbling over rubble, and made it back inside. She had survived. But survival, she
would come to understand, was not the same as being safe.

Now, decades later, Aroha lived in a small house in Portland with her husband, David. She had built
a careful life: quiet, predictable, controlled. But in recent years, that life had begun to collapse
inward. The smell of her own sweat—or David's after he came home from his morning walk—would
transport her instantly back to that street in Beirut. When she smelled body odour, the memory would
arrive not as a narrative but as a photographic intrusion: she could see the man's face, the exact
angle of the rubble, the way the light fell across the pavement. The image was so vivid, so
immediate, that she found herself unable to describe what she was seeing, even to David. The words
simply would not come. She knew what she had witnessed, but when she tried to speak it aloud, her
mind went blank. The sensory detail was overwhelming, but the language to contain it had vanished.

Equally distressing were the moments when she felt happy—when David made her laugh, or when she sat
in the garden on a warm afternoon. These small pleasures triggered a cascade of dread. Happiness
felt dangerous, as though contentment was a vulnerability that might expose her to harm. More
troubling still was her workplace. At the audit firm where she still worked part-time, the open
office environment had become unbearable. The proximity of colleagues, the unpredictability of who
might approach her desk, the way conversations could shift unexpectedly—all of it felt like a threat
she could not quite name. When she was at her desk, she became hypervigilant about her surroundings,
and this constant alertness seemed to activate the memories again, as if her body's threat detection
was pulling the trauma back into consciousness.

As the memories intruded more frequently, Aroha had developed ways of managing them—though she could
not have explained why these strategies made sense except that they seemed to hold the chaos at bay.
She would rehearse conversations obsessively before any interaction, scripting what she might say
and how others might respond, as if she could prevent harm through perfect preparation. She would
ruminate endlessly about the day itself: if she had not gone out, if she had waited another hour, if
she had taken a different route. She replayed the event over and over, searching for the moment
where she might have changed the outcome, as though understanding the mechanism of her survival
might protect her from future catastrophe. The more she rehearsed and ruminated, the more vivid the
memories became. The act of turning the event over in her mind seemed to sharpen the images, to make
the sensory details even more intrusive, and to deepen her conviction that she was somehow marked by
what had happened.

This belief—that she was marked, exposed—had taken root and spread. She had come to believe that
others could see the trauma written on her face, that her colleagues at work could sense her
fragility, that David could see the damage beneath her skin. She was certain that she would never
feel normal emotions again, that the person she had been before Beirut was gone forever. This
conviction fed a growing sense that her life was no longer worth living. The future stretched ahead
as a grey, diminished thing. And because she believed herself damaged and visible, she began to feel
genuinely unsafe. She could no longer sit with her back to a door. At work, she would position
herself so that she could see the entire office, and she found herself frequently looking over her
shoulder, checking to see if someone was following her. She had even begun taking small
risks—driving too fast, walking alone in unfamiliar areas—as though part of her was testing whether
she might deserve the harm she feared. The rumination that had begun as a way to understand the past
had become a mechanism that kept the past alive, and the safety behaviours she had adopted to
protect herself seemed only to reinforce her conviction that the world was dangerous and that she,
Aroha, was uniquely vulnerable.

It was David who finally insisted she seek help. He had watched his wife disappear into a world of
hypervigilance and dread, and he could no longer bear it. Aroha came to the clinic exhausted, her
hope nearly extinguished, but willing to try.
