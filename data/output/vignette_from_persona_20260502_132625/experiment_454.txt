# Priya

Priya was forty-two when she came to the clinic, though her face carried the exhaustion of someone
much older. She had been working as a maid in a downtown office building for seven years—a job that
had once felt stable, even respectable—when a man who worked in the accounting department began to
follow her. It started with comments about her body as she cleaned the hallways, escalating over
months into lingering stares that made her skin crawl, notes left in her cleaning cart, and
eventually, messages on social media he had somehow found. He would position himself where she
worked, watching her bend and reach, his gaze heavy and deliberate. The smell of alcohol on his
breath became a signature of his presence; he would lean close under the pretense of conversation,
and that sour, sharp smell would flood her nostrils and her fear simultaneously. When she reported
him to management, they suggested she change her schedule instead of addressing his behavior. Six
months later, after he appeared at a grocery store where she shopped with her husband, she stopped
going out altogether.

The stalking had ended—he was eventually fired after his behavior escalated—but Priya remained
trapped in the aftermath. What happened to her refused to settle into a coherent narrative. When she
tried to recount the months of stalking to her therapist, the story fragmented. She would begin to
describe a particular encounter in the break room, feel the memory pressing forward with visceral
detail—the metallic taste of her own fear, copper and salt on her tongue—and then she would push it
away, her mind recoiling before the full picture could form. The incompleteness itself became
frightening; she could not trust her own mind to hold what had happened, and the gaps made
everything feel more dangerous, less contained.

The triggers were everywhere, though they seemed to multiply with each passing month. Being looked
at in a certain way—a man's gaze lingering a fraction too long in the supermarket—sent her heart
racing. Any smell of alcohol, even from across a room, could collapse her into panic. Physical pain,
a sudden ache in her lower back from hours of scrubbing floors, would sometimes arrive alongside a
flash of the memory she had been pushing away, as though her body was remembering what her mind
refused to hold. Each trigger pulled the fragmented memory closer to the surface, and each fragment
of memory that broke through sent her threat system into overdrive.

At home, Priya had become hypervigilant in ways that exhausted her marriage. She checked the locks
on every door and window—front door, back door, bedroom windows, garage—sometimes multiple times an
hour, particularly at night. Even inside the house, surrounded by locked doors, she felt on guard,
as though danger were seeping through the walls. Silence became unbearable; the absence of sound
felt pregnant with threat, as though something terrible was gathering in the quiet. Her husband
would come home to find her standing at the kitchen window in the dark, watching the street. When he
tried to comfort her, she could barely receive it.

Her avoidance had metastasized into something that governed her entire life. She had stopped seeing
friends, making excuses about being tired or busy, but the truth was that being around people—being
looked at, being in situations she could not control—felt impossible. Before any interaction she did
have to navigate, she would rehearse conversations obsessively, playing out scenarios, trying to
anticipate what others might say or do, trying to script safety into moments that felt inherently
dangerous. This rehearsal would trigger fragmented memories, which would then sharpen her sense of
threat, which would make her more vigilant about potential triggers, which would send her back into
rehearsal. The cycle had become so tight she could barely see the edges of it.

What terrified her most was that she had come to believe something fundamental had changed in her
ability to be safe in the world. The stalking had shown her that her boundaries could be violated,
that her discomfort could be ignored, that she could be watched and hunted. Now, three years later,
she found herself catastrophizing constantly about what might happen to her husband, her teenage
daughter, anyone she loved. The thought that they might experience what she had experienced—that
violation, that helplessness—kept her awake at night. She had finally sought help because her
daughter had asked why she never came to her school events anymore, and Priya realized she had
become so consumed by threat and avoidance that she was disappearing from her own life. What she
wanted most was to reclaim it, but what she feared most was that the person she had been before—the
woman who could move through the world without flinching—was gone.
