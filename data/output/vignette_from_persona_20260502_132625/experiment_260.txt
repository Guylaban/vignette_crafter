# Clinical Vignette: Vinh

Vinh was sixty-three when the memories began to surface with any regularity, though the events
themselves belonged to a different lifetime entirely. He had been twenty-two when Saigon fell in
1975, a conscripted soldier pressed into service during the final days of the war. He had not
volunteered. In the chaos of April, as the North Vietnamese closed in and the American withdrawal
accelerated, Vinh found himself among the last contingent of ARVN troops ordered to hold a
checkpoint on the outskirts of the city. The order came with no expectation of victory—only delay.
On the afternoon of April 29th, Viet Cong sappers overran the position. Vinh was captured along with
three others, forced to the ground by soldiers whose weight pinned him against the dirt and gravel,
his arms wrenched behind his back, a rifle butt pressing into his spine. They held him there for
what felt like hours but was probably minutes, screaming orders he did not fully understand, the
smell of diesel fuel and cordite and human sweat filling his nostrils as he gasped for breath. He
was released before nightfall—a minor miracle he had never fully understood—but the sensation of
being held down, of his body no longer belonging to him, had never truly left.

For decades, Vinh had managed. He emigrated to the United States in 1992, found work at a warehouse
in Long Beach, kept to himself. The memories existed at the periphery of his life, manageable,
contained. But in his mid-seventies, something shifted. A colleague accidentally backed him into a
loading dock door—nothing serious, just a moment of contact, hands on his shoulders to steady
him—and Vinh found himself seized by panic so acute he could not work for three days. The sensation
of being held, even gently, had collapsed fifty years of distance in an instant. His coworkers found
him on the warehouse floor, breathing in short gasps, his supervisor's hands still on his arms, and
in that touch was the entire April afternoon: the weight, the helplessness, the smell of fuel and
sweat and fear.

From that moment, Vinh's world began to contract. Any physical contact became a threat. When his
sister visited from Orange County and reached to embrace him, he stiffened, stepping back with an
explanation that felt both true and false: his back was troubling him, nothing personal. At the
warehouse, he began to avoid the loading dock entirely, taking longer routes through the facility.
His supervisor, concerned, asked to speak with him about his performance. The conversation itself—a
confrontation, however well-intentioned—sent Vinh spiraling. The raised voice, the sense of being
questioned, of conflict, triggered the memory again: the soldiers shouting, the loss of control. He
could see the warehouse office with perfect clarity, but beneath it, overlaid like a photograph, was
the checkpoint. The smell of diesel from the truck bay became the smell of cordite. His heart raced.
He had to leave the conversation, had to step outside, had to breathe.

What frightened Vinh most was not the memories themselves, though they were vivid and overwhelming
when they came—so detailed he could feel the individual stones pressing into his cheek, could taste
the dust. What frightened him was the randomness of their arrival. A smell in the parking lot—the
acrid tang of someone's cigarette mingling with exhaust—could pull the entire event into the present
without warning. He would be standing at his locker, and suddenly he was on the ground. A raised
voice from a supervisor to another worker, a moment of conflict that had nothing to do with him,
could flood his mind with the memory so completely that he had to stop what he was doing, had to sit
down, had to wait for the overwhelming wave of emotion to subside before he could function again.
The vividness of it was almost worse than the triggering itself; he could not convince himself it
was only memory. It felt like it was happening.

Vinh had begun to eat his lunch alone in his car. He had stopped attending the warehouse social
events. He had become, in the careful language of his own thinking, safer in isolation. But
isolation, he had come to understand in the past year, was its own kind of trap. At seventy-six, he
had no family nearby, no close friends, only the structure of work to anchor his days. When his
supervisor suggested a medical leave, Vinh finally made an appointment with a therapist. He did not
expect to be cured. He only hoped, in a way he had not quite articulated to himself, that someone
might help him understand why his body still belonged to 1975, and whether there was any way to
reclaim the five decades in between.
