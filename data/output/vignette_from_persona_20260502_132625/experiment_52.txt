# Clinical Vignette: Priya

Priya was sixty-one years old when she came to the clinic, though the weight she carried made her
seem older. She had worked the fields outside Fresno for nearly forty years—harvesting almonds and
cotton, her hands callused and her back permanently curved from decades of bending. She was married
to Rajesh, a quiet man who worked construction, and they had two adult children who lived in the
city. To anyone who knew her only from the farm, Priya was reliable, efficient, and unobtrusive.
What almost no one knew was that she had been raped in the irrigation ditch behind the fields in
1998, by a man whose face she could still see with perfect clarity when she closed her eyes at
night.

The attack had happened on a late afternoon in September, when she had gone to the far corner of the
property to check the water flow. A man had emerged from behind the equipment shed—someone she did
not know, someone who had no business being there. He had forced her down into the ditch, and what
followed was an assault that lasted, she believed, though she could never be certain, perhaps
fifteen minutes. She had screamed, but the nearest workers were too far away, and the sound of the
irrigation pump had drowned her out. When it was over, he had run toward the road, and she had lain
there in the dirt and water, her clothes torn, her body shaking so violently she could not stand.

For the first few years after the assault, Priya had told almost no one. She had cleaned herself,
changed her clothes, and returned to work the next day. She had not reported it to the police—a
choice born partly from shame, partly from a fear of the police themselves, partly from the belief
that nothing would come of it anyway. She had tried to continue as though the afternoon had been
erased. But the mind, she had come to understand, does not permit such erasure. By 2000, the
symptoms had begun to crystallize into the pattern that now defined her life.

The triggers were specific and inescapable. Dogs, first and foremost: she had heard the dog barking
just before the man had appeared that day, a sound that had seemed, in retrospect, like a warning
she had not heeded. Now, whenever she heard barking—at a neighbor's fence, or in the distance as she
walked to the market—her entire body seized with dread. The memory would come without warning, fully
formed and overwhelming, as though the ditch and the man and the violation were happening again in
real time, her heart hammering against her ribs as though she were still pinned beneath him. Sexual
situations triggered the same response: when Rajesh would reach for her in the night, her body would
react before her mind could catch up. She would flinch, or her breathing would become shallow, or a
wave of nausea would rise in her throat. She had come to dread intimacy with a terror that seemed to
bypass thought altogether—her nervous system had learned to interpret her husband's touch as a
threat, and no amount of reasoning could convince her body otherwise.

The memories themselves had become a kind of torment. They would arrive unbidden, often triggered by
something as mundane as the smell of irrigation water or the sight of a man in a dark shirt. When
they came, they did not arrive as a narrative she could observe from a distance; instead, they
replayed like a film she was forced to watch from inside the screen, her body present in the ditch,
the panic as immediate and real as it had been twenty-five years ago. She would lie awake at night,
her mind cycling through the memory, unable to sleep because some part of her remained convinced
that she needed to stay alert, to watch for danger, to ensure that nothing like this could happen
again. When Rajesh would move suddenly in bed, or when the neighbor's dog would bark at three in the
morning, she would startle violently, her body bracing for attack. The hypervigilance had become so
habitual that she could no longer distinguish between genuine threat and the phantom danger her
nervous system had been trained to expect.

Over the years, this vigilance had begun to erode her life. She had stopped going to the far fields,
which meant her income had decreased and her work had become more limited. She had begun to avoid
being alone, yet she also could not bear to be touched. She had become withdrawn from Rajesh, who
loved her but could not understand why his wife flinched from his hand. She came to the clinic
because her daughter had insisted, because she was exhausted in a way that sleep could no longer
touch, and because she was afraid—not just of the memory, but of the possibility that she might live
the rest of her life imprisoned by it.
