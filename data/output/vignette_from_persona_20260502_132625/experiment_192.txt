# Carmen's Story

Carmen Rodríguez was fifty-seven when the hurricane came. She had built a quiet life in her adopted
city—a thriving physical therapy practice, a small house three blocks from the beach that she had
bought after her divorce, a routine of early mornings and patient care that felt, finally, like it
was hers alone. She had grown up in Puerto Rico and had moved to Miami as a young woman, and the
ocean had always been a comfort to her, a reminder of home. On the morning of the storm, she had not
evacuated. The warnings felt distant, abstract. She had weathered hurricanes before.

The water came at dawn. Not the way she had imagined it—not a wall, but a creeping, then a rushing,
then a drowning of everything. Her bedroom was on the first floor. She remembered the sound first: a
roar that was not wind but something alive, something with intention. She remembered her hands
scrabbling at the headboard, trying to pull herself up as the water rose around her chest, her neck.
She remembered the moment she could not touch the bottom anymore, the moment her body became
weightless and useless in her own home. The water was brown and cold and filled with things—wood,
glass, the debris of other people's lives—and she was one of those things now, suspended in it. A
neighbor's hand had found hers in the dark water. Together they had clung to a piece of her kitchen
counter until the water receded enough for them to wade out.

She had survived. Her house had not. In the weeks after, as she moved into a rental apartment and
began the work of rebuilding, something had shifted inside her in a way that no amount of physical
reconstruction could touch. She returned to work because she had to, because her patients needed her
and because sitting alone in the apartment made the trembling worse. But the clinic, which had been
her sanctuary, had become a place where her body no longer obeyed her. She would be working with a
patient on their range of motion, her hands steady and professional, when a sound would startle
her—a door closing, a car backfiring outside—and her heart would explode in her chest. Her breath
would come short. In those moments, the memory would not come as a picture but as a pure sensation:
the cold, the weightlessness, the absolute certainty that she was about to die. Sometimes it arrived
as a wave of grief so overwhelming that she had to excuse herself to the bathroom and lock the door.

The triggers had multiplied and entangled themselves into her daily life in ways that made no
logical sense to her. Hearing the name "Maria"—her sister's name, the name of the hurricane—could
send her into a spiral of nausea and dread that lasted hours. The smell of wet concrete, the sound
of rain on the roof, even the sight of a large puddle in a parking lot would pull her back into that
underwater moment, her body reacting as though the danger were happening again. Water itself had
become something she could not bear. She had stopped showering, taking only quick baths with the
bathroom door open, unable to let water cover her face. Her patients had begun to notice that she
seemed distant, that she would sometimes grip the treatment table as though bracing for impact, that
her hands occasionally trembled. One regular patient had asked if she was all right, and Carmen had
nearly wept at the kindness in the question.

What frightened her most was the quality of the memory itself. When it surfaced—triggered by a
sound, a smell, the sight of water—it felt as though it belonged to someone else, as though she were
watching a film of another woman's drowning rather than reliving her own. Yet the emotion was hers
entirely. She would feel the overwhelming grief, the terror, the sense of absolute abandonment, but
the image would be distant, almost unreal, as though her mind had split the experience into pieces
that no longer fit together. Sometimes the emotion came without any image at all—just a crushing
weight in her chest, a certainty of death, with no clear picture of why.

Over the months, Carmen had developed ways of managing these intrusions, though she understood now
that they were not really managing at all. When the memory threatened to surface, when she felt the
nausea rising or heard an unexpected loud sound that sent her into high alert, she would
deliberately leave her body. She had learned to dissociate—to blank out, to float above herself, to
make the feeling go away by refusing to feel it. For a few minutes or sometimes an hour, this
worked. But the dissociation itself had become a trigger. The more she tried to escape the memory by
disconnecting from her body, the more fragmented the memory became, and the more likely it was to
return as pure emotion without context, catching her off guard. The cycle had tightened around her.

She had stopped going out. It had happened gradually—first avoiding the beach, then avoiding any
street that led toward the water, then avoiding the clinic on rainy days, then avoiding the clinic
altogether, calling in sick more often than she could justify. The safe zone had shrunk to her
apartment, to the bedroom on the second floor where she felt some distance from the ground level,
from the possibility of water. She had not been to a restaurant in six months. She had not attended
her sister's birthday dinner. Her sleep had become fragmented and haunted; she would lie awake
listening to every sound, her body rigid with vigilance, or she would fall into a shallow sleep
broken by nightmares of drowning. The isolation had deepened the grief. She dwelt constantly on how
she used to be—the woman who had loved the ocean, who had been capable and present, who had not been
afraid. That woman felt as distant now as the memory itself, belonging to someone else's life.

It was her ex-husband who had finally pushed her to seek help. He had run into her at a pharmacy and
had been shocked by her appearance—the weight loss, the haunted eyes. He had told her, not unkindly,
that she could not live like this. Carmen had resisted at first, but the truth was that she was
exhausted. The avoidance had cost her her practice—she had taken a leave of absence that was
stretching into permanent. It had cost her her relationships, her sense of self, her ability to
imagine a future. What she wanted most was to feel safe again, to be able to hear her sister's name
without her stomach turning, to stand in a shower without panic. What she feared most was that the
woman she had been before the water came was truly gone, and that all the dissociation and avoidance
in the world could not bring her back.
