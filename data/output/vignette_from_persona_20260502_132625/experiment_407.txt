# James

James was thirty-eight when the police stopped him. He'd been driving the same bus route for twelve
years—the 7 AM crosstown that picked up shift workers, students, and regulars he'd come to know by
sight. He was respected on that route, the kind of driver who waited for the elderly woman with the
walker, who knew which passengers preferred the back seat. On a Tuesday morning in March, he was
pulled over by two state troopers while parked at the downtown terminal. They said there had been a
report—a passenger complaint about suspicious behavior. James was confused; he'd done nothing wrong.
The officers approached with hands near their belts, voices clipped and authoritative. One demanded
his license and registration. The other stood at the passenger window, watching him. James's hands
shook as he reached for the glove compartment. The officer at his window suddenly shouted, "Keep
your hands visible!" James froze. What followed was a search of his vehicle, aggressive questioning,
and a pat-down conducted in front of his coworkers and a gathering of passengers. The complaint
turned out to be baseless—a misunderstanding, a case of mistaken identity. The officers apologized,
almost perfunctorily, and left. But something in James had fractured that morning. He felt exposed,
humiliated, and for the first time in his life, he understood that his presence alone could be
interpreted as a threat.

In the months that followed, James began to notice things he'd never paid attention to before.
Police cars on the street made his chest tighten. If he saw an officer at a bus stop, his palms
would sweat. The memory of that morning—the officer's shout, the feeling of being trapped in his own
vehicle—would surface without warning, often at the worst moments: mid-route, surrounded by
passengers, unable to escape. When the memory came, his body would flood with adrenaline, and he'd
grip the steering wheel so hard his knuckles went white. To manage these moments, James had
developed a habit of turning up the radio or the in-cabin audio system, flooding the space with
noise and distraction. The loudness helped push the memory away, at least temporarily, though it
left him feeling raw and exhausted.

Over time, James's understanding of the world had shifted in ways he couldn't quite articulate. He
had come to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous—that authority figures, even those
meant to protect, could turn on you without cause. The police had shown him that. This conviction
had begun to seep into his thinking about people more broadly. If the police couldn't be trusted,
who could? His coworkers, once friendly, now felt like potential witnesses to his humiliation. His
sister, who worked in city administration, suddenly seemed complicit in a system that had wronged
him. He had largely withdrawn from the community center where he'd once played basketball on
weekends, telling himself it wasn't safe anymore, though what exactly he feared remained vague. The
loss felt profound—he'd lost not just his sense of safety but his sense of belonging.

This erosion of trust fed his avoidance. When passengers tried to engage him in conversation, James
would keep his eyes forward, offering only curt responses. When he thought about attending his
nephew's school event or going to the local diner where he used to meet friends, he'd find reasons
not to go. He avoided people, period. The thought of being around others, of being seen, had become
intolerable. His apartment had become his refuge, though even there he wasn't at peace. He'd begun
checking the locks obsessively—the front door, the back door, the windows—sometimes five or six
times an evening. His mind wouldn't settle. At work, he found it harder to concentrate on the road;
his thoughts scattered between hypervigilance and dread. When passengers asked him questions, he'd
snap at them, irritable and sharp in ways that surprised even him. One regular, an elderly man he'd
chatted with for years, asked if he was okay. James nearly bit his head off, then felt a wave of
shame that made him want to hide.

The avoidance and isolation had created a vicious loop. The less he engaged with people, the more
his conviction that they couldn't be trusted seemed confirmed. The more he believed the world was
dangerous, the more he retreated, checked his locks, turned up the noise. And each time he
encountered a police car, or someone mentioned authority, or he caught sight of a uniform, the
memory would crash through again, sending him scrambling for his maladaptive strategies—the music,
the isolation, the hiding. By the time James finally called a therapist, he'd been on medical leave
from his job for two weeks. His supervisor had suggested it gently, noting that passengers had
complained about his behavior, that he seemed "on edge." James knew he was falling apart. What
terrified him most wasn't just the memory of that morning, but the person he was becoming—isolated,
angry, trapped in a cage of his own making. He desperately wanted to believe that people were
trustworthy again, that he could return to his bus route and his life. But right now, the thought of
facing either felt impossible.
