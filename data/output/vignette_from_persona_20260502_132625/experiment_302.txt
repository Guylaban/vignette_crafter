# Urszula's Containment

Urszula was fifty-one when she walked into the clinic, her handbag held tightly against her chest,
her eyes tracking the movement of the receptionist's hand as it reached for the appointment book.
The gesture—sudden, unexpected—made her flinch. She had learned to brace for the unpredictable, and
after three years, unpredictability felt less like a fact of life and more like a threat that could
materialize at any moment. She was a civil servant, had been for nearly twenty years, working in an
administrative office where routine and procedure governed almost everything. That structure had
been her anchor. Then came the morning of the chemical spill.

It was February 2021, a Tuesday, when the seal on the storage container in the basement failed.
Urszula had been retrieving archived files when she noticed the smell—sharp, chemical, wrong—and
then the burning in her throat, the sudden fog in her vision. She had stumbled upstairs, gasping,
and the evacuation that followed was chaos: sirens, paramedics in masks, her colleagues scattered,
the building cordoned off. The hospital visit afterward was a blur of incomprehensible medical
jargon and strangers in uniforms asking questions she couldn't quite process. What had been exposed,
for how long, what symptoms might emerge—no one seemed to have clear answers. The uncertainty had
burrowed into her, and it had never left.

Now, three years later, Urszula found herself confined to a shrinking world. The office had reopened
months ago, but she could not return to the basement; the very thought of descending those stairs
triggered a cascade of physical symptoms—her heart racing, her skin prickling with phantom chemical
burn. The unpredictability of her own body's responses had become as frightening as the original
exposure. When unexpected sounds erupted—a car backfiring, a colleague's raised voice—she was
instantly alert, her nervous system convinced that danger was imminent. These moments of alarm were
followed by an exhausting vigilance that lasted hours. She had come to believe that only her home,
and perhaps her husband's office building where she occasionally sat in a familiar conference room,
were truly safe. Everywhere else carried risk.

The triggers and the threat had become inseparable. When her boss called an unscheduled meeting,
Urszula's mind raced not just with anxiety but with an almost physical sensation of danger—as if the
unpredictability of the agenda itself was a form of exposure. Loud, incomprehensible chatter in
crowded spaces sent her into a state of high alert; she could not distinguish between ordinary noise
and something portentous, and that confusion felt like a warning sign. These moments of threat
awareness then activated an intricate system of avoidance and preparation that had come to dominate
her days.

Before any interaction—a phone call to the utility company, a conversation with her husband about
the week ahead—Urszula rehearsed what she would say. She wrote scripts, practiced inflection,
anticipated objections. This rehearsal gave her a fragile sense of control in a world that felt
fundamentally unpredictable. But the more she rehearsed, the more she noticed that any deviation
from her script, any unexpected response from the other person, would destabilize her. She had also
begun to avoid physical contact entirely. Her husband, who had been patient and loving, had learned
not to touch her without explicit permission. Even then, she often flinched. The prospect of a
medical examination—something her doctor had gently suggested—was unbearable; hospitals meant loss
of control, meant strangers in masks, meant the possibility of incomprehensible information
delivered in a clinical voice that echoed the paramedics from that morning.

What made everything worse was that she could not talk about the event coherently. When her
therapist or her husband asked her to describe what had happened, Urszula would begin—"I was in the
basement, and I smelled..."—and then the thread would dissolve. The memory itself seemed fragmented,
inaccessible when she tried to summon it deliberately. She could feel the sensation of burning,
could recall the panic, but the narrative collapsed. She could not put words to the nonverbal terror
that lived in her body. This inability to articulate the trauma meant she could not fully explain to
anyone why she was so afraid, why she had become so constrained. The memory's resistance to language
then fed back into her threat perception; if she could not understand or explain what had happened,
how could she predict or prevent it from happening again? And that sense of unpredictability, that
inability to make sense of her own experience, would trigger another round of rehearsal, another
tightening of her safe zone, another moment of flinching at a stranger's approach.

She had come to the clinic because her husband had finally insisted, his own patience fraying.
Urszula was terrified that nothing could be done, that she would spend the rest of her life
rehearsing conversations in a handful of safe rooms, unable to touch or be touched, unable to
explain the thing that had broken her.
