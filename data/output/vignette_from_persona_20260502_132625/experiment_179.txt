# Aroha's Story

Aroha had always been the kind of person who lit up a room. Her colleagues at the university
remembered her that way—animated in seminars, quick with laughter, the sort of professor who stayed
late to help struggling students find their footing. Her wife, Mere, had fallen in love with exactly
that brightness. But that was before the collision on the Pacific Coast Highway, before the moment
when everything that felt solid about her life simply shattered.

It was a Tuesday afternoon in March, six months ago. Aroha was driving back to campus after a
department meeting in Los Angeles, lost in thought about a lecture she needed to revise. The road
was clear, the sun high. She never saw the truck until it was already crossing into her lane. The
impact came from the driver's side—metal screaming, glass exploding inward, her body thrown sideways
as the car spun. She remembered the smell of burning rubber and the terrible silence afterward. She
remembered a paramedic's voice, very far away. She remembered her own hand shaking. Then the
memories fractured. There were gaps—blank stretches where she should have a clear picture but found
only white noise. When she tried to reconstruct what happened, the pieces wouldn't cohere. The
moment before the impact felt sharp and vivid, almost crystalline, but the collision itself came in
disconnected flashes: the sound of the horn, a flash of red metal, a sensation of weightlessness.
The aftermath was a blur she could not quite grasp or string together into a coherent narrative.

In the weeks that followed, Aroha returned to work. She told people she was fine, that she'd been
lucky. But something had shifted in the architecture of her mind. She had come to believe that the
world was fundamentally unsafe, that her body could fail her, that control was an illusion. These
convictions sat heavy in her chest. At night, she would jolt awake, her heart hammering, gripped by
the urgent need to check that the doors were locked, that Mere was still breathing beside her. Some
nights she didn't sleep at all, just lay rigid in the dark, hypervigilant to every creak of the
house, every distant car engine. This constant scanning for threat had become second nature to
her—an exhausting, unshakeable baseline. The threat felt imminent and real, not something that had
happened in the past but something that could happen again at any moment.

The fragments of her broken memory fed this vigilance. Because she couldn't hold the event in her
mind as a coherent whole, it never felt finished or resolved. Instead, the disconnected pieces kept
surfacing—a flash of the truck, the smell of smoke, the sensation of impact—each fragment arriving
without warning, each one triggering a fresh surge of fear that the crash was happening again. Her
body would respond as if she were still in danger, her heart rate spiking, her breath coming shallow
and fast. These moments felt real in a way that her waking life increasingly did not. Because the
threat felt so immediate and omnipresent, Aroha found herself hyperaware of anything that might
signal danger. When a colleague mentioned a car accident he'd witnessed on the news, Aroha felt her
chest tighten. When she saw a car that resembled the truck—even from a distance—her pulse quickened.
Crash imagery, accident discussions, anything that brushed against the event, triggered a cascade of
physiological alarm. Her mind had learned to scan for these cues the way it scanned for physical
threats.

The meaning had drained out of everything. She used to be happy, genuinely happy, and she could no
longer access that person. She felt hollowed out, as if the collision had not only broken her body
but had taken something essential from her sense of self. Nothing felt worth doing anymore. Her
lectures felt hollow; she went through the motions but couldn't find the engagement that had once
come so naturally. Mere tried to reach her, but Aroha felt increasingly isolated, as if she belonged
nowhere—not in her marriage, not in her profession, not in her own skin. This conviction that she
was fundamentally changed, that she could never return to who she'd been, only deepened her sense of
threat. If she was damaged in this irreversible way, then anything could happen. If the world had
already taken so much from her, what was left to protect? And when she tried to talk to Mere about
what had happened, the words would scatter. She would start to explain, then lose the thread
entirely, unable to finish a sentence, unable to make the experience coherent even in language. The
fragmentation kept it alive, kept it present, kept it dangerous.

Aroha has finally asked for help. She sits in the office with a therapist now, exhausted and afraid,
wondering if she will ever feel safe again, or if the person she used to be is simply gone.
