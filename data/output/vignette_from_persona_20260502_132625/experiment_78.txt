# Khalid

Khalid was twenty-two when it happened—a night in late September that fractured the person he had
believed himself to be. He had gone to a friend's apartment in a neighborhood he didn't know well,
somewhere on the north side of the city. The party was crowded, loud, the kind of evening that
should have been unremarkable. He remembered the smell of cologne and spilled beer, the press of
bodies, the way someone had handed him a drink. The next hours existed in fragments: a man he didn't
recognize leading him to a bedroom, his own confusion turning to resistance, then to the crushing
realization that resistance was not stopping what was happening. He remembered the smell most
vividly—a specific cologne, sharp and suffocating, that would later become a trigger he could not
escape.

In the weeks that followed, Khalid told no one. He returned to his job as an auditor at a mid-sized
firm, where precision and control were the currency of his work. But something had shifted
irrevocably. When he caught the faintest trace of that cologne in the office—on a colleague, in the
elevator, once even on his own clothes after a dry cleaning mishap—his body would seize. His heart
would race, his breath would shallow, and he would find himself scanning the room for exits, his
muscles coiled as though danger were imminent. The smell had become a trigger, and it led him almost
automatically to position himself near doorways during meetings, to sit at the edge of conference
tables, to engineer routes through the office that kept him closest to the stairs. He had come to
believe that if he could just stay near an escape, if he could just maintain control over his
physical space, he might prevent something like that from happening again.

But the triggers multiplied. When a colleague made a casual remark about his ex-girlfriend—a woman
Khalid had dated briefly before the assault—his mind spiraled. The comment was innocent, but it
yanked him back into the apartment, into the smell, into the helplessness. When he witnessed someone
being unkind to a junior staff member, something about that moral violation of trust and safety
would send him spiraling into dark comparisons: *Someone else would never have let themselves be put
in that position. Someone else would have fought harder, gotten away, not ended up there at all.*
The triggers were not just sensory anymore; they were everywhere, embedded in ordinary social
interactions, and each one fed a corrosive sense of shame and humiliation that had become the
texture of his daily existence.

To manage the triggers, Khalid began to withdraw. He started wearing loose, dark clothing—oversized
sweaters and button-ups that concealed his frame—convinced that if he made himself invisible,
unremarkable, he might avoid drawing attention that could lead to danger. He declined social
invitations, telling colleagues he was busy with family matters. He avoided the downtown area where
the assault had occurred, and then began avoiding any unfamiliar places, because how could he assess
danger in a space he didn't know? His body had become a constant sentinel. His shoulders were
perpetually tight, his jaw clenched. Sudden movements by others—a colleague reaching for something
on a shelf, someone turning quickly in the hallway—would send a jolt of adrenaline through him, and
his mind would flood with threat calculations. This hypervigilance fed his conviction that the world
was unsafe, that his body was in constant danger, which in turn made him avoid evaluation situations
where people might look at him closely, might notice his discomfort, might judge him.

The maladaptive strategies that had felt necessary—the concealing clothes, the exit positioning, the
avoidance of unfamiliar places—only deepened the cycle. Each time he avoided a situation, he
reinforced his belief that the world was dangerous and that he was powerless within it. When he
stayed home instead of attending his firm's annual retreat, he felt the temporary relief of safety,
but it was followed by shame that he was not the confident, capable person he used to be. He used to
be happy. That felt like a lie now, a memory of someone else. The person he was now was permanently
marked by what had happened, humiliated beyond recovery, and the strategies he had adopted to
protect himself only seemed to tighten the knot. Three months after the assault, Khalid finally
called a therapist. He sat in the waiting room, positioned near the door, and told himself he was
seeking help because he wanted his life back. What he was really afraid of was that the person who
had been happy, who had been unburdened, was gone for good.
