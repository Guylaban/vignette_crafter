# Rajesh

Rajesh had been a paramedic for thirty-four years. He knew the weight of a stretcher, the smell of
antiseptic, the particular silence that falls over a crew when they know they've done everything
they can. On a Tuesday evening in March, responding to a chemical spill at an industrial facility
thirty miles north of the city, he had learned what it meant to breathe poison. The warehouse had
been poorly ventilated, the drums inadequately labeled. When he and his partner arrived, the acrid
fumes were already thick in the air—something caustic and alien that burned his throat and made his
eyes stream. He'd worked through it, as he always did, moving a contaminated worker to the
decontamination area, stripping off his own gear afterward in the parking lot. By the next morning,
his lungs felt scraped raw. Within seventy-two hours, he was in the hospital with chemical
pneumonitis, struggling to breathe, certain he was dying. He recovered, physically. The doctors said
he was lucky. But luck, Rajesh came to understand, was a relative thing.

Within weeks, the workplace had become a minefield. The smell of hand sanitizer in the ambulance
station—something he'd never particularly noticed before—now sent his heart racing. The darkness of
the equipment bay at night triggered a cascade of dread that made him grip the door frame. And the
worst was the olfactory phantom: the smell of alcohol, which bore some chemical similarity to the
fumes, would appear without warning—on a colleague's breath, in a cleaning solution, wafting from
someone's hand sanitizer—and suddenly he was back in that warehouse, lungs burning, certain that
this time he wouldn't survive it. Each trigger yanked him back into the full sensory nightmare, the
terror as immediate as if the exposure were happening again.

The memories themselves had become unbearable. When he lay in bed at night and the memory surfaced,
he was flooded with such overwhelming emotion that he had to get up, turn on every light, find
something—anything—to do. He couldn't sit with it. The feeling was too large, too raw. So he began
to structure his life around never being alone with his thoughts. He volunteered for extra shifts,
filled his evenings with dinner parties and social gatherings, kept the television on constantly.
His wife, Priya, initially supportive, began to notice that he couldn't sit through a meal without
checking his phone, that he seemed almost frantic in his need for company. The rumination had
started too—lying awake, running through the scenario obsessively, convinced that if he'd only
arrived five minutes earlier, if he'd insisted on better ventilation checks, if he'd worn a
different mask, none of this would have happened. The more he ruminated about how the event could
have been prevented, the more agitated he became, and the more desperate he felt to escape into
socializing, into the noise and distraction of other people's voices.

But avoidance had its own logic, one that tightened around him like a knot. Every time he stayed
late at the station to avoid going home, every time he filled his schedule so completely that there
was no space for silence, he was reinforcing the belief that his thoughts were genuinely dangerous,
that the memory was something he couldn't survive alone. The constant socializing became its own
kind of exposure: he'd be at a friend's house, someone would open a bottle of wine, and the smell
would hit him before he could brace himself, catapulting him back into that warehouse. And now,
trapped in a room full of people, he couldn't show his distress. He'd excuse himself to the
bathroom, close his eyes, and wait for his heart to stop racing. The avoidance of hospitals and
ambulances—the very spaces that defined his profession—had become impossible to maintain while still
working. He'd started making excuses to stay off certain routes, to avoid the emergency department
when dropping off patients. His colleagues had noticed. The respect that had taken three decades to
build was beginning to fracture.

What finally brought Rajesh to seek help was a moment of clarity that terrified him more than the
memories themselves. He realized he was becoming a stranger to his own life. His marriage was
strained; his work performance was deteriorating; and the avoidance strategies that had seemed like
survival had become their own kind of slow suffocation. He was sixty-one years old, and he was
afraid that if he didn't find a way to break the cycle, he would lose everything he'd built—not to
the chemical exposure, but to the architecture of his own escape.
