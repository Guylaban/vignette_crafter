# Thom's War

Thom arrived at the clinic on a Tuesday morning in November, moving with the careful deliberation of
a man who has learned not to trust his own body. At seventy-four, his hands bore the permanent
scarring of forty years at the welding torch—dark, leathered creases that mapped decades of honest
work. His wife, Mei, sat beside him in the waiting room, her hand resting on his arm in a gesture
that suggested both affection and something closer to vigilance. When asked what had brought him in,
Thom's jaw tightened. He had not wanted to come. It was Mei who had insisted, after finding him one
morning at three o'clock standing in the kitchen with all the lights on, unable to explain why he
could not sleep, why he needed to see the corners of the room.

In 1968, when Thom was twenty-two, he had been living in Saigon with his family—his father a
merchant, his mother a seamstress. The city had felt normal enough until the day it became something
else entirely. During the Tet Offensive, shells had begun falling without warning. Thom had been in
the street when the first volley came, the sound so immense and unexpected that his body had simply
shut down for a moment. He remembered the smell of concrete dust, the screaming, the way his own
legs would not move fast enough. He had made it to a doorway, but others had not. A girl—he could
not quite hold her face in his mind, which troubled him—had been standing where he had been standing
moments before. What remained were fragments: the sound, the dust, the terrible geometry of what was
left. He had survived. That was the fact, simple and unadorable. But something about his presence in
that street, his failure to move faster, his being in the wrong place—these thoughts had
crystallized into a conviction he could not shake: he should have done something differently.

In the decades since, Thom had built a life. He had married Mei in 1972, emigrated to California in
1975, worked steadily as a welder, raised two children who now had children of their own. On the
surface, he had moved forward. But the war had never truly released him. For years the symptoms had
been manageable—a startle response that Mei had learned to accommodate, a preference for staying
home in the evenings. But in the past eighteen months, something had shifted. The hypervigilance
that had once been background noise had become consuming. Thom found himself unable to concentrate
at work, his mind fragmenting into pieces whenever he tried to focus on a weld. He lay awake at
night, his body convinced that sleep was a luxury he could not afford, that remaining alert was the
only thing standing between his family and disaster. The need to stay watchful had become almost
sacred to him—a penance, perhaps, or a ward against the guilt that lived in his chest.

The triggers had multiplied and sharpened. Car sounds—the backfire of an engine, the screech of
brakes—would send him rigid, his breath catching in his throat as though his lungs had forgotten
their function. When this happened, he would reach for distraction immediately, forcing his mind
onto something concrete: the specifications of a job, the names of his grandchildren, anything to
pull himself away from the feeling of being twenty-two again and trapped. The shortness of breath
itself had become a trigger, a physical reminder that his body was not entirely his own, and when he
felt it coming on, he would retreat into silence rather than try to explain it. Mei had learned not
to ask. He also found himself avoiding the open spaces around their neighborhood—the park where they
used to walk together, the community center where his welding club met. These places felt exposed,
vulnerable in a way that made his skin crawl. He stayed indoors more often now, and when he did
venture out, he moved quickly from one enclosed space to another, his eyes scanning for exits, for
cover, for anything that might offer protection.

What made the avoidance feel necessary, what drove it deeper each week, was a conviction that had
hardened over the years into something approaching certainty: that what had happened to him was
profoundly unjust, and that no one had ever paid for it. The soldiers who had ordered the shelling
had never faced consequences. The city had been rebuilt. The world had moved on. His own role in his
survival—the accident of being in the doorway rather than the street—had come to feel like something
he should have controlled, as though his guilt could somehow be redeemed through perfect vigilance
now. When he tried to talk about any of this, the words felt impossible. He would start to explain
to Mei why he could not go to his granddaughter's school event, and the weight of the injustice, the
unfairness, the absence of accountability, would rise in his throat and choke him silent. So he said
nothing. He redirected his thoughts. He stayed home.

The memory itself had become strange to him in a way that made everything worse. He could recall the
sound, the dust, the doorway, but the girl's face remained absent, and other details had become
fuzzy, unreliable. Sometimes he wondered if he was remembering what had actually happened or if his
mind had rewritten the event so many times that the original had been lost. What disturbed him most
was the sense that the memory did not quite feel like his own—it felt like something he had been
told about, something that had happened to someone else, yet he carried its weight in his body every
moment. The extreme nature of what he had witnessed made him doubt his own recollection sometimes.
Had it really been that terrible? The intensity of his current fear suggested yes, but his
fragmented memory suggested that perhaps his mind was playing tricks, catastrophizing, making him
suffer for something that might not have been as bad as he believed. This uncertainty only deepened
his need to stay alert, to keep watch, because if he could not trust his own mind to hold the truth
of what had happened, then perhaps remaining hypervigilant was the only way to prevent it from
happening again. The gaps in his memory, the sense of alienation from the event, the difficulty
believing it had been real—all of these pushed him further into avoidance, because acknowledging the
memory meant acknowledging the injustice, and acknowledging the injustice meant confronting the
possibility that his guilt might be irrational, unfounded, a lie his mind had been telling him for
fifty-six years. It was easier not to think about it at all. It was easier to stay awake, to avoid
open spaces, to say nothing.

By the time he arrived at the clinic, Thom had become a man increasingly confined by his own
defenses. His work had suffered; he had taken early retirement at seventy because he could no longer
concentrate long enough to complete a weld safely. His relationship with Mei, once warm and easy,
had become strained by his silence and his refusal to engage with the world. He had not seen his
grandchildren in four months because the school events, the parks, the open spaces where families
gathered, all felt like traps. He was certain that if he let his guard down, something catastrophic
would happen—not just to him, but to everyone he loved. The unfairness of what had happened to him
in Saigon, the absence of justice, the weight of his own suspected complicity in his survival, had
fused with his hypervigilance into a self-perpetuating cycle that was slowly erasing the life he had
built. What frightened Thom most now was not the memory itself, but the possibility that he had
already lost everything that mattered by trying to protect it.
