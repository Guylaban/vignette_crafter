# Clinical Vignette: Priya

Priya was sixty-six years old when she came to the clinic, though her posture and the careful way
she moved suggested someone older, someone who had learned to take up less space. She had been
working in retail for thirty-eight years, a steady job that had sustained her through her marriage
to Rajesh and, after his death five years ago, had kept her anchored to routine and purpose. That
anchor had come loose two years before she sought help.

The assault happened on a March evening in 2021, in the storage room behind the clothing store where
she worked. A man came in as she was closing—someone she did not recognize, someone who had drifted
past the registers and slipped through the back door while she was restocking. He was younger than
her, with a smell like stale cigarettes and something chemical, and he moved with the kind of
deliberate slowness that told her immediately what was about to happen. The attack lasted perhaps
ten minutes, though Priya could never say for certain. What remained were not minutes but moments:
his hand over her mouth, the concrete wall against her back, the particular angle of the fluorescent
light above her head, the smell—that smell—which had seemed to coat everything, including her skin.
She had not reported it to police. She had not told anyone for six months. When she finally told her
daughter, Priya had felt something shift in the family's regard for her, as though she had become a
different kind of person, someone marked. Nothing in her life felt meaningful anymore. The job that
had defined her, the routines that had steadied her, the sense that she had built something—all of
it had collapsed into a kind of gray meaninglessness.

The first few months after the assault, Priya had tried to return to work. But the storage room had
become impossible. More than that, the store itself had become a place where her body would not
cooperate. A customer would enter who was male, someone she did not know, and her heart would begin
to race. She found herself unable to be alone with any man—a coworker, a customer, even the
owner—without her chest tightening, without the urge to move away becoming overwhelming. Being alone
with men had become a trigger that pulled her back into the storage room, and when she was pulled
back, the memories came with a clarity that was almost photographic. She could see the light
fixture. She could see the exact expression on his face. She could see the position of his hands.
The memories did not feel like memories; they felt like looking at photographs, vivid and
unchanging, except that some of them were missing entirely. There were blank spaces in the
sequence—gaps where her mind had simply refused to record what was happening—and those blanks were
somehow worse than the clarity, because they left her with the feeling that she did not even have
the full story of what had been done to her.

After three months of not being able to work, Priya had stopped going in. She told her manager she
was ill. She told her daughter she needed time. What she meant was that she could not bear the
unpredictability of who might walk through the door, the constant vigilance required to know whether
she would be alone with a man, the way that particular kind of fear would trigger the memories again
and again—the photographs in her mind playing in sequence, the smell returning even though she was
safe, the feeling of unfairness rising in her chest like something that would choke her. What
happened to her was deeply unfair, and nothing had been done about it. No one had been caught. No
one had been held accountable. And every time a man approached her, every time she caught the smell
of smoke or fire on someone's clothes, every time she witnessed someone behaving in a way that felt
like a violation of decency—someone being rude to a cashier, someone cutting in line, someone
speaking harshly—the memories would flood back, and with them came the certainty that she could not
deal with even the slightest upset. The smallest frustration would send her into a state of near-
collapse. She had become, in her own mind, someone fragile and broken, someone who had lost the
capacity to manage ordinary life.

She came to the clinic because her daughter had insisted, because the isolation had become complete,
because she had begun to wonder if there was any point in continuing at all. What she wanted most
was to feel that her life still meant something. What she feared most was that it never would again.
