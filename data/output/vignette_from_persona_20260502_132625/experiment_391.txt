# Clinical Vignette: Thao

Thao was sixty-two when his youngest son, Minh, drowned during a fishing expedition off the coast of
North Carolina. It was a summer morning in 2019, the kind of bright, ordinary day that Thao had
known for forty years at sea. Minh, then twenty-eight, had wanted to learn the family trade—had
insisted on it, in fact, over his mother's protests. They were checking nets three miles offshore
when the current shifted. Minh, who had never been a strong swimmer despite growing up in a fishing
family, lost his footing on the slick deck. Thao saw him go over the rail. He heard the splash. He
dove, but the water was dark and cold, and by the time the Coast Guard arrived, Minh had been under
for nearly ten minutes. They recovered his body two days later. At the funeral, Thao sat motionless
in his dark suit, feeling nothing—or rather, feeling as though something essential had been hollowed
out and replaced with a weight that would never lift.

In the months after Minh's death, Thao returned to fishing. His wife, Linh, had begged him not to.
But the boat was how he had always made sense of the world, and the alternative—sitting at home with
his grief—felt impossible. What he did not anticipate was that the water itself had become a
landscape of danger. Within weeks of going back out, he began to experience sudden, overwhelming
physical reactions. The smell of salt spray would trigger a racing heart and a sensation of drowning
that lasted for hours. Once, when a younger crew member slipped on deck, Thao's hands began to shake
so violently he had to sit down, gripping the rail until his knuckles whitened. He was certain
something terrible was about to happen again—that the sea was waiting to take someone else, perhaps
him, perhaps one of the men who depended on him. His sleep deteriorated. He would lie awake beside
Linh, his body rigid with tension, replaying the moment of the splash over and over, wondering if
there was something he should have seen, something he could have done differently. The anger came
too, sudden and hot—at Minh for being careless, at himself for allowing his son on the boat, at God
for the senselessness of it all.

What made everything worse was the certainty that had settled into Thao's mind: people could not be
trusted to keep themselves safe, not even—or especially not—the ones you loved most. He had come to
believe that vigilance was the only thing standing between his family and disaster. His wife had
survived; his older son had survived. But they could be taken at any moment, and there was nothing
he could do to prevent it. This belief hardened into something almost paranoid. When Linh mentioned
going to the grocery store, Thao felt a spike of dread—what if something happened while she was
driving? When his older son called late, Thao's first thought was that something had happened to
him. These fears fed the physical reactivity that now governed his days. A car horn outside the
house would send his heart into his throat. The sight of water—even the rain-filled gutter beside
their driveway—could trigger that suffocating sensation of drowning, that certainty that death was
close. And each time his body reacted this way, it confirmed what he now believed: the world was
fundamentally unsafe, and the people around him were vulnerable in ways he could not control. He
felt dead inside most of the time, as though the part of him that had died with Minh was the only
part that had ever truly been alive. The guilt was relentless—he had survived, and Minh had not.
Why? What had he done to deserve another day when his son would never have another?

Thao stopped going out on the boat regularly, though he could not admit this to his fishing crew.
Instead, he found reasons to stay ashore—equipment repairs, administrative tasks, things that kept
him from the water. His income dropped noticeably. Linh began working extra shifts at the hospital
where she was a nurse, and the tension between them grew thick and silent. He withdrew from the
Vietnamese community gatherings they had attended for decades, afraid of the questions, the
sympathy, the inevitable conversations that would circle back to Minh. Now, at sixty-two, Thao sits
in the waiting room of a therapist's office, referred by his primary care physician who had noted
his blood pressure climbing steadily over the past three years. He is not sure why he agreed to
come. Part of him believes that talking about what happened will only make it worse, that
acknowledging the danger he feels will somehow invite it closer. But another part of him—the part
that watches Linh's exhaustion, that hears the strain in his older son's voice when he calls—knows
that something has to change. He is terrified that if he loosens his grip on the vigilance that has
kept him alive, something catastrophic will happen. And he is equally terrified that he will feel
this way forever, dead inside, unable to trust that anyone he loves will make it through another
day.
