# Clinical Vignette: Marco

Marco was twenty-three when he fled Caracas, but the city had already claimed something from him
that no border crossing could retrieve. He had been working as a junior analyst for a tech startup
when the protests turned violent—the kind of escalation that happened in hours rather than days. He
was walking home from the office on a Tuesday afternoon when the first tear gas canister detonated
three blocks away. The sound was a dull metallic *thump*, followed by screams that seemed to come
from everywhere at once. He ran without thinking, his lungs burning, his eyes streaming, unsure if
he was being chased or if the chaos itself was chasing him. A motorcycle swerved toward the crowd
and he threw himself against a storefront, certain in that moment that he would die on a street
whose name he had walked a hundred times before. The bike passed. He made it home. But the relief
lasted only until the next day, when his hands began to shake during a meeting and he couldn't stop
seeing the blur of the motorcycle's headlight, couldn't stop hearing that sound.

By the time he arrived in Miami six weeks later, Marco had already begun the work of trying to
survive what had happened. His body had become a stranger to him—constantly tense, muscles locked as
though bracing for impact that never came. He would lie awake at night feeling his heart race,
convinced something was wrong with him, and he'd press his fingers against his neck to count his
pulse, run his hands down his ribs checking for wounds he knew weren't there. He started a new job
as a Product Manager at a software company in Brickell, and for the first three weeks he managed to
pass as functional. But then a colleague rolled his chair backward suddenly during a standup
meeting, and Marco's entire body seized. He flinched so violently that everyone stopped talking. His
face burned. He excused himself to the bathroom and didn't come back for twenty minutes.

The hypervigilance never truly left him. At the office, Marco found himself unable to focus on his
laptop screen because part of his attention was always elsewhere—monitoring the movements of people
around him, tracking who came in and out of the conference room, noting exits and open spaces. His
manager commented that he seemed "distracted." What Marco experienced was something closer to a
constant state of readiness, a vigilance so consuming that it felt like his body was running a
program he couldn't shut down. Every unexpected sound made him flinch. A door closing too quickly, a
raised voice from another team, the metallic *ding* of the elevator arriving—each one sent a jolt
through his nervous system that felt indistinguishable from actual danger.

It was this relentless state of alert that drove him to begin hurting himself. Not dramatically, not
in ways that left permanent marks, but with deliberate intention. He would pinch the skin on his
inner arm until it bruised, or press his fingernails into his palm hard enough to leave crescents of
white before the blood returned. The pain was clarifying in a way nothing else was—it gave him
something real to focus on, something he could control and understand, and it seemed to break the
cycle of his body's constant vigilance, at least for a few minutes. It was as though his nervous
system had so much energy trapped inside it that physical pain was the only release valve. After he
hurt himself, his muscles would relax slightly, his breathing would slow. The temporary relief was
powerful enough that he began doing it more often—in his apartment after work, in the bathroom stall
at the office, sometimes just before bed when the anxiety felt like it might swallow him whole.

But the relief never lasted. Within hours, sometimes within minutes, the tension would rebuild. His
body would resume its state of high alert, scanning for threats, waiting for something terrible to
happen. And because he was hurting himself, his body monitoring intensified—he became hyperaware of
any pain or sensation, constantly checking his arms and hands for marks, worried that someone would
see them and ask questions he couldn't answer. He also began seeking reassurance obsessively,
texting friends late at night asking if they thought he was "okay," if something seemed "wrong" with
him, if they noticed he seemed "off." His best friend from high school eventually stopped responding
to these messages. Marco's own vigilance had taught him to interpret silence as confirmation that
something was indeed very wrong.

He came to therapy after his manager pulled him aside and suggested he might benefit from "support."
Marco sat across from the clinician with his hands folded tightly in his lap, and when asked what
brought him in, he said only: "I don't feel safe in my own body anymore." What he meant was that the
line between the war zone and his present life had blurred so completely that he could no longer
tell the difference between a real threat and the ghost of one. His body was still running the
program it had learned in Caracas, and nothing he did—not the pain, not the reassurance, not the
checking—seemed to convince it that he was actually safe.
