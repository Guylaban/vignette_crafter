# Amara

Amara was forty-two when she was taken. She had been a human rights lawyer then, working for an
international NGO in Lagos, documenting cases of state violence. The men came to her office on a
Tuesday morning—she remembers the light through the window, the sound of her assistant's chair
scraping back, and then nothing clear until much later. There was a room. There were voices speaking
a language she understood but could not quite place, hands that held her down against a surface, the
smell of a cologne so sharp and chemical it coated her throat. She remembers struggling, remembers
the sensation of her wrists being bound, remembers gasping for air—or trying to—but the sequence
dissolves. There are hours, perhaps days, that exist now only as fragments: a voice, a pressure, a
smell that would not leave her skin even after she was released.

When she was freed, three weeks later, she did not speak about it for months. She finished her
degree, moved to America, built a new life. She married Daniel, a good man, a surgeon. She became a
judge—a position of authority, of control, of safety. For years, the trauma seemed to belong to
someone else, a story she had read rather than lived. Then, six months ago, Daniel had come home
wearing a new cologne. It was not the same scent—she knew that intellectually—but her body did not
care. The moment the smell reached her, her breath caught. She found herself unable to move from the
couch, her chest tight, her mind suddenly flooded with fragments: the pressure on her wrists, the
sound of breathing that was not her own, the chemical burn in her nostrils. She had not thought
about those details in years. They had been locked away, inaccessible, as though they belonged to a
different person entirely. But the cologne had opened something, and now the memories were there,
vivid and terrifying, bleeding into her present.

The triggers came more frequently after that. A patient in the waiting room at Daniel's hospital was
breathing heavily—asthma, she learned later—and Amara felt her entire body seize. She had to leave.
In the courthouse, during a recess, a young attorney sat beside her wearing perfume, and suddenly
she could not remember how to stand. The memories would come then, but they came wrong: fragmented,
out of sequence, with gaps she could not fill. When she tried to deliberately recall what had
happened, to piece it together into something coherent, the details would scatter. She would grasp
for a moment—the voice, the restraint, the smell—and it would slip away, replaced by a sensation of
unreality, as though the memory belonged to someone she had only heard about in a case file. The
harder she tried to hold the narrative together, the more it fractured.

Over the months, she began to avoid anything that might trigger the sensory memories. She asked
Daniel to stop wearing cologne entirely. She started excusing herself from courthouse gatherings
where she could not control the air. She stopped going to the hospital to meet him for lunch. Each
avoidance felt necessary, protective, but it was also isolating her. Daniel had begun to ask
questions. Her colleagues had noticed her absences. She was a judge, a woman who had survived
something unspeakable and built a career on the rule of law, but now she was trapped in a loop where
her own mind had become the courtroom and the verdict was already in: she was not safe, and no
amount of control could change that.

It was Daniel who finally insisted she see someone. He had found her on the bathroom floor one
morning after she had caught a whiff of someone's perfume on the news—a commercial she had not been
able to mute fast enough. She was not injured, but she was not present either. That was when she
called, finally, to make an appointment. What frightened her most was not the memories themselves,
but the feeling that they belonged to a stranger, and that this stranger was now running her life.
