# Amara's Story

Amara was twenty-two when her older brother Kofi died. It was a Tuesday in March, unremarkable in
every way except that he never came home. He had been driving back from a consulting meeting in
Boston, a route he had taken a hundred times, and somewhere on I-91 near Hartford his car had
crossed the median. The state police said it was sudden—no skid marks, no indication he had braked.
A seizure, perhaps, or a stroke. Kofi was twenty-eight, fit, and had never been sick a day in his
life. By the time Amara's mother called her at the lab where she was analyzing soil samples, he had
already been dead for four hours.

The weeks that followed blurred into a haze of funeral arrangements and well-meaning relatives. But
it was in the months after, when the house grew quiet again, that Amara began to understand what had
broken inside her. She could not sleep properly anymore—her body would jolt awake at 2 a.m.,
convinced something was wrong, her heart hammering before her mind had even registered why she was
afraid. At work, her colleagues learned not to ask her to wait for results. If an analysis took
longer than expected, if a colleague was five minutes late to a meeting, Amara's stomach would
clench with a sick certainty that something terrible had happened. She had come to believe that
delays were omens, that the world operated on a logic of punishment and warning that only she seemed
unable to read in time.

What tormented her most was her own conviction that she should have known. She replayed the morning
of his death obsessively, searching for the moment she should have called him, insisted he stay
home, done something—anything. If she had been a different kind of person, someone more attentive,
more intuitive, someone like her mother or her best friend Zara, she would have sensed the danger.
But she was the sort of person who missed things, who was too absorbed in her own work, too self-
focused. The event had happened to her precisely because of who she was. This belief drove her to
pick fights with Zara over nothing—a forgotten text message, a perceived slight—just to feel
something other than the crushing weight of her own inadequacy. The arguments would leave her hollow
and ashamed, but in the moment they were a relief, a way to push the unbearable thought of Kofi
away.

She had also begun avoiding anything that might remind her of the accident. She stopped watching
television, stopped scrolling through news feeds, and when colleagues mentioned a documentary about
transportation safety, she left the room. Yet the memory itself resisted her attempts at control.
When she tried to think through what had happened, to make sense of it, the details would scatter.
She knew the facts—the highway, the median, the seizure—but when she tried to hold the full picture,
to understand it as a coherent story, her mind would recoil. The memory felt fragmented, almost as
though it were still unfolding, still happening to her in the present moment rather than something
safely in the past. This quality of immediacy, this sense that the trauma was not behind her but
lodged in her body and mind as an ongoing threat, made her hypervigilant. Every unexpected noise,
every text message that took too long to arrive, every moment of silence triggered the same cascade:
her body would flood with adrenaline, her mind would race to the worst possible conclusion, and she
would find herself unable to distinguish between what was actually happening and what might happen.

The rumination and conflict-seeking had begun to isolate her. Zara had stopped reaching out after
the third or fourth argument. At the lab, her supervisor had gently suggested she might benefit from
some time away, though they both knew she could not afford it. It was only when she found herself
unable to focus on a critical soil contamination project—her hands shaking as she tried to prepare
samples, her thoughts fracturing into catastrophic predictions—that Amara finally admitted she
needed help. She sat in the waiting room of the university counseling center, terrified that talking
about Kofi would somehow make the memory more real, more present, more capable of destroying her.
But she was more terrified of continuing as she was: trapped in a loop of blame and hypervigilance,
unable to trust her own mind or body, convinced that some essential flaw in her character had made
her unable to save the person she loved most.
