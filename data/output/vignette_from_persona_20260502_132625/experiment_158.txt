# Clinical Vignette: Katerina

Katerina was fifty-eight when she first sought help, though her colleagues on the city council would
have guessed older. She had spent nearly three decades in humanitarian work before her election, and
the particular decade that mattered—the one that had fractured something essential in her—began in
2011 when she deployed to Syria as a field coordinator for a medical NGO. She had been competent
then, unafraid. The hospital compound where she worked sat in a contested zone, and the shelling
came without pattern. On the morning of March 14th, a mortar round struck the east wing while she
was in the central courtyard reviewing supply manifests. The blast threw her backward. She
remembered the sound first—not the explosion itself but the breathing of the man who fell across her
legs, his chest heaving in the dust, his lungs working overtime as if the air itself had become
hostile. She remembered his face only as a blur, a visual impression of terror that her mind had
photographed and locked away. When the second round came, closer, she had crawled toward the
basement, her own breathing ragged and panicked, and she had stayed there for six hours while the
building shook and dust filtered down like ash from some interior apocalypse. No one she knew had
died. She had been, objectively, fortunate. But the mind does not keep accounts in objective
ledgers.

She returned to the States that summer and resumed her life with deliberate normalcy. She took a job
in local government, built a reputation as a thoughtful advocate for refugee resettlement, and told
herself the nightmares would fade. They did not. Instead, they metastasized into something more
insidious. Any loud noise—a car backfiring on the street, the sharp bang of a construction site
barrier—would hijack her nervous system entirely. Her body would flood with adrenaline before her
mind had even registered what she had heard. And in those moments of panic, the memory would force
itself forward, unbidden and total. She would see the courtyard again, the dust hanging in geometric
light, the body across her legs. The memory felt alien to her now, as though it belonged to someone
else's biography, some other woman's trauma. Yet it arrived with such vivid, intrusive insistence
that she could not shake the conviction that it was happening again. The boundary between then and
now had become permeable.

By her early fifties, Katerina had developed an intricate architecture of avoidance. She could not
tolerate sounds that resembled breathing—the hiss of a radiator, someone's labored respiration in a
quiet meeting room—because those sounds reliably summoned the memory of that man gasping in the
rubble, and with it came the certainty that she would not be able to endure the flood of feeling
that followed. She had come to believe that if she allowed herself to think about what had happened,
if she sat with the memory long enough to examine it, she would shatter entirely. The world, she was
certain, was fundamentally dangerous. Aid work had taught her that; Syria had confirmed it.
Stability was an illusion. Safety was temporary. And because the world was genuinely unsafe, her
hypervigilance was not pathological—it was rational, even necessary. This conviction made her afraid
of any situation she could not control or predict. She began declining invitations to restaurants
and theaters. She stopped attending council meetings in person when possible, conducting her
business by email and video call from her apartment, where she could control the acoustic
environment. Her colleagues noticed her withdrawal but attributed it to age or temperament. What
they did not see was the exhausting mental labor of threat-scanning, the way her attention would
fracture at any unexpected sound, or how the fear of her own fear—the terror that she might lose
emotional control in public—had become as confining as the original trauma itself.

The triggers had multiplied and become entrenched. A siren, a door slamming, even the sound of
someone taking a deep breath in the office would send her into a state of hyperarousal that lasted
hours. Each time, the memory would resurface with its alien, photographic quality, and each time it
did, she would feel the weight of her negative appraisals settle more firmly: she would never feel
normal again; she was broken in some fundamental way; the world was not a place where she could
truly be safe. These beliefs, in turn, made her more vigilant, more prone to interpret ambiguous
sounds as threats, more likely to scan her environment for danger. The cycle had tightened over
years until she was living in a progressively smaller world, her sphere of movement and connection
contracting month by month.

It was a junior staff member's observation—kindly meant but devastating—that finally moved her to
seek help. "You seem afraid all the time," the young woman had said after Katerina had flinched at a
door slam during a budget meeting. The words landed because they were true. Katerina was afraid all
the time. She was afraid of sounds, afraid of memories, afraid of her own emotional fragility,
afraid that she had permanently lost the capacity to feel anything but dread. She came to the clinic
on a gray November afternoon, sat in a quiet room, and for the first time in thirteen years, began
to speak about the courtyard, the dust, the breathing, and the woman she had been before the world
had revealed itself as the dangerous place she now knew it to be.
