# Clinical Vignette: Margaret

Margaret was sixty-three when her daughter's car was struck broadside by a delivery truck running a
red light. The impact happened on a Tuesday afternoon in March—a moment that would cleave her life
into before and after with a precision that still startled her. Margaret had not been there. She had
been at home, folding laundry, when the hospital called. But in the weeks that followed, she came to
know the details with a terrible intimacy: the angle of the collision, the sound witnesses
described, the fact that Sarah had died at the scene. Margaret found herself able to recall the
smell of the hospital—that particular combination of industrial cleaner and something like
copper—with an acuity that made her stomach turn. When she closed her eyes and tried to reconstruct
what must have happened in those final seconds before impact, the memory would slip away,
fragmentary and incomplete, as if her mind had built a wall against the full picture. Yet at other
moments, without warning, the memory would crash through—the image of a twisted bumper, the sound of
metal screaming—and she would be gasping, her heart hammering, unable to distinguish between the
memory and present danger.

It had been three years now. Margaret's husband had died of a stroke two years into her grief, as
though his body had simply given up the work of carrying both their losses. She lived alone in the
house where she and Robert had raised their daughter, and she had not worked since the accident. The
days had a strange, suspended quality.

The anniversary of Sarah's death had become something Margaret dreaded with increasing intensity. In
the weeks leading up to March, she found herself hypervigilant in a way that seemed to have no off
switch. Every unexpected movement—a neighbor's cat darting past the window, the sudden appearance of
someone rounding a corner—sent a jolt of terror through her body, as though her nervous system was
convinced that danger was imminent. The heightened alertness that began as the anniversary
approached would intensify her startle responses, making her flinch at sounds, her breath catching
in her throat before her rational mind could catch up. She had come to understand that her body was
bracing itself for catastrophe, as if the trauma could happen again, and this year it might happen
to her.

Ambulance sirens had become intolerable. The first time she heard one after Sarah's death, she had
been at the grocery store, and the sound had sent her into a panic so severe that a clerk had to
help her to a chair. Now, whenever she heard that wail, the full sensory memory would descend—not
just the visual fragments she struggled to access deliberately, but the smell, the terrible smell of
that hospital room, the fluorescent lights, the weight of Robert's hand in hers. The siren would
trigger the memory unbidden, and the memory itself would spike her sense of threat, making her
certain that something catastrophic was about to unfold. She had begun timing her errands to avoid
the hours when ambulances were most likely to pass. Cars that had been damaged in accidents—she had
seen one last month, a sedan with a caved-in door—would send her into a spiral of intrusive imagery
and dread that could last for hours.

At night, Margaret lay awake, her mind cycling through the anniversary, through the possibility of
loss, through scenarios she could not control. She had taken to checking the locks on every door and
window in the house—a ritual that began as a practical precaution but had evolved into something
compulsive, something she found herself repeating even after she had just checked them minutes
before. The checking brought only momentary relief before the anxiety crept back, the certainty that
danger was still present, that she had missed something, that one unlocked window or unlatched door
was all it would take. She was trapped in a loop she could no longer interrupt: the threat felt real
and imminent, the memory fragments kept surfacing unbidden, the triggers seemed to be everywhere,
and her attempts to manage them only seemed to tighten the knot.

It was her therapist at the community mental health center—someone she had started seeing after
Robert's death—who had finally suggested that what Margaret was experiencing had a name and a
structure to it, and that there might be ways to interrupt the cycle. Margaret had come in last
week, exhausted and afraid, wondering if she would spend the rest of her life waiting for the next
anniversary, the next siren, the next moment when the past would shatter her present.
