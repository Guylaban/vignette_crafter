# Rajesh

Rajesh was seventy-nine years old when he first came to the clinic, though his weathered face and
the careful way he moved suggested someone older. He had spent the last three decades working the
night shift at a distribution warehouse on the outskirts of the city, a job he had held since
immigrating to America in his early fifties. His hands bore the marks of that labour—calluses, a
permanent tremor in his left fingers—but it was his eyes that told the deeper story. They had a
quality of perpetual watchfulness, the kind that made younger coworkers uncomfortable without quite
knowing why.

The trauma that shaped Rajesh's life had occurred fifty-seven years earlier, during the 1967 Indo-
Pakistani War. He had been conscripted at twenty-two, assigned to an infantry unit near the western
border. In the chaos of a mortar attack on his battalion's position, shrapnel had torn through the
shoulder of the soldier standing beside him—a young man named Vikram whom Rajesh had known for only
three weeks. The image of Vikram's collapse, the blood spreading across his uniform, the sound of
his screaming before the medics arrived, had never left Rajesh's mind. In the decades that followed,
Rajesh had built a life in America, married, fathered two children, divorced, and worked steadily.
Yet the war remained lodged inside him like shrapnel that never fully healed.

For most of his adult life, Rajesh had managed by not thinking about it. He did not speak of the war
to his ex-wife or his adult children. He avoided the news when it covered military conflicts. But in
the last two years, something had shifted. He had begun to notice that loud sounds—the backfire of a
truck in the warehouse parking lot, the crash of metal bins being emptied—sent a jolt through his
entire body. His heart would race for minutes afterward. When the Fourth of July arrived, he found
himself unable to sleep the night before, knowing that fireworks would sound outside his apartment.
He had started taking sleeping pills, borrowed from a neighbor, just to get through the day after
the holiday.

The warehouse itself, which had been his sanctuary of routine for so long, had become a minefield of
triggers. A new supervisor, a younger man with a sharp voice and a tendency to bark orders, reminded
Rajesh of his old commanding officer. Whenever this supervisor approached, Rajesh felt his shoulders
tense, his breathing shallow. He began arriving earlier and leaving later, timing his shifts to
minimize contact. The physical work, which had once grounded him, now felt precarious. When a
forklift backfired near him one evening, Rajesh had frozen entirely, unable to move for what felt
like an eternity but was probably only seconds. A coworker had to guide him to a chair.

His sleep had deteriorated. Most nights, he lay awake in his small apartment, the television
murmuring to fill the silence, afraid of what he might dream. When sleep did come, he often found
himself back in that moment—the heat of the sun, the smell of dust and smoke, Vikram's voice calling
out. He would wake gasping, his sheets soaked through with sweat. The exhaustion accumulated. At
work, he made mistakes he would never have made before. His supervisor noted it. There were
conversations about his performance, veiled suggestions that perhaps it was time to think about
retirement.

What had finally driven Rajesh to seek help was a moment of clarity that had arrived unbidden. He
was sitting alone in his apartment on a Tuesday evening, the apartment exactly as it had been for
years, and he had realized with sudden, crushing certainty that he had spent fifty-seven years
running from something that had never actually left. The war was not behind him. It was still
happening, every day, in the space between his ribs and the space between his thoughts. He was
seventy-nine years old. How much longer could he keep running? The question terrified him, but not
as much as the thought of spending whatever time remained the way he had been living—isolated,
vigilant, afraid of his own mind.
