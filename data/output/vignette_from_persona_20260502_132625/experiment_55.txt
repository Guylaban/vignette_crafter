# Amara

Amara was forty-seven when she first came to the clinic, though the exhaustion in her eyes made her
look older. She had been a plumber for eighteen years—a profession she'd chosen deliberately, she
said, because the work was straightforward, because pipes either leaked or they didn't, because
there was no ambiguity in the solving. What she did not say, at least not directly, was that the job
had allowed her to move through the world largely unsupervised, on her own schedule, answerable to
no one but her clients and herself. That autonomy had felt like survival.

The trauma that shaped her began when she was five years old. Her uncle, the man her mother had
asked to watch her while she worked, used to take her into the basement of his house in Lagos. He
was a man with a particular voice—thin, reedy, with a habit of hissing his words through his teeth
when he was angry. He would call her name in that voice, drawing it out in a way that made her
stomach drop before he ever touched her. The physical abuse was severe and prolonged; what stayed
with her most vividly was not the pain itself but the sound of his voice preceding it, the way he
would call her name as if summoning her to something inevitable.

Amara's parents had brought her to America when she was eight, away from him, but the damage was
already woven into her nervous system. For decades she had functioned reasonably well—she had
married a kind man, built a career, maintained friendships. But in the past year, something had
shifted. It had started with a sound. One of her clients, a woman in a professional office building,
had a receptionist with a voice that was similar to her uncle's—not identical, but similar enough:
that same thin quality, the same slight hiss. Amara had heard it through the office door as she was
leaving a job, and her body had locked. She'd sat in her truck in the parking garage for twenty
minutes, hands shaking, unable to drive.

After that, the world had begun to narrow. She started hearing her uncle's voice everywhere—in a
cashier at the hardware store, in a neighbor's son visiting next door, in the voice of a police
officer who had stopped her for a broken taillight. Each time, the old feeling returned: the sense
that something terrible was about to happen, that she was about to be hurt. Worse, she had begun to
believe that the reason these sounds triggered her so powerfully was something fundamental about who
she was. She was broken. She was damaged in a way that made her incapable of functioning like a
normal person. There was something defective in her that made her vulnerable to hurt, that made her
unable to move past what had happened to her. The triggering sounds seemed to confirm this
belief—they proved that she was weak, that she was the kind of person to whom bad things happened
because of something intrinsic to her character.

To manage the mounting fear, Amara had begun avoiding anything that might expose her to these
sounds. She started turning down jobs in office buildings and professional spaces. She stopped going
to the grocery store at certain times, avoiding the cashiers whose voices unsettled her. She refused
to go to the police station when she needed to renew her license, asking her husband to do it
instead. Her avoidance also extended to people in uniforms or positions of authority—she would cross
the street to avoid police officers, she would not attend community meetings where officials might
speak. And when she was alone with her thoughts, she found herself dwelling on what her uncle had
done, replaying the scenarios, imagining what he might have done had the circumstances been slightly
different. She ruminated endlessly on his actions, on his cruelty, on the fact that he had never
faced any real consequence for what he had done to her.

The rumination and the avoidance had begun to feed something darker. The more she avoided situations
and people, the more her world shrank, the more she felt like a prisoner in her own life. And the
more her world shrank, the more convinced she became that there truly was something fundamentally
wrong with her—that a healthy person would not be so afraid, would not need to hide so much. Nothing
in her life felt meaningful anymore. Her work, which had once been a source of quiet pride, now felt
like a series of jobs she was canceling or avoiding. Her marriage, which had been genuinely warm,
had grown distant; her husband could not understand why she would not leave the house some days, why
she had become so rigid about where she would and would not go. The avoidance fed the negative
thoughts, and the negative thoughts made the triggers feel even more dangerous. When she heard a
voice that resembled her uncle's, she was certain it meant something was wrong with her for being so
afraid. And because she believed something was fundamentally wrong with her, she could not imagine
that exposure to these sounds might ever feel manageable.

It was her husband who finally insisted she seek help. He had noticed her pulling away, the way she
flinched at certain sounds, the way she had begun to decline invitations and isolate herself. What
frightened Amara most was not the prospect of therapy itself, but the possibility that a therapist
might confirm what she already believed—that she was too broken to be helped, that the damage her
uncle had done was permanent and defining. What she hoped for, though she barely dared to speak it
aloud, was that somehow she might reclaim her life, that the sounds that now controlled her might
lose their power, that she might become the kind of person who could simply hear a voice and move
forward without her entire body betraying her.
