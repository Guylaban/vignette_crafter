# Clinical Vignette: Marcus Webb

Marcus Webb was thirty-two when he first felt the tightness in his chest. He'd gone to the emergency
room on a Tuesday evening, certain he was having a heart attack—the pressure radiating through his
ribs, the shortness of breath that wouldn't ease even when he sat down. The diagnosis came back:
acute myocarditis, inflammation of the heart muscle, likely viral in origin. For three weeks he was
admitted to the cardiac unit, hooked to monitors, subjected to echocardiograms and stress tests,
needles threaded into his forearms for blood draws and IV lines. The doctors assured him he'd
recover fully with rest and medication. He did recover, physically. But something else had taken
root during those nights listening to the beep of monitors, watching nurses approach with syringes,
feeling his heart skip and stutter beneath the electrode pads.

Five years later, Marcus sits in his apartment in Portland, a database administrator for a mid-sized
tech firm, and he cannot remember the last time he felt truly safe in his own body. The illness
itself is long resolved—his cardiologist has cleared him repeatedly—yet the fear has metastasized
into something larger, something that now governs how he moves through the world. He had come to
believe that his body was fundamentally unreliable, a vessel that could betray him without warning.
This conviction sits beneath everything he does, a constant low-frequency hum of dread. When he
feels even the slightest flutter in his chest, or a moment of shallow breathing, the old panic
rises: *it's happening again, you're dying, your body is failing you.* The certainty is absolute and
immediate, and it drives everything that follows.

Because of this constant threat, Marcus has constructed an elaborate architecture of control. He
monitors his eating with obsessive precision, restricting his caloric intake and avoiding foods he
believes might stress his heart—salt, sugar, anything that makes him feel heavy or sluggish. The act
of refusing food, of saying no to his body's hunger, gives him a temporary sense of mastery. If he
can control what goes in, perhaps he can prevent catastrophe. He has stopped exercising entirely,
despite knowing intellectually that his cardiologist recommended gentle activity. But the thought of
elevating his heart rate, of feeling it pound in his chest, is intolerable. He tells himself that
rest is safer, that movement invites danger. He avoids hospitals, refuses routine physical exams,
and has not seen his primary care physician in three years. When his company scheduled a health
screening last fall, he called in sick. The thought of sitting in a medical office, of having a
blood pressure cuff around his arm or a stethoscope on his chest, triggered such acute panic that he
could not bring himself to attend.

Yet these strategies, meant to protect him, have become their own source of threat. His restricted
eating has left him feeling weak and depleted; his avoidance of exercise has deconditioned his body,
making even climbing stairs leave him breathless—which then confirms his fear that something is
wrong. The avoidance itself generates new moments of panic. Last month, a colleague mentioned chest
pain during a meeting, and Marcus felt his own chest tighten in sympathetic dread. His body
responded as though the danger were his own: muscles rigid, breath shallow, a wave of hot shame that
he could not control his reaction. He has noticed, too, that the more he avoids medical situations,
the more catastrophic they seem in his imagination. He has begun to experience his body as
constantly on alert, perpetually braced for the next crisis. His shoulders are chronically tense,
his sleep is fragmented, and he startles at unexpected sounds. He finds himself scanning his body
for symptoms throughout the day—*is my heart rate elevated? Do I feel dizzy? What was that sensation
in my left arm?*—a vigilance that exhausts him but which he cannot seem to stop.

His divorce two years ago coincided with a period of particular isolation; his ex-wife had grown
weary of his health anxiety, and he had pushed away friends who suggested he see a therapist. Now,
at thirty-seven, he works from home most days, a choice that began as practical but has become a
refuge. He avoids the office, avoids chance encounters with colleagues, avoids the unpredictability
of the outside world. The isolation has deepened his rumination. What finally brought him to seek
help was a panic attack so severe that he convinced himself he was having a stroke—the fear was so
real, so physically overwhelming, that he sat on his bathroom floor for two hours unable to move. In
the days after, something shifted. He realized that the illness he survived five years ago had not
killed him, but the fear of it might yet destroy his life. He is terrified that therapy will require
him to face medical situations, to feel his heart race without fleeing. But he is also, for the
first time, wondering whether the control he has built is actually keeping him safe, or whether it
has simply become another prison.
