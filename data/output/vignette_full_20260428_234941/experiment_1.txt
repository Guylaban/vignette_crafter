head. That night he arrived to smoke, shouting, and the harsh chemical-burnt smell of a kitchen
already engulfed. Someone nearby had spilled liquor; even now, what stays with him is the sickening

his partner’s—but the sequence will not hold together. There are blank stretches he cannot recover
at all. What he can recall does not feel concluded. When it comes back, it is not like remembering;
it is as if he is standing there again, urgently trying to work out what to do before it is too
late.

In the months afterward, Ravi became increasingly preoccupied with what the night meant about him.
He had always seen himself as competent and dependable, someone whose judgment under pressure
defined him. Now he had come to believe that this image had been exposed as false. He was certain
that if he had truly been capable, he would have known what to do, moved faster, chosen differently.
The very gaps in his memory seemed, to him, like proof of weakness rather than shock. Not being able
to reconstruct the event left him doubting his own mind, and the way the memory arrived in jagged
pieces only deepened his conviction that he could not trust himself to do the right thing when it
mattered. He began to say, quietly and with shame, that he was no longer the man he had been.

Those beliefs altered what he noticed around him. At work as a cybersecurity analyst, he had once
been comfortable talking about long-term projects, promotions, and plans for the next quarter. Now
even casual conversations about the future—where he and his partner might travel, whether they
should think about retirement, what a team rollout might look like six months ahead—caught him
sharply. The future no longer felt like something he could enter with confidence. It felt like a
demand to imagine himself as reliable and effective, and he did not believe that version of himself
existed anymore. In the office break room, if a colleague reheated something until it singed, the
burnt smell would stop him mid-thought. If he passed a cluster of coworkers joking over drinks after
hours, the smell of alcohol could make his body tense before he had consciously registered why.

seemed to pull the event forward intact, and he would suddenly be back in the night of the fire,
with no protective sense of distance. At his desk, staring at network logs, he could lose several
minutes to an image of flame under a doorframe or to a disconnected impression of shouting in a
stairwell. Just as often, the memory itself primed him to scan for those cues. After a morning of
intrusive recollections, he would find himself noticing every faint smoky odor in the building,
every mention of weekend drinking, every conversation that leaned toward what was coming next in
life. His world had narrowed around these signals, as though the memory kept teaching him where
danger lived, and those signals in turn reopened the memory with full force.

failed. Being thrown without warning into a scene that felt current and immediate left him shaken

in pieces around him. What frightened him most was not only the memory itself, but the conclusion he
had drawn from it: that when everything mattered, he had been weak, and that the man who once knew
how to act had not come back. He said he wanted help finding out whether the night had truly
revealed who he was, or whether he had been living inside that moment for so long that he could no
longer tell the difference.
