Dmitri Hale is a 46-year-old graphic designer of Eastern European descent who lives with his wife in
a mid-sized Pacific Northwest city. He was referred for psychological assessment following a
significant deterioration in his functioning over the past eighteen months, and he arrived at the
intake appointment visibly tense, choosing a seat near the door and glancing at it more than once
before the session began.

Approximately nineteen months ago, Dmitri was walking home from a late evening at a client's studio
when he witnessed a confrontation between police officers and a young man on the sidewalk outside a
convenience store. What began as a stop escalated rapidly. Dmitri was close enough to see the young
man's face when he was forced to the ground, close enough to hear the sounds, and close enough to
see the injuries that resulted — the torn skin at the man's temple, the blood spreading against the
concrete. He stood frozen for what he estimates was several minutes, unable to move or speak, before
an officer turned and told him sharply to keep walking. He did. He has not been able to fully
account for what happened in those minutes since.

In the weeks that followed, Dmitri noticed that something had shifted. He would be working at his
desk — adjusting a layout, reviewing colour proofs — when a thought would arrive unbidden and fully
formed, not as a vague impression but as something insistent and visual. The image of the wound. The
particular angle of the young man's head against the pavement. He found that if he tried to sit with
these intrusions and describe them, even to himself, the thread dissolved. He would begin and then
simply stop, unable to locate the sequence, unable to finish the account. He told his wife once,
early on, that he couldn't explain it properly. He hasn't tried again since.

Over the following months his world contracted in ways that accumulated quietly. He began avoiding
the neighbourhood where the incident occurred, then broader areas of the city where police presence
was likely. When he passes a patrol car now, or sees an officer on the street, the full memory
returns without transition — not as a recollection but as an event happening again in the present.
The same thing occurs when he encounters images of injuries on others, even incidentally: a news
photograph, a scene in a film his wife is watching in the next room. His body responds before his
mind catches up — shoulders rigid, jaw clenched, a readiness that does not discharge. He is unable
to relax even at home, even in the evenings when he knows, rationally, that he is safe. Before
entering any space — a restaurant, a client's office, even a room in his own house he hasn't been in
recently — he locates the exits. He is not always aware he is doing it until it is done.

Dmitri's work has suffered in ways that are now visible to clients. He misses deadlines he would
previously have met without difficulty. He spends hours on his phone between tasks — scrolling,
playing games, cycling through websites — not because he finds pleasure in it but because keeping
his mind occupied prevents it from going somewhere he cannot manage. He has declined two
collaborative projects that would have required him to work on-site with other creatives, telling
his wife he simply wasn't interested. He has become harder to reach socially, even for her. Friends
have stopped calling as frequently. She has begun asking questions he doesn't know how to answer.

What brought Dmitri to seek help was not a single event but an accumulation of losses. He had come
to believe, with a quiet and settled certainty, that his life before that evening was simply gone —
not damaged but destroyed, in a way that could not be repaired. What made this harder to carry was
his conviction that what happened was profoundly unjust, that nothing had been done, that no one had
been held accountable for what he saw, and that this absence of accountability was itself a wound
that remained open. He is not certain therapy can change any of that. What he said, near the end of
the intake, was that he hoped at minimum to feel like himself again — though he acknowledged he was
no longer entirely sure what that meant.
