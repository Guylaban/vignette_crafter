Arjun Mehta is a 37-year-old South Asian American psychologist, married, who presented for treatment
following a terrorist attack he survived approximately fourteen months ago. He had been attending a
professional conference at a hotel in a mid-sized American city when gunmen entered the building and
opened fire in the lobby and adjacent corridors. Arjun had been walking toward the elevator bank
when the first shots rang out — a sound that seemed, in that initial half-second, almost abstract,
almost like something falling. Then the screaming began. He pressed himself behind a luggage cart,
unable to move, unable to think, watching a man he had spoken with at breakfast fall to the floor
twenty feet away. He lay there for what he later learned was forty-seven minutes before law
enforcement cleared the building. He was physically unharmed. He has described that period of
waiting as the most total and consuming helplessness he has ever known — not a fleeting feeling but
a state that seemed to rewrite something fundamental about how the world worked.

In the weeks that followed, Arjun returned to his practice and his marriage, and for a time he
convinced himself that the event was behind him. He was, after all, a clinician who understood
trauma. He knew the literature. He had treated patients with PTSD. What he had not anticipated was
how thoroughly that knowledge would fail to protect him from his own experience. The nights became
the first problem. He began waking in the dark in a state of acute alarm, heart hammering, room
unrecognisable for several seconds. He started leaving a lamp on, then the hallway light, then the
television running through the night. His wife, Priya, adapted without complaint at first, but
months of disrupted sleep have left a quiet strain between them that neither has fully named.

Sounds became a persistent difficulty. A car backfiring in the parking structure beside his office
building once sent him to the floor behind his desk — an episode he has not disclosed to any
colleague. He works in a suite shared with two other clinicians, and the proximity of a busy street
means that sharp, percussive sounds arrive without warning. He has come to dread the walk from his
car to the building entrance, scanning the environment in a way that he recognises, intellectually,
as hypervigilant, but that he cannot simply decide to stop. He had come to believe, with a certainty
that felt less like a thought than a physical fact, that the world was genuinely and
indiscriminately dangerous — that safety was a story people told themselves before something
happened to them.

What frightened him most, in a way that cut deeper than the startle responses or the sleeplessness,
was what he saw when he looked at himself. The person who had spent fifteen years helping others
articulate and metabolise their suffering now found himself drifting away during sessions — sliding
into a kind of internal elsewhere, a diffuse, imagined space where nothing required his attention.
He recognised this in himself with a clinician's precision and a patient's shame. He had also begun
drinking more than he ever had — a glass of whiskey before bed, then two, then occasionally
something in the early evening as well, framed to himself as unwinding, as managing, as what anyone
might need. He has been careful about it, he says, though he acknowledges the carefulness itself is
a signal worth noting.

Arjun has avoided, wherever possible, situations in which he feels his control over outcomes is
uncertain — cancelling a planned flight to visit family, stepping back from a co-facilitated trauma
group at a community clinic, declining a conference invitation. Each decision felt reasonable in
isolation. Together, over fourteen months, they have narrowed his life considerably. He had come to
believe he simply could not prevent bad things from happening, and so the only available response
was to reduce exposure to anything that might require him to find out.

What finally brought him to seek help was a session three weeks ago in which a patient described
feeling trapped and powerless, and Arjun found he could not locate any distance between the
patient's words and his own interior. He sat with his face composed and his chest tight and
afterwards sat alone in his car for twenty minutes. He is most afraid, he says, that the person he
was before that hotel lobby is simply gone. What he is hoping for, more cautiously, is evidence that
this is not true.
