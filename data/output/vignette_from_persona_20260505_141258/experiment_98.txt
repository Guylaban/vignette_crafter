Kwame is a 58-year-old Ghanaian-born man who has lived in the United States for over two decades,
working as a hairdresser in a mid-sized city in Georgia. He is married, softly spoken, and, until
approximately fourteen months ago, described himself as a man who had built a quiet, reliable life.
That changed on a Tuesday evening last autumn when he was walking from his salon to his car after
closing up alone. A man approached him from the shadow of a doorway, close enough that Kwame could
smell the alcohol on his breath before he could see his face clearly. The man demanded his phone and
wallet, and when Kwame hesitated — more from shock than defiance — the man shoved him hard against a
parked car and pressed something cold and metallic to his ribs. Kwame handed everything over. The
man moved away quickly, and Kwame stood on the pavement for what felt like a very long time, unable
to call for help, unable to name what had just happened to him.

In the weeks that followed, Kwame found that the event had lodged itself somewhere beyond ordinary
language. He could not explain it to his wife in any way that felt adequate. He knew the sequence of
what had happened, but when he tried to speak it aloud, the words dissolved before they reached his
mouth. What came instead were images — the shadow of the doorway, the man's face catching the
streetlight, his own hands moving toward his pockets — arriving in flashes, unbidden, or unspooling
in sequence like footage he could not switch off. These replays came in the salon, at the dinner
table, in the early hours of the morning, and they carried the same texture of immediacy each time,
as though he were still on that pavement.

Certain things in his daily life became unbearable. The smell of alcohol — present more often than
he had previously noticed, on clients who came in for haircuts on weekend afternoons, on the breath
of a man standing too close at a petrol station — reliably produced a lurch of dread that he could
not reason away. When someone in the salon behaved in a way that struck him as unpredictable, or
loitered near the window a moment too long, he felt the same cold certainty he had felt that night:
that something terrible was about to happen. He had come to believe that the world was populated
with a kind of threat he had simply failed to see before, and that his failure to see it had cost
him. He dwelt on this repeatedly — replaying the moment he hesitated, certain that a more alert man,
a more prepared man, would have responded differently. He could not stop returning to what he had
done wrong.

The consequences spread gradually into his work and his marriage. He began scheduling fewer evening
appointments, then stopped taking them entirely. He found reasons to avoid the stretch of pavement
outside the salon, parking farther away and entering through the back. Clients he found difficult to
read — those who seemed restless, or who corrected him about how they wanted their hair cut — left
him shaken in ways he could not account for. Criticism from a client, even mild and well-meaning,
landed as something sharper, and he would spend the rest of the day returning to it. He began
avoiding social contact beyond what the job required, declining invitations, pulling back from the
community ties he had spent years building. His wife noticed the withdrawal but did not fully
understand it, and Kwame found he could not bridge the gap.

He has come to therapy now because his wife told him plainly that she was frightened for him, and
because he recognises, distantly, that the life he is living has become very small. What he fears
most, sitting in the consulting room, is that speaking about what happened will make it more real,
not less — that the images will become permanent if he gives them words. What he hopes for, though
he has not quite said this aloud, is that he might one day walk to his car after work without
bracing himself for what is waiting in the dark.
