Rajan Choudhary is a 44-year-old South Asian man who came to the United States as a refugee in his
late twenties, having fled a region of prolonged civil conflict in which he witnessed sustained
political violence, displacement, and loss. He now works as a chef at a mid-sized restaurant in a
city he has lived in for over a decade, and he presents for his first formal psychological intake
with a PCL-5 score of 42, describing himself as someone who has "managed" for years but can no
longer do so.

The event that organizes Rajan's current suffering occurred during the final days before his
departure from his home country. He was part of a group attempting to cross a checkpoint when
soldiers opened fire on the crowd. Several people fell around him — people he knew, including a
younger cousin. Rajan ran. He did not stop to help. He crossed the border. His cousin did not. In
the chaos, there had been a moment — a specific, irreducible moment — when he had turned and seen
his cousin on the ground, and had kept moving. That moment has never resolved into a coherent
narrative for him. When he tries to describe it, language fails him entirely; the event exists in
him as sensation and image rather than story, something he has never been able to give proper words
to even in his native tongue. Occasionally he can recount the surface facts with an eerie flatness,
as though reading someone else's file, feeling nothing at all — and that numbness frightens him
almost as much as the grief that arrives without warning at other times, a grief so total it leaves
him unable to function for hours.

In the years since resettlement, Rajan built a life organized quietly around safety. He chose his
apartment for its ground-floor exit. In every room he enters — the restaurant kitchen, a grocery
store, a waiting room — he locates the doors before he does anything else, standing near them when
possible, mapping the space before he can settle. He remains in a state of near-constant alertness
that his colleagues have interpreted as intensity or professionalism; he notices everything, and the
noticing is exhausting. Concentration on complex tasks has become increasingly difficult. He will
lose a sauce, forget a ticket, have to re-read the same order three times.

The kitchen, which was once his refuge, has become a site of exposure. When a line cook came in last
spring with a deep cut across his palm, Rajan had to leave the floor for twenty minutes, gripping
the edge of a sink in the back hallway. Visible injuries on others produce in him an immediate,
overwhelming response that he cannot explain to coworkers. He has told no one why. When the
restaurant television in the bar area carries news coverage of accidents, or when customers discuss
a recent crash at a nearby table, he finds ways to move to the far side of the kitchen. He avoids
the streets near the hospital several blocks away, rerouting his commute so he does not pass
ambulances idling outside the emergency bay.

Outside the kitchen, Rajan avoids open plazas and wide streets where he feels exposed. He crosses to
the side of the block with buildings when he can. He has not attended a street fair or outdoor
market in years. When he encounters police officers or security personnel in uniform, something in
him goes very still and very accommodating — he becomes deferential to the point of self-erasure,
agreeable in a way that he himself recognizes as disconnected from his actual wishes, a posture his
body assumes before his mind catches up.

What Rajan is most certain of, sitting in the intake office, is that he is not the person he was
before that checkpoint, and that he never will be again. He believes he should have gone back. He
believes surviving when his cousin did not is something he has not earned and cannot justify. These
are not doubts he entertains — they are convictions he carries as fact.

What finally brought him in was a phone call from his cousin's mother, made to wish him well on a
holiday. He sat with the phone in his hand for a long time afterward. He is hoping, tentatively,
that someone can help him find words for what happened. He is afraid that if he does, what he finds
there will confirm everything he already believes about himself.
