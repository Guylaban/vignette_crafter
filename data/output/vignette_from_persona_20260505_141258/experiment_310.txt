Dmitri Hollenbeck is a 36-year-old married man of Eastern European descent who works the floor of a
large home-improvement retail store in a mid-sized Midwestern city. He presents for an initial
psychological assessment reporting significant distress following a man-made disaster that occurred
approximately fourteen months ago, and his PCL-5 score of 59 places him firmly in the severe range
of posttraumatic stress.

The incident took place during a weekend shift at the store. A structural failure in the warehouse
annex — later attributed to improper load stacking by a contracted crew who had repeatedly ignored
safety protocols posted throughout the area — caused a partial ceiling collapse. Dmitri was working
nearby when the failure began. He was struck by falling shelving and pinned briefly beneath debris
that included torn insulation batting, a coarse fibreglass material that pressed against the back of
his neck and hands as he lay face-down waiting for colleagues to reach him. Several co-workers were
injured; one, a man Dmitri had known for three years, sustained a severe head injury and spent weeks
in intensive care. The contracted crew, whose disregard for the posted warnings Dmitri had
personally noticed and quietly resented earlier that afternoon, were uninjured and left the site
before emergency services arrived.

In the days and weeks that followed, Dmitri found that what remained of the event was not a
continuous memory but a series of frozen images — the particular angle of a buckled beam, the
expression on his colleague's face before the noise began, the grey fibreglass pressed against his
palm. Each image arrived with a quality he struggled to describe to his wife, Petra, except to say
that they felt more present, more undeniably real, than anything else he could remember — more real
than their wedding, more vivid than the birth of their daughter. This quality frightened him, and
alongside it came a wash of shame so acute that he had told almost no one what had happened in those
minutes under the debris, certain that something in how he had responded — the stillness, the
silence, the way he had not called out — revealed something contemptible about his character.

He had come to believe, in a way he could not fully articulate but felt with conviction, that if he
simply refused to acknowledge the full weight of what had occurred, it might lose its grip on him.
He told himself, sometimes aloud when alone in the car, that it had not been as serious as people
made it seem. He worked hard to push the images away when they surfaced, filling his mind with noise
— music, phone scrolling, anything. When those efforts failed and the images pressed forward anyway,
he had, on several occasions, pressed his thumbnail hard into the skin of his forearm until the
physical sensation overrode the mental one. He had not told Petra about this.

His daily work environment had become a persistent source of difficulty in ways that were difficult
to explain to his supervisor. The store's aisles of insulation products — rolls of the same
fibreglass batting that had pressed against his skin during the collapse — required him to pass
within feet of them during restocking rotations. He had begun rerouting his path through the floor,
adding minutes to every task, and a supervisor had commented on his efficiency. More disruptive
still was his response to customers and colleagues who violated the store's safety signage —
blocking emergency exits with flatbed carts, ignoring wet-floor markers, moving barriers around
displays. What frightened him most in those moments was not the hazard itself but the recognition
that people simply did not care, and the rage that recognition produced in him felt disproportionate
and shameful in equal measure.

Over the past several months Dmitri's marriage had grown strained in ways neither he nor Petra had
named directly. He had withdrawn from conversations about his workdays, declined social invitations,
and was sleeping poorly. It was Petra who finally said, plainly, that something was wrong and that
he needed to speak to someone. He agreed, though what he most feared in coming to therapy was that
articulating the event fully — giving it the reality of spoken words — would confirm what he had
been trying to deny: that it had happened, that it had changed him, and that the shame he carried
about it was deserved.
