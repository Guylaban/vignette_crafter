Rangi Taufa is a 63-year-old Tongan-American man who works as a community organizer in a mid-sized
Pacific Islander enclave on the West Coast. He came to the clinic after what he described, with some
difficulty, as "not being able to keep going the way I have been." He is divorced and lives alone,
and the quietness of his apartment, he noted during intake, is something he both needs and finds
oppressive.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Rangi was involved in a serious road accident during a community
outreach event. He had been driving a van transporting several elderly members of his congregation
to a health fair when a commercial truck ran a red light and struck the passenger side at high
speed. Emergency responders arrived quickly, and in the chaos that followed, uniformed police
officers and paramedics surrounded the vehicle, issuing rapid commands and physically pulling people
from the wreckage. Rangi, though injured himself, had been conscious throughout. He remembered with
excruciating clarity the sound of metal compressing, the smell of something burning, and the face of
one elderly woman as she was lifted past him — and then almost nothing for a period he estimates as
perhaps twenty minutes. Those minutes are simply gone. He has no access to them regardless of how
hard he tries to recover them, and what surrounds that gap on either side remains fragmentary and
inconsistent. He can describe the impact. He cannot describe what happened to the people in his care
during the time that matters most to him.

In the weeks after the accident, Rangi found himself returning compulsively to questions of
responsibility. He had been the driver. He had chosen that route. He had, he became increasingly
certain, made a decision in the seconds before impact — he could not remember what it was, but he
was sure it had been wrong. This conviction settled into him quietly and deeply, the way water finds
the lowest point in a room. He did not speak of it to anyone.

What he noticed instead, and what confused him, was that his body seemed to have its own
understanding of the event that had nothing to do with his conscious reasoning. He would be sitting
in a city council meeting, perfectly safe, and his heart would be slamming against his ribs as
though he were still in the van. He found this frightening and humiliating in equal measure — he had
come to feel that his body was no longer his, that it had turned into something unreliable,
something that responded to the world on its own terms without consulting him.

The triggers that came to govern his days were specific and, to him, bewildering in their
ordinariness. Police officers at community events — a routine presence in his work — became nearly
unbearable. The sight of a uniform, even at a distance, produced a physical response he could
neither explain nor suppress, and he began routing his movements through spaces where he was
unlikely to encounter them. More disruptive still was his reaction to anger. In his role as a
community organizer, conflict is a professional constant — raised voices at meetings, tense
negotiations with city officials, a resident's frustrated outburst. Even measured expressions of
displeasure from a colleague or a community member were enough to send him somewhere he could not
name and could not describe. He had begun canceling meetings, delegating facilitation to junior
staff, and withdrawing from the coalition work that had defined his professional life for two
decades. Colleagues noticed. He was asked, gently, whether he was all right. He said he was tired.

What he told the intake clinician, haltingly and with long pauses, was that he had stopped feeling
like himself some time ago. He used the phrase "dead inside" without apparent distress, as though
reporting a fact about the weather. He said he did not know how to talk about what had happened —
not because it was too painful, but because the words genuinely did not come. He sat with that for a
moment and then said that what had finally brought him in was the recognition that he was
disappearing from his own community, and that he did not want to finish his life that way.
