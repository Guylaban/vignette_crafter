Linh Phạm is a 48-year-old Vietnamese-American woman, divorced, who works as a waitress at a mid-
sized restaurant in a suburban strip mall. She was referred for psychological assessment following a
brief conversation with her primary care physician, to whom she had gone complaining of fatigue and
difficulty sleeping. When the physician asked a few careful questions, Linh began to cry in a way
that surprised even her — quietly, and with what looked like shame.

Approximately fourteen months ago, a man began appearing at the restaurant where Linh works. At
first she assumed he was simply a regular customer who had taken a liking to the food. He sat in her
section each time, always ordered the same thing, and left generous tips with handwritten notes
tucked beneath the receipt. Within a few weeks the notes had become personal — commenting on her
hair, her laugh, the way she moved between tables. Then he began waiting for her in the parking lot
after her shifts, stepping out from between cars as she walked to her vehicle. He knew her name, her
schedule, and eventually her home address. Over the following three months he appeared outside her
apartment building on multiple occasions, once pressing his face against the glass of the lobby door
while she stood frozen in the hallway, unable to move toward the exit or back toward the elevator.
She had come to believe, in those moments, that her body had simply stopped belonging to her — that
it was a thing being watched and catalogued by someone else, and that she had no authority over it.
A restraining order was eventually obtained, and the man relocated out of state following a criminal
complaint. Legally, the threat was resolved. Psychologically, it was not.

In the months that followed, Linh found that the memory of his face at the lobby glass did not
behave like an ordinary memory. It arrived without invitation — while she was folding napkins at the
start of a shift, while she was rinsing a cup at her kitchen sink — and when it came, it brought
with it a wave of physical sensation so complete that her hands would shake and her chest would
tighten as though the event were still unfolding. What disturbed her most was that she could not
seem to narrate what had happened to her in any coherent way. When her daughter asked what the man
had done, Linh found herself unable to answer. The experience existed somewhere in her body and in
flashes of image and sensation, but it resisted language. And stranger still, when she tried to
recall it deliberately, it felt as though it had happened to someone else entirely — a woman she had
read about, not herself.

At work, the consequences became concrete and cumulative. Linh began to dread tables occupied by men
who sat alone and watched the room. When the restaurant was short-staffed and she was asked to cover
a section she didn't know well, she would feel the familiar tightening begin — she had come to feel
that unfamiliar spaces were spaces she could not read for danger, and so she avoided them when
possible, trading sections with younger colleagues and absorbing their confusion with tight-lipped
apologies. Any minor friction with a customer — a complaint about wait time, a returned dish — sent
her into a state of acute distress she could not explain to her managers. She had stopped seeing the
two friends she used to meet for coffee on Sunday mornings, finding reasons to cancel until the
invitations stopped coming. She no longer glanced at herself in the bathroom mirror before her
shifts. She had come to feel that attending to her own body in any deliberate way was something she
could not bear.

At home, she lay awake listening for sounds at the door — the elevator, footsteps in the corridor,
any creak that might mean someone was approaching. She described herself to her physician as feeling
dead inside, and said it without drama, as a simple report. She used to be someone who laughed
easily, she said. She used to care about things. She could no longer locate that person.

What finally brought her to the intake appointment was her daughter's wedding, scheduled for six
months from now. Linh wanted to be present for it — truly present — and she was afraid, with a quiet
and specific terror, that she no longer knew how.
