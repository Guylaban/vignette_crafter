Kofi Mensah is a 30-year-old Ghanaian-American curriculum designer who presented for an initial
intake following a period of physical abuse sustained within a romantic relationship that ended
approximately fourteen months ago. He came to the first session dressed carefully, speaking in
measured sentences, and described himself as someone who had always prided himself on being composed
and self-aware. By the end of that session, it was clear that the composed exterior was costing him
considerably.

Kofi had been in a relationship for nearly two years with a partner whose drinking escalated
gradually and then, one evening, catastrophically. The night that brought him to treatment began
with the smell of whiskey when his partner came home — a particular, sharp smell he would later find
impossible to separate from what followed. An argument started over something trivial, and within
minutes his partner struck him repeatedly, including blows to his ribs and the back of his head. At
one point something in the kitchen caught briefly on the stove — a dish towel — and the acrid smell
of scorched fabric filled the apartment while Kofi was on the floor trying to cover himself. He
remembered that smell almost as vividly as the pain. He did not call the police that night. He told
himself he was not sure anyone would believe him.

In the weeks after he left, the memories did not behave the way he expected memories to. They
arrived with a physical weight. When the recollection surfaced — sometimes unbidden while he was
reviewing a lesson plan, sometimes in the grey space before sleep — he felt a tightening across his
ribs and a dull pressure at the back of his skull, as though the event were happening in his body
all over again. The terror was not a faded echo; it returned at full volume, the same spike of
helplessness he had felt on the floor of that apartment. And sometimes, with the memory, came the
smell — the whiskey, or the scorched cloth — arriving without any external source, as though his
nervous system had preserved the scene in full sensory detail and was replaying it without his
consent.

His daily work as a curriculum designer, which he had once loved for its quiet intellectual demands,
became one of the more complicated arenas of his symptom landscape. His office shared a small
kitchen with colleagues who occasionally microwaved food until it burned, and the smell — that
particular char — could stop him mid-sentence, his attention suddenly pulled entirely inward. He
began eating lunch at his desk with the door closed and declining coffee runs that took him past the
kitchen. Over months, he grew quieter in team meetings, contributing less, and a supervisor noted in
a recent review that he seemed disengaged. Kofi said nothing to explain it.

Social life contracted in a different direction. When relatives visited or called, their well-
meaning questions about whether he was seeing anyone, or comments about family and partnership,
landed with an almost physical sting. He had come to believe that what happened to him had left a
mark that was not entirely invisible — that something about him had been altered and dirtied in a
way he could not fully articulate but could not stop feeling. He described it once, haltingly, as
feeling like something had gotten into him that he could not wash out. He was also certain, with a
bitterness that surprised him when it surfaced, that nothing had been done and nothing would be done
— that the person who hurt him had faced no consequence, and that the asymmetry of that was simply
something he was expected to absorb.

What troubled him most when he looked in the mirror, or sat with his own thoughts long enough, was
that he no longer recognised the person he had become. The Kofi he knew before had been open,
socially easy, forward-looking. The man who now flinched at a burnt smell in an office kitchen, who
deflected family calls, who kept his door closed — he did not know that person, and he was not sure
he could find his way back to himself. It was this last fear, more than any single symptom, that
finally brought him through the door.
