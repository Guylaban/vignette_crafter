Gerald Mauer is a 74-year-old man of German descent who lives with his long-term partner in a mid-
sized town in the Pacific Northwest. He works part-time as a private tutor, primarily helping high
school students with mathematics and German language, a role he has held since retiring from
secondary school teaching nearly a decade ago. He presented for psychological assessment following
what he described, with considerable understatement, as "a rough couple of years."

Approximately eighteen months ago, Gerald was at his partner's lakeside cabin in a rural area when a
fast-moving wildfire swept through the surrounding hillside. The fire had been reported several
miles away the previous evening, and emergency services had not yet issued an evacuation order for
the cabin's zone. Gerald was alone that afternoon — his partner had driven into town for supplies —
when the wind shifted and the fire crested the ridge above the property within minutes. He had no
time to gather belongings. The air turned a dense, acrid orange, and the smell of burning pine and
char filled the cabin before he even opened the door. He drove out along a single-lane road with
flames visible on both sides, unable to see more than a few metres ahead through the smoke, certain
at several points that he would not make it. The cabin was destroyed. Gerald was physically
unharmed, but he did not speak to anyone about what had happened for several weeks.

In the months that followed, the memory of that afternoon began to intrude on Gerald's life in ways
he found deeply unsettling. The memory did not arrive when he invited it; it arrived without
warning, forcing itself into his awareness during tutoring sessions, during meals, during the quiet
of early morning. When it came, it sometimes brought with it the smell — that particular combination
of burning resin and hot ash — as though the fire were still present in the room. At other times, he
found he could revisit the events in a flat, detached way, recounting what had happened to his
partner as though describing a film he had once seen, the words accurate but emptied of any feeling
he could recognise as his own. He had come to think of this as a kind of mercy, though it also
disturbed him, because he was certain the man on that road had been terrified, and he could not
always locate that terror in himself.

Gerald began to restructure his days around the avoidance of anything that felt unpredictable or
potentially stressful. He declined tutoring students who struck him as difficult or whose parents
seemed demanding. He stopped accepting new clients entirely by the autumn, reducing his working
hours to a handful of familiar students. When one of his remaining students failed to arrive at the
scheduled time, Gerald would sit rigidly at the table, convinced that something had gone wrong — an
accident, a crisis — his mind moving immediately toward catastrophe. The same response came in other
contexts: a delayed text from his partner, a slow internet connection, a package that had not
arrived. Any gap between expectation and reality had become, in his experience, a signal that
something bad was unfolding.

In public spaces — a café, a waiting room, a library — Gerald found he could not settle unless he
could position himself with his back to a wall and a clear view of the exits and other occupants. He
scanned every environment he entered, cataloguing faces and doorways, tracking movement. He was
aware this looked odd, and he had begun to avoid social engagements partly for this reason, which
had placed a quiet strain on his relationship. His partner had noticed the withdrawal, the shortened
temper when routines were disrupted, and had gently encouraged him to seek help.

Gerald also spent considerable time turning the events of that afternoon over in his mind, returning
again and again to the moments before the fire crested the ridge — the weather reports he had read,
the decision to stay, the window of time in which he might have left sooner. He was not sure he
could have known, but he was equally not sure he could not have. This uncertainty had become its own
kind of occupation.

What finally brought Gerald to the clinic was a session with a student in which he smelled, briefly
and unmistakably, the smoke from a neighbour's fireplace drifting through the window — and found
himself unable to continue for several minutes. He was embarrassed by this, and frightened by it.
What he hoped for, he said quietly, was to feel like himself again. What he feared was that the man
he had been before that afternoon no longer existed.
