Dmitri Hale is a 28-year-old financial analyst of Eastern European descent who presents for an
initial psychological assessment accompanied by his wife, though he insists on being seen alone. He
is well-dressed, speaks precisely, and holds his hands clasped tightly in his lap throughout the
session. He describes himself, with a kind of exhausted bewilderment, as someone who used to be good
at his life.

Fourteen months ago, Dmitri and his wife brought their two-year-old son, Nikolai, to the emergency
department on a cold, overcast February morning after noticing he had become lethargic and
unresponsive overnight. What followed were four days in a paediatric intensive care unit as doctors
worked to identify and treat what was eventually confirmed as bacterial meningitis. Dmitri describes
standing at the foot of his son's hospital bed watching monitors, listening to alarms, and being
told twice by different physicians that Nikolai's prognosis was uncertain. He recalls the particular
grey light coming through the ward windows, the smell of the hand sanitiser at the door, the moment
a nurse asked him to step outside and he understood, with total bodily certainty, that his child
might die. Nikolai survived and has since recovered fully, but Dmitri has not returned to the man he
was before that corridor.

In the weeks that followed his son's discharge, Dmitri began noticing that certain things undid him
without warning. Overcast skies — the kind that had hung over the city that February — would produce
a sudden tightening in his chest and a feeling of being back inside that waiting room. He began
checking weather forecasts each morning not out of practical interest but out of a need to prepare
himself, though he has come to accept that preparation offers little protection. When a colleague's
child was brought up in conversation, or when he encountered children at a birthday party or in a
supermarket aisle, the warmth he had once felt around them gave way to an acute, nameless dread. He
found himself scanning for exits in those moments — noting which door was closest, how many steps it
would take to reach it — a habit that has since extended to every room he enters, including the
open-plan office where he works. His desk now faces the door.

Dmitri's memories of those four days are not vague or fragmented in the way he might have hoped.
They come to him as images: the blue of Nikolai's hospital gown, the particular angle of a monitor
screen, his wife's face when she was crying and trying not to. The memories are vivid and precise,
and yet he finds that when they begin to surface fully he pushes them back before they can complete
themselves, as though there is something at the end of the sequence he is not yet able to reach. He
has not spoken about this to his wife.

He had come to believe, with a conviction that resisted any counterargument, that he had failed. He
had noticed Nikolai was tired the day before and attributed it to teething. He had waited until
morning to call the doctor. In Dmitri's accounting of events, those hours were the hours that
mattered, and he had spent them doing nothing. What frightened him most was not the memory itself
but what it confirmed about him — that he was not the father, the husband, the person he had
believed himself to be. He looks in the mirror and does not recognise the man looking back. The
analyst who models risk for a living had failed to model the most important risk of all, and he
carries that as a structural fact about himself rather than a thought he might examine.

He has stopped watching the news. He leaves the room when parenting content appears on television.
He has declined two social invitations involving families with young children, telling his wife he
was tired, which is also true — he rarely falls asleep before two in the morning, listening for
sounds from Nikolai's room.

What finally brought Dmitri in was a performance review at work in which his manager noted that he
seemed distracted and had missed two deadlines — the first in his professional life. He describes
this not as a crisis but as evidence. He is hoping, he says carefully, that someone can help him
become useful again. What he is most afraid of, though he takes longer to say it, is that the person
he used to be is simply gone.
