Kofi Mensah is a 33-year-old Ghanaian-American man, married and currently employed as a social media
manager for a mid-sized nonprofit organization. He was referred for psychological assessment
following a period of sustained occupational difficulty and what his wife described, in a brief note
accompanying the referral, as him "not being the same person who came back." Kofi presents with a
PCL-5 score of 68, consistent with severe posttraumatic stress, and has been reluctant to seek help
until now, having spent the better part of eighteen months persuading himself that what he
experienced was simply part of the job.

Approximately two years ago, Kofi was deployed as a logistics coordinator for a humanitarian relief
organization operating in a conflict zone in the eastern Democratic Republic of Congo. He had done
similar work before — shorter stints, more stable regions — and had considered himself experienced
enough to manage whatever he encountered. What he was not prepared for was a morning in early spring
when a supply convoy he had personally organized and dispatched was ambushed on a road he had
cleared through standard protocol. Three local colleagues were killed. Kofi was at the distribution
point when the radio call came in. He heard the voices, heard the chaos, and then heard nothing. He
was the one who had approved the route. He was the one who had signed off on the departure time. In
the weeks that followed, he moved through the remaining deployment in a kind of functional
dissociation, completing tasks, filing reports, and saying very little to anyone about what he
carried.

Since returning to the United States and resuming work, Kofi has found that the ordinary rhythms of
his job have become unexpectedly treacherous. His role requires him to monitor multiple content
streams simultaneously, tracking breaking news, conflict updates, and humanitarian crises across
social platforms in real time. The scrolling feeds — the sudden appearance of images from active war
zones, the audio clips embedded in news posts, the ambient noise of urgency that defines his screen
— place him in a sustained state of alertness that does not resolve when he closes his laptop. He
sits at his desk with his back to the wall when he can arrange it, and colleagues have noticed that
he flinches visibly when someone approaches him from behind or when a phone alarm sounds
unexpectedly nearby. He has difficulty sustaining concentration on single tasks, often re-reading
the same paragraph of a content brief several times without retaining it, which he has begun to
attribute to some fundamental deterioration in his cognitive capacity.

What frightens Kofi most is not the external world but his own internal one. He had come to believe,
with a quiet and absolute certainty, that he cannot trust his own judgment — that the man who
approved that route, who followed protocol and still got people killed, is the man he still is. He
does not experience this as guilt in any simple sense; it is more like a structural conclusion about
his reliability as a person. When memories of that morning surface, he pushes them away
deliberately, holding them at a distance through a practiced detachment that he describes as "going
somewhere else for a second." He does not allow himself to think through the events in any sustained
way, and has told his wife only the barest outline of what happened.

Alongside this sits something harder and more outward-facing: Kofi is consumed by the knowledge that
nothing has been done. The men responsible for the ambush have faced no accountability. The
organization conducted an internal review and issued no findings. He had come to understand his own
suffering as not merely personal but as evidence of a profound injustice that the world had simply
absorbed and moved on from, and this understanding has made it difficult for him to invest in
anything that feels like recovery. Why heal, some part of him seems to ask, when the record has not
been corrected?

What finally brought him to seek help was his wife telling him, quietly and without accusation, that
she felt like she was living with someone who was always about to leave. Kofi does not know exactly
what he is hoping for. He knows only that he is exhausted by the vigilance, and that he would like,
at minimum, to be able to sit at his desk without feeling that something is still coming.
