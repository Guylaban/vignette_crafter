Declan Marsh is a 29-year-old Coast Guard petty officer of Irish and Polish descent who lives with
his long-term partner on the outskirts of a coastal town in New England. He presented for
psychological assessment following a referral from his unit's medical officer, though the events
that brought him to this point began nearly fourteen months ago, on a night he still cannot fully
account for in words.

It was a Thursday evening in late autumn. Declan had finished a long shift and stopped at a
convenience store near the waterfront on his way home. The parking lot was poorly lit, and as he
returned to his truck he was approached by two men. What followed lasted perhaps four minutes,
though he has no reliable sense of that duration. One of the men produced a knife. Declan was struck
from behind, shoved against the hood of his vehicle, and held there while his wallet, phone, and
keys were taken. The cold metal of the hood pressed against his cheek. At some point he registered
the sound of footsteps retreating. Then he was alone in the parking lot, standing without knowing
how he had come to be standing.

In the weeks after the incident, Declan found himself unable to do what seemed like the simplest
thing: tell his partner what had happened. Not because he was unwilling, but because when he tried,
the words simply did not organise themselves into anything coherent. He could open his mouth and
produce nothing that felt like an account. He knew something had happened to him, but the shape of
it resisted language. His partner, Nora, would ask gentle questions, and Declan would sit across
from her at the kitchen table looking, she later told him, as though he were watching a film in a
language he didn't speak. On some occasions he could recount a sequence of events in a flat,
detached tone that seemed to disturb Nora more than silence — a recitation that felt to him as
though he were describing something that had happened to a person he had read about, someone with
his name but no claim on him. At other times what came to him were not scenes at all but pieces: the
specific grain of the truck's hood beneath his palm, a smell he could not identify, the weight of
stillness after the footsteps stopped. These fragments arrived without context and without
connection to each other, and he had come to believe that his inability to assemble them into a
coherent whole meant something was fundamentally wrong with his mind.

Declan's work as a Coast Guard petty officer placed him regularly in environments that intersected
with these unresolved fragments in ways he found difficult to manage. Patrol shifts along the
waterfront — the same general geography as the parking lot — required him to function with authority
and composure in poorly lit outdoor spaces at night, and he was certain that his colleagues had
noticed something altered in him, though no one had said so directly. He began volunteering for
daytime administrative duties in a way that struck his supervisor as out of character for someone
who had previously preferred active deployment. Over the months, this quiet repositioning had begun
to affect his standing and his own sense of professional identity.

At home, the distance between Declan and Nora had widened in ways neither of them had fully named.
He was present but unreachable on certain evenings — not hostile, simply absent in a way that Nora
described as like talking to someone through glass. She had eventually suggested, with care, that he
speak to someone. He had agreed, though what frightened him most was not the prospect of revisiting
the event but the possibility that the fragmentation itself was permanent — that the pieces would
never cohere, that he would spend the rest of his life in possession of an experience he could not
actually hold.

What finally brought him to the intake appointment was a moment during a routine night patrol when
he stood at the edge of a dock, looked out at the dark water, and realised he could not remember the
drive there. He made the call to the medical officer the following morning.
