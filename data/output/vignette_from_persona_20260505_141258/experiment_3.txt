Kofi Mensah is a 55-year-old married man of Ghanaian descent who has spent the better part of three
decades as a bomb disposal technician, a profession that has always demanded that he hold chaos at
arm's length through discipline, procedure, and an almost preternatural calm. That professional
identity makes what happened to him three years ago feel, in his own words, like a particular kind
of betrayal — not by an enemy or a device, but by the earth itself.

In the late summer of 2021, Kofi was visiting extended family in a coastal region of West Africa
when a powerful tropical storm made landfall with almost no warning. The meteorological services had
tracked the system, but its rapid intensification in the final hours meant that the community had
perhaps forty minutes between the first serious alerts and the storm's arrival. Kofi was in his
uncle's concrete-block house when the roof of the adjacent structure — a neighbour's home where
children had been playing — collapsed under the combined force of wind and flooding. The noise was
enormous and immediate: a percussive crack followed by a sustained roar that he has since described
as the sound of everything solid becoming unreliable. He worked alongside neighbours to pull people
from the debris for hours, in darkness, in water, amid the continuing violence of the wind. He does
not know, even now, the full sequence of what he saw and did during those hours. The gaps in his
recall are not modest. Significant stretches are simply absent, and what does surface comes in
fragments — a child's sandal, a particular quality of grey light at dawn, the feeling of his hands
on rubble — without narrative continuity to hold them together. When those fragments do surface,
what accompanies them is not grief or fear but a white-hot rage that he finds almost impossible to
account for and deeply disturbing to experience.

In the months after he returned to the United States, Kofi noticed that his body had begun operating
on a logic he did not recognise. A car backfiring in the street near his home in suburban Maryland
would send his heart into his throat and his muscles into a defensive crouch before he had
consciously registered the sound. Colleagues raising their voices during a briefing — even in
ordinary frustration, not directed at him — produced the same flooding physical response. He had
come to understand intellectually that nothing was wrong in these moments, but the understanding
arrived after the alarm, never before it, and the gap between the two felt humiliating to a man
whose professional competence depended on his nervous system being an instrument of precision.
Situations that resisted prediction — a change to a work schedule, an unplanned social event, any
context where he could not know what was coming — produced a sustained, grinding vigilance that left
him exhausted by midday.

What formed gradually, across weeks and then months, was a set of habits organised around reducing
the possibility of being caught off guard. Kofi began declining any assignment that involved
ambiguity about the operational environment, a pattern his supervisors initially accommodated but
which has increasingly limited his deployment eligibility. He stopped attending the monthly team
gatherings he had previously valued. At home, he keeps the television or a podcast running at
virtually all hours — not to watch or listen, but because silence, he has found, leaves too much
room. He eats heavily in the evenings, working through food in a way that his wife, Adwoa, has
watched with quiet alarm, and which he knows is connected to the distress he cannot otherwise
discharge. Adwoa has said, carefully and more than once, that she feels she is living with someone
who is always waiting for the next disaster. He has not been able to tell her she is wrong.

What finally brought Kofi to the clinic was a near-miss at work six weeks ago: a moment of sensory
overload during a training exercise that produced a flashback so complete that for several seconds
he was not in Maryland but in the dark water of that night, hands on rubble, the roar still present.
A colleague noticed and covered for him. Kofi drove home afterwards and sat in his car for an hour.
He is most afraid, he said at intake, that he is becoming unreliable — in the field, and to his
wife. What he hopes for, more tentatively, is to remember what happened without the rage, and to
trust his own body again.
