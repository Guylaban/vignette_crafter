Madeleine is a 39-year-old dentist of French-American descent who presents for an initial
psychological evaluation approximately fourteen months after being discharged from a six-week
inpatient admission for a rare and aggressive autoimmune condition that attacked her cardiac tissue.
She lives with her long-term partner in a mid-sized city and, until recently, maintained a busy
private practice. She describes herself, with some bitterness, as someone who had always prided
herself on physical resilience and professional competence — qualities she no longer feels she can
claim.

The illness arrived without warning. Madeleine had noticed fatigue over a few weeks but attributed
it to a heavy caseload. One afternoon she collapsed in her operatory between patients, and the next
coherent memory she has is of waking in a cardiac intensive care unit, her wrists threaded with
lines, surrounded by a smell she can only describe as cold and chemical — the sharp antiseptic scent
that, she would later discover, had permeated every hour of her hospitalization. For several days
her prognosis was genuinely uncertain. There were conversations she was too sedated to participate
in but later learned had included discussions about transplant candidacy. She remembers a particular
afternoon when a consultant explained her treatment options in a tone that made clear there were
very few of them — a moment she returns to involuntarily, often, as the point at which she
understood that her body had become something that could simply decide to stop.

In the months following discharge, certain things became unbearable in ways that surprised her.
Walking into any clinical space — including her own practice — and encountering the smell of
antiseptic solution would produce an abrupt, overwhelming sense of being back in that bed, helpless.
She began scheduling her days so that the sterilization room was always staffed by her dental nurse
when she was present, and she rerouted her path through the clinic to avoid passing its door. At
home, she stopped using the medical-grade hand sanitiser she had always kept on her desk. These
arrangements felt, to her, entirely rational.

She also stopped looking at photographs from before the illness. A box of printed photos from a
family holiday — taken perhaps two years prior — sat in a drawer she had not opened since coming
home from hospital. What frightened her most about those images was not grief exactly, but the
confirmation they would offer that the person in them was gone. Madeleine had come to believe, with
a quiet and settled certainty, that she had been permanently altered — not just physically but in
some more fundamental way that she could not fully articulate, only feel. She was certain she was
not the same woman who had run half-marathons and managed a twelve-chair practice, and she was
equally certain she never would be again.

Her relationship with her own body had become one of vigilance and mistrust rather than partnership.
She had stopped the light exercise her cardiologist recommended, not from laziness but from a
conviction that any unusual heartbeat or shortness of breath was the beginning of another collapse.
She avoided mirrors when undressing and had declined a follow-up echocardiogram twice, rescheduling
it each time with a different excuse. To attend that appointment would mean looking directly at
evidence of what her body had become — and she was not ready for whatever that evidence might
confirm. She had come to believe that her body was no longer a reliable instrument, that it operated
by rules she could not predict or influence, and that this made her, in some essential way, weak.

Socially, she had retreated. Her partner had tried repeatedly to talk about what happened, and she
had deflected each attempt — changing the subject, leaving the room, citing tiredness. Two close
colleagues who had visited during her hospitalization had not heard from her since. At work, she
functioned adequately in the operatory but declined staff lunches and had stopped attending the
study group she had helped found. She told herself she simply had nothing to offer.

What finally brought Madeleine to seek help was a moment at work last month when a new patient
arrived wearing a hospital ID bracelet from a recent admission, and the sight of it — combined with
the background smell of the autoclave — produced a response so intense that she had to excuse
herself and sit alone in her office for twenty minutes before she could continue. She made the
referral herself that evening. She arrived at the intake appointment composed and articulate, and
said, near the end of it, that what she most hoped for was to stop feeling like a stranger inside
her own life.
