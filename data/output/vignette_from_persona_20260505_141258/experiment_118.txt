Fatima is a 53-year-old woman of Lebanese descent, married, and currently serving as a ship captain
operating cargo routes along the Gulf Coast. She presented for psychological assessment describing
herself as exhausted in a way that sleep no longer touched, and it took several sessions before the
full shape of what she carried began to emerge.

Fatima grew up in a household governed by fear. Her father, a man whose rages arrived without
warning and departed without apology, beat her regularly from the time she was perhaps five or six
years old until she left for university at eighteen. The violence was not chaotic — it had a
terrible orderliness to it. He would come to her room at night, and the sound of footsteps in the
hallway, the specific creak of a door being tested, became the architecture of dread. She learned to
lie still in the dark and listen, to parse the quality of silence for what it concealed. Extended
family knew, or suspected, and said nothing. The mosque community, to which her mother clung as a
social lifeline, offered no intervention. Fatima left Lebanon as soon as she could and built a life
defined by forward motion — maritime studies, certifications, command. For decades, the strategy
worked well enough.

The symptoms she describes now did not announce themselves all at once. In her late forties,
following her mother's death and a return to Beirut for the funeral, something shifted. She found
herself lying awake on the ship at night, listening for sounds that might mean someone was entering
her quarters — a habit so automatic she barely recognized it as unusual until her first officer
commented that she seemed always to be waiting for something. She cannot relax even when she knows,
rationally, that she is safe; the body simply refuses the information. Nighttime is categorically
different from day. On deck in full sun, surrounded by her crew, she functions with the authority
and composure her position demands. But after dark, even anchored in a familiar port, the sense of
safety drains away and something older takes its place.

Her memories of her father's abuse come to her in fragments — visual images, mostly, frozen and
specific: the particular angle of light from a hallway, the pattern on a pillowcase, a hand raised.
Sometimes these images arrive with their full emotional charge intact but with no narrative context,
a wave of terror or shame that seems to come from nowhere and belongs to nothing she can consciously
name. At other times she can review what happened with a strange, dissociated clarity, as though
reading a case file about someone else — the facts present, the feeling entirely absent. She finds
both states disturbing in different ways.

What Fatima dwells on most, in the long watches of the night, is the question of prevention. She
returns again and again to the people who knew and did nothing — the aunts, the neighbors, the
religious figures her mother trusted — and she has come to believe, with a quiet and settled
certainty, that institutions designed to protect are fundamentally unreliable. She also mourns a
version of herself she believes existed before the abuse shaped her — a girl who could trust, who
belonged somewhere, who did not experience her own cultural community as a place of abandonment.
That lost connection is not abstract to her. When Lebanese colleagues invite her to community
events, she declines. She has told herself she simply does not have the time.

She tries hard not to think about any of it. The effort is constant and largely unsuccessful, and
she is aware, at some level, that it is costing her something.

What finally brought Fatima to seek help was a conversation with her husband, who told her gently
that she had not seemed present with him in over a year. She had not realized he had been counting.
She came to the first appointment hoping to be told there was something specific and fixable, and
what she fears most is the opposite — that this is simply who she has become, and that the girl who
might have been otherwise is genuinely, permanently gone.
