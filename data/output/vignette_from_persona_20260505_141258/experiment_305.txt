Farida is a 43-year-old Iranian American pharmacist who presented for treatment following what she
described, with visible reluctance, as "finally running out of ways to cope." Married for sixteen
years and the mother of two teenage children, she arrived at the intake appointment dressed with
careful precision, speaking in measured, professional sentences that occasionally broke apart
without warning.

The history she disclosed across the first several sessions traced back to her childhood in a
household governed by her father's volatility and, most reliably, by his drinking. She could not
identify a single defining incident so much as a years-long architecture of fear, though certain
moments stood out with terrible clarity. Her father would return home in the evenings smelling of
alcohol — a smell she learned, before she could name it, to read as a weather system moving in. What
followed was unpredictable in its specific shape but reliable in its essence: commands issued in a
voice that did not permit questions, and then, when she was slow to comply or when compliance was
simply not enough, physical punishment delivered with a certainty that communicated she had brought
it upon herself. She grew up understanding that people with authority over her were dangerous, that
instructions were preludes to harm, and that her own body was not entirely hers to protect.

Farida immigrated to the United States in her early twenties, completed her pharmacy degree, and
built, by external measures, a life that looked nothing like the one she had escaped. For years she
managed. Then, approximately eighteen months ago, a new pharmacy director joined her hospital and
introduced a top-down management style that involved frequent direct instructions delivered in a
clipped, non-negotiable tone. The effect on Farida was immediate and disproportionate in ways that
frightened her. Standing at the dispensing counter when the director approached with a directive,
she would feel her body flood with something she could not name — not quite fear, not quite memory,
but indistinguishable from both. She had come to believe that something was fundamentally wrong with
her nervous system, that she could no longer trust her own read of a situation, because she was
certain, in those moments, that she was in danger even when nothing in the room confirmed it.

The smell of alcohol sanitizer, used throughout the hospital, began to affect her in ways she
struggled to explain even to herself. It arrived before she could prepare for it, and when it did,
the memories returned not as recollections but as events — vivid, immediate, more present than the
fluorescent-lit pharmacy around her. At other times she could sit with the memory and feel nothing
at all, as though it had happened to someone she had read about, which frightened her in a different
way. She had stopped being able to concentrate reliably on medication calculations she had performed
thousands of times, and she began double- and triple-checking her work, arriving earlier and leaving
later, until the pharmacy had become both the place she feared most and the place she could not
leave, because leaving felt like losing the last thing that still told her who she was.

At home she had begun to pull away from moments of warmth — her daughter's excitement about a school
performance, her husband's suggestion of a holiday — as though happiness were a room she no longer
had the right to enter, or a signal that something worse was approaching. She had failed, she was
certain, in what mattered most: being present for her children in the way she had promised herself
she would be. She had hurt herself on several occasions, pressing her nails into her forearm or
holding something cold and sharp against her skin, not to end anything but to interrupt the
overwhelming emotion that arrived with the memories when they came in full force. She had told no
one. She could not sleep without lying rigid and alert, listening, though she could not have said
for what.

What finally brought her to the clinic was her daughter asking, quietly, whether she was all right.
Farida had no answer. What she feared most, she said at the end of the intake, was that nothing
would ever feel meaningful again. What she hoped for, in a voice barely above a whisper, was that
she might be wrong about that.
