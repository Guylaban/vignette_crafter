Priya Subramaniam is a 79-year-old South Asian woman of Indian descent, married and still actively
working as a commercial airline pilot — a distinction that has defined her identity for more than
five decades. She presented for psychological assessment after her husband of forty-one years urged
her, with quiet but firm insistence, to speak with someone. She had resisted for months, telling
herself that what she was experiencing was simply the accumulated weight of age, of long-haul
schedules, of a body and mind that had simply grown tired. But in recent weeks she had found herself
sitting in the cockpit during pre-flight checks, hands steady on the instruments, while something
ancient and formless pressed against the inside of her chest — and she had begun to wonder whether
she could trust herself at all.

Priya grew up in a household in Chennai that was, by outward appearances, orderly and respectable.
Her father was a man of considerable standing in the community and considerable violence in private.
What she witnessed as a child was not a single incident but a sustained, recurring pattern — her
mother thrown against walls, dragged by her hair, silenced with a hand across the face — and it
unfolded most often at night, after the doors were locked and the neighbors could not hear. The
smells of those nights are precise in Priya's memory: the antiseptic her mother applied to her own
wounds in the bathroom afterward, the particular stillness of a house holding its breath. There were
songs, too — film songs playing softly on the radio in the next room, a detail so incongruous that
even now, decades later, certain melodies will stop her mid-step. She cannot always identify which
songs they are before the reaction is already upon her.

Priya emigrated to the United States in her late twenties, trained rigorously, broke barriers that
were not designed to bend for women who looked like her, and built a life that seemed, from the
outside, to be a complete departure from everything she had survived. For many years she believed
this was true. It was only after a hospitalization two years ago — a routine cardiac procedure,
nothing serious — that something shifted. The smell of the antiseptic in the recovery ward was so
precise, so chemically identical to her mother's bathroom in Chennai, that she found herself unable
to speak for several minutes. The nurses attributed it to the anesthesia. Priya did not correct
them.

In the time since, her memories of her childhood have become increasingly intrusive and
disorienting. When they arrive, they do not feel like recollections — she feels like it is happening
now, in the room she is standing in, to the body she currently inhabits. The memories come in pieces
that do not assemble into a coherent sequence: a hand, a sound, a particular angle of light, her
mother's expression. These fragments feel more immediate and vivid than anything from her ordinary
life, which has left Priya deeply unsettled about the reliability of her own mind.

She has come to believe that her difficulty managing these experiences is evidence of a fundamental
weakness in her character. She has told herself she is a lousy coper — that a woman who flew
aircraft through storms and across continents for fifty years should not be undone by old memories —
and this appraisal has hardened into something she treats as established fact. She feels she cannot
rely on herself, and that the slightest additional stress will exceed whatever capacity she has
left.

At home, even in the apartment she has shared with her husband for decades, she remains watchful.
She wakes in the night to check that she is safe, scanning the room before she can settle. On nights
when her husband travels, she cannot bring herself to sleep in the bed, and will sit in the kitchen
until the sky lightens. She has developed a habit of eating late into the night during these vigils
— large, unfocused meals that she describes without pleasure. She stays up as long as she can,
delaying sleep, and in the darker hours she finds her thoughts turning toward her father, who died
years ago, imagining confrontations that never happened and now never can.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was a moment in the crew lounge when a colleague played
music on a portable speaker and something in the melody reached her before she could prepare. She
excused herself, sat in a bathroom stall for twelve minutes, and thought: I cannot keep doing this
alone. What she fears most is that she will be told she is no longer fit to fly. What she hopes,
quietly, is that someone will tell her she is not beyond repair.
