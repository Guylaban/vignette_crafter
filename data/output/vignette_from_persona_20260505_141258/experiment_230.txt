Kareem is a 54-year-old single man of Lebanese descent who works as a software engineer at a mid-
sized technology firm in the Chicago area. He presents for psychological assessment approximately
fourteen months after a sexual assault that occurred in the parking structure adjacent to his office
building. On a late evening when Kareem had stayed past midnight to meet a deployment deadline, two
men in what he initially took to be security uniforms approached him as he walked to his car. One of
them displayed what appeared to be a badge. Kareem, who had grown up navigating authority figures
with careful deference, complied when they instructed him to stop. What followed was a prolonged
assault in the concrete stairwell of the structure, punctuated by the distant wail of a passing
ambulance that he has never been able to unhear. The men left without a word. Kareem drove home,
showered, and did not report the assault. He was certain, in the hours and days that followed, that
something in the way he had presented himself — his compliance, his willingness to follow their
instruction, the fact that he had been alone so late — had invited what happened. That conviction
has not loosened its grip.

In the weeks after the assault, Kareem began to reorganize his life around the geometry of threat.
He stopped using the parking structure entirely and now parks four blocks away, adding a long detour
that takes him nowhere near the building's eastern side. He will not enter any space where uniformed
personnel are present — he has avoided the post office, declined a company all-hands meeting held in
a building lobby staffed by security guards, and quietly stopped attending the quarterly technology
conferences he once found professionally sustaining. When a police cruiser idles at the curb outside
his apartment, he moves to the back of the building until it leaves. These adjustments accumulated
quietly over months, each one feeling not like retreat but like reasonable precaution, until the
radius of places where he could function had narrowed to his apartment and, on most days, his desk
at work — provided he arrives early enough to be settled before colleagues fill the floor.

At work, the open-plan office has become a source of sustained vigilance. Kareem sits with his back
to the wall and monitors the room continuously, tracking who enters and where they stand. When a
male colleague stops by his desk to talk, Kareem manages the interaction with visible efficiency,
ending it as quickly as courtesy allows. The idea of being alone in a room with a male colleague —
in a conference room, in the elevator — produces a physical alertness that he describes as his body
deciding things before his mind catches up. He has turned down two project lead opportunities in the
past year because both would have required him to manage small teams and hold one-on-one meetings.
His manager has noted the withdrawal and interpreted it as disengagement; their relationship, once
collegial, has grown formal and cool.

Sirens reach him wherever he is. In the middle of a code review, the sound of an ambulance on the
street below pulls him entirely out of the present. He does not always know, in those moments, where
he is. The return to the room is effortful and leaves him hollowed.

What troubles Kareem most deeply, beyond the fear, is a conviction about what the experience has
done to him permanently. He had come to believe, with a quiet certainty that he found more
frightening than the intrusions themselves, that the capacity for ordinary feeling — warmth,
connection, the modest pleasure he once took in his work — had simply been taken from him and would
not return. Alongside this sits a grief that has nowhere to go: he knows that nothing has been done
about what happened, that the men have never been identified, and the unfairness of that fact sits
in him like something structural.

He sought help after his younger sister visited from Dearborn and told him, gently and directly,
that she did not recognise him. He came to the intake session hoping, with considerable doubt, that
he might be told he was not permanently changed. That is what he most wants to be wrong about.
