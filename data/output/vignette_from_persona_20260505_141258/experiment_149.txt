Rodrigo Mendes is a 54-year-old married Latino man who has worked as a parole officer for nearly two
decades, a career built on the conviction that systems of accountability, when functioning properly,
can redirect lives. He presents now with a PCL-5 score of 68, having resisted seeking help for what
he describes, with visible discomfort, as something that should not have happened to him — not in
the way it did, not by the hands it came from.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Rodrigo was returning home after a late shift, still in civilian
clothes, when he was stopped by two officers in a neighborhood adjacent to his jurisdiction. What
began as a routine stop escalated with a speed he still cannot fully account for. He was ordered to
the ground, and when he moved to identify himself as law enforcement, one of the officers struck him
repeatedly — across the back, then across the side of his head — before he could finish the
sentence. He remembers the cold of the pavement against his cheek, the sound of his own breathing
loud in his ears, and then a period he cannot reconstruct with any confidence. What he can recall
arrives in fragments: a voice on a radio, the pressure of a knee, a silence that fell over the scene
that felt, he says, like the world had stopped deciding what would happen to him. He was eventually
released without charge. No report was filed. No officer faced review.

In the weeks that followed, Rodrigo found himself unable to sit comfortably in the silences that are
unavoidable in his work — the pause before a parolee answers a question, the quiet of an empty
hallway between interviews. Each of those silences carries for him now the quality of that moment on
the pavement, when nothing was happening yet and everything was about to. He has come to feel, with
a certainty he cannot reason his way out of, that unusual quiet signals imminent danger, and he
finds himself scanning the room constantly during client meetings — checking exits, watching hands,
reading posture in ways that exceed professional habit. He startles badly when doors open behind
him, something his colleagues have noticed and quietly remarked upon.

His own body has become unreliable in a different way. When his heart rate rises — from climbing
stairs between floors at the office, from a difficult phone call, from nothing he can identify — he
is pulled immediately toward the physical memory of that night, the adrenaline and the helplessness
arriving together. He does not complete the thought when it begins. He has developed a practiced
interruption, a way of pushing the memory back before it can fully surface, which means he has never
been able to reconstruct a coherent account of the event even for himself. The story feels, he says,
like something that happened to someone else — too extreme, too wrong to have been his actual life —
and yet the gaps in it trouble him precisely because he knows something is missing and cannot
retrieve it.

What sits most heavily on Rodrigo is not the physical memory but the meaning he has drawn from it.
He had trusted, implicitly and professionally, in the integrity of law enforcement accountability.
He had built his working life on that trust. What happened to him shattered that framework entirely,
and the absence of any institutional response — no acknowledgment, no consequence, no correction —
has left him with a sense of injustice that has no outlet. He was betrayed, he believes, not only by
the officers who harmed him but by every structure that was supposed to prevent exactly this. That
belief has altered how he sits across from his own clients — men and women navigating a system he
once defended without reservation — in ways he finds deeply disorienting.

His wife noticed the change in him long before he named it, and it was her quiet insistence, over
many months, that finally brought him to this office. What Rodrigo fears most is that telling the
story fully — to the extent he even can — will confirm that nothing about it will ever be made
right. What he hopes for, more tentatively, is that he might find a way to carry it without it
continuing to occupy every silence in the room.
