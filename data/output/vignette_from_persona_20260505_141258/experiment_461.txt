Kosal Phan is a 77-year-old Cambodian American man who has worked as a firefighter for a municipal
department in Southern California for nearly three decades. He is married, lives with his wife in a
quiet residential neighborhood, and by most outward measures presents as a composed, capable man
whose professional life has been defined by running toward danger rather than away from it. What
brought him to the clinic was not, however, anything he encountered on a fire line. It was something
that happened long before he ever held a hose — something from a time when he was not a rescuer but
a child in the middle of a war he had no power to stop.

Kosal was a teenager in Phnom Penh during the years of intense aerial bombardment and ground
conflict that preceded the Khmer Rouge takeover. One afternoon, a series of explosions struck his
neighborhood in rapid succession. He was separated from his family in the chaos, pinned for a period
beneath rubble, surrounded by sounds he has never been able to name adequately in English — a
concussive pressure that was less like noise and more like the air itself being torn apart. People
around him were screaming names. He lay still, covered in dust, certain he was going to die, and
equally certain that no one was coming. He was eventually pulled free, but several people he knew
were not. He left Cambodia not long after, eventually making his way to the United States, where he
built a life with deliberate, almost ferocious intention. He did not speak of those years. He had
come to believe, with quiet conviction, that thinking about what happened would undo him entirely —
that the memories, if fully entered, would be more than he could survive. And so for decades, he
simply did not go there.

What he could not prevent was the memories finding him. They arrive without invitation: clear,
textured, and immediate, as though no time has passed. The sound of a car backfiring, a construction
blast, a firework — any sharp percussive bang — pulls him back to that afternoon with a vividness
that is almost physical. At work, the wail of a passing ambulance is enough to stop him mid-task,
his body going rigid before his mind has caught up. Even hearing certain names — names that were
being called out by people searching for their loved ones in the rubble — can surface the memory
without warning. What disturbs him most about these intrusions is not their content alone but their
texture: the memory feels, he has said, like it belongs to someone else, like footage from another
person's life that has been incorrectly filed in his mind. He cannot reconcile the boy under the
rubble with the man he believes himself to be, and this dissociation has become its own source of
distress.

On the fire line, Kosal's habit of scanning his surroundings — methodically, constantly, tracking
exits and movement and sound — reads as professional vigilance to his colleagues. He has never told
them it does not feel like a choice. At night, he avoids going to bed until exhaustion forces the
issue, sitting up in the living room long after his wife has retired, unwilling to lie in the dark
where the memories have more room. His wife has noticed the distance this creates, the way he is
present in the house but somehow not quite reachable, and the strain between them has grown in ways
neither has fully articulated.

Kosal has also found himself, in quiet moments, rehearsing scenarios of retribution against those he
holds responsible for what happened to his country and to him — not plans, exactly, but vivid
imaginings that feel like the only form of agency available. He is ashamed of these thoughts and has
told no one. What troubles him most is a feeling he cannot quite shake: that someone stronger, or
smarter, or simply less unlucky, would never have been in that position to begin with. He does not
feel like a person who was harmed. He feels, in his own words, like something that was used up.

He came to the clinic after his department mandated a wellness check following a near-incident at a
call where a controlled burn produced a sound that dropped him to his knees. He is hoping,
cautiously, to sleep through the night again. What he fears most is that the therapy will require
him to go back — and that, once there, he will not be able to return.
