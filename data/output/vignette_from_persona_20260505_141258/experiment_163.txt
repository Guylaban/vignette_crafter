Adaeze Okonkwo is a 47-year-old Nigerian-American architect, married for nineteen years, who
presents with a PCL-5 score of 62 following an incident that occurred fourteen months ago in which
she accidentally struck a pedestrian with her car. She had been leaving a project site on a grey
Tuesday afternoon, running behind schedule and mentally rehearsing a client presentation, when a
cyclist emerged from between two parked delivery trucks. She braked, but not quickly enough. She
remembers the sound — a dense, hollow impact against the passenger door — and then the taste of the
coffee she had been drinking, bitter and suddenly wrong in her mouth, flooding back with every
detail. The cyclist, a man in his thirties, survived but sustained a fractured pelvis and spinal
injuries that left him with a permanent limp. Adaeze was not charged. The investigation confirmed
she had been travelling within the speed limit. None of that has mattered to her.

In the weeks following the accident, Adaeze found herself returning compulsively to the sequence of
events, searching for the precise moment she had failed. She had come to believe, with a certainty
that felt irrefutable, that another driver — a more careful, more present person — would never have
been in that position at all. The thought that someone else would simply not have gotten into this
situation has become a fixture of her inner life, arriving unbidden during meetings, during dinner,
in the small hours of the morning. Alongside it runs a parallel and equally relentless current: a
detailed inventory of everything she did wrong, replayed and annotated, sometimes for hours at a
stretch. She occasionally catches herself thinking that at least the man did not die, reaching for
that fact like a handhold on a cliff face. But the relief it offers is brief and unconvincing, and
she knows it.

The memory itself behaves strangely. When Adaeze sits down deliberately and tries to reconstruct
what happened — as her previous therapist once encouraged her to do — she finds the event oddly
inaccessible, as though it is behind frosted glass. Yet it arrives on its own terms without warning.
She will be reviewing blueprints when she suddenly hears, internally and with startling clarity, the
sound of that impact, followed by the screech of her own braking. The taste of coffee returns at the
same moment, acrid and specific, as though she has just taken a sip. These involuntary returns bear
no resemblance to the blankness she encounters when she tries to remember deliberately.

Her daily life as an architect places her in close proximity to the conditions she now finds most
difficult. Site visits require her to drive, and driving has become an exercise in sustained
vigilance. She scans continuously for cyclists, for gaps between parked vehicles, for anything
moving at the periphery of her vision. Her body responds to a pedestrian stepping off a kerb the
same way it responds to genuine danger — a surge of alarm, a physical bracing — even when she is
stationary at a red light. Ordinary sounds in the office, a door slamming, a colleague dropping a
binder, produce a startle response she cannot modulate and cannot explain to the people around her.
Concentration, always one of her professional strengths, has become unreliable. She has missed
details in technical drawings she would once have caught immediately, and six months ago she quietly
removed herself from a high-profile urban transit project, telling her firm she had a scheduling
conflict. She has not told her husband the real reason. She has not told anyone the real reason.

That isolation — the sense of standing behind a partition through which others cannot see and she
cannot pass — has deepened over the past year. The person who once moved through the world with
professional confidence and relational ease feels, to Adaeze, like someone she no longer recognises
when she looks inward. She has begun performing small private rituals before getting into the car: a
specific sequence of checks, a repeated phrase said silently, a way of touching the steering wheel.
She cannot fully articulate what she believes these rituals accomplish, only that skipping them
feels intolerable.

What finally brought her to seek help was her husband noticing that she had not driven their
daughter to school in four months. She told him she preferred the child to take the bus. He did not
argue, but she saw his face, and she understood that the distance between them had become visible.
She comes to treatment hoping to feel like herself again, and terrified that the self she remembers
may no longer exist.
