psychological assessment following a physical and sexual assault that occurred approximately
fourteen months ago. She presents with a PCL-5 score of 54, indicating severe posttraumatic stress,
and has only recently acknowledged to herself that she needs professional help.

The assault took place on a Thursday evening after Renée had stayed late to finish a custom

decades. A man she recognized as a former occasional customer — someone who had come in twice to
have a watch repaired — entered the studio just as she was locking up. What followed was a prolonged
assault during which he restrained her against the workbench where she cuts and sets stones. The
assault ended only when a passing delivery driver knocked at the glass door. The attacker left
through the back. Renée was left on the floor of her own studio, surrounded by the tools of her
trade, the half-finished commission still under the loupe on her bench.

In the weeks that followed, Renée's memory of the event took on a fractured, unreliable quality that
she found deeply disorienting. Certain moments are etched with unbearable precision — the particular
smell of the man's jacket, the sensation of the metal edge of the workbench against her back — while
other stretches of the assault are entirely blank, as though the tape had been cut. When fragments
surface without warning, they arrive mid-thought and then vanish before she can examine them,
leaving her shaken and disoriented. She has found herself stopping the memory deliberately whenever
it begins to unspool, pressing it back before it can reach its fullest extent. What frightened her
most about this was not the remembering itself but the blanks — she had come to believe that the
gaps meant something worse had happened than she could consciously hold, and that if she ever let
the memory run its full course, she would not survive what she found there.

The physical sensation of nausea has become one of the most reliable signals that her body is
registering something her mind has not yet caught up to. It arrives without obvious cause — at the
grocery store, in traffic — and by the time she identifies it as connected to the assault, the
moment has already reorganized her entire day around managing it. On two occasions she has seen the
attacker at a distance in her neighborhood, and both times she was unable to return to work that
day. Talk of loss among colleagues or clients — someone mentioning a death, a bereavement — produces
a visceral response she cannot easily explain to others and has stopped trying to.

Renée now enters every room — her studio, a restaurant, the post office — with her attention moving
immediately to the exits. She has come to feel safe in only a handful of places: her apartment, her
car, and one friend's home. Strangers who approach her in ordinary contexts, a man asking
directions, a customer stepping too quickly toward the counter, register to her as immediate threats
requiring assessment. At work, this has had concrete consequences. She has stopped accepting walk-in
clients entirely, citing appointment-only hours she invented after the assault, and she has had to
turn away several long-standing customers who noticed the change and did not return. The studio,
once a source of deep professional pride, now feels like a place she endures rather than inhabits.

What troubles Renée as much as any of this is what she has come to believe about herself in the
aftermath. She had always thought of herself as capable and self-sufficient, and the fact that she
has not simply moved past this event reads to her as evidence that she is a lousy coper — that other
people would have managed better, recovered faster, resumed their lives more completely. She
describes feeling dirty in a way that ordinary hygiene cannot address, a sense that something about
her is permanently altered and marked. Most of the time she feels nothing at all — a flatness she
describes as feeling dead inside, which she finds almost more disturbing than grief would be.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment three weeks ago when a longtime client, a woman
she has known for years, came in to collect a repaired necklace and mentioned offhandedly that her
husband had just died. Renée excused herself to the back room and could not stop shaking for twenty
minutes. She booked the appointment that afternoon. She is not sure what she hopes for, but she
knows she cannot continue to disappear from her own life.
