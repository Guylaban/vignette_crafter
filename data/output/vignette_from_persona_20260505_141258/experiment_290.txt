Marama Tuilagi is a 46-year-old Fijian-American woman who works as a forensic analyst for a county
medical examiner's office. She is married and has lived in the same mid-sized Pacific Northwest city
for nearly two decades. She presented for treatment approximately eight months after the sudden
death of her younger brother, Sione, who collapsed from an undiagnosed cardiac arrhythmia at the age
of forty-one. Marama was with him when it happened. They had been sitting at her kitchen table on a
Sunday afternoon, laughing over a meal she had cooked — a rare visit, since Sione lived three states
away. He stood up to refill his glass and simply fell. She heard the sound before she saw him: a
sharp exhalation, almost like a grunt of surprise, and then the crack of his head against the tile
floor. She called emergency services and stayed on the line, kneeling beside him, repeating his name
into the silence of the kitchen while the dispatcher's voice — steady, procedural, unbearably calm —
instructed her to begin compressions. Paramedics arrived within minutes, but Sione was pronounced
dead at the hospital that evening. He had shown no symptoms. There had been no warning. He had been
laughing.

In the weeks that followed, Marama found herself ambushed by the memory in discrete, vivid fragments
— not as a continuous narrative but as a series of frozen images: the angle of Sione's arm against
the floor, the particular shade of afternoon light through the kitchen window, his untouched glass
still sitting at the edge of the table. Each image arrived with full sensory weight. She would be
reviewing case files at work and hear, without warning, the dispatcher's voice in her head — that
same measured, professional tone — and the intrusion was so complete, so acoustically present, that
she would have to set down whatever she was holding and press her hands flat against her desk until
the wave of emotion passed. She had come to dread those moments not only for their pain but for what
she feared they revealed: that she was losing her grip on the boundary between the dead and the
living, between her professional world and her private devastation.

That professional world became its own source of difficulty. Marama's work requires her to analyze
physical evidence associated with sudden and violent deaths. She had managed this for sixteen years
with the careful emotional compartmentalization her field demands. After Sione's death, however, the
office became a place she associated with the clinical language of dying — the same language that
had been used to describe her brother in the hours after his collapse. She began arriving late,
leaving early, calling in on days when she knew particular cases were scheduled. Her supervisor had
spoken to her twice. A colleague she had mentored for three years told her quietly that the team
felt her absence. Marama heard this and felt nothing she could name as guilt — only a flat, grey
confirmation of something she had already concluded: that nothing in her work, or anywhere else,
carried the weight it once had.

At home, she had stopped eating regular meals. She would prepare food for her husband, Tomasi, and
then find she had no appetite for it herself, as though the act of nourishing herself had become
irrelevant. She stayed up until two or three in the morning most nights, not doing anything in
particular — sitting with her phone, watching nothing, simply refusing the transition into sleep and
whatever the night might bring. She had lost her faith, not in any single institution, but in the
underlying premise she had once held without examining it: that the world was navigable, that love
was not simply exposure to loss. She had come to believe, with a certainty that felt more like
knowledge than despair, that her life as she had understood it was finished — that what remained was
a diminished version she was simply moving through.

What finally brought Marama to seek help was Tomasi. He did not issue an ultimatum; he simply sat
beside her one night and told her he was frightened for her. She made the appointment the following
morning. What she is most afraid of, she said at intake, is that there is nothing left to recover.
What she is hoping for, she could not quite say — only that she wanted to be able to remember Sione
without having to stop.
