Kwame Asante is a 46-year-old Ghanaian-American man who has been married for nineteen years and
works as a park ranger in a mid-Atlantic state forest. He presented for psychological evaluation
describing a cluster of distressing experiences that have persisted, in varying intensity, since his
deployment with the U.S. Army more than a decade ago. His PCL-5 score of 33 places him at the
threshold of clinical significance, and the picture that emerged across the intake and early
sessions was one of a man who has managed, through discipline and routine, to contain something he
has never fully confronted.

During his second deployment, Kwame's unit was providing security for a forward operating base when
a coordinated mortar attack struck a convoy staging area nearby. He was close enough to hear the
rotors of a medevac helicopter arriving before he could see the casualties — the aircraft sound
reaching him first, then the smoke, then the smell of burning fuel and something else he has never
been able to name precisely but has never forgotten. Several soldiers from a unit he knew personally
were killed. Kwame had been positioned at a checkpoint roughly two hundred metres away. He had
noticed, earlier that morning, unusual movement at the perimeter and had flagged it informally to a
superior, but the report did not travel up the chain in time. He has carried that detail ever since
— the gap between what he noticed and what was done — as though it were a wound that never quite
closed.

In the years following his discharge, Kwame found work that suited both his need for open space and,
he believed, his ability to manage solitude. The forest was quiet. The job made sense to him. But
the work has not been the refuge he anticipated. On patrol, the distant thrum of a news or rescue
helicopter crossing the park sends him rigid. He stops walking, his body orienting toward the sound
before his mind has caught up. There is a quality to those moments that he struggles to articulate —
a sense of the present becoming thin, of himself becoming slightly less solid, as though he is
beginning to dissolve at the edges. He does not lose time, but the feeling frightens him, and he has
come to dread it in a way that is distinct from the alertness he feels around other unexpected
sounds: a branch cracking, a car backfiring on the access road, a visitor dropping equipment. Those
sounds pull his attention like a hand on his collar. He scans the tree line, watches the body
language of hikers and campers with a vigilance he knows is disproportionate but cannot argue
himself out of, certain that relaxing his watch is precisely the kind of lapse that costs people
their lives.

His memories of the attack are not vague. When they come — unbidden during a quiet stretch of trail,
or when he allows himself to think about that period directly — they arrive with a sensory
completeness that ordinary memories do not have. He can smell the air from that morning. The images
are sharp in a way that feels less like remembering and more like being shown something under very
bright light. He has described them as more real than things that happened last week, and that
quality disturbs him as much as the content.

What Kwame believes about himself now is perhaps the most clinically significant dimension of his
presentation. He has come to feel that he is not the man he was before that deployment — not in a
way that might improve, but in a way that is settled and permanent. He tells himself that a man who
stays alert at all times is simply being responsible, but underneath that framing is a conviction
that he cannot afford to be otherwise, that the version of himself capable of ease or inattention
already failed once. The guilt about what he did not do — the report that went nowhere, the men who
died — sits in him not as grief but as verdict.

Kwame sought help after his wife told him quietly one evening that she felt she was living with
someone who was always waiting for something terrible to happen. He had no rebuttal. He came to the
first session hoping, in his words, to be useful again — to his wife, to the job, to himself — and
most afraid that the person he was before no longer exists to be recovered.
