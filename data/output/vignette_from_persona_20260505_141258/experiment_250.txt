Dmitri Volkov is a 32-year-old single man of Eastern European descent who serves as a combat pilot
at a military installation in the southwestern United States. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 79,
reflecting severe post-traumatic stress, and has sought clinical help for the first time
approximately fourteen months after the event that precipitated his current difficulties.

Dmitri was sexually assaulted by a fellow officer — a man he had known for nearly four years,
someone he had flown alongside, shared deployments with, and considered among his closest friends.
The assault occurred late at night in the shared accommodation block during a training rotation.
What made the event particularly disorienting was not only its violence but its origin: Dmitri had
no reason to be vigilant. He was in a familiar place, with a trusted person, behind a locked door.
The person who harmed him was someone whose footsteps he would have recognised, whose knock he would
have answered without hesitation. In the months that followed, that detail — the locked door, the
familiarity, the absence of any warning — became impossible for him to integrate. He had come to
believe, with a quiet and absolute certainty, that safety is an illusion that people construct to
make themselves feel better. The lock had meant nothing. Knowing someone for years had meant
nothing.

In the immediate aftermath, Dmitri told no one. He completed the training rotation, flew his
sorties, filed his reports, and returned to base. From the outside, he functioned. Internally,
something had reorganised. He began sleeping lightly, waking at the smallest sounds — a door closing
down the corridor, a vehicle reversing in the lot outside — and lying rigid in the dark, listening
for evidence that someone might be approaching his quarters. He installed a secondary bolt on his
door and began leaving a light on in the hallway. He was not sure what he expected to hear, only
that the silence between sounds had become unbearable. Even inside his own home, a small apartment
off-base where he had lived for two years without incident, he could not settle. He sat with his
back to walls, faced doorways, and found himself scanning the room during conversations in ways he
struggled to conceal.

His professional environment introduced its own complications. The flight line is an unpredictable
setting by nature — sudden radio traffic, unexpected changes to mission parameters, last-minute
personnel rotations. Before the assault, Dmitri had experienced this unpredictability as a feature
of the work he loved. Now, any deviation from a known plan produced a spike of alarm that he found
difficult to explain and harder to manage. When a new crew member was assigned to his unit without
advance notice, he spent the better part of a week watching the man's movements with a vigilance he
recognised as disproportionate but could not suspend. He had lost the internal shorthand by which he
had previously sorted people into categories of safe and unsafe, and in its absence, anyone acting
in a manner he could not immediately read — hesitating before speaking, moving toward him from an
unexpected angle — registered as a potential threat.

Nights remained the most difficult period. When nightmares came, as they did several times a week,
waking from them did not bring relief. The transition back into consciousness carried its own
momentum, and Dmitri would find himself already scanning the room, already listening, already
certain that something was wrong, before he had fully oriented to where he was. He had come to dread
sleep in part because of what it produced, and in part because waking felt like its own form of
ambush.

What Dmitri is most explicit about, sitting in the intake session with his hands folded and his
posture carefully composed, is not the hypervigilance or the nightmares but the loss he cannot name
precisely. He describes himself as a lousy coper — not with self-pity but with a kind of flat
conviction, as though he has reviewed the evidence and reached a verdict. He says he no longer
believes people are who they present themselves to be. He stopped attending the informal social
gatherings his unit holds on Friday evenings eight months ago, and no one has asked why.

What finally brought him in was a near-miss on a training flight — not caused by his symptoms, he is
careful to say, but close enough in time to a particularly bad week that he could no longer tell
himself the two were unrelated. He is not sure what he is hoping for. He says, quietly, that he
would like to trust his own judgement again.
