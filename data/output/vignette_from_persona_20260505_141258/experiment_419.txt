Mireille is a 19-year-old dancer of French descent who grew up in a mid-sized American city where
she trained at a regional ballet conservatory from the age of seven. She came to the clinic eight
months after being caught in a crowd crush at an outdoor music festival, an event that has since
reorganised her life around fear in ways she is only beginning to name.

The evening had started ordinarily enough — Mireille had gone with two friends, and the crowd was
dense but festive. Then, without warning, a surge from the rear of the general admission area
compressed thousands of people into a shrinking space. Mireille was separated from her friends
within seconds. She remembers the exact moment the crowd closed around her: she could not lift her
arms, could not turn her body, could not move her feet. The pressure against her ribs made breathing
a conscious labour. She was upright only because the bodies around her held her upright, and for
nearly forty minutes she was carried and compressed and pushed, unable to control a single inch of
her own movement. The smell of sweat, spilled beer, and something chemical — a smoke machine still
running somewhere nearby — saturated everything. She survived without physical injury, but she left
that field a different person.

In the weeks that followed, Mireille found that her body had retained the event in ways that
bypassed language entirely. She knows what happened; she can state the facts in a sentence. But when
she tries to describe the texture of it — the particular helplessness, the quality of the fear — she
reaches for words and finds none. What comes instead is the memory arriving whole: not recalled so
much as re-entered, complete with the pressure across her chest, the smell of the crowd, the sound
swallowed by noise. These episodes leave her disoriented and trembling, convinced for a moment that
she is still there.

Certain things bring the memory flooding back with particular force. Physical exhaustion, which
accumulates steadily across a full day of rehearsal, carries a bodily echo of the depleted
helplessness she felt trapped in that crowd. Any sense that someone is behind her or moving in her
direction without her awareness — a student crossing the studio floor, a stranger closing the gap on
a sidewalk — produces a jolt of alarm that is immediate and total. And being touched unexpectedly,
held, or even guided by a partner during a lift can trigger a cascade of panic that she cannot
always contain.

Mireille had come to believe that thinking carefully about what happened would break something in
her — that if she allowed the memory full access, she would not come back from it. This belief made
her vigilant about sealing the event off, and the sealing required effort that left little room for
much else. She stopped attending the social events her conservatory cohort organised. She gave up
the contemporary dance elective she had been genuinely excited about, because the partnering work
required close physical contact she could no longer tolerate. She began covering her face with her
hands in moments of overwhelm — in the studio, in the dining hall — a gesture that had become
reflexive. She stopped going out in the evenings almost entirely, and when she did leave her
dormitory she chose routes that kept her away from crowds.

She wakes most nights to check whether her room is locked, whether anyone is there. Unexpected
movement at the edge of her vision — a door swinging open, a classmate raising an arm — makes her
flinch and brace. She has difficulty holding choreography in her head during class, a failure that
bewilders and shames her.

What she feels most acutely, underneath all of it, is a sense of profound injustice: nothing was
investigated, no one was held accountable, and the festival's organisers have faced no consequences.
She had come to feel that she no longer fits anywhere — not among her peers, who seem untouched, not
in the studio that once felt like home. It was her director, noticing the deterioration in her
attendance and her affect, who finally encouraged her to make an appointment. Mireille arrived
hoping, above all, to be able to dance again without her body turning against her — and terrified
that the price of getting there would be having to go back inside the memory she has worked so hard
to keep at a distance.
