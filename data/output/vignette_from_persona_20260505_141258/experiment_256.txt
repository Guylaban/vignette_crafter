Liam Kowalski is a 35-year-old single man of Polish descent who works as a special education teacher
at a public elementary school in a mid-sized city. He presented for treatment approximately eight
months after being mugged on his way home from a school staff event on a Friday evening in early
autumn. He had stayed late to help a colleague prepare materials for the following week and was
walking the four blocks to his car when two men approached him from behind. One grabbed him by the
collar and shoved him hard into a chain-link fence, wrenching his neck in the process, while the
other pressed close and demanded his wallet and phone. Liam complied immediately, handing everything
over without resistance. The men ran. The entire encounter lasted perhaps ninety seconds. He stood
alone on the pavement afterward, neck aching, hands shaking, and the sound of one of the men
shouting the initial demand still ringing in his ears. He did not call the police that night. He
told himself he was fine, walked to his car, and drove home.

In the weeks that followed, the memory did not soften. It arrived instead with the quality of still
images — the fence's diamond-wire pattern against his cheek, the man's face close to his, the
precise angle of the streetlight overhead — each frame exact and unwanted. When these images
surfaced, Liam noticed they were accompanied first by a surge of rage so acute it surprised him, and
then, almost immediately, by something heavier: guilt. He had come to believe, with a certainty that
felt unassailable, that another man — someone more alert, more capable — simply would not have found
himself in that situation. He had stayed late. He had walked that route. He had frozen. The guilt
and the rage coiled together whenever the memory arrived, and he began to avoid letting his mind go
quiet enough for either to find him.

At school, the environment that had once felt like his domain became newly complicated. Liam works
with children who sometimes escalate — who shout when frustrated, who cry out without warning.
Before the mugging, he had understood this as part of his professional landscape. Now, when a
student's voice rose sharply in the classroom, something in his body responded before his mind could
intervene: his neck would tighten along the same line of pain from that night, and for a
disorienting moment the room around him would collapse and he would be back against the fence, the
shouting immediate and close. He had begun positioning himself near the classroom door during group
activities, and he catalogued the exits of every space he entered — the gym, the staff room, the
school library — within moments of arriving, though he could not have articulated a coherent plan
for what he would do with that information.

When a police officer visited the school for a community safety presentation in the spring, Liam
spent the duration of the session at the back of the room, unable to attend to what was being said,
his body rigid and his attention fixed on the officer's presence in a way that exhausted him. He had
not reported the mugging, and the sight of a uniform seemed to press on that fact in ways he found
difficult to name. He had come to feel that he was inadequate in some fundamental sense — not merely
that he had made a mistake, but that the mistake had revealed something that had always been true
about him. He was unable to relax even in his own apartment in the evenings, the stillness feeling
less like safety than like exposure.

It was a parent-teacher conference in late spring that finally moved him toward help. A parent
raised their voice in frustration during the meeting, and Liam felt himself go somewhere else
entirely — not metaphorically, but with the full sensory weight of that Friday night — before
returning, shaken, to the conference room. He apologised and ended the meeting early. He sat in his
car for twenty minutes afterward. What he most hopes for, he said at intake, is to be able to do his
job again without his body treating his classroom like a crime scene. What he is most afraid of is
that the person he was before that night no longer exists.
