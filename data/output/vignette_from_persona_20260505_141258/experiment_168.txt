Renata Kowalski is a 54-year-old married woman of Polish descent who has worked as a chef for nearly
three decades, most recently as the head chef at a mid-sized restaurant in the city where she has
lived with her husband for the past twenty years. She came to the initial intake session composed
but visibly guarded, choosing a seat with her back to the wall and tracking the door throughout the
conversation. She described herself as someone who had always prided herself on her competence and
self-sufficiency, qualities she believed had served her well across a demanding career. What brought
her to treatment, she said quietly, was the recognition that she could no longer manage on her own.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Renata was assaulted by a male colleague — a sous-chef she had
worked alongside for six years, a man she had mentored, advocated for, and trusted without
reservation. The assault occurred late on a weeknight after the kitchen had closed, when the two of
them were alone finishing inventory. He had moved toward her suddenly, and what followed happened in
a space she now cannot fully reconstruct in language. She has tried many times to write it down or
explain it clearly, even to herself, and finds that the experience resists being put into words —
not because she chooses silence, but because the event seems to exist in a register that precedes
language. What she does have are pieces: the sound of a walk-in refrigerator door, the particular
angle at which she was pressed against a steel counter, a smell she cannot name but that returns
without warning. These fragments arrive without sequence and without the sense that they belong to a
coherent story she can hold at a distance. When they surface, she does not merely remember — her
shoulders curl forward, her arms draw inward, her body returning to the posture of that night as
though the interval between then and now had briefly ceased to exist.

In the weeks after the assault, Renata continued going to work because she could not imagine what
stopping would mean. But the kitchen, which had always been her domain, became a landscape of
threat. Unexpected movement — a line cook stepping quickly into her peripheral vision, a server
pushing through the swing door — would produce an immediate physical response, her body bracing
before her mind had registered anything. She became hypervigilant in a way that exhausted her,
scanning the room constantly, noting where every person stood, who was approaching and from which
direction. Strangers entering the kitchen for deliveries felt dangerous in a way she could not
justify to herself but could not override. She had come to believe, with a certainty that felt like
knowledge rather than fear, that there was simply no way to know which man would harm her — that the
six years she had spent trusting her colleague had demonstrated only her own blindness. The betrayal
felt less like an event than a revelation about the nature of people, and she found herself
measuring everyone against it.

Over the following months, Renata began finding reasons to ensure she was never alone in a room with
a male colleague. She rearranged scheduling in ways that her staff noticed. She shortened
conversations that moved toward anything personal or emotionally weighted, deflecting with
efficiency and humor until the other person withdrew. Her husband, a patient man who had initially
responded to her withdrawal with gentle concern, had grown quieter himself. She was aware that she
had placed a distance between them she did not fully understand how to close, and that his attempts
at emotional closeness now produced in her something close to panic, though she had not told him
this. Their evenings, once companionable, had become careful.

What finally brought Renata to seek help was a moment three weeks ago when a trusted female
colleague placed a hand on her arm during a difficult conversation, and Renata felt herself go
entirely cold. She drove home and sat in her car for an hour. She had not cried. What she felt
instead was a clear and frightening recognition that she was disappearing from her own life. She
came to treatment hoping, she said, to understand what had happened to her. What she feared most was
that the answer would confirm what she had already begun to suspect — that the person she had been
before that night was simply gone.
