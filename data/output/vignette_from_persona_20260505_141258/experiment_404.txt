67 places her symptoms in a severe range. What brought her finally to the clinic door was not a

no longer recognized as a life.

a rural transit bus for the forty-minute ride home, as she had done hundreds of times before. The
bus was nearly empty. A man she did not know sat beside her, and before she could fully register
what was happening, he had assaulted her in the back of that near-deserted vehicle. She did not
scream. She has never been fully able to explain to herself why she did not scream, and that silence
has become one of the heaviest things she carries. When the bus reached her stop she walked home,
told no one, and went to bed.

In the weeks that followed, the memory did not stay where she tried to put it. It arrived without
warning — while she was bent over a row of crops, while she was washing dishes, while her husband
was speaking to her about something ordinary. The memory did not announce itself; it simply
appeared, sudden and complete, pulling her back into the particular quality of light on that bus,
the smell of the vinyl seat. Her husband noticed she had become distant but did not press her, and
she offered nothing. She had come to believe, in a way she could not quite articulate but felt with
certainty, that she was somehow permanently altered — that the woman who had boarded that bus and
the woman who got off it were not the same person, and that no one around her could understand what
that meant.

She stopped taking the bus entirely. This had concrete consequences: the fields where she works are
accessible by bus or by a ride she must ask others for, and asking felt unbearable. She began
arriving late or missing days, and her supervisor had spoken to her twice about her reliability. She
said nothing to explain herself. She had also begun avoiding the social rhythms that had once
structured her days — the small conversations between rows, the shared lunches, the neighborly talk
at the end of a shift. Certain topics, she had learned, could pull the memory forward without
warning: anything touching on being alone, on men, on buses, on not being believed. She could not
predict exactly when a conversation would turn that way, so she found it safer to be absent from
conversations altogether.

At home, Dolores drinks in the evenings. She does not call it a problem; she calls it the only time
her body feels quiet. She has also developed a habit of mentally walking herself through every
possible way a situation could go wrong before she enters it — rehearsing the worst so that, she
reasons, she will not be caught off guard again. She checks behind her when she walks, particularly
in the fields and in parking areas, scanning for anyone approaching from behind. She feels safe only
in her own kitchen and, on good days, in a particular corner of the field where she can see in all
directions. Everywhere else, her body remains on alert.

What frightens her most is not the memory itself but what she has concluded about herself because of
it: that she cannot trust her own judgment, that she failed herself at the moment it mattered most,
that she is now somehow outside the ordinary human community — an outsider to her own marriage, to

her — quiet, persistent — is not yet willing to accept that this is simply who she is now.
