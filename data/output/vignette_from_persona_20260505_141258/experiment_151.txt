Desmond Holt is a 60-year-old warehouse worker of British descent who has been in a relationship
with his partner of eleven years. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 62, seeking help for the first
time regarding experiences that have their roots in his military service nearly four decades ago,
though he describes the past two years as a period in which something has shifted, and the weight he
has carried quietly for so long has become impossible to manage alone.

Desmond served in the infantry during a period of active combat deployment in his late twenties.
During one particular engagement, his unit came under sustained and unexpected fire in a narrow
clearing. In the chaos that followed, several of his fellow soldiers were killed within yards of
him. Desmond was wearing standard-issue combat fatigues — the rough texture of the collar, the
particular weight of the fabric across his shoulders — when he watched men he had known for years
die in a matter of minutes. He survived, largely by chance of position. He has never fully accepted
that distinction between luck and deserving.

In the years immediately following his discharge, Desmond managed to build a functional life. He
found work in logistics and warehousing, married briefly, and eventually settled into his current
relationship. He did not speak about what had happened, and for a long time the silence felt like
containment. He had come to believe that as long as he did not look directly at the memory, it would
remain at a manageable distance. He stopped visiting the town in the south where he had been
stationed for training before deployment — a place he had once known well — and he stopped wearing
any clothing that resembled military-style garments. These were not decisions he announced; they
were quiet rearrangements of his life that accumulated over years. His partner noticed that he
refused to wear the olive-green work jacket she bought him one winter, but he offered no explanation
she found satisfying.

At the warehouse where he works, Desmond operates a forklift on the floor of a large industrial
space. The environment is loud, physically demanding, and populated almost entirely by younger men.
On one occasion roughly two years ago, a colleague arrived wearing a surplus military jacket — the
kind sold at army disposal stores — and something in the cut of the collar and the dull green of the
fabric stopped Desmond mid-movement. What followed was not a flashback in the cinematic sense, but a
flooding of terror so complete and so immediate that he was certain, in that moment, that he was
back in the clearing. The terror did not feel like a memory. It felt like the present tense. He had
to leave the floor and sit in his car for forty minutes before he could return to work. He told no
one what had happened.

Since then, when he tries to recall the event in any deliberate way, the same terror returns with an
intensity that feels indistinguishable from the original experience. Alongside it comes guilt — a
guilt he cannot reason his way out of, a certainty that the men who died deserved to live more than
he did, that his survival was not neutral but was somehow taken from them. What frightens him most
is that he cannot articulate any of this clearly. He knows what happened. He can sequence the
events. But when he tries to put the experience into words — with his partner, in the single
conversation they have attempted, or now in the intake session — he reaches for language and finds
nothing adequate. The sentences stop before they carry any weight.

He has come to feel that the man he is now bears little relationship to the man he believed himself
to be. He describes this not dramatically but with a flat bewilderment, as though he has misplaced
something essential and cannot remember where he last saw it. He is also increasingly convinced that
the world is a place in which catastrophe is always proximate, always possible.

What finally brought Desmond to seek help was his partner's quiet statement that she felt she was
losing him. He came not because he believes he can be helped, but because he does not want to lose
her too.
