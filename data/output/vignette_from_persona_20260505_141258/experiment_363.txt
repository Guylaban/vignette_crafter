Destiny is a 19-year-old Native American woman who lives with her husband in a small apartment on
the outskirts of a mid-sized city. She has been unemployed since the assault occurred approximately
eight months ago, and she presents to the clinic visibly tense, her arms folded tightly across her
body, her eyes moving frequently toward the door.

On a warm evening in early spring, Destiny had stepped outside a convenience store after buying a
drink when a man she did not know approached her from behind. He smelled strongly of alcohol — she
would later describe it as something sharp and sweet that she could not name — and within seconds
had grabbed her and forced her into the narrow alley beside the building. Throughout the assault, he
shouted at her in Arabic, a language she did not understand, and the words felt to her like an
additional violation, incomprehensible and therefore boundless in their threat. The roar of a
motorcycle passing on the street at some point during the attack became threaded into the experience
in a way she cannot fully explain, only that the sound seemed to mark the moment she understood no
one was coming to help her. She managed to break free and ran, but the man was never identified or
apprehended.

In the weeks that followed, Destiny found that her body and the world around her had become
unreliable in equal measure. A motorcycle accelerating on a nearby road could, without any
transitional moment of recognition, return her entirely to the alley — not as a memory she was
recalling but as something happening to her right then, her heart slamming, her breath gone. The
smell of alcohol on a stranger's breath, or even passing a bar with its doors open, produced the
same collapse of time. She began to hear, on certain days, fragments of shouted syllables in her
mind, sounds she could not parse but that carried the full weight of that night. She could not
predict when any of this would happen, and the unpredictability itself became its own source of
dread.

She had come to believe, with a quiet and terrible certainty, that the world she had understood
before the assault — one in which strangers were mostly neutral, in which public spaces were
navigable, in which she had some basic standing in her own life — no longer existed. What frightened
her most was not any single trigger but the conclusion she had drawn: that the event had happened
because she was the kind of person it could happen to, and that someone with more presence or
strength or instinct would have stopped it before it reached that point. She returned to this
thought often, turning it over without resolution.

Destiny stopped going outside after dark entirely. Her husband sometimes asked her to walk with him
in the evenings, and she would decline without explanation, and the distance between them grew in
ways neither of them named directly. She avoided the date on the calendar with a vigilance that
surprised even her — rerouting her awareness around it for days on either side. She had also,
gradually and without deciding to, stopped looking at herself in the mirror or paying attention to
her own body in any deliberate way, as though her physical self had become a site she could not
safely visit.

Inside the apartment, she listened. She was often positioned near walls, and she would register
every sound at the door — footsteps in the hallway, the particular creak of the building settling —
and assess each one before she could move on. When she did go out during daylight, she checked
behind her repeatedly, certain on some level that the man, still unidentified, was capable of
finding her.

She had not worked since the assault. She had previously taken pride in her independence and her
plans, and she was certain now that the person who had held those plans no longer existed in any
functional sense.

What finally brought her to seek help was a conversation with her husband in which he told her,
gently, that he did not know how to reach her anymore. She came to the clinic not entirely sure what
she was hoping for, but most afraid that what she had lost about herself was not recoverable.
