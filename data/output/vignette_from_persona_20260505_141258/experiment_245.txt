Rangi Taufa is a 53-year-old Tongan-American airline pilot who has been married for twenty-six years
and has, by most external measures, built a life of considerable stability and accomplishment. He
presents now with a PCL-5 score of 70, referred through his airline's employee assistance programme
after a colleague noticed him gripping a cockpit door handle for nearly a full minute before he
could bring himself to let go of it.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Rangi was travelling on a layover in a country he has asked not
to be named in his file. He was detained without explanation at a checkpoint, taken to a facility he
could not identify, and held for eleven days. During that time he was subjected to systematic
torture. He was kept in a cell that was intermittently plunged into total silence — no ventilation
sound, no voices, nothing — and he came to understand, through repetition, that the silence meant
someone was about to come through the door. The door itself was thick and industrial, with a heavy
bolt that he could hear sliding before every session. The smell in the room was a specific compound
of mildew, chemical disinfectant, and something metallic he has never been able to name precisely.
On two occasions another detainee in an adjacent cell wept loudly and uncontrollably, and both times
what followed for Rangi was among the worst of what he experienced. He was eventually released
without charge and repatriated. He told his wife he had been in an accident. He told his employer he
had been ill.

In the months that followed, Rangi continued flying. The professional structure of the cockpit — its
checklists, its clear hierarchies, its noise — initially felt like the one place he could function.
But the job also placed him in proximity to the very things that had become unbearable. Crowded
boarding gates, which he must pass through on every trip, produce in him a vigilance so acute that
he finds himself scanning faces methodically, reading posture, watching hands, certain that
somewhere in the press of bodies there is a person who intends him harm. He has begun arriving at
gates late, after most passengers have boarded, and his punctuality record — once impeccable — has
attracted comment from his chief pilot.

At home, he checks the locks on every door and window before he can sleep, and again when he wakes
in the night. His wife, Mele, initially interpreted this as a quirk that would pass. It has not
passed. He cannot explain to her, or fully to himself, why the possibility of an unlocked door feels
less like a risk and more like a certainty of catastrophe. When the house goes quiet — when the
refrigerator hum drops out or the street outside stills — he is immediately and completely
elsewhere, his body convinced that something is about to enter.

When Rangi attempts to recall what happened to him in any deliberate way, the memory does not arrive
as a narrative. It arrives as a series of still images, precise and flat, like photographs laid out
on a table — the bolt on the door, the particular angle of a fluorescent light, a man's shoes.
Alongside those images comes the smell, immediate and specific, and when it arrives he cannot locate
himself in his kitchen or his office or his cockpit. He has learned to stop the attempt quickly,
because if he does not, the emotion that follows is of a magnitude he cannot manage in any setting
where he is visible to others. He has not told Mele what the images contain.

What finally brought him to seek help was not the door handle incident, though that frightened him.
It was a moment three weeks ago in the crew lounge when a junior flight attendant began crying after
a difficult passenger interaction, and Rangi had to leave the room and sit alone in a stairwell for
forty minutes before he could return to work. He had come to believe he was managing. That afternoon
made clear to him that he was not. What he fears most, sitting in the intake room, is that he will
be grounded. What he hopes for, though he has not yet said this aloud, is that someone will tell him
it is possible to stop waiting for the door.
