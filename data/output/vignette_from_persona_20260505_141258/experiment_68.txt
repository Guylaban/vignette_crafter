Fatima Khalil is a 51-year-old married woman of Lebanese descent who has worked in hotel management
for nearly two decades. She presents for psychological assessment approximately fourteen months
after a toxic chemical exposure incident that occurred at the large downtown hotel she manages, and
which she describes as the most terrifying experience of her life.

The incident took place on a busy Tuesday morning during a routine inspection of the hotel's
maintenance level. A contractor had been working with industrial cleaning agents in a poorly
ventilated utility corridor, and when Fatima arrived to sign off on the work order, she walked
directly into a cloud of aerosolized chemical compound. Within seconds her eyes were burning, her
throat had closed to what felt like a pinhole, and she lost her footing against the wall, unable to
call for help. She remembered the contractor's panicked face above her, the sharp antiseptic-
chemical smell filling her lungs, and then the fluorescent lights of an emergency room ceiling. She
spent three days hospitalized for chemical burns to her airways, and for weeks afterward her voice
carried a rasp she was convinced would never fully leave her.

In the months that followed her discharge, Fatima returned to work because she believed she had no
choice — the hotel was her responsibility and she had a staff of forty depending on her. But the
building that had been her professional home for eleven years had become something she could no
longer navigate without cost. The maintenance corridors remained off-limits to her; she reassigned
all utility inspections to her deputy manager without ever fully explaining why. What had once been
a source of pride — her hands-on presence in every corner of the property — quietly contracted. Her
deputy noticed. Her husband noticed. Fatima told them both she was simply delegating more
effectively now that she was at a senior level, and she had repeated this to herself often enough
that she almost believed it.

The hotel environment itself had become a source of sustained alertness she could not switch off. On
the floor, surrounded by the ordinary noise of a working hotel — a luggage cart dropping
unexpectedly, a door slamming at the end of a corridor — Fatima found her body responding before her
mind had time to assess the situation. Her heart would slam against her ribs and her hands would go
cold and she would stand very still, scanning. She had come to believe that her body was simply
doing what the situation required, that a hotel was a genuinely unpredictable environment and that
staying alert was professional competence rather than anything else. But the alertness did not stop
when she left the building. At night she lay beside her husband unable to let herself fall fully
asleep, certain in a way she could not articulate that something required her attention. She had
told him she was going through a stressful period at work, which was true, and he had accepted this.

What she found hardest to explain, even to herself, was the smell. Any antiseptic or clinical odor —
the hand sanitizer dispensers in the hotel lobby, the cleaning products used on the bathroom
fixtures, and above all the particular sharp scent of a hospital corridor — produced an immediate
and overwhelming physical response that she experienced as entirely disproportionate and deeply
humiliating. She had accompanied her husband to a routine cardiology appointment eight months ago
and had stood in the hospital entrance, unable to move past the reception desk, her body rigid and
her vision narrowing. She had told him she would wait in the car. She had not returned to a medical
building since.

It was her husband who finally insisted she speak to someone, after she woke one night rigid and
sweating and could not explain to him what had frightened her. What brought Fatima to the intake
appointment was less any single crisis than a slow accumulation: the shrinking of her professional
authority, the distance that had grown between her and her husband in the small hours of the night,
and a private fear she had not yet named aloud — that she was not recovering, and that she may never
fully return to the person she was before she walked into that corridor.
