Rajan is a 49-year-old South Asian man who has spent the better part of his adult life as a military
medic, a career built on the premise that he could hold others together under the worst imaginable
conditions. He is married, though the marriage has grown strained in ways he struggles to
articulate, and it was his wife who ultimately persuaded him to seek help after months of watching
him disappear into himself. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 56, and what he describes in the first
sessions arrives haltingly, in fragments, as though the words themselves are refusing to cooperate.

The abuse was perpetrated by a former partner, a woman he was with for nearly three years before his
current marriage. He has never spoken about it in those terms before — *abuse* — and the word sits
uncomfortably in his mouth. What he can describe, though not in any linear sequence, is the physical
reality of it: being struck, being held down, the particular smell of her sweat during the moments
she became violent, the way certain blows landed in his ribs and jaw. He had told himself, and
others, that it was complicated. She had faced no consequences. As far as he knows, she has
continued her life without interruption, and he had come to understand this not as an injustice but
as confirmation of something worse — that perhaps he had not been worth protecting, that something
in his constitution had made this possible, had made him deserving of it in some way he could not
name but could not stop believing.

The symptoms did not arrive all at once. In the months following the relationship's end, he threw
himself back into his work, which in a military medical context meant long shifts, controlled chaos,
and the useful numbness of procedure. But over time, certain things began to accumulate. Colleagues
who raised their voices during drills. The smell of a fellow medic after a long exercise. These
moments would pull him somewhere he did not want to go, and what frightened him most was that when
the memory returned it did not feel like remembering — it felt like being back inside it, with the
physical sensation of impact returning in his ribs, a tightening in his jaw that had no present-day
cause. He cannot find words for what the memory is. When asked to describe the event in session, he
goes quiet in a way that is not reluctance but something more like blankness, as though the
experience exists in a register that language cannot reach.

He stopped exercising. This was not a conscious decision so much as a discovered boundary — the
first time he pushed through a run and felt his own sweat soaking through his shirt, the smell of it
triggered something so immediate and physical that he turned back and did not try again. He began
avoiding the communal areas of the base, finding reasons to complete paperwork alone, eating at odd
hours. He avoided family gatherings, particularly those where certain relatives — people who had
known him during that relationship, who might look at him with knowledge or pity — would be present.
He rotated through three or four sets of clothes, having quietly retired anything that resembled
what he had worn during that period, though he could not have explained the logic to anyone without
it sounding strange. These habits formed slowly and then became the architecture of his days.

He sleeps poorly, lying awake with a vigilance he recognises from deployment but that now has no
tactical object. Ordinary sounds — a door, a voice in the corridor — bring him upright. He feels
safe, genuinely safe, only in his car and in one room of his house, and he is aware that this is not
a sustainable perimeter. At work, the presence of senior officers or MPs moving through the ward
produces a tension he cannot account for rationally but cannot override.

What finally brought him in was a moment at a family gathering when a relative — someone who had
known her — walked through the door, and Rajan found himself unable to stay in the room. He left
without explanation. Driving home, he thought: *nothing in my life feels like mine anymore.* He is
most afraid, he says, that this is simply who he is now. He is hoping, though he does not quite
believe it yet, to be told otherwise.
