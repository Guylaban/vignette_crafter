Kwame Asante is a 55-year-old Ghanaian-American painter, married for nearly three decades, who
presented for treatment approximately eight months after the sudden death of his adult son, Kofi.
Kofi was thirty-one years old and had complained of chest tightness during a phone call with Kwame
on a Tuesday evening. Kwame told him to rest, assuming it was stress. By the following morning, Kofi
was dead of a massive cardiac event, discovered by his wife when she woke. Kwame received the call
while standing in the parking lot of a job site, roller in hand, paint still wet on his forearms. He
had not been there. He had not gone to him. That fact — ordinary and unavoidable as it was — had
lodged itself at the center of everything that followed.

In the weeks after the funeral, Kwame moved through the necessary rituals of grief with a composure
that those around him mistook for strength. He returned to work within ten days. He kept painting.
But something had shifted in the texture of ordinary life that he could not name and could not
reverse. The job site, which had once been simply where he worked, became a place threaded with
unbearable association. The sound of a passing ambulance on a nearby street — a sound common to any
urban work environment — would stop him mid-stroke. His chest would tighten, his hands would still,
and without any coherent image forming in his mind, the full weight of that Tuesday evening would
descend on him: not as a picture, but as a feeling so complete it seemed to occupy his body rather
than his thoughts. He came to dread the ambulance's wail the way some men dread a particular smell
or song, though he could not always explain to himself exactly why the sound hit him the way it did.

At home, the evenings became their own kind of difficulty. When the light went, something in Kwame
shifted into a state of readiness he could not stand down from. He would lie beside his wife, Abena,
listening to the house settle, his body taut against the mattress, as though waiting for news that
had not yet arrived but was certain to. He was convinced, in a way he recognized as irrational but
could not dismantle, that the darkness carried a particular danger — that the night was when things
happened, when calls came, when the world rearranged itself without warning. He felt no safer in his
own home than he did anywhere else; the familiarity of the rooms offered nothing. He had come to
believe that safety was simply no longer something available to him.

What frightened him most, perhaps, was how unrecognizable he had become to himself. Kwame had always
been a man who knew who he was — a craftsman, a father, a husband, a man of steady habits and quiet
faith. He could no longer locate that person. He described himself in the intake session as feeling
"hollowed out," as though the man who had answered that parking-lot phone call had not fully
returned from it. Conversations in which others mentioned losing someone — a neighbor's passing, a
colleague's bereavement — would cause him to withdraw into silence so complete that coworkers had
begun to give him a wide berth. He had not explained himself to them, and they had stopped asking.
The distance between himself and other people felt both enormous and fixed. He was certain they
could not understand what he carried, and he had largely stopped trying to let them in.

Abena had finally asked him, quietly and without accusation, whether he was going to let someone
help him. He had not answered her that night. But he called the clinic the following week. He
arrived presenting with a PCL-5 score of 67, visibly guarded, sitting forward in his chair as though
prepared to leave. What he said he wanted was to stop feeling like his life was over. What he seemed
most afraid of was that it might be.
