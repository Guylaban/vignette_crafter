Miriam Kovač is a forty-year-old data scientist of Croatian descent who presents for her initial
psychological assessment accompanied by a referral letter from her primary care physician, who had
grown concerned after she disclosed, almost in passing during a routine appointment, that she no
longer felt certain her life was worth living. She is soft-spoken, precisely articulate in the way
that people who work with numbers often are, and she answers questions with a kind of careful
economy that, over the first two sessions, begins to feel less like reticence and more like a method
of containment.

Eighteen months ago, Miriam was travelling with her husband through northern Israel during what was
meant to be a long-delayed anniversary trip. They had rented a car and were driving through a rural
stretch of road that passed near a cluster of Arab villages when they came upon the aftermath of
what appeared to be a communal conflict that had escalated into violence. A building at the edge of
the road was fully alight. Miriam's husband slowed the car instinctively, and in the seconds before
either of them understood what they were seeing, Miriam watched through the windshield as a young
man, perhaps twenty years old, stumbled from the structure and collapsed in the road. Other figures
moved at the periphery of the fire. She could hear shouting — she is not certain in what language —
and what she believes was screaming, though she has never been able to confirm whether the sounds
were human or structural. Her husband accelerated and drove until they reached a petrol station,
where they called emergency services. The young man's fate was never confirmed to them. They flew
home four days later, and Miriam told almost no one what she had seen.

In the months that followed, she began working longer hours. At first this seemed reasonable — a
deadline, then another — but her manager eventually noted that she was logging into the company's
systems past midnight on weekdays and through most weekends. Miriam had come to believe that as long
as her mind was occupied with model outputs and data pipelines, it could not turn on her. The logic
held, imperfectly, until it didn't. During a particularly stressful sprint before a product launch,
she was reviewing visualisation outputs when a colleague set a candle on her desk — a small wellness
gesture from the office manager — and within seconds Miriam was no longer in the open-plan office.
She was at the windshield again. She heard the shouting. The candle flame was the fire, completely
and without transition. She left the building without explanation and did not return for two days,
telling her team she had a migraine.

Her memories of the event do not behave like ordinary memories. They do not arrive as recollections
— they arrive as reinstatements, more vivid and textured than anything from her actual daily life.
She hears the shouting with a clarity that her husband's voice at the dinner table does not carry. A
drive through any neighbourhood she perceives as ethnically or geographically tense — a tension she
now reads into places she would never have scrutinised before — can return her to that roadside
without warning or transition. She checks the locks on their apartment door several times before bed
each evening, and again before she sleeps, and she cannot fully account for why except that
strangers who approach her on the street now carry a charge of threat that she knows,
intellectually, is disproportionate, and yet cannot reduce.

What weighs on her most heavily is not the memory itself but the conclusion she has drawn from it.
She had seen a young man fall and she had driven away. She is certain that someone else — someone
less frozen, less passive, less constitutionally inadequate — would not have been in that car, or
would have stopped, or would have done something other than watch. She goes over and over the
question of why she was there at all, why it happened to her, though the question never resolves.
Her husband has noticed her withdrawal but does not know its shape. Their evenings are quiet in a
way they were not before.

What finally brought her to the appointment was a morning when she sat at her desk, opened a blank
notebook to begin her day's task list, and found she could not think of a reason to write anything
in it. She is most afraid, she says, that this is simply who she is now. What she is hoping for,
though she offers it tentatively, is to find out whether that is true.
