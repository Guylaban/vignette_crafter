Priya Subramaniam is a 47-year-old South Asian woman, divorced, who serves as a pastor at a small
interfaith congregation in the mid-Atlantic region. She came to the clinic after more than two years
of what she described, haltingly, as "not being myself anymore" — a phrase she returned to several
times across the intake session, each time with the same flat, bewildered expression.

Approximately three years ago, Priya was detained during a humanitarian mission abroad. Over the
course of several days, she was subjected to systematic torture by individuals who appeared to be
affiliated with a paramilitary faction operating in the region. Among the methods used against her
was forced ingestion of substances — contaminated water and spoiled food administered as both
punishment and humiliation, accompanied by prolonged interrogation during which she was made to
speak, repeatedly, about her identity, her faith, and her associations. She was eventually released,
the circumstances of which remain, in her own account, genuinely unclear to her. She returned to the
United States physically depleted and, in her words, "hollowed out."

In the months that followed, Priya noticed that certain ordinary moments would fracture without
warning. The taste of anything bitter or metallic — a particular herbal tea she had once loved,
certain tinned foods — would produce an immediate, overwhelming response that seemed to bypass any
conscious recognition. She had come to believe that her body simply knew things her mind had not yet
processed, that some animal part of her was still scanning for what came next. This sense of her own
physiology as ungovernable had become one of the most frightening aspects of her daily experience.
She was certain, in those moments, that she was not safe — and no reassurance from colleagues, her
adult daughter, or her physician had been able to touch that certainty. When people told her she was
home, that it was over, she understood the words but could not feel their truth.

Her memories of the detention itself arrive in a way she struggles to describe: some moments are
almost unbearably vivid — the texture of a particular surface, a specific phrase repeated at her —
while others are simply absent, blank stretches she cannot account for. The fragments do not
assemble into a coherent sequence. When she does recall what she can, she is overtaken by guilt so
acute it is almost physical, a conviction that she failed in ways she cannot fully articulate but
cannot dismiss.

Priya has come to avoid several things with a consistency that has quietly restructured her life.
She eats very little and controls what she eats with rigid precision — not out of any stated concern
about weight, but because the act of choosing exactly what enters her body is one of the few domains
in which she feels any agency. She has stopped drinking tea entirely. She does not speak about what
happened to her, and when pastoral conversations with congregants have moved toward suffering,
captivity, or loss, she has found reasons to redirect or conclude the session. She works hard, she
said, not to think about it at all — a project that consumes enormous energy and that she knows, on
some level, is not working.

The occupational consequences have accumulated. Priya's role requires her to be emotionally present,
to sit with people in their grief, to offer homilies that draw on her own spiritual journey. She has
delivered fewer sermons in the past eighteen months. She has delegated more. Several congregants
have gently noted that she seems "elsewhere." She scans every new face that enters the sanctuary,
watching for something she cannot name but recognises as threat. She had come to believe that the
person she was — the pastor, the one who could hold other people's pain — no longer existed, and
that what remained was a diminished version who had no particular future to move toward.

What finally brought her to seek help was a funeral service two months ago, during which she began
to cry unexpectedly and could not stop. Crying had become, for her, one of the most destabilising
experiences she knew — she associated it directly with those days of detention, when she had wept
and it had been used against her. Afterward, she sat alone in the vestry for an hour. She came to
therapy, she said, not because she believed it would work, but because she could no longer afford
not to try.
