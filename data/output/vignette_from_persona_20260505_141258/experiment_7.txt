Kiet Phommasack is a 33-year-old Lao-American physical therapist who presented for an initial
evaluation approximately eight months after surviving a near-fatal collision on a rain-slicked
highway outside Portland. He had been driving home from an evening shift when a semi-truck

impact spun his car into a concrete barrier. Kiet was conscious throughout — he remembers the shriek
of metal, the airbag detonating against his face, and the sudden, terrible stillness that followed,
during which he was certain he was dying. He sustained a fractured clavicle, two broken ribs, and a
severe concussion. He was extracted by emergency responders and spent four days in the ICU before
being discharged to outpatient care. By every medical measure, his recovery was considered a
success.

Kiet does not experience it that way. In the weeks following his discharge, unwanted thoughts about
the collision began arriving without warning — not as deliberate recollections but as intrusions
that interrupted ordinary moments, the image of the truck's grille filling his windshield appearing
while he was charting notes or eating dinner. He began waking from nightmares with a residue of
dread he could not immediately locate, lying rigid in the dark beside his wife, Naly, before the
room reassembled itself around him. Loud noises became intolerable in a way that felt physical
rather than emotional: a car backfiring in the clinic parking lot, the clatter of equipment dropped
on a treatment floor, the hydraulic hiss of a bus door — each one arriving in his body before he had
time to think, his heart rate spiking, his hands going cold.

What frightened Kiet most, in a way he found difficult to articulate but returned to repeatedly in
session, was what the accident had revealed about his body. He had been physically capable his
entire adult life — an athlete in college, now a clinician who understood musculature and
biomechanics with professional precision — and the collision had overwritten all of that. He had
come to believe that his body was no longer reliable, that its signals could not be trusted, and
that the injuries had altered something fundamental in him that the discharge paperwork had failed
to account for. He was certain the damage was permanent in ways no imaging had captured. This belief
had begun to shape how he moved through his work: he noticed himself hesitating before demonstrating
exercises with patients, second-guessing his own proprioception, quietly dreading the moment a
patient's sudden movement might startle him in front of a room full of colleagues.

amounts of time and energy. He cleaned and reorganised his home and clinic workspace repeatedly —
the counters, the equipment cart, the supply drawers — finding in the ordered surfaces a momentary
sense of predictability that the rest of his life no longer provided. He scanned every room he
entered with a thoroughness that he recognised, intellectually, was disproportionate, but could not
suspend: his eyes moving automatically to the corners, the exits, the objects that could, under the
right circumstances, become dangerous. A pair of scissors on a treatment table. A standing lamp with
an exposed base. He had come to believe that you could never truly know who might harm you, and this
had made the ordinary social texture of the clinic — patients he had treated for months, colleagues
he trusted — feel subtly unreliable. When his attention was not occupied by scanning, it scattered.
Concentration, once one of his professional strengths, had become effortful and fragile.

At home, Naly had noticed his withdrawal and the hours he spent mentally rehearsing catastrophic
scenarios — running through what he would do if something went wrong during a patient transfer, if a
car ran a red light, if the worst happened again. He framed this to her as preparation. She framed
it as exhaustion.

It was Naly, ultimately, who persuaded him to seek help, after he declined a family road trip for
the second time in three months. Kiet came to his first session guarded but present. What he most
hoped for, he said carefully, was to feel like himself at work again. What he most feared was that
the self he was describing no longer existed.
