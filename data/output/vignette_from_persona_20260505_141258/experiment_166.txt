Rajan Mehta is a 36-year-old South Asian man who works as an accountant at a mid-sized firm in the
Chicago suburbs. He came to the clinic alone, dressed carefully, speaking in measured sentences that
occasionally broke apart mid-thought. He has been divorced for two years. He described his childhood
home as a place where, on most evenings, the air changed before anything happened — a particular
quality of silence, a shift in his father's posture — and then the violence would begin. From
roughly age six through his early teens, Rajan witnessed his father assault his mother repeatedly:
thrown objects, hands raised, his mother's body against the kitchen wall while Rajan stood in the
hallway or crouched at the top of the stairs, unable to intervene, unable to look away. He was the
eldest of three children. He remembered feeling that his smallness was a kind of betrayal — that he
should have been able to stop it and could not. His father faced no legal consequences, no formal
reckoning of any kind. The family remained intact. His mother never spoke of it afterward, and
neither did anyone else.

Rajan left for university at eighteen and told himself he had left all of it behind. He married in
his late twenties. He described his ex-wife as kind, and the marriage as one he slowly made
uninhabitable, though he struggled to say exactly how. The divorce was finalised two years ago. He
has had little contact with his family since.

The symptoms that brought him to seek help did not announce themselves as connected to his
childhood. They crept in through his body first. At work, where Rajan spends long hours in an open-
plan office, a colleague raising his voice during a phone call — even in frustration, even about
something trivial — can produce in Rajan a physical response that is immediate and total: his chest
tightens, his hands go cold, his jaw clamps shut. He does not always know why until several minutes
later, and sometimes not at all. He described sitting at his desk afterward with his heart still
hammering, feeling certain something terrible was about to happen, though nothing in the room had
changed. His body, he said, seems to operate on a frequency he cannot tune out — always scanning,
always braced. He sleeps poorly and wakes already tense.

What disturbs him most is what happens when the memories come. They do not arrive gradually. A
smell, a shift in light, the particular way a door closes can return him instantly to that hallway —
not as a recollection but as something indistinguishable from the present moment. The terror he felt
as a child arrives with the memory, unchanged by the intervening decades. And yet when he tries to
speak about what he witnessed, the words dissolve. He can gesture at the shape of it, but the
experience itself resists language in a way that frightens and humiliates him. He had come to
believe this meant something was broken in him specifically — that other people could process and
narrate their pasts, and he could not.

He had come to believe other things too. That happiness was something that had belonged to an
earlier version of himself and had been quietly taken from him without his consent. That the people
who had shaped the worst years of his life had moved through the world entirely unburdened by what
they had done. And underneath both of these, something more corrosive: that bad things would
continue to find him regardless of what he did, that he had no meaningful power to alter the course
of his own life.

What finally brought him in was a performance review at work. His manager noted, gently, that Rajan
had become increasingly withdrawn from the team over the past year — that he no longer joined
meetings he wasn't required to attend, that colleagues found him hard to reach. Rajan had not
realised how far he had contracted. He sat in his car afterward for a long time. He said he did not
want to keep shrinking. What he hopes for, though he offered it cautiously, is the possibility of
feeling like himself again — whoever that was.
