Rangi Taufa is a 42-year-old Tongan-Australian electrician living in a mid-sized city in the Pacific
Northwest, currently in a long-term relationship. He was referred for psychological assessment
following a disclosure to his general practitioner, whom he had visited ostensibly for sleep
difficulties. His PCL-5 score of 78 places him in a range consistent with severe posttraumatic
stress, and the clinical picture that has emerged across the intake and first two sessions is one of
a man whose life has contracted quietly and systematically around a single night approximately
fourteen months ago.

Rangi was working late on a commercial rewiring job — it was around 9 in the evening — when he was
assaulted by two men in the car park of the building he had been contracted to service. The attack
was sudden and prolonged. He had heard the car park door slam behind him as he carried equipment to
his van, and within seconds he was struck from behind. What followed involved repeated blows and, at
one point, one of the men grabbing him by the collar and forcing his face against the side of the
vehicle. He does not have a continuous memory of the assault. What he retains are discrete images:
the grain of the van's panelling close to his eyes, the specific quality of the light from a single
overhead lamp, the sensation of cold asphalt under his hands. He describes these not as memories so
much as photographs he did not choose to keep, each one sharp and bounded, with nothing connecting
them. The gaps between those images disturb him almost as much as the images themselves. He has come
to believe that what happened in those missing intervals must have been worse than what he can
recall, and that not knowing is itself a kind of evidence of something he cannot face.

He returned to work within two weeks, which he now recognises as a mistake — not because he was
unfit, he insists, but because he is certain the assault happened to him for reasons rooted in who
he is. He has said, with quiet conviction, that another man, a more careful or more capable man,
would not have been alone in that car park at that hour, would have noticed something, would have
walked differently. This belief has settled into him like a structural fault: invisible from the
outside, load-bearing. When he recalls the attack, the guilt that surfaces is immediate and
physical, a tightening across his chest that he finds difficult to articulate but impossible to
dismiss.

The evenings have become the hardest part of his day. As 9 p.m. approaches, something shifts in him
that he cannot always name in advance — a loosening of the boundary between then and now, a feeling
that the world is becoming slightly less real, that he is watching himself from somewhere nearby. On
job sites, which often require him to move through unfamiliar commercial buildings, he finds himself
mapping exits and sightlines before he can begin work. When a colleague moves suddenly into his
peripheral vision, or lays a hand on his shoulder to get his attention, his body responds before his
mind catches up — a lurch, a raised arm, a surge of adrenaline that takes long minutes to subside.
Doors are particularly difficult. The sound of a heavy door slamming — a common enough event on
construction sites — can stop him mid-task.

Over the months since the assault, Rangi has quietly reorganised his evenings around his phone and a
games console, staying absorbed in screens until he is too tired to lie awake. He drinks most
nights, usually alone after his partner has gone to bed, and he has stopped watching anything on
television that might touch the subject of violence or assault. His partner has noticed the drinking
and the withdrawal, and the distance between them has grown in ways neither has directly named. He
has declined two social invitations from workmates in the past three months, citing tiredness.

What finally brought him to his GP was a comment his partner made — gently, he says, not as a
criticism — that she no longer recognised the person he was becoming. What frightens him most,
sitting in the consulting room, is the possibility that she is right, and that there is no version
of himself left to recover. What he hopes for, more tentatively, is that he might be wrong about
that.
