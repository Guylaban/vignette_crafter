Kayla Merritt is a 25-year-old single woman who works as a city bus driver and was referred for
psychological assessment following persistent difficulties that have escalated over the past
eighteen months. She served in the Army and was deployed overseas, where, during her second tour,
her unit's vehicle was struck by an improvised explosive device on a route that had been cleared and
deemed safe just hours before. The blast came without warning — no unusual roadside debris, no
change in radio traffic, nothing to scan for and find. One moment Kayla was watching the road
through a dusty windshield; the next she was on her back, ears ringing, the vehicle tilted at a
sickening angle, smoke thickening the air. She lost consciousness briefly. When she came to, she was
disoriented and dizzy, the world spinning as she tried to locate her fellow soldiers. Two of them
did not walk out. Kayla did, with a mild traumatic brain injury and a body that, in the weeks that
followed, kept producing that same vertiginous spinning sensation at unpredictable intervals.

After her discharge and medical clearance, Kayla returned home and took the bus driving job because
it was structured, because she knew vehicles, and because she thought routine might help her feel
anchored. For a time it did. But the job has gradually become one of the primary arenas in which her
distress plays out. She drives the same routes daily, and yet no two shifts feel the same —
passengers board and exit unpredictably, dispatchers interrupt with last-minute reroutes, mechanical
sounds shift without pattern. Kayla has come to dread anything that departs from the expected
sequence of her day. When a passenger stumbles into the aisle or a supervisor radios an unscheduled
stop, her body responds with the same intensity it did in the seconds after the blast: her heart
pounds, her vision blurs at the edges, and the dizziness returns — that particular spinning she
first felt lying in the wreckage — and she is momentarily certain she is back there. She white-
knuckles the steering wheel until the sensation passes, which can take several minutes. She has not
told her supervisor.

Off the bus, Kayla has reorganized her life around managing what she can see coming. She no longer
goes to bars, crowded restaurants, or any space where she cannot immediately account for every
entrance. At home she eats at the same corner of her kitchen table, back pressed against the wall,
facing the door. She has done this so consistently that having a guest over has become effectively
impossible — the last time a friend visited and sat in her usual chair, Kayla spent the entire
evening standing near the counter, unable to eat. The friend has not been invited back. In public
spaces she moves along the perimeter of rooms, and she keeps her eyes in constant motion — doorways,
windows, the hands of strangers — in a vigilance that exhausts her by mid-morning and that she
experiences not as a choice but as an absolute necessity.

What frightens Kayla most, sitting with these symptoms, is not the memories themselves but what she
has concluded about herself in their wake. She had been, in her own understanding, someone defined
by competence under pressure — a soldier, a driver, someone who could be counted on precisely when
things went wrong. She is now certain that version of herself is gone. She describes her current
life as hollowed out: she goes to work, she comes home, she sleeps poorly, and nothing in that cycle
carries any weight or purpose. She had come to believe, with quiet conviction, that the trauma did
not merely change her life but ended it in some meaningful sense — that what remains is a diminished
aftermath rather than a life she is still living.

What finally brought Kayla to seek help was a near-miss on her route last month. She felt the
dizziness come on sharply while the bus was moving and had to pull over, hazard lights on, for four
minutes before she felt safe enough to continue. She sat there thinking that she was going to hurt
someone. It was the first time her distress had crossed into her sense of herself as a danger to
others, and that terrified her in a way that her own suffering had not. She comes to treatment
hoping, tentatively, that something of who she was might still be recoverable — though she does not
quite believe it.
