the gates were locked, the radio was down, and the relief workers had been ordered at gunpoint to
remain still. He stood and watched. He watched a young man roughly his grandson's age dragged by the
collar across gravel, watched the guards laugh, watched a woman cover her child's face with her own
body. He could do nothing. That absolute foreclosure of choice — the locked gate, the pointed
weapon, the paralysis — became the fulcrum around which everything that followed would turn.

In the months after he returned home to his apartment in the Twin Cities, the memories did not
recede as he had expected them to. Instead they arrived with a vividness that stripped away all
temporal distance. When the images came — and they came often, cycling with the relentless
regularity of a film reel that cannot be stopped — Thavisouk did not experience them as
recollections. He experienced them as recurrence. The checkpoint was not behind him; it was, in
those moments, exactly where he was. He began waking from sleep mid-shout, the nightmare so
thoroughly saturating his body that the transition to waking offered no relief, only a continuation
of dread that would linger through the morning hours and colour whatever work he attempted that day.

occasionally present findings to colleagues. He had come to believe that any unguarded word from him

brief.

humanitarian crisis carried the risk of pulling him back through the gate. The avoidance was total

philosophical position but as a felt, bodily conviction. Seated at his desk or walking to collect
his mail, he found himself scanning doorways, windows, the movements of strangers, cataloguing

offscreen. The tension was not metaphorical; his shoulders ached, his jaw was clenched when he woke
each morning.

man his age, a man who had buried his wife and raised children and built a careful, rational life
around data and evidence — such a man should not be coming apart at the seams. He had come to
believe that the intensity of his reactions meant something was fundamentally breaking down in him,
that the boundary between memory and present reality was dissolving in a way that could not be
reversed.

It was his eldest daughter who finally persuaded him to seek help, after finding him one morning
sitting rigid in the dark, unable to explain where, in his mind, he had just been. He arrived at his
first appointment hoping, above all else, to be told that he was not losing his mind — and afraid,
with equal force, that he was.
