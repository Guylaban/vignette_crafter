Kiet Bùi is a twenty-two-year-old Vietnamese-American UX designer who presented for an initial
psychological evaluation approximately eight months after surviving a catastrophic tornado that tore
through the rural Oklahoma town where he had been visiting his aunt's family over a long weekend. He
had no prior history of trauma or mental health treatment, and at the time of the disaster he
described himself as someone who moved through the world with relative ease — sociable, curious,
absorbed in his work.

The tornado struck without adequate warning on a Saturday afternoon in early spring. Kiet had been
sitting at his aunt's kitchen table eating a bowl of bún bò Huế when the sky outside the window
turned a colour he later struggled to name — not green exactly, but wrong in a way that registered
in his chest before his mind caught up. Within seconds the structure around him began to come apart.
He was thrown from his chair and pinned beneath collapsed cabinetry for what he estimates was nearly
forty minutes before emergency responders reached him. During that time he could not move his neck,
could feel something warm and wet along his collarbone, and was certain, with a clarity that still
disturbs him, that he was going to die alone in a stranger's kitchen. The smell of the spilled soup
— star anise, lemongrass, a faint char from the gas line — was everywhere. His aunt was found in
another part of the house, badly injured but alive. Two neighbours were not.

In the weeks that followed, Kiet returned to his apartment in Austin and attempted to resume his
work as a UX designer at a mid-sized software firm. The physical injury to his neck had been
treated, but a residual stiffness remained, and he noticed that whenever the tension in his neck
flared — during long hours hunched over wireframes and prototype mockups — something would shift
inside him that he could not name or contain. He would not necessarily see anything, would not
replay the kitchen or the collapsed ceiling, but the full weight of that afternoon would arrive in
him without an image attached to it, like a tide coming in through a door he couldn't locate. On
other occasions the memory returned with a sensory precision that bewildered him: he would be
sitting at his standing desk reviewing user-flow diagrams and suddenly taste star anise on the back
of his tongue, sharp and unmistakable, as though the bowl were in front of him again. He found
himself checking the weather application on his phone compulsively whenever he felt physically
unwell — a mild headache, a wave of nausea — interpreting bodily discomfort as a signal that
something catastrophic was imminent. Any news coverage of tornadoes, storm systems, or disaster
relief efforts sent him immediately out of the room.

What unsettled Kiet most was not the physical intrusions but a quieter, more corrosive shift in how
the world appeared to him. He had come to believe that people were fundamentally unknowable — that
the warmth colleagues showed him at the office, the friendliness of his roommate, even his family's
concern, concealed something unreliable beneath it. He was not sure what he expected people to
reveal; he only knew that he no longer trusted the surface of things. He felt set apart from
everyone around him, as though he were observing his own life through the glass of a user-testing
booth — present but separated, watching behaviours he could no longer participate in naturally. The
design work that had once felt collaborative and energising now required a performance of engagement
he found exhausting. He stopped attending team stand-ups in person, citing audio quality, and began
working almost exclusively through asynchronous messages. He had not seen friends socially in four
months. What frightened him most was not any single symptom but the growing sense that there was no
place — not Austin, not his family's home in San Jose, not anywhere — where he felt he still
belonged. The memory of the tornado itself sometimes struck him as impossible, so extreme in its
rupture of ordinary life that part of him could not fully accept that it had happened to him at all.

He sought help after his manager gently raised concerns about his withdrawal from the team, and
after he found himself unable to finish a bowl of pho his roommate had made — leaving the table
without explanation, unable to account for his own reaction. He came to the intake session hoping,
in his words, to feel like he was part of things again.
