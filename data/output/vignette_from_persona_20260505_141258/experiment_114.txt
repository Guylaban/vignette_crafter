Kofi Mensah is a 19-year-old Ghanaian-American man who joined a metropolitan police department's
detective unit through a highly competitive youth pipeline programme, one of the youngest people
ever to hold such a position in his precinct. He presents for psychological assessment following a
period of captivity that occurred approximately four months ago, during which he was held against
his will for nearly thirty-six hours in the course of an undercover operation that went
catastrophically wrong. What happened in that warehouse on the outskirts of the city has left him,
in his own words, "not the same person who walked in there."

During the operation, Kofi was identified by the individuals he had been surveilling. He was
restrained, blindfolded for long stretches, and subjected to sustained psychological intimidation
and physical handling — men grabbing him by the collar, by the wrists, forcing him to kneel on a
concrete floor. The space was dark except for intermittent harsh lighting, and throughout the ordeal
there were specific sounds that have since lodged themselves in his nervous system: the metallic
scrape of a chair being dragged across concrete, raised voices cycling between threats and laughter,
and a persistent low hum from what he later learned was a faulty generator. It was dark when he was
finally released into the street outside, disoriented and alone, the night air pressing in around
him.

In the weeks that followed, Kofi returned to work because he did not know what else to do with
himself. He is still employed as a detective, and the precinct environment — with its metal chair
legs scraping across interview room floors, its sudden bursts of raised voices from the bullpen —
has become a landscape of ambush. He will be reviewing case files at his desk when something shifts
in him without warning: not a clear image, not a scene replaying itself, but a wave of terror so
complete and sourceless that he grips the edge of the desk and waits for it to pass. He cannot
explain these moments to his colleagues, partly because he has come to believe that articulating
what happened would make it more real, and partly because when he reaches for the words, they simply
are not there. He knows the sequence of events with clinical precision — he is, after all, trained
to reconstruct narratives — and yet when he tries to speak about those thirty-six hours, the
language dissolves. He has sat across from his supervising detective, who has gently asked how he is
doing, and found himself staring at the table, unable to begin.

There are things Kofi now does not do. He does not go outside after dark. He arranges his schedule,
his social life, his entire geography of movement around this fact. His girlfriend, Adaeze, has
noticed. They used to walk to a late-night diner on Friday evenings, a small ritual that has simply
stopped. He has offered explanations that satisfy neither of them, and the distance between what she
observes and what he can give her has grown into something they do not yet have a name for. He also
does not allow people to touch him. Adaeze reached for his hand on the sofa three weeks ago and he
pulled away before he had consciously registered the movement. He saw her face and felt something
collapse inside him, but he could not undo the reflex. At the precinct, the casual shoulder-grip
that colleagues use to signal solidarity has become something he steers around, repositioning
himself in rooms so that he is not within arm's reach.

He tries, actively and exhaustingly, not to think about what happened. Some days this works for
hours at a time. But there are moments — particularly when a voice rises sharply in the bullpen, or
when the building's ventilation system produces a low, cycling hum — when he hears, with
uncomfortable clarity, sounds that belong to the warehouse rather than to the present room.

What finally brought Kofi in was a near-miss during a routine interview last week, when the suspect
scraped his chair back suddenly and Kofi's hand moved toward his holster. Nothing happened. But he
sat in his car afterward for forty minutes, and he was afraid of himself. He is hoping, more than
anything, to feel like a detective again. What frightens him most is that he no longer trusts his
own body to know where it is.
