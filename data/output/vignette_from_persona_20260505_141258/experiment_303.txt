Priya Krishnamurthy is a 28-year-old South Asian woman who works as a tailor from the converted back
room of her home in a mid-sized American city, where she has lived with her husband for the past
three years. She came to the United States as a refugee following a period of detention and
systematic torture in her country of origin, an experience that has continued to shape nearly every
hour of her waking life. She has only recently, and reluctantly, agreed to speak with a psychologist
— a step that took months of gentle encouragement from her husband and that she describes as feeling
more frightening than she can fully explain.

The events that brought Priya to this point occurred over several weeks during a period of political
detention. She was held in a facility where she was subjected to repeated physical and psychological
torture by state agents. What she remembers most vividly is the particular sequence of it: being
restrained in a confined room with a single metal door, the sounds of that door opening and closing
as a prelude to each session, the sensation of having no possible exit, and the way her captors
would sometimes leave her in silence for long stretches before returning. She learned, in those
weeks, that stillness and quiet were not safety — they were waiting. By the time she was released
and eventually resettled, that knowledge had been written into her body in ways she did not yet have
words for.

In the months following her arrival in the United States, Priya began to notice that entering any
enclosed space — a grocery store, a waiting room, a friend's apartment — produced an immediate,
involuntary sweep of the room for exits. She had come to believe, with a certainty that felt
entirely rational to her, that knowing where the doors were was the only preparation that made
sense. She does this now without thinking, clocking the layout of every room before she can settle
into any other thought. At her sewing table, positioned against the interior wall of her workroom,
she keeps her chair angled so that the doorway to the hall is always in her peripheral vision.

There are moments, particularly when a door opens unexpectedly or when a sound in the house catches
her off guard, when Priya is no longer in her workroom at all. She is back in the detention
facility, heart hammering, body braced. These episodes pass, but they leave her shaken and exhausted
in ways she finds difficult to account for to her husband, who knows the broad outlines of what
happened to her but not the texture of how it persists. She remains superalert throughout her days —
monitoring sounds, movements, shifts in the quality of light — in a state of readiness she cannot
voluntarily lower.

What frightened Priya most, in the period after resettlement, was the discovery that relaxing made
things worse rather than better. When she tried to rest — lying still, letting her mind quiet — the
sensations of restraint and helplessness returned with particular force. She had come to understand
stillness as the condition most resembling captivity, and so she filled her hours with work. The
tailoring, which she had learned from her mother and which she genuinely loves, became something she
also used to stay in motion — hands always occupied, attention always directed outward. She takes on
more orders than are reasonable, works late into the evenings, and has declined social invitations
that would require her to sit without occupation.

She has avoided all medical and psychological care since her arrival, cancelling two previous
referrals without explanation. She has also withdrawn from situations she anticipates will produce
strong emotion — she stopped attending a community group for South Asian women after one meeting
left her feeling destabilised for days. The consequences have accumulated quietly: her husband has
grown worried and somewhat distant in his worry, unsure how to reach her; her world has contracted
to the dimensions of her workroom and the immediate routines around it.

What finally brought Priya to the intake appointment was a fainting episode during a routine errand
— her body, she felt, had made the decision her mind kept deferring. She arrived guarded and precise
in her language, but said, near the end of the session, that she hoped someone might be able to help
her feel less like she was still waiting for the door to open.
