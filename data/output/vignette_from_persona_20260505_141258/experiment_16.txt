Valentina Reyes is a 38-year-old Salvadoran American woman who works as a community organizer in a
mid-sized city in the Southwest, coordinating tenant rights campaigns and neighborhood advocacy
efforts. She came to the clinic after her partner, noticing months of sleeplessness and emotional
distance, gently insisted she speak to someone. Valentina agreed, though she arrived at the first
session with the quiet wariness of someone who had spent decades convincing herself she did not need
to.

The history she offered was careful and partial, offered in pieces. Growing up in a household where
her stepfather's rages were unpredictable and her mother's attention was unreliable, Valentina had
learned early to read rooms and people. The abuse was physical and sustained across several years of
her childhood — beatings that could be triggered by something as ordinary as a door left open or a
plate set down too loudly. What she returned to most often in session was one particular incident,
perhaps when she was nine or ten: being cornered in the kitchen, the counter at her back, her
stepfather blocking the only exit, the sensation of having absolutely nowhere to go. She could
recall certain images from that moment with a sharpness that startled her — the pattern of the
linoleum, the color of his shirt — while other parts of the memory were simply absent, as if someone
had cut them from the reel. At other times she could narrate the event with a flat, factual calm
that unnerved even her, describing it as though it had happened to a girl she once read about rather
than to herself.

In the years that followed, Valentina built a life that looked, from the outside, like one of
remarkable resilience. She was good at her work — genuinely good — and she threw herself into it
with an intensity that left little room for anything else. Her calendar was always full. When a
difficult memory surfaced without warning during a meeting or while drafting a report, she learned
to push harder into the task in front of her, to bury the intrusion under the weight of action. Her
colleagues admired her drive. What they did not see was that she was also, in every meeting room and
community center she entered, quietly scanning for where to sit. She chose her chair the way someone
might choose a position in enemy territory: back against the wall, full view of the door, no one
behind her. If the only available seat placed her back to the room, she would wait, or rearrange, or
make an excuse. In her work, which required constant presence in unfamiliar spaces — church halls,
apartment lobbies, city council chambers — this was not a minor inconvenience. There were organizing
sessions she left early, coalition meetings where she could not focus because the seating
arrangement felt wrong, moments where her attention was less on the agenda than on who was moving
behind her.

She had come to believe, with a certainty that sat beneath her conscious reasoning, that a stronger
person would have found a way out of that kitchen. Someone else would have done something — screamed
louder, run faster, fought back. That she had not was, in her private accounting, evidence of
something fundamentally insufficient in her. This belief coexisted with a numbness she described as
feeling dead inside, a flatness that settled over her particularly in moments of intimacy or
stillness. Her partner had noticed it. Valentina had noticed it too, and found it frightening in a
way she struggled to articulate — as though the emotional life she was supposed to have was
operating behind glass.

At night, the vigilance that she managed to channel into productivity during the day had nowhere to
go. She lay awake listening, certain that relaxing her attention entirely was not safe, though she
could not always say what she was listening for. The exhaustion was accumulating.

What finally brought her in was a community meeting three months ago in which a tenant described
being trapped in a lease with no legal recourse, no options, no way out. Valentina had felt
something shift dangerously inside her and had excused herself to stand in the parking lot until it
passed. She came to therapy hoping, she said, to feel something real again. What she feared most was
that she no longer could.
