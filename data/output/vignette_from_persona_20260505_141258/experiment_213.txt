Aroha Tane is a 39-year-old Māori woman who works as a park ranger in the Pacific Northwest, a role
she had held for nearly a decade before the deployment that changed her. Raised in a close-knit
community with deep ties to land and whanau, she had volunteered through an international relief
organisation to spend eight months providing humanitarian support in a conflict-affected region of
the Middle East. She arrived believing that her practical skills — logistics, first aid, navigating
difficult terrain — would be useful. What she encountered was a scale of suffering that no training
had prepared her for.

The incident that marked the turning point occurred during a distribution run at a makeshift medical
post. A colleague — a local man named Tariq who had worked alongside her for months — embraced her
in greeting just as a blast struck the adjacent building. In the chaos that followed, Aroha made
decisions she has never been able to fully account for: she moved away from the wounded rather than
toward them, sheltering in a supply corridor while others called for help. Tariq did not survive.
She is not certain of the sequence of events. Some moments — the specific scent of Tariq's cologne,
the warmth of his arms just before the world collapsed — are seared into her with a clarity that
feels almost hallucinatory. Others are simply gone, swallowed by a blankness she cannot explain.
When she has tried to piece together what happened, even in her own mind, the thread dissolves
before it reaches any coherent end.

She returned to the United States six months ago. The transition back to her patrol work in the
forest, which she had once found grounding, has been quietly disorienting. She had come to believe
that she no longer belonged to the world she had left — not to the forest, not to her team, not to
her community. The land that had once felt like an extension of herself now felt indifferent. When a
fellow ranger clapped her on the shoulder in welcome, she pulled away sharply, and the look on his
face stayed with her for days. Physical affection — something her culture and her marriage had
always been generous with — now triggered an abrupt, wordless recoil that she could not explain to
her husband Damon, who had grown quieter in response. Their evenings, once easy, had become careful.

nothing to do with wildlife or visitor safety. Sudden movements from hikers emerging from tree cover
put her body on alert before her mind could register them. She began routing her patrols away from
unfamiliar sections of the park, telling herself she was being efficient, though she knew she was
avoiding the discomfort of not being able to read an unknown space for danger. She had stopped
attending the community church gatherings her mother-in-law hosted, offering logistical excuses each

understood herself to be. She was not sure, in her darker moments, that her life held enough value
