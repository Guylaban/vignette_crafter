Valentina Reyes is a 39-year-old Salvadoran American woman who works as a program coordinator for a
nonprofit serving immigrant families in a mid-sized city in Texas. She is married and, until
recently, had built a life she described as deeply rooted — in her faith community, in her extended
family, and in the advocacy work she had dedicated herself to for over a decade. She presented for
treatment approximately eight months after being assaulted, reporting a PCL-5 score of 78 and
describing her current life as one she no longer recognizes.

The assault occurred on an evening in late autumn when Valentina stayed late at the nonprofit's
office to finish grant documentation. The building was largely empty. A man entered through a side
entrance she had left unlocked and attacked her in the hallway outside the storage room. He held a
box cutter to her throat and forced her into a back office. The assault was prolonged and violent.
What Valentina remembers is not a continuous narrative but a series of disconnected images — the
fluorescent light overhead, the sound of her own breathing, the way the blade caught the light. She
cannot place these images in any reliable order, and when she tries to reconstruct the sequence of
what happened, the pieces refuse to cohere. She told her intake clinician, with evident frustration,
that she knows what was done to her but cannot find the language to say it — not in English, not in
Spanish. The words, she said, simply do not exist.

In the weeks after the assault, Valentina returned to work because she did not know what else to do.
Her role regularly brings her into contact with donated goods, including kitchen supplies and tools
collected for resettlement families. The first time a colleague set a large kitchen knife on the
sorting table, Valentina felt her body respond before her mind had registered what she was seeing.
She now routes herself around that room whenever donations are being processed, telling colleagues
she is busy with paperwork. She has said nothing to her supervisor about why. At night, the office
building where she was attacked sits at the edge of her awareness even when she is at home — she
finds it almost impossible to feel safe after dark, checking door locks repeatedly and lying awake
while her husband sleeps beside her. When she does sleep, she startles awake at ordinary sounds — a
car door, the house settling — and cannot return to calm. Even when her husband holds her and tells
her she is safe, she cannot absorb it. She understands the words but they do not reach her.

When memories of the assault surface during the day, what Valentina experiences is not grief or fear
but rage — a sudden, overwhelming fury that she finds shameful and confusing in equal measure. She
had come to believe, in the months since the attack, that she was a weak person — that someone
stronger, more careful, more streetwise would not have been alone in that building, would not have
left that door unlocked. She was certain that the assault had happened because of something
essentially insufficient in her. These convictions sat alongside a growing withdrawal from the
Salvadoran community she had once anchored herself in — the parish group, the quinceañera planning
committees, the informal networks of mothers and aunts she had known for years. She stopped
attending, first because she could not find words to explain what had happened, and then simply
because she felt she no longer belonged among people who seemed, to her, intact. Her husband noticed
the withdrawal but did not press. That distance between them had quietly widened.

What finally brought Valentina to seek help was a moment at work three weeks before her intake
appointment. A teenage girl she was case-managing broke down crying during a session, and Valentina,
who had spent years holding space for others' trauma, felt nothing — only a flat, remote absence
where her empathy had once lived. She drove home that evening and told her husband she thought
something was seriously wrong with her. She is most afraid, she said at intake, that the person she
was before that night in the office no longer exists. What she is hoping for, though she offered
this quietly and with evident uncertainty, is that someone might help her find words for what
happened — and that those words might, somehow, begin to give it a shape she can bear.
