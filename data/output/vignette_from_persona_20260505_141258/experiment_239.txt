moved through the crowd, firing weapons and shouting directives. Ernesto was pinned behind a

ring out at irregular intervals, watching uniformed figures move past him with weapons raised. He
was not physically wounded, but a man standing near him was struck and fell. Ernesto did not run. He
stayed still, and he has never fully made peace with that fact.

In the weeks after the attack, Ernesto returned to his practice because he did not know what else to
do. The clinic's routine — the hum of the suction unit, the bright overhead lights, the predictable
sequencing of appointments — offered something that resembled normalcy. But the world outside the
operatory had become a different place. When a car backfired on the street near his clinic, Ernesto
found himself gripping the edge of the sink, unable to move for several seconds, the park flooding
back through him not as a recollection but as a full sensory return: the smell of the grass, the
weight of the ground beneath him, the sound of the shots as immediate and present as if they were
occurring in the room. When the memory came in those moments, it did not arrive as narrative — he
could not have told anyone, in ordered sentences, what had happened or in what sequence. It came as
sensation, as texture, as the particular quality of light that afternoon, and it overwhelmed him so
completely that he sometimes had to excuse himself from a patient mid-procedure and stand in the
back hallway until the wave passed.

conclusion repeatedly, rehearsing the moment from every angle, always arriving at the same verdict
against himself. He began avoiding the evening news, because any report touching on political
violence or armed groups confirmed, in his mind, that the world remained exactly as dangerous as it
had been that afternoon. He started carrying a licensed firearm, which he kept in his car and
sometimes in the inner pocket of his jacket, because what frightened him most was the idea of being
caught without any means of response again.

conceal. When a patient arrived wearing a security guard's uniform for an afternoon appointment,
Ernesto felt his chest tighten and his attention fracture entirely. He got through the appointment

door — not consciously cataloguing exits, but moving his eyes to every corner and doorway in a sweep
that has become as automatic as washing his hands. He monitors his patients and his staff with a
vigilance that has no clinical purpose, reading faces for signs of something he cannot name
precisely but recognises immediately as threat.

himself, and had not for over a year. Ernesto made the appointment the following week. What he most
hopes for is to be able to remember what happened without being destroyed by it each time. What he
fears most is that the man he was before the park no longer exists.
