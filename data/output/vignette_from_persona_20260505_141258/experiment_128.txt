Fatima is an eighty-year-old widowed Palestinian-American woman who has spent the better part of
five decades working as a social worker, accompanying other people through their worst moments with
a steadiness she had always considered inseparable from who she was. That sense of herself — as
someone capable, present, and useful in crisis — was profoundly disrupted eighteen months ago when
her youngest son, Tariq, was admitted to the intensive care unit following a sudden and catastrophic
cardiac event. He was forty-six years old. For eleven days, Fatima sat beside him in a room that
hummed with monitors and smelled of antiseptic so sharp it seemed to coat the inside of her throat.
She watched nurses thread IV lines into his arms, adjust drips, and replace bags of fluid with a
clinical efficiency that she found both necessary and unbearable. She held his hand when he was
conscious and stared at the ceiling when he was not, trying to find somewhere inside herself the
composure she had offered to grieving families for decades. She could not find it. She remembers —
though the memory arrives more often as sensation than image — the particular quality of
helplessness she felt when a physician spoke to her in the corridor and she understood, for a few
hours, that Tariq might not survive. Tariq did survive, but Fatima has not returned to the person
she was before those eleven days.

In the weeks that followed his discharge, she began noticing that certain things undid her without
warning. Driving past a hospital, catching sight of an ambulance, or glimpsing a needle on a medical
television programme would produce a wave of distress so immediate it felt less like remembering and
more like being returned. More disorienting still were the moments when the emotional weight of
those eleven days arrived with no image attached — a sudden crush of grief and dread while she was
washing dishes, with nothing she could point to as the cause. She has tried, with considerable
effort, to push these moments away, to redirect her thoughts the way she might redirect a distressed
client, but the memories do not respond to that kind of management. They arrive when they choose to.

What frightened Fatima most, as the months passed, was not any single symptom but the conclusion she
was drawing from all of them together. She had come to believe that she was permanently altered —
that the woman who had sat with bereaved parents and traumatised children and had remained, if not
unmoved, then at least functional, was simply gone. She felt certain she had failed Tariq during
those eleven days, that she had been passive and silent when she should have advocated more
forcefully, and that this failure was a betrayal of values she had organised her life around. She
also carried a quieter, more frightening conviction: that she could not protect the people she
loved, that bad things would continue to find her, and that there was nothing she could do to
prevent them.

These beliefs have shaped her days in ways that have accumulated into significant loss. Fatima, who
once made home visits and attended community meetings and sat with families in hospitals without a
second thought, has largely stopped going out. She declined a referral that would have required her
to visit a client in a rehabilitation facility, citing administrative reasons, and her supervisor
has gently noted her reduced caseload. She no longer attends the grief support group she once co-
facilitated — conversations about people losing loved ones now feel less like professional territory
and more like direct threat. A colleague who lost a parent recently mentioned it in passing, and
Fatima excused herself from the room. Her social world, already quieted by widowhood, has contracted
further.

It was her daughter who finally persuaded her to seek help, after Fatima cancelled a medical check-
up for the third consecutive time and admitted, haltingly, that she could not make herself walk
through those doors. Fatima came to the first session composed and precise, describing her symptoms
in the careful language of someone who has spent a career naming other people's distress. What she
is most afraid of, she said, is that she has used up whatever it was that made her good at her work.
What she is hoping for, more quietly, is that she might be wrong about that.
