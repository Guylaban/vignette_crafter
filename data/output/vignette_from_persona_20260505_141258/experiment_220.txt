Somchai Viriyapong is a 77-year-old Thai-American surgeon, still practicing in a semi-retired
capacity at a regional medical center in the Pacific Northwest, where he performs a reduced surgical
caseload and supervises residents. He is married, the father of grown children, and by most outward
measures has led a life of uncommon discipline and achievement. He presented for psychological
assessment following a referral from his primary care physician, who had grown concerned after
Somchai disclosed, almost in passing during a routine appointment, that he had not slept more than
four hours at a stretch in several months and that he had recently made two uncharacteristically
impulsive decisions at work that had alarmed his colleagues.

The event that preceded his current difficulties occurred approximately eight months ago. Somchai
was present in an operating theater when a cauterizing unit malfunctioned during a procedure being
performed by a junior colleague. The equipment ignited surgical draping, and before the fire
suppression system engaged, a circulating nurse sustained significant burns to her hands and
forearms. Somchai was the senior physician in the room. He assisted in managing the immediate crisis
— the smell of burning tissue and scorched fabric filling the theater, the heat radiating from the
flames close enough that he felt it against the exposed skin of his own forearms above his gloves —
before emergency responders arrived. The nurse survived and has since recovered, but the event
lasted perhaps four minutes. Those four minutes have not, for Somchai, ended.

In the weeks that followed, he noticed that the smell of anything burnt — a forgotten piece of toast
in the hospital break room, a patient's wound being cauterized in routine surgery — produced in him
an immediate, overwhelming sense that something was catastrophically wrong, though he could rarely
retrieve a clear image to accompany the feeling. He described it to his evaluator as being flooded
by a terror that seemed to belong to no particular moment, as though the emotion had been separated
from its story. When asked to recount the sequence of events in the operating theater, he found he
could not place them in order with any confidence. He knew the nurse's name, knew the outcome, and
yet the memory felt, in his words, "like something I read about, not something I lived through." He
has come to believe this dissociation from the memory means something is fundamentally wrong with
him — that a man of his training and experience should not be undone by something he witnessed
rather than suffered himself. The conviction that he is defective in some essential way has settled
into him quietly but persistently.

His body has not agreed to remain quiet. He startles violently at unexpected sounds — the clatter of
a tray in the corridor outside his office, the sudden beep of a monitor. In the OR, where ambient
noise is constant and unpredictable, he has found himself scanning the room continuously rather than
attending fully to the operative field. He has taken to wearing long sleeves even in warm conditions
because a certain temperature against the skin of his forearms — the particular warmth he felt that
day — sends his nervous system into a state he cannot talk himself out of. He is certain his body is
betraying him, reacting to dangers that are not present, and he finds this both frightening and
humiliating.

He has stopped watching television in the evenings, having encountered a documentary segment about
fire safety that he could not tolerate. He pushes away moments of satisfaction — a successful
procedure, a resident's progress — because contentment feels, in some way he cannot fully
articulate, precarious and dangerous. He tries hard not to think about the event at all, though he
acknowledges this effort rarely succeeds. In recent months, colleagues have noted that he has
volunteered for unusually complex, high-risk cases — behavior that is out of character for a surgeon
of his seniority who could reasonably be selective. He does not explain this to them. He cannot
fully explain it to himself.

What finally brought him to seek help was his wife's quiet observation, offered one evening without
accusation, that he had not laughed in months. He is most afraid that what is happening to him is
permanent. He is most hoping, though he has not yet said this directly, to feel safe in the room
where he has spent his life doing good work.
