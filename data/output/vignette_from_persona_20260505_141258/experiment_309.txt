Gerald Howarth is a 68-year-old married politician of European descent who has spent the better part
of four decades in public life, accustomed to scrutiny and the performance of composure. He
presented for psychological assessment approximately eight months after a life-threatening road
traffic accident that he has struggled, in his own words, to "put behind him the way a man of my
experience should be able to."

The accident occurred on a wet November evening when Gerald was being driven home from a late
legislative session. His driver, who Gerald later learned had consumed alcohol before the journey,
lost control of the vehicle on a highway interchange. The car was struck broadside by a lorry, spun
across two lanes, and came to rest against a concrete barrier. Gerald sustained fractured ribs, a
collapsed lung, and lacerations to his face and left arm. He was conscious throughout, pinned
against the crumpled door, aware of the smell of alcohol emanating from the driver and of the
particular silence that descended after the impact — a silence that preceded the sound of
approaching sirens by what felt to him like an eternity. He remembers the event not as a continuous
sequence but as a series of frozen images: the headlights of the lorry filling the window, the
driver's hands leaving the wheel, the angle of the crushed metal against his chest. He has found, in
the months since, that when he attempts to recount the accident — to his wife, to colleagues who
have asked — he loses the thread somewhere in the middle, the words dissolving before he can reach
the end, leaving him staring at whoever is listening with a sentence unfinished.

In the weeks following his discharge from hospital, Gerald noticed that the smell of alcohol — at
receptions, at the parliamentary bar, at the dinner table when wine was poured — produced an
immediate and overwhelming wave of discomfort that he could not rationalise away. He began declining
evening events where drinking was likely, offering scheduling conflicts as the reason. He also
stopped travelling by car entirely. This was not a minor inconvenience for a working politician. His
constituency is rural, his diary demanding. He began arriving late to votes, missing engagements he
had previously considered non-negotiable, and delegating travel to junior staff under the pretence
of workload. His chief of staff has quietly noted the change. Gerald is aware that colleagues have
begun to talk, and this awareness compounds something he has come to believe with a conviction that
surprises even him: that people can look at him now and see what happened, that the accident is
somehow visible on him, legible in his face or his bearing. In committee rooms and on the floor of
the chamber, he has found concentration increasingly difficult to sustain, his attention drifting
mid-debate in ways that would once have been unthinkable.

At night, Gerald wakes repeatedly, often rising to check that the house is locked, that his wife is
breathing, that nothing is wrong — unable to articulate what he is checking for, only that the
waking feels necessary. Unusual quiet — the absence of traffic, a lull in conversation — produces in
him a specific and visceral dread, as though silence itself has become a harbinger. When the
memories return, they are accompanied by a physical sensation of pressure across his chest and pain
along the left side of his ribcage, the body apparently rehearsing what the mind cannot finish.

What has weighed on Gerald most persistently is a form of self-recrimination he cannot seem to
reason his way out of. He dwells repeatedly on the fact that he chose that driver, that he had
ignored a prior complaint about the man's reliability, that he had been in a hurry. He has come to
believe, with a quiet but immovable certainty, that the accident happened because of the kind of man
he is — someone who prioritises efficiency over care, whose judgment cannot be trusted. This belief
sits beneath his public life like a fault line.

Gerald sought help only after his wife told him she was frightened — not of him, but for him. What
he most fears, sitting in the consulting room, is that speaking about it will confirm what he
already suspects about himself. What he most hopes is that it will not.
