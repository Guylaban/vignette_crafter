Minh Tran is a 51-year-old Vietnamese American man who works as a tax inspector for a regional
government office. He has been married for eleven years to his current wife and presents to therapy
with a PCL-5 score of 39, describing a constellation of symptoms that have quietly reshaped the
architecture of his daily life over the past several years. He came to the intake appointment
wearing a collared shirt buttoned to the top, and he chose the chair in the far corner of the room
before the clinician had a chance to direct him to a seat.

The trauma Minh describes occurred within a previous long-term relationship, with a woman he had
been with for nearly seven years. He characterizes those years as ones in which the violence
escalated so gradually that he had difficulty, even in retrospect, identifying when it began in
earnest. What he can describe with precision is a particular evening in the kitchen of the apartment
they shared — an argument that began over something trivial, a missed phone call, and then the sound
of a ceramic mug shattering against the wall beside his head. What followed was not an isolated
incident but a sustained period during which he was shoved, struck, and on at least two occasions
thrown against furniture. He stayed. He has spent considerable time trying to understand why, and
has arrived at an explanation that offers him little comfort: he trusted her completely. She was, he
says, the person he had organized his life around. The betrayal he feels is not abstract — it lives
in the specific memory of her voice, which could shift without warning from warmth to violence, and
it is that unpredictability, the impossibility of reading what was coming, that marked him most
deeply.

In the months after the relationship ended, Minh threw himself into his work. Tax inspection suited
him — structured, rule-governed, predictable. But the symptoms he had managed to outpace began
catching up with him. Fragments of memory from those evenings would surface without invitation: not
full scenes but shards — the particular angle of light in that kitchen, the sensation of a grip on
his forearm — appearing suddenly and then dissolving before he could examine them. There are
portions of what happened that he cannot retrieve at all, no matter how deliberately he tries, and
this troubles him in a way he finds difficult to articulate, as though the gaps themselves are
evidence of something he is not permitted to know about himself.

At work, Minh conducts audits that require him to visit businesses and offices across the city.
These visits are, by nature, unpredictable — new environments, unfamiliar people, rooms he has never
been in before. He has developed a habit of pausing in doorways before entering, scanning the room,
and positioning himself so that his back is against a wall. His colleagues have noticed. One of them
made a joke about it at a team lunch, and Minh laughed along, but he did not change his behavior
because to him it does not feel like a choice. He also finds himself checking over his shoulder when
he walks between buildings, a reflex he cannot suppress even when he is certain, on a rational
level, that nothing is wrong. He will react to a door slamming two offices away as though the sound
were directed at him personally — a surge of alertness that takes minutes to subside.

What has become harder to manage is the exhaustion. On days when fatigue accumulates — long audits,
difficult clients, disrupted sleep — he finds himself more raw, more easily startled, more prone to
the intrusive fragments. He has come to understand tiredness not as a neutral physical state but as
a kind of vulnerability that he cannot afford.

Minh describes his inner life with a flatness that suggests long familiarity with disappointment. He
has come to believe that nothing genuinely good is available to him anymore, and when asked to
elaborate, he says simply that he feels more like a function than a person — someone who processes
paperwork and pays bills and occupies space, but who is not quite real in the way other people seem
to be. What finally brought him to seek help was his wife, who told him, carefully and without
accusation, that she could not reach him. He is afraid she is right. What he hopes for, he says, is
less to feel better than to feel like himself again — though he is no longer entirely sure who that
is.
