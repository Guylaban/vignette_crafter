Valentina Reyes is a 27-year-old Salvadoran American woman who works as an HVAC technician for a
commercial contracting company in the Houston area. She came to the intake appointment alone,
dressed in her work uniform, and spoke carefully, choosing words with a deliberateness that
suggested she had rehearsed what she was willing to say. She has never been in therapy before. She
described herself, before everything happened, as someone who was "good at handling things."

Approximately fourteen months ago, Valentina was abducted by two women connected to a debt dispute
involving a family member. She was held for several days in a residential property, restrained, and
subjected to repeated physical violence by her captors — both of whom were female. She described
moments when the door to the room was locked from the outside and she understood, with complete
clarity, that there was nothing she could do. No exit. No choice. No way to make it stop. She was
eventually released and found her way to a hospital, where her injuries were treated. She did not
report the incident to police. She told no one in her family what had happened to her. She returned
to work within two weeks.

In the months that followed, Valentina's world quietly contracted. The memory of what happened does
not come to her as a coherent story she can tell — it arrives in fragments that feel, she has said,
like they happened to a different person, someone she watches from a distance rather than remembers
from the inside. And yet when those fragments surface, they bring with them a wave of emotion so
total that she has to put down whatever she is holding, leave the room, and wait for it to pass.
What accompanies those moments, more than fear, is shame — a shame she cannot fully explain but
cannot escape, a feeling that something about what was done to her reflects something about who she
is.

She has come to believe, in a way that feels less like a thought and more like a fact, that she
cannot know who will harm her. This is not paranoia she can talk herself out of. It sits beneath her
reasoning like a foundation. Women, in particular, have become a source of vigilance she cannot
suppress. When her job requires her to work alone in a building with a female facilities manager or
a female colleague, something in her body goes on alert. She has begun scheduling her work orders to
minimize those situations, sometimes telling her supervisor she needs to revisit a site solo for
"equipment checks" that do not strictly require a second visit. Her supervisor has noticed the
pattern. A performance review last month flagged her as "inconsistent in her team coordination," and
she did not explain why.

At home, her evenings follow a fixed sequence she did not consciously design. She checks the locks
on her front door and windows before bed, and again if she wakes in the night — which she does,
regularly, surfacing from sleep already scanning the room for something she cannot name. She
startles at sounds she would once have ignored: a cabinet closing in the next apartment, a car door
in the parking lot. She has told herself that this alertness is reasonable, that it would be foolish
not to stay ready, and yet some part of her knows it is costing her something she is not getting
back.

What troubles Valentina most, perhaps, is a quieter damage she is less able to articulate. She has
come to believe she is not the same person she was before, that whatever was broken during those
days has not healed but calcified. She doubts her own judgment in ways she never did before — not
just about other people, but about herself, whether she will respond correctly, whether she can be
trusted to do the right thing in a moment that matters. She has not been in a relationship since the
assault and does not expect to be.

She sought help now because the job review frightened her. Not because she feared losing her
position, but because she realized, reading her supervisor's words, that what had happened to her
was becoming visible — that it was changing the shape of her life in ways other people could see,
even if they did not know what they were looking at.
