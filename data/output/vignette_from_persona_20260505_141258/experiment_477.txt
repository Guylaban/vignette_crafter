Harold Metzger is a 74-year-old widowed man who spent the better part of four decades as a combat
pilot, a career defined by precision, controlled aggression, and an unwavering capacity to manage
threat from the cockpit of a machine traveling at lethal speed. He is accustomed to danger arriving
from the outside, from the environment, from an enemy he could see on radar or in the sky ahead.
Nothing in that career prepared him for what happened inside his own home.

Following the death of his wife of thirty-one years, Harold entered a period of profound grief and,
after some years of solitude, began a relationship with a woman he met through a veterans' social
group. The relationship moved quickly, and within months she had moved into his home. What began as
companionship gradually became something he struggled to name. She drank heavily, and when she
drank, her personality shifted in ways that were, at first, unpredictable, and then, over time,
entirely predictable. The smell of alcohol on her breath became a reliable prelude. On the nights
that are most vivid to him now, she would come close — her hand on his arm, or gripping his wrist —
and the physical contact that might have signaled affection instead preceded explosions of rage and,
eventually, violence. She struck him on multiple occasions, once badly enough that he required
medical attention he sought under a false explanation. He stayed far longer than he now understands,
in part because he had come to believe that a man with his history — a man who had flown through
anti-aircraft fire — had no right to name what was happening to him as something he could not
handle.

The relationship ended approximately eighteen months ago when she was removed from his home
following a particularly severe incident. But Harold has found that her departure did not restore
the life he had before. In the weeks that followed, he noticed that the smell of alcohol — a
neighbor's wine at a dinner party, the bar section of a restaurant, even hand sanitizer on certain
days — produced an immediate and overwhelming physical response: his heart rate spiked, his hands
went cold, and he felt a nauseating certainty that something was about to go wrong. He began
organizing his life around avoiding these moments. He declined social invitations. He stopped going
to his regular veterans' lunch because one of the men there drank beer with the meal.

Physical touch became its own territory of difficulty. When his daughter embraced him at Christmas,
he felt his body go rigid in a way he could not explain to her and has not tried to. He has since
kept even his children at a careful distance, finding reasons not to visit, keeping phone calls
pleasant but brief. He has come to believe that closeness of any kind carries a cost he is no longer
willing to pay, though he would not put it in those terms. He would say only that he prefers his own
company.

At night, Harold does not sleep easily. He lies in the dark with a quality of alertness that feels
less like wakefulness than like a combat readiness he cannot stand down from, his attention moving
through the sounds of the house. He has not allowed himself to feel angry about what was done to him
— anger, in his private accounting, is a loss of control he associates with her, not with himself —
and so whatever lives beneath that suppression has no outlet he permits. He remains watchful during
the day as well, tracking the movements of people around him in public spaces with an attention that
exhausts him.

What finally brought Harold to seek help was not a crisis but an accumulation. His daughter told him
plainly that she felt she had lost him. He sat with that for several weeks before making the
appointment. He arrived at the intake session composed and precise in his language, describing his
symptoms the way he might file a maintenance report. What he is most afraid of is that the clinician
will not take him seriously. What he is most hoping for, though he has not said this aloud, is that
someone will.
