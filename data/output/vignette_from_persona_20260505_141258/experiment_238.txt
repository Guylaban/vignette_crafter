Garrett Holloway is a 49-year-old married man of European descent who serves as a city council
member in a mid-sized Midwestern municipality. He presented for psychological evaluation
approximately eighteen months after returning from a reserve deployment to a combat zone, carrying a
PCL-5 score of 42 and a wariness in his posture that made even the intake waiting room seem, to him,
like a space that required monitoring.

Garrett had served in the Army Reserve for over two decades, deploying twice in his thirties without
lasting psychological consequence — or so he had believed. His third deployment, at 47, was
different. During a convoy escort mission, the vehicle ahead of his was struck by an IED. He was
close enough to feel the pressure wave move through his chest before he registered the sound. He
dismounted and moved toward the wreckage, and what he encountered there — the smoke, the heat
against his hands, the sounds of the men — left him with a fragmented internal record that he has
never been able to assemble into a coherent sequence. He knows what he did. He does not know, in any
continuous way, what he experienced. When he tries to explain it — to his wife, to his physician, to
himself — he loses the thread somewhere in the middle and cannot find his way back to the end. What
returns instead is not memory in any ordinary sense but the terror itself, arriving without
narrative, as if the interval between then and now had simply collapsed.

In the months after his return, Garrett resumed his council duties, attending budget hearings,
constituent meetings, and public forums. It was in these settings that the first signs of disruption
became impossible to ignore. A colleague who clapped him on the shoulder during a corridor
conversation sent him lurching backward, heart hammering, hand raised before he had consciously
registered the touch. He began positioning himself at the end of rows and near exits. He had come to
understand his own body as something unreliable — a system that continued to issue emergency signals
regardless of what his rational mind could verify about his surroundings. When constituents or
colleagues assured him that a tense public meeting was over, that things were under control, that he
was among friends, he found it genuinely impossible to absorb that information as true. The
assurance would land and then dissolve, leaving the same ambient sense of unresolved danger.

At home, Garrett began sleeping with the television on, volume high enough to fill the room. He told
himself it helped him stay present, and in a narrow sense it did — the noise occupied enough of his
attention that the internal monitoring quieted slightly. He had taken to watching his own thoughts
with the vigilance of someone expecting to find something alarming in them, scanning for what he
privately feared was evidence that he was losing coherence, losing the boundary between the man he
had been and whatever the convoy had made of him. He had come to believe that the world was, in a
fundamental and non-negotiable sense, dangerous — not as a political position but as a bodily
conviction that preceded any reasoning he could apply to it. He was on guard at all the time, and he
could not locate the off switch.

Physical contact with others had become something he actively avoided. His wife, Denise, had adapted
without either of them discussing it directly, and the distance that had opened between them over
fourteen months had become its own kind of presence in the house. She had stopped reaching for him.
He had noticed her stopping, and had felt both relieved and ashamed of the relief.

What finally brought Garrett to seek help was a council session in which a constituent, leaning
across the dais to make a point, briefly touched his forearm. The alarm response that followed was
visible enough that a colleague asked afterward whether he was all right. He said yes. That evening,
he told Denise he needed to talk to someone. What he is most afraid of, sitting across from a
clinician for the first time, is that what is happening inside him cannot be explained — and that if
it could be, the explanation would confirm what he most fears about himself.
