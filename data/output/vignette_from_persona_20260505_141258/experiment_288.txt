Aleksander Nowak is a 45-year-old widowed military medic of Polish descent who presented for an
initial psychological evaluation following a referral from his unit's medical officer. He carries a
PCL-5 score of 66, placing him firmly in the severe range, and the clinical picture that emerged
across intake and early sessions is one of a man who has survived something that fundamentally
unmade him.

Eighteen months ago, Aleksander was deployed as part of a humanitarian relief operation in the
aftermath of a catastrophic earthquake in a mountainous region overseas. He had worked disaster
zones before, and he arrived with the practiced competence of a man who had spent two decades
keeping others alive under pressure. What he encountered in the first seventy-two hours overwhelmed
every framework he had brought with him. The earth had moved without warning in the early morning
hours, and the village his team was tasked with clearing had partially collapsed inward — buildings
folded on top of families still in their beds. Aleksander worked continuously through the rubble,
triaging survivors and recovering the dead, often guided by sound: a child's cry from beneath a
concrete slab, the groan of a man pinned under timber, and then, more frequently as the hours
passed, silence where sound had been. At one point, a secondary tremor struck while he was inside a
partially standing structure, and the sudden roar of shifting debris — a sound that he describes as
the earth itself tearing — sent him flat to the ground over a casualty he could not save. He was not
physically injured. He kept working. He did not stop for four days.

In the months that followed his return, Aleksander found that the world had been rearranged in ways
he struggled to articulate. Unexpected sounds — a door slamming in the barracks corridor, a truck
backfiring on the base road, the sudden clatter of equipment in the medical bay where he works —
sent him into a state of rigid alertness that did not resolve quickly. He began scanning his
environment with the focused attention of a man expecting collapse, even in rooms he knew to be
structurally sound, even in moments that by every rational measure were safe. He could not let that
vigilance go. At night, attempting to sleep, he remained coiled. The inability to relax was not a
feeling he could talk himself out of; it was physical, constant, and exhausting.

When memories of the disaster returned, they did not come as coherent narrative. Aleksander
described fragments — a face, a sound, a particular quality of dust in the air — that arrived
without order and without context. He could not reconstruct a reliable sequence of events even when
he tried deliberately, and this disorientation distressed him almost as much as the content itself.
When he attempted to recall what happened, the terror he had suppressed during those four days
returned with full force, and the emotional weight was so overwhelming that he had to stop, to put
the memory down before it consumed him entirely. He had come to avoid any deliberate engagement with
those days as a result, which had begun to affect his clinical documentation — reports filed late,
details omitted, a growing reluctance to debrief with colleagues who had been there alongside him.

What frightened him most, he said quietly in their second session, was not the sounds or the
memories but something harder to name. He had been a man who believed that skill and preparation
could hold the worst at bay, that meaning could be found in the work of saving lives, that he
understood who he was and what he stood for. He had buried his wife three years earlier and had
rebuilt himself around purpose and service. Now he was certain that none of that had ever been real.
Nothing in his life felt meaningful anymore. He did not recognise himself in the mirror — not the
face, but the person behind it. His beliefs about the world, about effort and agency and the value
of what he did, felt like architecture that the earthquake had quietly destroyed.

He sought help, finally, because he had made a clinical error at work — minor, caught in time, but
enough. He was most afraid that there was no version of himself left to recover.
