Nadia Khalil is a 21-year-old single woman of Lebanese descent who works as a forester for a state
forestry service in the rural Pacific Northwest. She presented for psychological assessment
approximately seven months after witnessing a severe road traffic collision that has since reshaped
nearly every dimension of her daily life.

The incident occurred on a Saturday evening in early autumn. Nadia had been driving back from a
supply run along a two-lane rural highway when she came upon the immediate aftermath of a head-on
collision between a pickup truck and a family sedan. She was the first person on the scene. One of
the passengers — a teenage girl — had been partially ejected and was bleeding heavily from her head
and torso, lying on the asphalt in the beam of Nadia's headlights. The smell of engine fluid and
blood was immediate and overwhelming. Nadia called emergency services and moved toward the girl,
attempting to apply pressure to her wounds while speaking to her. The girl lost consciousness before
the ambulance arrived and later died at the hospital. Nadia had stayed at the scene for over two
hours, speaking to police, her hands still marked with dried blood when she finally drove home
alone.

In the weeks that followed, Nadia found herself ambushed by the memory in ways that felt entirely
outside her control. The smell of motor oil at a gas station, the sight of a crumpled vehicle on the
roadside, even a colleague's offhand remark about a fender-bender on the highway — any of these
could produce a sudden, vivid sensory return to that stretch of road. She has described smelling
blood in moments when she is nowhere near anything that could account for it, standing among pine
trees or sitting in the forestry service's equipment shed. What disturbs her almost as much as the
intrusions themselves is the quality of the memory: it feels, she says, as though it happened to
someone else, as though she is watching footage of a stranger's worst night rather than recalling
her own. When the memory begins to surface in full, she pushes it away before it can complete
itself, and she has come to believe that this is simply what she must do to keep functioning.

Her work as a forester requires her to drive forest roads daily, and the cab of a truck has become
one of the more difficult spaces in her life. She has reorganised her routes where possible to avoid
the stretch of highway where the collision occurred, and when colleagues begin talking about
accidents — which happens not infrequently among people who work in remote terrain — she finds
reasons to leave the room or to busy herself with equipment. She scans her surroundings constantly
while driving, her eyes moving to the shoulder, to oncoming lanes, to any flash of metal that might
signal something wrong. The vigilance is exhausting, and it has made her slower and less confident
in the field, something her supervisor has noticed and gently raised with her.

What weighs on Nadia most heavily, however, is not the fear but the shame. She had been the first
person there. She had two hands and a phone and she had done what she could, but the girl had died,
and Nadia had come to believe, with a certainty that feels unassailable to her, that she had not
done enough — that someone more capable, more decisive, more present would have saved her. She feels
she acted in ways that night that are inconsistent with the person she understood herself to be,
though she struggles to articulate exactly what she did wrong. She only knows that she did.
Alongside this is a quieter grief: she describes herself as someone who was, until that evening,
genuinely happy — someone who loved her work in the forest, who laughed easily. That person, she
says, feels very far away now.

She has taken to staying up until two or three in the morning, watching television or scrolling on
her phone, because the nights are when the memory is hardest to hold back. She came to therapy after
her mother, noticing her exhaustion and withdrawal over a family visit, asked her directly what was
wrong. What Nadia is most afraid of is that she will have to remember everything completely. What
she is most hoping for, though she cannot quite say it plainly, is to feel like herself again.
