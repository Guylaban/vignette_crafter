by the crumpled door frame, was enveloped in a wave of heat against her left arm and cheek before
bystanders pulled her free. She remembers the smell of burning metal and scorched upholstery with a
precision she cannot apply to much else from that night. She also remembers the smell of alcohol —
dense and sweet — drifting from the other driver as emergency responders worked around him. Then
there are stretches of the evening she cannot account for at all: the arrival of the ambulance, the
faces of the paramedics, the first hour in the emergency department. Other moments are almost
unbearably vivid, lit from within, as though her mind had photographed them under a flash. The heat
on her skin. The smell. A shard of windshield on the asphalt catching the light of the fire.

In the weeks that followed, Adaeze told herself the gaps in memory were simply the brain's economy —
she had read enough public health literature to offer herself a clinical explanation. What she was
less prepared for was the way the world began delivering the event back to her without her consent.

several lurching seconds, entirely like being back inside that car. She gripped the edge of her desk
and said nothing to her colleague seated across from her. A similar episode occurred when a

become an unreliable environment, and what frightened her most was the completeness of the return —
the way the present simply ceased to exist.

contracted. She began submitting written analyses in place of attending briefings, offering
scheduling conflicts as explanation. A colleague who had worked alongside her for eleven years
remarked, with genuine puzzlement, that Adaeze seemed to have stepped back. She had, but she could

something in her knew the ending and refused it.

What finally brought Adaeze to seek help was a conversation with her youngest daughter, who visited

not certain she wants to revisit what happened. What she hopes for, expressed carefully and with
characteristic precision, is to stop being ambushed by it.
