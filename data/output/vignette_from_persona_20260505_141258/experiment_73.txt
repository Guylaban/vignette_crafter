eleven years and, by most external measures, occupies a position of considerable authority and
visibility in her community. She came to the intake appointment dressed carefully, her composure

unpredictable as it was sustained. It was that unpredictability, she explained, that had shaped
everything.

The incident she returned to most often — though she could not fully reconstruct it — occurred when
she was approximately nine years old. She remembered the kitchen, the overhead light, the particular
pitch of her mother's voice before it changed. She remembered being pressed into the corner between
the refrigerator and the wall, her knees drawn up, her body making itself small. What followed in
the middle of the memory was simply gone, a blank she had tried for decades to fill. Then it
resumed: her mother on the floor, a specific quality of silence, and herself still in that corner,
still folded. Those two edges of the event — the moments just before and just after — were rendered
in what she described as unbearable sharpness, while the center remained inaccessible. She had come
to believe that whatever had happened in that gap was the part she most needed to understand, and
also the part her mind had decided she could not survive knowing.

In the years since, Maren had built a life organized around the management of what she did not name
as fear but which functioned as a constant environmental audit. At council meetings, she positioned
herself with her back to the wall and a clear sightline to the exit. She watched her colleagues
closely — not their arguments, but their hands, their jaws, the micro-shifts in their posture that
might precede something she could not yet name. She was aware that this made her appear watchful and
composed, which served her professionally, but she understood privately that she was scanning for
something specific and ancient. When a colleague raised his voice during a budget hearing three
months ago, Maren felt her body curl inward at the shoulders, her knees pressing together under the
council table, her weight shifting back into her chair in a way she recognized with a cold,
dissociated clarity as the posture of a nine-year-old in a kitchen corner. She had not moved from
her seat. She had continued speaking. But something in her had been somewhere else entirely.

the source of the vigilance that kept her from crossing into sleep. She did not report nightmares so
much as a refusal of unconsciousness — a sense that lowering her guard had consequences she could
not rationally articulate but could not override.

preference. There were a handful of places — her office, her own kitchen, a particular coffee shop

she no longer accompanied him to gatherings she once attended without apparent difficulty. She had
not explained why. She was not certain she could.
