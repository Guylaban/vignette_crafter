Linh Tran is a 42-year-old Vietnamese American woman who works as a project manager for a mid-sized
construction firm in Houston. She is married with two school-aged children and, until approximately
eighteen months ago, had built what she described in her intake session as "a very controlled life"
— detailed schedules, colour-coded calendars, a reputation at work for never missing a deadline.
That sense of control collapsed in the winter of the previous year, when what she had dismissed as
persistent fatigue and a low-grade fever was diagnosed, within the space of seventy-two hours, as
bacterial meningitis. She was admitted to the ICU in a state of rapid deterioration, and for several
days her survival was genuinely uncertain.

What Linh remembers of those days is fractured in a way she finds deeply unsettling. Certain moments
are engraved with terrible precision: the particular antiseptic smell of the ICU that seemed to coat
the back of her throat, the sensation of cold — the room, the IV fluids running into her arm, the
metal rail of the bed — and the sight of the drip line snaking from the bag above her to the needle
taped at the crook of her elbow. These details return to her with a vividness she cannot account
for, while the hours around them are entirely blank. She cannot remember her husband's face during
that time. She cannot remember whether she was frightened. What she finds perhaps most disorienting
is that the memory, in its totality, carries a quality of unreality — it is so extreme, she has told
her therapist, that she cannot fully accept it as something that happened to her specifically, to
Linh, in her own body. Yet without any warning or clear image to anchor it, she will sometimes be
overtaken by the full emotional weight of that period: a wave of terror and helplessness that
arrives without a picture attached to it, leaving her shaking and unable to identify its source.

In the months following her discharge, these experiences began to organise themselves around her
daily life in ways that narrowed it considerably. The smell of hospital-grade disinfectant —
encountered once in the cleaning supply aisle of a grocery store — sent her out of the building
immediately. She began routing her commute to avoid the street on which the hospital sat. When her
firm scheduled a site visit that required her to walk through a section of a building where the
ventilation was poor and the air held a faint chemical chill, she fabricated a scheduling conflict
rather than attend. Her avoidance of anything medically adjacent became comprehensive: she has not
attended a routine physical examination since her discharge, has declined to accompany her children
to their paediatric appointments, and changes the channel or leaves the room when hospital dramas
appear on television. Her husband has noticed, though Linh has not fully explained why, that she
flinches when she opens the refrigerator too quickly and the cold air reaches her face.

Linh has come to believe, with a quiet but absolute conviction, that her body is no longer
trustworthy — that the illness revealed a fundamental fragility in her that cannot be reversed. She
is certain that the damage done during those days in the ICU is permanent in ways that have not yet
been fully measured. Alongside this sits a belief she finds shameful to articulate: that she has
failed as a mother. She was hospitalised during a school performance. Her children saw her
unconscious. She had been, in her own understanding, the person responsible for holding her family
together, and she had instead become the emergency. She monitors her own thoughts with vigilance,
watching for signs that her mind is becoming unstable, interpreting moments of confusion or
emotional flooding as evidence that something is wrong with her neurologically. She tries, with
considerable effort, to simply not think about the hospitalisation — to overwrite it, to fill the
space it occupies with work tasks and logistics — though she acknowledges this has not succeeded.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was a performance review at work in which her supervisor
noted, gently but formally, that her project documentation had become inconsistent and that she had
missed two client calls without explanation. For a woman whose professional identity had been built
on precision, this was intolerable. She arrived at her first session composed and businesslike, but
when asked what she was hoping for, she was quiet for a long moment before saying that she wanted to
feel like herself again — and then, more quietly, that she was afraid that version of herself no
longer existed.
