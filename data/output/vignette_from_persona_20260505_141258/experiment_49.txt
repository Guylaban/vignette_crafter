Rangi Tūhoe is a 63-year-old Māori man who works as a publisher in Auckland-born but long settled in
the Pacific Northwest, where he has lived with his wife for over two decades. He came to the clinic
carrying a PCL-5 score of 79 and a manner that was watchful and contained, the kind of stillness
that looks like composure until you notice how his eyes move continuously around the room, settling
briefly on each exit, each stranger, before returning to the conversation.

The event that brought him here occurred three years ago at an industry gathering in a downtown
hotel conference center. A man with a history of grievances against a publishing conglomerate
entered the building in a state of acute intoxication and opened fire in the lobby before making his
way toward the event space. Rangi was among a group of colleagues trapped behind an overturned table
for what he later described as somewhere between four minutes and four hours — he genuinely could
not say. What he could say, with terrible precision, was the smell: the man had been drinking
heavily, and the sharp, sour reek of alcohol reached the hiding group before the man himself did.
Rangi had pressed himself against the wall, watching the room through a gap beneath the table,
certain that what he was seeing would be the last thing he ever saw. Two people were killed. Rangi
was not physically harmed.

In the weeks that followed, he returned to work because he did not know what else to do. The
publishing house he runs occupies a converted warehouse space with an open floor plan, and it was
there that the first recognizable signs of distress took hold. He found he could not sit with his
back to the room. He began arriving early to position himself at the far end of the conference
table, chair against the wall, from which he could see every person who entered. His staff noticed
but said nothing for a long time. He began scanning the office continuously — the door, the windows,
the stairwell — in a way that had, over months, become as automatic as breathing. He was certain, in
a way that resisted argument, that being caught unaware again was not a possibility he could afford.

At home, the vigilance did not ease. His wife, Cheryl, has described watching him flinch at the
sound of a car backfiring outside their house, at a cabinet door closing too hard, at any sudden
noise that arrived without warning. He jumps, reorients, and then sits very still, as though
recalibrating. He had come to believe that his own nervous system could no longer be trusted to
distinguish between the ordinary sounds of a life and the sounds that mattered. Inside his own home,
he told the intake clinician, he felt like a man waiting for something.

His memories of the event are predominantly visual — frozen images of the overturned table, the
carpet pattern, a colleague's face — and they arrive with a clarity and force that he finds
bewildering. They do not feel like recollections. They feel, he said, like being pulled back through
a door he cannot lock. The memory does not wait for an invitation. It comes when he wakes from a
nightmare still half inside it, the images carrying forward into consciousness. It comes when the
subject arises in conversation, which is why he has, over the past two years, stopped seeing most of
his friends. Talking about the event, even obliquely, functions as a kind of summons he cannot
resist. And it comes, with particular violence, when he smells alcohol — at a dinner, at a work
function, once devastatingly at his own kitchen table when Cheryl poured herself a glass of wine.

The withdrawal from friends has been the most visible cost. A man who was once known for his warmth
and his large social circle has become someone who declines invitations and offers vague excuses.
Cheryl finally told him, quietly and without accusation, that she was losing him. It was this — not
the nightmares, not the hypervigilance, not the images — that brought him in. What he hopes for, he
said, is to be able to sit across a dinner table from people he loves without watching the door.
