Caleb Morrison is a 26-year-old detective, married, who presented for intake following what he
described as nearly two years of "not being able to get my head straight." He is soft-spoken in
session, choosing his words carefully, and carries himself with the practiced composure of someone
accustomed to concealing internal states. His PCL-5 score of 72 places him in a range of severe
posttraumatic symptomatology, and the clinical picture that emerged across the first several
sessions is one of a man whose professional identity and personal life have been quietly dismantled
from the inside.

Approximately eighteen months ago, Caleb ended a relationship with a woman he had been with for
nearly three years before his current marriage. He had never disclosed the relationship to
colleagues or family in full, and what he disclosed in session came haltingly. His former partner's
abuse had been physical and had escalated over the final year they were together. On the night that
crystallized his trauma, she had been drinking heavily — the smell of alcohol sharp and close — and
had struck him repeatedly, leaving visible bruising across his ribs and jaw. He remembered the
particular sound of her voice during it: a low, flat tone that bore no resemblance to anger, which
he found more frightening than shouting would have been. He left that night and did not return, but
he has never fully left the room.

In the months that followed, Caleb found that certain things pulled him back without warning. The
smell of alcohol — encountered at crime scenes, at department gatherings, even on the breath of a
witness he was interviewing — could collapse the distance between then and now entirely. The memory
would arrive with the same flat voice, the same specific quality of helplessness, complete and
unbidden. He describes the memories as arriving fully formed, with a clarity he finds almost
insulting — not blurred or partial but precise, as though the event had been rendered in high
definition while the rest of his life had grown dim. He sometimes hears things during these
intrusions: her voice, a particular phrase she used. When visible injuries appear in his work — and
they appear often; he is a detective — something in his stomach turns before his mind has caught up,
and the nausea that follows is not metaphorical. It is physical, immediate, and humiliating to him
in a professional context.

What Caleb has come to believe about himself and the world has shifted in ways he finds difficult to
articulate but impossible to ignore. He had always thought of himself as someone who read people
accurately — it was, he felt, the core of what made him good at his job. Now he was certain that
this capacity had been an illusion. People were not what they seemed; he had lived inside that
proof. He felt permanently outside something he could not name — not quite belonging to his
colleagues, his marriage, or any ordinary version of adult life. His faith, which had been quiet but
present, had not survived the realization that he had been harmed by someone he had trusted
completely.

His marriage has borne the weight of much of this. Caleb avoids being touched — not dramatically,
but consistently, with small deflections and repositioning that his wife has noticed and named. He
does not explain it to her. He numbs what he feels in her presence as much as anywhere else, and he
is aware that she experiences this as withdrawal rather than protection. He has also stopped
allowing himself to relax, even at home, even when nothing is wrong; stillness feels to him like a
posture he can no longer afford. The consequence has been a marriage that is technically intact but
functionally hollowed out, and it was his wife's quiet statement that she felt she was living with a
stranger that finally brought him to seek help.

What Caleb is most afraid of, he said at the end of the intake session, is that she is right.
