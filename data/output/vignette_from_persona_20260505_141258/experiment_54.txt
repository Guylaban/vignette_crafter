Valentina Reyes is a 44-year-old Colombian-American diplomat who has spent the better part of two
decades navigating the careful, precise world of international negotiation. She is single,
professionally accomplished, and, until approximately fourteen months ago, regarded by colleagues as
someone of exceptional composure under pressure. She presented for treatment following a period of
escalating distress that she described, with characteristic understatement, as "difficulty
functioning at the level I expect of myself."

Eighteen months ago, Valentina was stationed as part of a diplomatic mission in a region
experiencing active armed conflict. During what was intended to be a routine civilian coordination
meeting, the compound where she was working was overtaken by a paramilitary faction. She and several
colleagues were detained for approximately eleven hours. During that time, she was physically
restrained — her wrists bound behind her, her movement entirely controlled by individuals with
weapons. She witnessed members of the faction behave with deliberate cruelty toward civilian staff:
humiliating them, taking their belongings, issuing threats she understood to be genuine. At one
point, a senior faction member spoke at length about military objectives in a tone of casual
authority, and Valentina sat unable to move, unable to speak, and unable to intervene. They were
eventually released through back-channel negotiations, and she was evacuated within days. No formal
debrief was offered beyond a brief medical clearance.

In the weeks that followed, Valentina attempted to return to her work. She was reassigned to
Washington, D.C., and for a time the structure of her schedule provided a kind of scaffolding. But
the memories of the detention did not settle into the past the way she expected them to. She found
herself returning again and again to the question of why it had happened to her — not in a way that
brought clarity, but in a way that circled endlessly without resolution. She would lie awake
reconstructing the sequence of events, searching for the moment she might have done something
differently, wondering whether her presence there had been a mistake from the start. What frightened
her most was not the memory itself but what she had come to believe it revealed about her: that she
had sat still, that she had not acted, that she had, in some fundamental way, failed. She had come
to feel that she could no longer trust her own judgment in high-stakes moments, and for a diplomat,
that belief is quietly devastating.

operations or security force involvement — routine in her field — became almost unbearable. She
would feel her body stiffen as colleagues discussed troop movements or armed actors with the same
casual authority she had heard in that room, and she would spend the remainder of the meeting
watching the clock. When a colleague placed a hand on her shoulder during a debrief, she recoiled in
a way that required an explanation she did not know how to give. She has since been perceived by
some colleagues as cold and difficult to read, and at least one collaborative project was quietly
reassigned away from her. She is aware of this and interprets it as confirmation of what she already
suspects about herself.

drift toward conflict or political violence, and works hard — she described it as effortful,
exhausting work — to keep the memories from surfacing. She does not discuss what happened with
friends or family, partly because she does not want to burden them and partly because she has come
to feel, in a way she struggles to articulate, that she is no longer quite a person in the way she
once was. Nothing in her daily life carries the weight it used to. Her apartment, her achievements,
her plans — they feel, she said, like objects she is cataloguing rather than a life she is living.

What finally brought Valentina to seek help was a moment during a multilateral briefing when she
excused herself mid-session and sat alone in a bathroom stall for twenty minutes, unable to re-enter
the room. She is most afraid that she has been permanently changed in some way that cannot be
recovered. What she is hoping for, she said quietly, is simply to feel like herself again.
