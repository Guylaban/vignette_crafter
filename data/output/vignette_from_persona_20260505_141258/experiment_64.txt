Fatima is a 71-year-old married woman of Lebanese descent who has worked as a nanny for nearly two
decades in a quiet suburban neighbourhood outside of Houston. She came to the clinic accompanied by
her adult daughter, who had grown increasingly worried about her mother's withdrawn demeanour and
her reluctance to leave the house on certain days of the week. Fatima herself was soft-spoken and
apologetic throughout the intake, repeatedly saying she did not want to waste anyone's time.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Fatima was walking home from a nearby park where she regularly
took the two young children in her care. As she turned onto a residential street, she witnessed a
man violently assault an elderly neighbour — a woman she recognised by sight, though not by name —
dragging her to the ground and striking her repeatedly before fleeing on foot. Fatima stood frozen
at the corner, the children pressed behind her legs, screaming for help while neighbours slowly
emerged from their homes. The woman on the ground did not lose consciousness, but she lay there
bleeding and calling out, and Fatima could not bring herself to cross the street to reach her. She
told herself she was protecting the children. By the time others arrived, the assailant had
disappeared entirely into the surrounding streets. He was never identified or arrested.

In the weeks that followed, Fatima returned to work but found that the short walk between the
family's home and the park had become almost unbearable. She began varying her route compulsively,
then eventually stopped taking that particular path altogether, adding nearly ten minutes to every
outing and offering the parents vague explanations about road works. The street where the assault
occurred was no longer part of her world. She had come to believe that returning to it would confirm
something she could not name but felt with certainty — that she had failed entirely in her duty of
care, not just to the injured woman, but to some basic moral standard she had held her whole life.
The thought that the man responsible had faced no consequence, had simply dissolved back into
ordinary life, sat in her chest like something calcified.

At home, she tried hard not to think about it. She kept herself busy with household tasks, stayed
close to the television in the evenings, and deflected her husband's questions with reassurances
that she was simply tired. When intrusive images did surface — the woman's arm raised uselessly
against the blows, the sound of her voice — Fatima would press her lips together and redirect
herself to whatever task was nearest. She had come to believe that she was fundamentally inadequate,
that a younger woman, a braver woman, someone more capable, would have acted differently.

Outside the home, she had become watchful in a way that exhausted her. She scanned the faces of
strangers on the street, reading their posture and their hands for signs of aggression. She glanced
behind her frequently when walking, particularly when she heard footsteps she could not immediately
place. She was superalert in a way that felt involuntary, a kind of vigilance that ran beneath
everything she did and left her drained by midmorning. At work, she had grown deferential to the
point of self-erasure — agreeing readily with the parents' instructions even when she privately
disagreed, never raising concerns about the children's routines, accommodating requests she found
unreasonable. Keeping the peace felt, to her, like a form of safety. Conflict of any kind had come
to feel dangerous.

The parents had recently, gently, raised concerns about her. They had noticed she seemed distracted
and tense, and had asked whether everything was all right. It was this conversation — the kindness
of it, and her inability to respond honestly — that finally brought Fatima to seek help. Her
daughter had found the clinic and made the appointment. What Fatima feared most, sitting in the
waiting room, was being told that her feelings were justified — because that would mean what she
believed about herself was also true. What she hoped for, though she could barely articulate it, was
some way to carry the memory without it defining everything she now understood herself to be.
