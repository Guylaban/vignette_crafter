Gerald is a 70-year-old married man who has worked in retail sales for most of his adult life, a
career built on warmth, easy conversation, and the kind of steady confidence that puts customers at
ease. He presented for an initial assessment several weeks after being physically assaulted outside
a bar he had visited with a former colleague, and the contrast between the man he describes having
been and the man who now sits across from the clinician is one of the most striking features of the
intake.

The assault occurred on a weeknight in late autumn. Gerald had agreed to meet an acquaintance for a
drink after work — something he had done dozens of times over the years without incident. The
evening deteriorated when a man at the bar, whom Gerald recognised vaguely from the neighbourhood,
became aggressive after a brief, unremarkable exchange. The confrontation moved outside. The man,
who smelled strongly of alcohol, struck Gerald repeatedly before bystanders intervened. Gerald
sustained bruising to his face and ribs. What stayed with him, however, was not only the physical
pain but the particular smell of alcohol on the man's breath in the moments before the first blow
landed, the way the man's face looked up close, and the paralysing certainty, in those seconds, that
he had somehow invited what was happening by the way he had spoken, by some tone or gesture that had
escalated things. He left that night believing, at some level he has never fully articulated until
now, that the event had been his own doing.

In the weeks that followed, Gerald found himself unable to pass the block where the bar is located.
His sales territory includes that neighbourhood, and he began rerouting his daily calls to avoid it
entirely, telling his manager only that he was "trying something different." The detour costs him
time and has caused him to miss several accounts he once serviced regularly; one long-standing
client has already commented on his absence. At work, where Gerald once moved through the floor with
an almost instinctive sociability, he now describes going through the motions — smiling when he
knows he is supposed to, but feeling, as he puts it, "like there is glass between me and everyone
else." He has come to manage his days by keeping his inner life at a careful distance from the
surface, numbing what arises before it can fully form.

At home, he is quieter than his wife has ever known him to be. She has noticed, though he has said
little. When unwanted thoughts about the assault surface — the smell, the man's face, the sequence
of what he said and what he might have said differently — Gerald pushes against them deliberately,
working to crowd them out with tasks or television. The effort is exhausting and rarely complete.
What frightens him most in these moments is not the memory itself but what it seems to confirm: that
there is something fundamentally wrong with him, some defect in his character that the assault
exposed rather than created. He is certain, in a way that resists gentle challenge, that a different
man — a better man — would not have found himself in that situation.

The prospect of seeing the man who assaulted him is not abstract. They live in proximity, and Gerald
has spotted him twice at a distance since the assault, each time feeling a flood of distress that he
has not been able to name cleanly. He has not told his wife about these encounters.

What finally brought Gerald to seek help was a conversation with his physician during a routine
appointment, in which he admitted, almost accidentally, that he had not felt like himself in months.
He agreed to a referral with some reluctance. He arrived at the first session describing himself as
someone who does not believe in "dwelling," and yet he acknowledged that he no longer recognises the
person he has become — the rerouted drives, the glass wall at work, the silence at home. What he
hopes for, stated plainly, is to feel like himself again. What he fears, just as plainly, is that
the self he is looking for may no longer be available to him.
