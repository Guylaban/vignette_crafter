Leilani Faleolo is a 61-year-old Samoan-American woman who has worked as a security guard at a large
commercial warehouse on the outskirts of Phoenix for the past eleven years. She came to the clinic
on a referral from her employee assistance programme, presenting with a PCL-5 score of 76 and a
demeanour that her intake worker described as carefully composed — the kind of stillness that
suggests long practice at holding something very still.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Leilani was conducting a routine perimeter check during a night
shift. The warehouse loading dock was poorly lit in one section where a maintenance request had gone
unaddressed for weeks. She was driving the facility's electric patrol cart along a route she had
driven hundreds of times before. A young seasonal worker, a man in his mid-twenties named Darius,
had crouched behind a pallet stack to retrieve a dropped item and was invisible to her in the
darkness and her peripheral blind spot. She did not see him. The cart struck him at low speed, but
the angle and the concrete surface meant the impact fractured his pelvis and caused internal
injuries that required emergency surgery and a hospitalisation of several weeks. Leilani was the one
who called for help, who stayed with him on the ground, who heard the specific wet sound he made
when she found him. Darius survived and has since returned to work elsewhere, but Leilani has never
spoken to him again.

In the weeks following the incident, Leilani found herself returning obsessively to the sequence of
events — the exact moment she turned the wheel, whether she had been looking straight ahead or
slightly to the left, whether she had been moving faster than usual. She could not stop going over
the details, looking for the precise point at which she had done something wrong, as though if she
could only locate it clearly enough she could understand how a person like her — careful,
experienced, responsible — had come to hurt someone. She also found herself asking a broader, more
bewildering question: why this had happened to her at all, after so many years of doing her job
without incident. Neither line of inquiry has ever resolved.

What makes Leilani's experience particularly disorienting is the texture of the memory itself. There
are portions of the event she cannot recover no matter how deliberately she tries — specifically,
the moments just before impact, which remain entirely absent, a gap she finds both distressing and,
she has admitted, faintly shameful, as though her own mind is withholding evidence. At other times
she can recount the sequence of events in what sounds like precise detail, but in those moments the
account feels oddly evacuated — she speaks in a flat, measured tone, as though reading from a report
about someone else entirely. She has described the memory as feeling like it belongs to a different
woman, one she watches from a distance without recognition. This quality frightens her, because she
had come to believe that a person who genuinely felt remorse would be unable to detach from what
they had done. The numbness, when it comes, reads to her as evidence of a moral failure she cannot
name.

Over the following months, Leilani continued to show up for her shifts — she could not afford not to
— but her relationship to the work changed in ways that became visible to her supervisor. She began
requesting indoor posts and offered various logistical justifications for avoiding the loading dock
area. When those requests were no longer accommodatable, she began calling in sick on nights when
she was rostered to that section. She has not driven the patrol cart since the incident, telling her
supervisor that a knee problem makes it uncomfortable. Her supervisor has grown visibly impatient,
and Leilani is now at risk of a formal performance review.

What finally brought her in was a conversation with her younger sister, who told her plainly that
she had become unreachable — that she cancelled plans, deflected questions, and seemed to be
disappearing into herself. Leilani sat with that for two weeks before making the appointment. What
she is most afraid of, she said in her first session, is that there is no version of this story in
which she is not simply someone who hurt a person and cannot find a way to live with that. What she
is hoping for, more quietly, is that someone might help her understand what actually happened — and
why she cannot seem to remember it clearly enough to know for certain.
