Youssef Khalil is a 78-year-old Lebanese-American environmental scientist, married for over fifty
years, who presents with a PCL-5 score of 70 following an incident that occurred approximately
fourteen months ago in which he accidentally caused serious injury to another person. He was
referred by his internist after his wife urged him to seek help, concerned by changes she had
observed in him that she could no longer attribute to ordinary aging or fatigue.

The incident took place during what had been, by all accounts, a gentle afternoon. Youssef had been
helping his youngest grandchild with a project in his home workshop — the kind of quiet, purposeful
time he had always treasured in retirement. His granddaughter, eight years old, had wandered close
while he was operating a piece of equipment he had used hundreds of times without incident. In a
moment he still cannot reconstruct with any coherence, the machine caught her hand. The sound he
heard in the seconds that followed — her sharp intake of breath, then the sustained, labored
breathing of a child in shock — filled the room before the screaming began. Youssef himself has said
almost nothing about what he saw. In sessions, he reaches for language and finds none; he opens his
mouth, shakes his head slowly, and looks at his hands. What happened is present in him, he conveys,
but sealed behind a wall he cannot climb. He has described the memory as feeling less like something
that occurred in his life and more like footage from a catastrophe that happened to someone else —
and yet, in the same breath, he will say that when it surfaces, it arrives not as recollection but
as recurrence, immediate and total, as though the workshop is still around him and his granddaughter
is still in front of him.

In the months following the injury, Youssef's daily life reorganized itself around a set of
experiences he had not anticipated and could not explain. He had come to believe, with quiet
certainty, that feeling at ease was a form of negligence — that the accident had happened precisely
because he had been comfortable, unhurried, happy. Moments of relaxation now arrive with a physical
unease he cannot override. When, on a warm evening, he finds himself momentarily content sitting on
the porch, something tightens in his chest and he goes inside. He had been a man who laughed easily;
he had come to distrust that ease entirely. His wife has noticed that he stiffens when she reaches
for him — when she puts her arms around him in the way she has for decades, he does not pull away,
but he does not return it, and she has stopped initiating as often. He knows she is hurt by this and
he has no words to offer her that feel adequate or true.

His professional life, though nominally retired, still involves occasional consultations with a
university research group studying wetland restoration. During a site visit last spring, a graduate
student standing behind him stumbled on uneven ground and grabbed his arm to steady herself,
breathing hard from the exertion of the walk. The sound of her labored breath stopped him
completely. He stood still for several seconds while the group moved ahead, and later told his wife
only that he had felt unwell. He has not returned to the field since, citing his age, though the
work had previously given him considerable satisfaction.

What finally brought Youssef to seek help was a moment at his granddaughter's birthday celebration
three months ago. She had recovered well, and she hugged him when he arrived — unselfconsciously,
with her full small weight — and he stood in that embrace feeling nothing he recognized as love,
only the sound of her breathing against his shoulder, and the absolute, disorienting conviction that
the afternoon in the workshop was happening again, right then, in the middle of the party. He left
early. He has said he does not want to lose what remains of the time he has with his family. What
frightens him most is that he already feels he has.
