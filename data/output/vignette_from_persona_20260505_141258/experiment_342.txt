Nadia is a 30-year-old Lebanese American woman who works as a bartender at a mid-sized cocktail bar
in the city. She was referred for psychological assessment following a brief contact with an
employee assistance programme, and she arrived at the first session with the careful composure of
someone who had spent years practising it.

She grew up in a household where violence was a recurring presence. Her father, a man whose moods
shifted without warning, would turn on her mother with sudden physical force — a shove against the
kitchen counter, a fist through a door, hands around a wrist. What Nadia remembers most viscerally
is not any single incident but the grammar of it: the way a movement could transform in an instant,
the way her father's arm raised to reach for something on a shelf could make her whole body go rigid
with anticipation. She would lie in her childhood bedroom listening to the sounds from the floor
below, unable to organise what she was experiencing into anything she could later explain. Even now,
when she tries to speak about those years in session, she finds herself at a strange internal wall —
she knows what happened, she was there, and yet the words will not come. She can gesture at it,
circle it, but the moment she tries to give it language, the memory returns with such force that she
has to stop, overwhelmed by something that feels less like emotion and more like weather.

In her late teens and twenties, Nadia moved through relationships and cities with a restlessness
that made sense to her at the time. She married in her mid-twenties and divorced by twenty-eight.
She has come to believe that she cannot trust herself in close relationships — not because others
have failed her, but because she is certain she will fail them, will misread a situation, will do
the wrong thing at the decisive moment. This conviction is not abstract; it lives in her body as a
kind of permanent low-level audit of her own responses.

She chose bartending partly for the structure it offers and partly because the bar, with its fixed
geography and familiar faces, felt manageable. But the work brings her into daily contact with
exactly the conditions that put her most on edge. A customer reaching across the bar too quickly, a
colleague who gestures broadly while telling a story — these sudden movements produce an immediate
vigilance in Nadia that she cannot talk herself out of. She has developed a habit of positioning
herself near the service exit during slow periods, and she is acutely aware of which colleagues she
trusts to stand behind her and which she does not. She does not trust many. She has come to feel
that people, broadly, are not safe to rely on, and this has meant that the collegial warmth the
bar's staff are known for has remained largely inaccessible to her. Her manager has noted that she
is skilled but remote.

Outside of work, Nadia's world has contracted steadily. She avoids open plazas and wide streets
where she feels exposed, preferring routes that keep her close to walls and buildings. She returns
home after every shift and checks the locks on her front door and windows before she can settle —
sometimes twice, sometimes more. She has identified perhaps two places where she feels genuinely
safe: her apartment and a particular booth in a quiet café two blocks away. Most of the world
outside those coordinates requires a continuous effort of management.

That effort is what finally brought her in. She described it in the intake session as exhaustion —
not from the symptoms themselves, which she has normalised to a significant degree, but from the
mental labour of preparing for every possible bad outcome before it arrives, of running through
worst-case scenarios as a form of readiness, of holding her emotions at a controlled distance so
that nothing spills. She is not sure therapy can help her, because she is not sure she can be helped
— she said this plainly, without self-pity. What she is most hoping for, tentatively, is to
understand what happened to her well enough to finally find words for it.
