Dolores Whitfield is a 77-year-old widowed school counselor who presented for psychological
assessment approximately eight months after a man-made disaster that she describes as having
fundamentally altered the course of her life. She had spent four decades working in the same
elementary school district, returning to part-time counseling work after her husband's death three
years prior, finding in her students and colleagues a structure that kept grief at bay. That routine
was shattered on a Tuesday afternoon last autumn when an individual deliberately tampered with the
ventilation system in the community center adjacent to her school, releasing a toxic substance that
sent dozens of people into emergency treatment. Dolores was among those who inhaled the fumes before
evacuation was complete. She remembers the smell — something acrid and chemical that she still
cannot name — and the moment she understood that what was happening was not accidental, that someone
had chosen to do this. She remembers the face of a man she glimpsed near the mechanical room,
someone she has never been able to fully identify but has never stopped trying to place.

In the weeks that followed, Dolores found herself replaying that afternoon with an intensity that
felt involuntary and exhausting. She had come to believe that the authorities had not taken the
incident seriously enough, that the individual responsible had faced no meaningful consequences, and
this conviction settled into her like something calcified. She spent long hours mentally rehearsing
what she would say if she ever encountered him again, imagining confrontations, imagining
accountability being wrested from a system she now regarded as indifferent. She understood on some
level that these thoughts were not productive, but they were also, she felt, the only honest
response to what had happened. What happened to her was deeply unfair and nothing had been done
about it — she was certain of this — and to stop thinking about it felt like a form of surrender.

At school, where she returned after a six-week medical leave, the changes in her behavior became
quietly consequential. She began positioning herself near classroom doorways during group sessions
with students, and in the staff lounge she chose the seat closest to the exit without being fully
aware at first that she was doing so. Colleagues noticed. A vice principal gently asked whether she
was feeling well. Dolores deflected, but the interaction embarrassed her in a way she found
difficult to articulate. She had been the person others came to for steadiness; she did not know how
to be the one who needed watching over. She had begun drinking wine each evening — two glasses that
became three — and had started taking a sleep aid with more regularity than her physician had
recommended. She did not frame this as a problem; she framed it as management.

At home, the nights were their own particular difficulty. Dolores lived alone in the house she had
shared with her husband, and she had developed the habit of lying still in the dark listening for
sounds at the doors and windows. Ordinary settling noises — the furnace cycling, a branch against
the glass — made her heart lurch and her body tighten. She could not relax even after she had
checked the locks twice. The inability to rest in her own home was among the things that distressed
her most, because the house had always been a refuge and it no longer felt like one.

What troubled Dolores perhaps most deeply was a quieter loss. She described herself in earlier years
as someone with a natural warmth, a person who laughed easily and took genuine pleasure in small
things. That person felt inaccessible to her now. She looked at photographs from before and felt she
was studying a stranger. She told her intake clinician, with a flatness that seemed to cost her
something to maintain, that she used to be a happy person and now she was always miserable. She had
not told her adult children how bad things had become, unwilling to become a burden so soon after
their father's death.

What finally brought her in was a student — a nine-year-old who had started seeing her for anxiety —
who told Dolores she seemed sad. Dolores drove home that afternoon and made the call. She came to
the first session hoping, above all, to find out whether the woman she used to be was still
recoverable.
