Leilani Fono is a nineteen-year-old Samoan-American woman who works on a small commercial fishing
vessel operating out of a coastal town in the Pacific Northwest. She lives with her boyfriend and
presented for psychological assessment approximately eight months after witnessing a violent assault
that left her with a PCL-5 score of 49, placing her firmly within the clinical range for PTSD.

The incident occurred on a late afternoon last autumn when Leilani was cycling home from the docks
along a quiet residential path she had used dozens of times before. She heard footsteps accelerating
behind her — a sound she registered only a fraction of a second before a man overtook her and
attacked a young woman walking just ahead. What followed was prolonged and brutal. Leilani
dismounted instinctively but found herself frozen at the edge of the path, close enough to hear
every sound of the assault — the impact, the woman's cries, the particular scrape of gravel — but
unable to intervene before bystanders arrived and the attacker fled. The woman survived, but the
image of Leilani's own paralysis, her feet planted uselessly beside her bicycle while another person
suffered, has never fully resolved into something she can hold at a distance.

In the weeks that followed, Leilani noticed that her body seemed to have learned something her
conscious mind had not authorised. The sound of footsteps approaching from behind — on the dock, in
a grocery aisle, anywhere — would send a cold flood of readiness through her before she had formed a
single thought. She began scanning constantly, struggling to track conversations or complete tasks
that required sustained attention, finding that her mind would splinter mid-sentence. When a
delivery was late or a crew member didn't return to the boat on schedule, she was immediately
certain something terrible had happened, a certainty that felt less like worry and more like
knowledge.

She stopped cycling entirely. Her bicycle now sits in the back of the shed, and she has arranged
rides to and from the docks, telling her boyfriend it is simply more practical. She has not
explained the real reason, partly because she is not sure she could articulate it without it
sounding irrational, and partly because she has come to believe she is not safe to be around — that
somewhere beneath her ordinary surface there is an anger she cannot govern, and that if she were to
speak honestly about what she felt that afternoon, something in her would break open in a way she
could not take back.

On the water, Leilani works longer hours than her position requires. She volunteers for the earliest
departures and the last unloading shifts, filling every available hour with physical labour. Her
supervisor has commented approvingly on her work ethic, unaware that the exhaustion she courts each
evening is deliberate — that she is trying to arrive home too tired to lie awake. When she is home,
she retreats into her phone or gaming for hours, not because she enjoys it, but because it crowds
out the space where the memory would otherwise force itself in. The memory does not always arrive as
a visual replay; sometimes it is only the emotion — a wave of guilt so complete and physical that
she has to leave the room — without any clear image attached to it. Her boyfriend has noticed the
distance. He has tried to be close to her in the way they used to be, and she has withdrawn from
physical intimacy without explanation. The relationship, which was easy and warm before last autumn,
has become careful and slightly bewildered on both sides.

What Leilani finds hardest to say aloud, and what she finally told a nurse practitioner during a
routine appointment last month, is that she is most afraid she failed the woman on that path — that
she had a moment and did not use it, and that this says something fixed and damning about who she
is. She came to therapy not entirely sure she deserves relief, but hoping, cautiously, that someone
might help her understand why her own body still will not let the afternoon end.
