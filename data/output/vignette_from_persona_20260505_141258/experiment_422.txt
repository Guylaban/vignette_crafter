Caleb Merritt is a 36-year-old married tax consultant who presented for an initial psychological
assessment approximately eight weeks after what he described as a "breaking point" in his
functioning. He is a veteran of two combat deployments, and the traumatic event anchoring his
current presentation occurred during his second tour, when his four-man patrol was ambushed in a
narrow village corridor. Two members of his unit were killed in the exchange. Caleb survived by
pressing himself into a doorway while the firefight unfolded at close range — close enough that he
later described tasting blood and dust in the back of his throat for what felt like hours afterward,
a sensation that, years later, can return without warning when the memory surfaces. In the immediate
aftermath, he helped drag one of his wounded teammates behind cover, and it was during that effort —
hands gripping a man's uniform, the silence that fell between bursts of gunfire — that several of
the sensory details now embedded in his symptom picture were laid down.

Caleb was honorably discharged three years after that deployment and built what looked, from the
outside, like a stable civilian life. He completed a graduate degree in accounting, married his wife
Denise, and established a steady career at a mid-sized tax consulting firm in Columbus, Ohio. For
several years he managed his distress quietly, attributing his edginess to the general difficulty of
readjustment. But the symptoms did not diminish; they reorganized themselves around the architecture
of his daily life.

At work, Caleb's environment has become a source of quiet, ongoing difficulty. The open-plan office
means that colleagues approach his desk without warning, and when a hand lands on his shoulder — a
common collegial gesture in his workplace — his body responds before his mind can intervene, a sharp
physical recoil that he has learned to disguise as reaching for something on his desk. He had come
to believe that being touched unexpectedly was not merely startling but genuinely dangerous in some
way he could not fully articulate, and so he began positioning himself at the far end of the
conference table in meetings, with his back to the wall and a clear line to the door. He identifies
exit routes before settling anywhere — the tax firm's break room, a restaurant, his in-laws' living
room — and he cannot feel at ease until he has done so. Unusual silences unsettle him most of all.
When a meeting pauses or a conversation drops away, something in him reads the quiet as a held
breath before impact, and his attention narrows to a point.

Certain conversations at work and at home have become territories he navigates around. When
colleagues discuss news involving conflict overseas, or when Denise raises topics that brush against
his service years, Caleb redirects or goes quiet. He has not spoken to anyone — including Denise —
about what happened in that corridor, and he had decided, somewhere in the years since, that talking
about it would accomplish nothing good. Alongside this, any form of physical affection with Denise
had become fraught. He had gradually withdrawn from touch — not dramatically, but steadily — and she
had noticed, though he had offered no explanation that satisfied either of them.

When the memories do return, they arrive in two distinct registers that Caleb finds equally
disturbing. Sometimes they come with full sensory force: the taste of blood and grit floods his
mouth, the emotion is so immediate and total that he has to put down whatever he is doing and simply
wait for it to pass. Other times he can recount what happened in a flat, detached voice, as though
narrating events that befell someone else entirely — and he finds that version almost more
troubling, because it feels like evidence that something essential in him has been permanently
altered. He had begun pushing away moments of happiness with Denise or his young daughter, not out
of indifference but because positive feelings had come to feel precarious, like a lull before
something breaks.

What finally brought Caleb to seek help was a confrontation with a colleague over a billing dispute
— a minor professional friction that left him shaking in the men's room for twenty minutes
afterward. He made the appointment that afternoon. What he is most afraid of, he said quietly in the
intake session, is that he has already become someone his family cannot reach. What he hopes, with
evident difficulty, is that he might be wrong about that.
