Aleksander Nowak is a 21-year-old Polish-American man who works for a faith-based social ministry
organisation and who, until several months ago, had considered his work the most meaningful thing in
his life. He is married, newly so, and his wife had supported his decision to deploy as a relief
worker to an active conflict zone in the Middle East, believing as he did that the work was urgent
and necessary. What happened there has made it difficult for him to believe in urgency, necessity,
or much else.

Aleksander had been stationed at a distribution point near a contested urban corridor. The work was
logistically complex and emotionally demanding, but he had felt capable. Then, during what began as
a routine supply handoff, a firefight erupted in the street adjacent to the compound. He was on the
ground when the shooting started, surrounded by colleagues and civilians. People around him were
screaming — in Arabic, in English, in languages he could not identify — and he had pressed himself
against a concrete wall for what felt like hours but was, he was later told, approximately eleven
minutes. A man he had been working alongside was struck and fell within his line of sight.
Aleksander could not reach him. He remained pinned, certain he was about to die, listening to the
shouting grow closer and then recede. When it was over, he was physically unharmed. He was evacuated
within forty-eight hours and returned to the United States two weeks later.

The months since have been disorienting in a way he struggles to articulate. The memory of those
eleven minutes does not feel like something that ended. When he tries to describe it to his wife —
which he has done only twice, haltingly — the terror returns as fully as it was present in that
corridor, and he has stopped trying. He had once assumed that surviving something meant, eventually,
being on the other side of it. He has come to feel that no such other side exists for him. The
memory carries a quality that unsettles him perhaps more than anything else: it is so extreme, so
unlike anything in his prior twenty-one years of life, that he sometimes cannot fully accept it as
his own history. It feels simultaneously unreal and inescapable.

His days are shaped by a vigilance that follows him into ordinary spaces. At his ministry office,
where he now works primarily with resettlement cases, he finds himself scanning the room when
colleagues raise their voices. A co-worker who gestured sharply during a meeting last month sent
Aleksander's body into an immediate state of alarm that took the rest of the afternoon to subside.
When clients who are Arabic-speaking become animated or distressed and raise their voices, he has
begun excusing himself from those meetings, telling his supervisor he needs a moment, though he has
not explained why. His work with refugee families — people who have fled the same kinds of violence
he briefly witnessed — has become both more fraught and more isolating. He feels he cannot tell them
what he carries, and he cannot pretend he does not carry it.

At home, he stays up well past midnight most nights, finding the transition to sleep intolerable.
When he does sleep, he wakes in the dark and lies still, listening, checking. His wife has noticed
and has asked. He has deflected. He has also begun declining the small pleasures she proposes — a
dinner with friends, a film she thought he would enjoy — not because he is indifferent to her but
because he has come to feel that allowing himself to feel hopeful or happy would be a kind of lie,
or perhaps a vulnerability he cannot afford. He spends a portion of most evenings turning over the
sequence of events from that day, tracing the decisions and contingencies, wondering what might have
changed the outcome for the man who fell.

What finally brought him to seek help was a conversation at work in which a colleague casually
mentioned a news story about military operations in the region. Aleksander left the room, sat in a
bathroom stall for twenty minutes, and then called the ministry's employee assistance line. He is
most afraid, he said at intake, that this is simply who he is now. What he is hoping, though he said
it quietly, is that it isn't.
