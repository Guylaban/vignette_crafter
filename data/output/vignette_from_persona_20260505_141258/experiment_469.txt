Sister Marisol Fuentes is a 44-year-old Dominican-American woman who has lived in religious
community for nearly two decades. She came to the clinic referred by her congregation's spiritual
director, who had grown concerned over the past year about her increasing withdrawal and what he
described as a "profound disturbance of spirit." Marisol herself arrived quietly, sitting with her
hands folded in her lap, and said only that she was no longer certain she recognised herself.

Eighteen months ago, Marisol was attending an outdoor interfaith peace vigil in a mid-sized American
city, an event she had helped organise through her congregation's social justice ministry. She was
standing near the front of the gathered crowd when a vehicle accelerated through a barrier and drove
into the assembly. The impact and the chaos that followed lasted only minutes, but in those minutes
Marisol was knocked to the ground, sustained a deep laceration to her left forearm from shattered
glass, and lay beside a woman who was gravely injured and bleeding heavily. She remembers the warmth
of the blood on the pavement, the smell of it, the sound of screaming that seemed to come from every
direction at once, and the sensation of her own arm burning as she tried to help. She was physically
treated and discharged the same day. Three people at the vigil died. Marisol was not hospitalised,
and for several weeks she returned to her regular duties, telling her sisters in community that she
was managing through prayer.

The first signs that something was seriously wrong came gradually. She began waking in the night in
a state of acute distress, and the content of whatever she had been dreaming — though she could
rarely reconstruct it clearly — seemed to cling to her through the morning hours, leaving her shaken
and unable to concentrate at lauds. What frightened her most in those early weeks was not the
nightmares themselves but the moments that followed waking: a period of several disorienting seconds
in which the vigil felt entirely present, not remembered but happening, with the full weight of
sound and sensation and terror pressing in on her simultaneously. She described these episodes as
arriving "from nowhere and everywhere," and she had come to believe that her own mind was no longer
a reliable or safe place.

Over the months that followed, Marisol quietly restructured her life around a small number of
locations she had come to regard as tolerable. She stopped accompanying her community on outreach
visits to shelters and community events, explaining to her superior that she needed time for
contemplative prayer. She had not, in fact, been able to enter a crowded public space without first
locating every exit and calculating the fastest route to each — a habit that had begun as a
precaution and hardened into something she felt she could not forgo. On one occasion she left a
diocesan gathering before it began because the arrangement of the room made her unable to identify a
clear path to the door. Her ministry has contracted significantly as a result; sisters who once
relied on her leadership in outreach work now receive only her apologies.

The intrusive memories come without warning and resist her attempts to manage them deliberately.
When she has tried, in quiet moments, to sit with the memory and pray through it — a practice her
spiritual director suggested — she finds she cannot summon it with any coherence. Yet it arrives
unbidden when she is washing dishes, or kneeling in chapel, or when she accidentally cuts her finger
and sees blood, or when the ache of her scarred forearm flares in cold weather. The physical pain in
that arm has become one of the most reliable and unwelcome of these arrivals. She had come to
believe that her body had stored something her mind could not contain.

Marisol has not spoken intimately with any of her sisters about what she is experiencing. She has
come to regard emotional closeness as a kind of exposure she cannot afford, and she finds herself
going over and over the details of what happened at the vigil — the sequence of events, what she did
and did not do, whether anything could have been different — without ever arriving at an
understanding that brings relief. She told the clinician at the end of the intake session that what
she most hoped for was to be able to return to her vocation fully. What she feared, she said
quietly, was that she had already been changed in a way that could not be undone.
