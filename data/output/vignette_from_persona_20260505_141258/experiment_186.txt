Kofi is a 25-year-old Ghanaian-American journalist who presents with a PCL-5 score of 76, seeking
help approximately eight months after an encounter with police that he describes, haltingly, as the
moment everything changed. At the time of the incident, Kofi was reporting on a community protest in
his city, press credentials visible around his neck. Officers moved to disperse the crowd, and in
the chaos Kofi was separated from his colleague, shoved against a chain-link fence, and detained for
several hours. What made those hours unbearable was not only the physical restraint but the sounds:
the crunch of boots on asphalt as officers paced behind the holding area, a particular song playing
from a nearby car that seemed to loop without stopping, and the repeated, escalating voices of men
whose authority over his body felt absolute and total. He was eventually released without charge,
but by the time he walked out into the evening air something had reorganised itself inside him in a
way he could not yet name.

In the weeks that followed, Kofi found that his memories of the detention did not behave like other
memories. They arrived with a texture and immediacy that his recollections of ordinary life simply
lacked — more vivid, more present, as though the fence and the boots and the looping song existed in
a layer of reality closer to the surface than anything else. When he tried to describe what had
happened to his editor, or to his partner Amara, he would begin clearly enough and then lose the
thread entirely, the account dissolving before he could finish it, leaving him frustrated and
ashamed. Sometimes, without warning, he would hear sounds in his head — not imagined but recalled
with a physical precision, the specific cadence of footsteps that had moved behind him while he was
restrained.

Those footsteps became a particular torment. On the street, in the corridor of the newspaper
building where he worked, the sound of someone walking behind him could stop him mid-stride. He had
developed the habit of turning to look, checking, confirming, and then checking again. He began
wearing looser, darker clothing — not a conscious decision at first, but a gradual drift toward
garments that he felt made him less visible, less legible as a target. He stopped going to
neighbourhoods he didn't know intimately, reasoning that in unfamiliar places he could not read the
exits, could not assess what might be coming. What frightened him most was the possibility that his
danger was not behind him but ahead of him, already waiting in some space he hadn't yet learned to
map.

Kofi had come to believe that the person he had been before the detention — curious, driven, someone
who had chosen journalism precisely because he moved toward difficult truths — was no longer
accessible to him. He was certain he was no longer capable of the work that had defined him.
Assignments that required him to attend public events or enter unfamiliar buildings felt impossible
rather than merely difficult. He had stopped attending the community vigils he used to cover,
telling himself it was a scheduling conflict, knowing it was not. He avoided anything that might
stir feeling in either direction — not just grief or fear, but even the small pleasures that had
once punctuated his days — because he had learned, in some wordless way, that feeling anything was
the beginning of feeling everything. Amara had noticed. She told him, quietly, that she felt she was
living with someone who was trying very hard to be absent. He did not disagree.

A colleague whose work Kofi had long admired was killed in a road accident two months ago, and the
grief Kofi could not allow himself to approach sat like a stone he kept walking around. It was, in
the end, a practical crisis that brought him to therapy: he had been assigned a long-form
investigation that required fieldwork in an unfamiliar part of the city, and he had sat in his car
outside the first address for forty minutes before driving home. He told his therapist that he used
to be a happy person, and that he would like to understand whether that person still existed
somewhere, or whether he was simply gone.
