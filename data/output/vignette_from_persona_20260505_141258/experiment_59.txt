Aroha Tane is a 21-year-old New Zealand Māori woman who grew up in a close-knit family in Auckland
before emigrating to the United States at eighteen to pursue nursing. Driven by a sense of duty that
her whānau had always described as her defining quality, she enrolled in an accelerated nursing
programme and, in her second year, accepted a placement with a military medical unit supporting
forward operating personnel overseas. She had wanted, she later told her intake clinician, to do
something that mattered. What she encountered during a seven-month deployment changed the
architecture of her inner life in ways she is still struggling to name.

The incident that organises her current presentation occurred during a mass casualty event at a
field medical station. A coordinated attack brought in wave after wave of critically injured
soldiers over the course of several hours. Aroha was working triage when an explosion detonated
close enough to shake the structure. The concussive blast was followed immediately by the shriek of
emergency alert sirens, and in the chaos that followed she worked over a soldier whose chest wound
was beyond saving. She performed compressions until her arms gave out, feeling his heart stop
beneath her palms, and she remembers with terrible precision the moment her own pulse became
indistinguishable from the effort — hammering so hard in her chest that she could not tell where her
body ended and his began. The sirens did not stop for nearly forty minutes.

She returned to the United States eight months ago and took a position in the emergency department
of a mid-sized urban hospital, believing that staying busy and staying useful would hold things
together. For a while, the logic seemed to hold. Then the symptoms found their footing.

In the ED, ambulances arrive with sirens still cycling down, and the sound moves through Aroha
before she can prepare for it. On two occasions she has found herself crouched behind a supply cart,
certain in her body if not entirely in her mind that the attack was resuming — that the noise meant
incoming, that she needed to get low. Her colleagues have noticed. Her charge nurse has asked,
carefully, whether she is sleeping. She has said yes. The more disruptive pattern, though, is the
one that surfaces when she is simply standing at a patient's bedside and her own heart rate climbs
during a difficult procedure. The racing of her pulse has become a cue she cannot override. What
frightened her most, she explained haltingly in her first session, was that she couldn't always tell
whether the fear was a memory or a fact.

Outside work, Aroha has quietly dismantled the parts of her life that once carried joy. She stopped
going to the gym months ago, because the elevated heart rate she felt on the treadmill was one she
had come to associate with something she did not want to revisit. She has declined birthday
gatherings and a trip her partner, Daniel, planned to celebrate their anniversary. She had come to
believe, in a way she could not fully articulate, that allowing herself to feel good was a kind of
exposure she could not afford — as though happiness had become its own threat, a state too open, too
unguarded. Daniel has grown quieter around her. She has noticed him choosing his words carefully,
and she understands, without being able to say it aloud, that the distance between them is widening.

When the memory of that night surfaces — and it surfaces often, without warning, with a vividness
that collapses time — Aroha does not feel grief. She feels rage so complete it frightens her, and
then an overwhelm so total that she has to put down whatever she is holding and leave the room. She
knows what happened. She was there for every second of it. But when her clinician asked her to
describe it, she sat for a long time looking at her hands. She knows what happened, she said
finally. She just cannot find the words.

What brought her in was a near-miss: she snapped at a junior nurse in front of a patient's family,
loudly and without proportion, and walked off the floor. She sat in the stairwell for twenty
minutes, scanning the door. She is afraid, she says, that she is becoming someone she does not
recognise. She hopes, more than anything, that she is not.
