Kofi Mensah is a 37-year-old Ghanaian-American psychologist who presents to therapy following what
he describes, with visible difficulty, as "something I've spent two years trying to outthink." He is
single, soft-spoken, and carries himself with the careful composure of someone accustomed to being
the most regulated person in the room. That composure, he acknowledges, has become harder to
maintain.

Two years ago, Kofi attended a professional conference in a mid-sized city several states from his
home. After an evening social event, a colleague he had known for several years offered to continue
a conversation in his hotel room. What followed was a rape. The door was locked from the inside —
Kofi remembers the sound of the deadbolt, the way the room seemed to contract around him, the
realisation that his professional familiarity with this man had given him no protection at all. He
did not disclose to conference staff. He drove home two days later and returned to his caseload the
following Monday.

In the months after, the memory did not organise itself into a coherent past event. Instead it
arrived in fragments — sensory, sudden, and without warning. The click of a lock. A colleague's
offhand remark during a case consultation about a client's sexual assault. These moments would
rupture the ordinary texture of his day without transition or preparation. He found himself unable
to place the memory as something that had already ended.

Kofi's work as a psychologist means he sits with clients for fifty-minute hours in small, closed
offices. He began requesting that consultation rooms be left slightly ajar, offering clinical
rationales his supervisors accepted without question. When a building policy required doors to be
shut during sessions, he found himself conducting the hour with his chair angled toward the exit,
his back pressed to the wall behind him, scanning the room in a way he recognised clinically but
could not interrupt. He has not been able to fall asleep before two or three in the morning for the
better part of eighteen months, lying in the particular alertness of someone who does not believe
the night is finished with them yet. Even on weekends, in his own apartment, with no appointment to
keep, the loosening that other people called rest did not come.

He had also begun to reorganise his professional and social life around the fact of other men. He no
longer accepted one-on-one supervision from male colleagues, citing scheduling conflicts. He
declined invitations to small gatherings if he could not be certain of the composition of the room.
When these arrangements failed — when he found himself alone with a male client, or walking to his
car with a male coworker — something in him registered the situation as structurally identical to
the hotel room, regardless of what he knew.

What has accumulated alongside these adjustments is a set of beliefs about himself that Kofi finds,
professionally, he cannot simply reframe away. He had come to believe, with a quiet and terrible
certainty, that what happened to him was somehow visible — that colleagues who looked at him a beat
too long were seeing it, that clients sensed something compromised in him, that the professional
authority he had spent a decade building was now a performance others could see through. He was
certain that bad things would continue to find him, that his history had marked him as someone
without the capacity to prevent his own harm. When he tried to imagine his life five or ten years
forward — a question he routinely asked his own clients — he found nothing there. Not grief, not
fear of a specific outcome. Simply an absence.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was a session three weeks ago in which a client disclosed a
recent sexual assault. He sat with her for the remainder of the hour, reflected her experience back
with skill, made the appropriate safety referrals. Afterward, alone in his office with the door
open, he understood that he could not continue to offer to others what he was not able to reach for
himself. He arrives now hoping, tentatively, that the absence where his future should be might not
be permanent. What he fears most is that a therapist will look at him and see exactly what he sees
when he looks in the mirror.
