Rangi Parata is a 46-year-old widowed Māori man who works as an auditor for a mid-sized accounting
firm in Auckland, though he relocated to the Pacific Northwest of the United States several years
ago following his wife's death. He presented for psychological assessment after his supervisor noted
a marked deterioration in his work performance over the preceding eight months, and after a close
colleague expressed concern that Rangi had become almost entirely unreachable outside of structured
work hours.

Eighteen months ago, Rangi was visiting relatives on a small island community when a severe tropical
cyclone made landfall with little warning. He had been staying in a low-lying coastal home with
several family members when the storm surge came — not as a dramatic wave, but as a silent,
relentless rise of dark water that crept under the doors and climbed the walls in the middle of the
night. He described waking in complete darkness to the sound of nothing: an absence of the usual
night insects, the ocean, any wind. That silence lasted perhaps forty seconds before the water
reached the bed frame. By the time he and his relatives reached the roof, the water was at chest
height inside the house. The smell of churned seawater, mud, and what he later understood was sewage
from ruptured pipes saturated everything. At one point, in the darkness, he swallowed a mouthful of
the floodwater. They remained on the roof for nearly six hours until rescue came at dawn. Two
community members did not survive. Rangi was physically unharmed.

In the months that followed, the memory of that night returned to him not as a coherent story but as
sensory fragments — the specific mineral-rot taste of the floodwater arriving without warning, the
smell of disturbed sediment appearing when he opened a drain or walked past a river, so vivid and
unbidden that he would stop mid-stride. He had come to believe that the memory was so extreme, so
outside the range of anything that should happen to a person, that it sometimes felt as though it
had happened to someone else entirely — a story he had read rather than lived. This unreality
offered no comfort. If anything, it deepened his sense that something fundamental had broken in him.

He had come to feel that happiness itself was a warning sign. When he noticed himself relaxed —
watching television, finishing a meal, sitting quietly on his porch — a cold alertness would move
through him, a certainty that ease was the condition that preceded catastrophe. He had stopped
allowing himself to settle. He smoked steadily throughout the day, a habit that had resumed after
fifteen years of abstinence. At work, where he spent long hours reviewing financial records in a
quiet open-plan office, unusual silences between colleagues made him look up and scan the room. When
a report took longer to load than expected, or when a colleague didn't respond to an email within
what he considered a reasonable window, he found himself certain that something had gone wrong — not
with the computer or the schedule, but in the world outside. He had declined every invitation to
visit new client sites, telling his supervisor he preferred to work remotely, because he was certain
that unfamiliar buildings and streets offered dangers he could not yet see or calculate.

He had not spoken to any of his relatives about the cyclone or its aftermath, and he deflected
whenever friends raised it. He had stopped visiting the waterfront neighbourhood where he initially
lived after relocating, and he kept his apartment curtains drawn after dark. Waking in the night to
a dark room would bring the floodwater back so completely — taste, smell, the weightless terror —
that he would sit on the edge of his bed until morning.

What Rangi expressed most quietly, almost as an aside, was that he felt dead inside — that the grief
and the fear had not so much overwhelmed him as hollowed him out. He was certain that bad things
would continue to find him regardless of what he did. He came to therapy not because he believed it
would help, but because his supervisor had made it a condition of continued employment, and because,
as he put it, he had nothing left to lose by trying.
