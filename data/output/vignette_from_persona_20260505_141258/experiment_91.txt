flying fighter aircraft, a career built on precision, composure, and an almost constitutional belief
that skill and discipline could hold catastrophe at bay. That belief was dismantled in a single
afternoon eighteen months ago, when a catastrophic hydraulic failure during a training sortie sent
his aircraft into an uncontrolled descent at low altitude. The sequence unfolded with a speed that
outpaced any procedural response: a sudden violent shudder through the airframe, warning tones
stacking on top of one another in the cockpit, and then an explosion from somewhere aft of the
fuselage — a percussive concussive blast that he felt in his chest and teeth before he consciously
registered hearing it. The ejection seat fired. What followed was a period of violent disorientation
— spinning, pressure, the world tilting without reference — before he lost consciousness briefly and
came to on the ground with a fractured vertebra, a ruptured eardrum, and the aircraft burning in a
field three hundred metres away.

In the weeks after the accident, Dmitri returned to his family home and attempted to resume
something resembling his former life. He told his wife he was fine. He believed, for a time, that he
meant it. But the body kept a different account. He noticed that unexpected movement at the
periphery of his vision — a technician stepping quickly around a corner, his wife reaching suddenly
across the table — produced an immediate and involuntary physical response: muscles bracing, breath
catching, hands moving before thought could intervene. He found himself scanning rooms when he
entered them, tracking exits, noting what was overhead and what might fall. He had come to believe,
in a way he could not fully articulate, that the world had revealed itself to be structurally
unreliable — that the orderly, skill-governed universe he had inhabited as a pilot no longer
existed, and that something dangerous could emerge from any ordinary moment without warning.

The triggers that reached him most reliably were physical ones. A sudden bang — a car backfiring on
the base road, a door slamming in the officers' mess — could stop him mid-stride. More disturbing to
him was what happened in his own body: episodes of dizziness or lightheadedness, which arose
unpredictably, would flood him with a sensory recognition he could not suppress. The spinning, the
loss of horizon, the feeling of a world that would not hold still — these sensations carried him
back to the ejection with an immediacy that bore no relationship to ordinary memory. On days when he
felt physically unwell for any reason, the effect was similar: a diffuse, nauseating sense that
something was already going wrong, that the body could not be trusted.

He had, over the months, quietly reorganised his life around these vulnerabilities. He declined a
mandatory flight medical, citing a scheduling conflict he never resolved, and had not set foot in
the base medical centre since his discharge from hospital. He had stopped attending the informal
social evenings he and his wife had shared with other pilot families for years, and had withdrawn
from a weekly hiking group he had joined before the accident. What frightened him most about these
activities was not their content but their unpredictability — the sense that enjoyment required a
kind of openness to experience that now felt dangerous. He had also begun controlling his food
intake with an unusual rigidity, eating the same small meals at the same times each day. He could
not fully explain this to himself, but the discipline of it — the one domain in which outcomes
remained exactly as anticipated — offered something that felt necessary.

His wife noticed the changes before he acknowledged them. She described a man who was physically
present but somehow absent, and her word — "hollow" — lodged in him because it matched something he
already knew. He felt dead inside in a way that no amount of effort seemed to reach. It was she who
finally suggested he speak to someone, and it was the prospect of losing her trust, more than any
symptom, that brought him to the intake appointment. What he most fears, sitting across from the
clinician now, is that nothing of who he was before that afternoon can be recovered. What he most
hopes, though he has not yet said it aloud, is to be proved wrong.
