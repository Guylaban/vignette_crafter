deployment in the early 1990s, when he served as a crew member aboard a military transport
helicopter operating in a conflict zone in the Pacific theatre.

deepest part of the night had passed — that particular grey, suspended hour when the sky is neither
dark nor light. Rangi's helicopter had come under fire during what was meant to be a routine
extraction. In the chaos that followed, he found himself pressing his hands against the torn flight
suit of a fellow crew member, trying to hold pressure against a wound. He remembers the texture of
that fabric — a rough, shredded weave soaked through — with a precision that has never dimmed. The
man died before they reached the ground. Rangi was twenty-four years old.

In the months after his discharge, he completed his civilian pilot training and built what appeared
from the outside to be an admirable life. He married, raised children, and accumulated tens of
thousands of flight hours without incident. But certain things accumulated too. He noticed that his
body responded to the sound of other aircraft — particularly helicopters — in ways he could not
explain and did not discuss. Working out of a major hub airport, he encounters helicopter traffic
regularly, and there are moments on the flight deck when a rotor sound cutting through the radio
frequency or drifting up from the tarmac below will snap him somewhere else entirely, without
transition, without warning. He has learned to grip the yoke and breathe and wait for it to pass,
but he has never learned why it keeps happening. What frightened him most was not the memory itself
but its arrival — sudden, total, and ungoverned.

irregular sleep. He does not name it as vigilance, but his wife has long known not to move quickly
in the dark near him. Sudden movements from other people — a colleague reaching across him in the
cockpit, a passenger rising unexpectedly from a seat during boarding — produce in him an immediate,
full-body alertness that he has come to regard as simply part of who he is now. Whenever he enters a
building, his eyes move to the exits before they move to anything else. He does this in hotel
lobbies, in crew briefing rooms, in his own children's homes. He has come to believe that the world
is genuinely dangerous in a way that others around him seem unable to perceive, and that this
perception is not a symptom but a form of hard-won knowledge.

What troubles him most deeply is something harder to articulate. When he tries to think about the
morning of the extraction, the memory feels as though it belongs to a different person entirely — a
young man he can observe but not fully inhabit. And yet when the memory comes without his choosing,
it brings the full terror of that moment as though no time has passed at all. He has come to believe
that there is something fundamentally wrong with him, and that the man his wife married — the one
who was, by his own account, genuinely happy — no longer exists.

What finally brought Rangi to seek help was his upcoming mandatory retirement at sixty-five. For
decades, the structure and identity of flying had contained something he could not name. Facing its
loss, he found he could no longer afford not to know what he was carrying.
