Rajan Mehta is a 61-year-old South Asian American man who has worked for a community development
non-profit for nearly two decades. He is married, describes his relationship as close, and until
roughly fourteen months ago considered himself a person of steady temperament — patient by nature,
unhurried in his habits. That self-understanding changed on a wet Tuesday evening when, driving home
from a grant-writing session that had run late, his sedan was struck on the driver's side by a
pickup truck that ran a red light at approximately forty miles per hour. The impact spun his car
across two lanes. What Rajan remembers most vividly in the aftermath is not the sound of the
collision — though the sound was enormous — but the sensation of the deployed airbag pressing
against his left hand and forearm, a dense, hot, powdery fabric that seemed to hold him in place
while the world outside his windshield continued to move. He was conscious throughout, and he
watched, through the cracked glass, a second car brake hard and clip the rear quarter of the truck,
crumpling its hood into a shape he has not been able to forget.

He was discharged from the emergency department that same night with bruised ribs and a laceration
on his forearm. Physically, his recovery was complete within six weeks. What did not recover was his
sense of the world as a predictable place. In the weeks following the crash, Rajan found that
memories of the event returned not as narrative recollections but as images — the folded hood of the
truck, the particular angle of the streetlight through the fractured windshield — vivid and static,
like photographs he had not chosen to take. When those images surfaced, he did not experience them
as memories of something past. He felt, with full conviction, that the collision was occurring in
the present moment, his body responding with the same terror it had produced on the wet Tuesday
evening itself.

He began noticing that certain sounds — a door slamming in the next office, a cart rolling
unexpectedly across a hard floor — would pull him upright in his chair, heart accelerating, before
he had any conscious thought about what the sound was. Colleagues who walked quickly past his desk,
entering his peripheral vision without warning, produced the same involuntary response. Rajan was
embarrassed by these reactions and said nothing about them, attributing them privately to stress and
age, though he had come to believe, with a quiet certainty he rarely voiced, that the world around
him had become genuinely less safe than it had previously been — that danger now arrived without
warning and that his body, at least, understood this even when his mind tried to argue otherwise.

At work, which requires him to attend community meetings and occasionally visit partner
organisations across the city, the difficulty became concrete and unavoidable. The non-profit's
offices are located on a busy urban corridor, and the parking structure adjacent to the building
means that Rajan passes crumpled or dented vehicles nearly every time he arrives or leaves. Each one
arrests him. What frightened him most was not the sight itself but what the sight did to him — the
way it collapsed the distance between then and now, so that standing in a parking structure in the
present felt indistinguishable from sitting in a car that was being destroyed. He stopped attending
two off-site meetings, offering scheduling conflicts as explanation, because the routes required
highway driving. His supervisor noticed the pattern without understanding it.

Conversations about road accidents, which arise naturally in a city where colleagues commute long
distances, became something he managed carefully — redirecting, excusing himself, finding reasons to
be elsewhere. And there was the texture. A canvas tote bag that a colleague kept on the shared
conference table had a dense, coarse weave that, when Rajan's hand brushed it accidentally one
afternoon, produced a visceral jolt he could not explain to anyone in the room. He set the bag aside
and said nothing.

It was his wife who finally named what she had been watching for over a year — a man who had grown
smaller inside his own life — and who sat with him one evening and asked him, gently, to consider
speaking with someone. Rajan arrived at his first appointment hoping, above all, that he might be
told his mind was not broken. What he feared most was being told it was.
