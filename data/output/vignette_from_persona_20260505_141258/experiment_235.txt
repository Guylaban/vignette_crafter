Kwame Asante is a 50-year-old Ghanaian-American plumber, married for twenty-two years, who presents
following a violent assault that occurred approximately eight months ago. He was working alone on a
late-afternoon job in a vacant commercial property when two men entered the building, demanded his
tools and wallet, and when he hesitated — not from defiance, he would later try to explain, but from
sheer disbelief — beat him severely. They struck him repeatedly, leaving him on the concrete floor
with broken ribs, a fractured orbital socket, and a laceration along his jaw that required seventeen
stitches. He lay there for nearly two hours before a passing contractor heard him and called for
help. No arrests have been made.

In the weeks that followed the assault, Kwame's wife noticed that he had become quieter in a way
that felt different from his usual steadiness. He returned to work after six weeks, cleared by his
physician, but something had not healed along with his ribs. What he carried from that vacant
building was not easily named — and in fact, Kwame himself cannot find language for the central
experience. When asked directly about the assault in his intake session, he went silent for a long
moment, then said only, "There are pictures. I see them. I can't make them into a story." What comes
to him are images: the shadow of a raised arm, the grain of the concrete floor inches from his face,
the particular quality of the late-afternoon light through a dirty window. These arrive without
warning and resist being assembled into a coherent account he can tell himself or anyone else. When
they surface, what accompanies them is not fear but a crushing guilt — he had hesitated, and he had
come to believe that hesitation was a form of failure, that a different man, the man he thought he
was, would have responded differently, would have protected himself, would not be lying on a floor.

That belief has quietly reorganized his life. Kwame has spent thirty years building a reputation as
someone who could handle anything a job site threw at him — tight crawl spaces, hostile contractors,
complicated systems that defeated other tradesmen. He was certain that competence was not just what
he did but what he was. Now he is no longer sure. He misses deadlines he would once have met easily.
He finds himself double-checking pipe fittings he could have done in his sleep, then checking them
again, then standing in a client's utility room wondering if he has missed something catastrophic.
He has turned down two contracts in the past three months — telling his wife they weren't worth the
money — because they required working alone in large, unfamiliar buildings. He feels safe only at
home, and to a lesser degree in his truck, and nowhere else with any reliability.

On job sites, any sudden movement in his peripheral vision — a co-worker stepping around a corner, a
door swinging open — produces an immediate, full-body reaction: he flinches hard, sometimes drops
tools, and has on one occasion shouted at an apprentice who appeared unexpectedly in a hallway. He
apologized, but the young man has kept his distance since, and Kwame is aware that his standing
among the crew has shifted. At night, sleep comes reluctantly and does not stay. He lies beside his
wife in a house he has owned for fifteen years and cannot feel that it fully belongs to the present.

What sits beneath all of this, and what he returns to again and again, is the matter of justice. He
had done nothing wrong. The men who hurt him have faced no consequence. He had filed a police
report, followed up twice, and been told, politely, that there were no leads. He is not a man given
to bitterness, but he has come to feel that something is fundamentally broken in the order of things
— that what happened to him was not only violent but wrong in a way that has gone unacknowledged by
the world.

It was his wife who finally insisted he speak to someone, after finding him sitting in his truck in
the driveway at midnight, unable to explain why he had not come inside. He agreed, though what he
fears most is the confirmation of what he already suspects — that the man who walked into that
building eight months ago is not coming back.
