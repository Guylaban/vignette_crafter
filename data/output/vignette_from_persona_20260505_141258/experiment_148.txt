Arjun Mehta is a 35-year-old South Asian American tax consultant who lives with his wife in a quiet
suburb of Chicago. He is soft-spoken, meticulous by professional habit, and until three years ago he
had spent his weekends volunteering with an international relief organization. It was through that
organization that he was deployed, for what was meant to be a six-week rotation, to a conflict zone
in the Middle East where his team was tasked with distributing food and medical supplies to
displaced families in a partially destroyed urban corridor.

The event that brought him to therapy occurred during his fourth week of deployment. His team had
established a temporary distribution point inside a damaged school building. It was a morning Arjun
remembers in fragments — the particular quality of the light through broken windows, the sound of
children's voices outside, and then the sudden, rolling pressure of an explosion from a nearby
strike that set fire to a supply vehicle parked at the building's entrance. In the chaos that
followed, Arjun was separated from two local volunteers he had been working alongside. He found one
of them badly injured and held the man's shoulders while others tried to help, his own body pressed
low against the rubble-strewn floor. The other volunteer did not survive. Arjun has never been able
to reconstruct in what order things happened — whether the fire came before the screaming, or
whether he had heard the second man call out before the smoke thickened and made it impossible to
move toward him. That gap in sequence has never closed. What he does know, with a certainty that
sits in his chest like something calcified, is that he should have done more. He was the senior
relief worker present. He had the training. He had failed.

He returned to the United States six weeks later. For the first several months he told himself the
difficulty sleeping, the jumpiness, the sense of unreality, were simply adjustment. He returned to
his work as a tax consultant, where he sits in a glass-fronted office in a shared professional
building, reviewing spreadsheets and meeting clients. But the ordinary sounds of that environment —
a door slamming in the corridor, a colleague raising their voice in frustration down the hall —
began to produce in Arjun a startle response so pronounced that he would grip the edge of his desk
and have to wait for his breathing to settle before he could continue. Even a colleague expressing
mild irritation during a meeting became something Arjun could not tolerate. He had come to believe,
without being able to fully articulate why, that anger in another person's voice was a signal that
something catastrophic was about to follow.

At night he found it harder to feel safe than during the day, often lying awake while his wife
slept, watching the ceiling. When memories surfaced — and they came without warning, in no reliable
order, the smoke, the weight of the man's shoulders, the light through the windows — he would find
himself curled slightly forward, shoulders drawn in, body arranged as it had been on that floor,
without having chosen to move that way. These moments left him flooded with shame so acute it felt
physical.

birthday party, and then a dinner his wife had been looking forward to for weeks. Situations that
might have produced happiness or hope had become, to Arjun, something close to intolerable — as
though he had forfeited the right to them, or as though feeling them would constitute a kind of lie
about who he now was. He has not visited a cemetery since a family friend's funeral the previous
year, where the smell of burning incense triggered a response so overwhelming he had to leave. He
has avoided driving past the neighborhood where his relief organization's local office is located.

and a moment last month when he covered his face with both hands during a routine client call
because a car alarm outside sounded too sudden, too close — that finally brought him in. What he is
most afraid of, he said at intake, is that the man he was before no longer exists. What he hopes,
though he holds the hope carefully, is that he might be wrong about that.
