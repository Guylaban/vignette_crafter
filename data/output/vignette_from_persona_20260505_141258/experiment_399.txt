Kiet Phommachanh is a 35-year-old Lao-American accountant who presented for an initial intake
following a referral from his primary care physician. He is currently divorced and lives alone in a
one-bedroom apartment. His PCL-5 score of 57 places him well within the severe range, and across
several sessions he has described an experience of captivity and systematic torture that occurred
approximately four years ago while he was visiting relatives in Southeast Asia. He was detained
without formal charge, held for eleven days, and subjected to repeated physical and psychological
abuse by men whose institutional affiliation was never made clear to him. During those eleven days
he was hooded during transport, beaten across the back and legs, deprived of sleep, and interrogated
in sessions that blurred together until he could no longer reliably distinguish one day from the
next. He was eventually released without explanation and flew back to the United States within the
week.

In the months that followed, Kiet found that his memory of the detention did not settle into
anything he could hold steadily. He describes the recollections as fragmentary and out of sequence —
he cannot reconstruct a coherent timeline of what happened on which day, and certain episodes
surface without any clear placement in the whole. More disorienting still, he often experiences the
memories as though they belong to another person entirely, as though he is reviewing footage of
someone else's ordeal rather than recalling something that happened to his own body. Yet at other
moments the memories arrive with the opposite quality: sudden, unbidden, and total — a film that
simply begins playing, pulling him out of whatever he is doing. He has been sitting at his desk
reviewing a quarterly report when the smell of sweat and concrete fills his awareness and he is
briefly, completely elsewhere.

The work environment has become one of the more complicated terrains in his daily life. Accounting
requires him to interact with clients he has never met — new vendors, auditors from outside firms,
representatives calling to verify figures. Each encounter with a stranger carries a weight that Kiet
finds difficult to explain to colleagues. He has come to believe, with a certainty that feels
irrefutable to him, that there is no reliable way to know who intends harm. The men who detained him
gave no warning. They looked, at first, like officials. Accordingly, Kiet now screens calls, delays
responses to new clients, and has asked his supervisor to assign him accounts that require minimal
outside contact. Two colleagues have noticed the change, and his last performance review noted that
he had become "less client-facing," a phrase that landed on him with quiet shame.

Conversations about the future have also become unexpectedly difficult. When his ex-wife, during
divorce proceedings, asked him what he wanted his life to look like in five years, he found he had
no answer and no access to one. Planning, projecting, imagining — these feel foreclosed. What
frightens him most in these moments is not sadness but a kind of blankness, as though the future is
simply a room he is not permitted to enter. He has come to believe that the person who might have
inhabited that future is gone, replaced by someone lesser and permanently altered. He does not say
this with self-pity but with a flat conviction: he was changed in those eleven days in ways that
cannot be undone.

Alongside this sits a corrosive belief that he has worked hard not to examine directly. He is
certain, at some level, that a different man — someone more capable, more alert, more decisive —
would have found a way to stop what happened or escape sooner. He has never shared this with anyone.
His divorce, which was finalised eighteen months after his return, unfolded against the backdrop of
his increasing withdrawal, and he describes the marriage's end with the same flat tone he uses for
most things now.

What brought Kiet to seek help was a moment at work three weeks ago when he stood in the office
corridor unable to enter a meeting room where a new client was waiting, his heart loud in his chest,
and understood that he could not continue managing this alone. He is not sure what he hopes for. He
says, carefully, that he would like to be able to do his job.
