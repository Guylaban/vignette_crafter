Linh Nguyen is a 19-year-old Vietnamese-American hairdresser who presented for an initial
psychological assessment approximately eight months after being involved in a serious road traffic
incident. She lives with her boyfriend in a small apartment and, until recently, had described
herself as someone who enjoyed driving, valued her independence, and took pride in being reliable
and careful. That self-image has not survived what happened on a Tuesday evening in early spring,
when Linh was at the wheel and a moment of misjudgement at an intersection resulted in her vehicle
striking a pedestrian — a middle-aged man who sustained a fractured pelvis, broken ribs, and a
severe head injury that required weeks of hospitalisation. He survived, but Linh learned through a
family member that he continues to experience lasting neurological effects. She was not charged
criminally; the investigation concluded the visibility conditions were poor and her speed was within
limits. None of that has mattered to her.

In the weeks that followed the collision, Linh stopped driving entirely. She told herself it was
temporary — just until she felt steadier — but eight months later she has not returned to the
driver's seat, and she refuses to ride as a passenger unless there is no alternative. When her
boyfriend drives her to work, she sits rigid in the passenger seat, hands pressed flat against her
thighs, eyes fixed on every pedestrian near a crossing. The salon where she works is on a busy urban
street, and each morning she passes the same kind of intersection where the accident occurred. She
has begun taking a longer walking route to avoid it, adding fifteen minutes to her commute each way.
Her colleagues have noticed that she flinches visibly when a car brakes hard outside the salon
window, and on two occasions the sharp metallic crash of a dropped styling tray sent her stumbling
backward, her heart hammering, her hands shaking so badly she had to excuse herself to the back
room. She has not told her coworkers why.

What frightens Linh most, in a way she struggles to articulate, is that she no longer trusts herself
to act correctly in the world. She had believed she was a careful person — attentive, responsible,
someone who looked out for others. The accident dismantled that belief entirely. She has come to
feel that her body itself is unreliable: her hands that held the steering wheel, her eyes that
failed to see in time, her reflexes that were not fast enough. At work, this manifests as a
compulsive second-checking of her movements — confirming the heat of a styling iron before she
brings it near a client's scalp, pausing mid-sentence to reassure herself she has not made an error.
When a client expresses even mild dissatisfaction, Linh becomes immediately, almost desperately
accommodating, agreeing with complaints she privately believes are unfair, offering refunds she
cannot afford, unable to hold any boundary that might produce conflict. She cannot explain this to
herself clearly, but there is a felt logic to it: she has already hurt someone badly; she cannot
risk being the cause of harm again.

Her memories of the collision are predominantly visual — the shape of the man's body against the
hood of her car, the particular angle at which he fell. Sometimes these images arrive without any
feeling attached, and she describes those moments with a flat, distant quality, as though recounting
something she watched happen to someone else. At other times, without warning, the grief arrives in
full — a wave of it that leaves her unable to speak. She has begun quietly avoiding the date of the
accident each month, staying home, keeping her phone off, not entirely sure what she is afraid will
happen if she does not.

Linh entered the waiting room for her first appointment having already checked the location of both
exits. She sat facing the door. She said she had come because her boyfriend had gently told her,
more than once, that he was worried — and because she had noticed, with some alarm, that she no
longer felt she could rely on anyone around her to keep her safe, including herself. What she hopes
for, she said quietly, is to stop feeling like a danger to the people she loves. What she fears most
is that the therapist will agree with her.
