Kiet Phanthavong is a 35-year-old Laotian-American UX designer who lives with his wife in a mid-
sized city in the Pacific Northwest. He was referred for psychological assessment following a
reluctant disclosure to his primary care physician — made only because his wife had quietly asked
the doctor to ask — that he had not been sleeping well and had been "off" since his hospitalisation
two years prior. By the time Kiet sat across from a therapist for the first time, his PCL-5 score of
73 placed him in the severe range, though he was visibly uncomfortable with the idea that a physical
illness could leave the kind of mark that warranted a mental health referral. He had come, he said,
mostly to reassure his wife.

Two years earlier, Kiet had been admitted to the ICU after a previously undetected cardiac
arrhythmia triggered a sudden collapse at his desk during a remote work call. He had been alone in
the apartment at the time. He regained partial consciousness on the floor to find his vision
narrowing, his chest seizing in a way he could not distinguish from dying, and no certainty
whatsoever about whether help was coming. The paramedics arrived within minutes, but those minutes —
the cold floor, the sound of his own laboured breathing, the absolute absence of control — had not
resolved themselves into a past event. They had settled into him.

In the weeks following his discharge, Kiet found that the sight of an ambulance on his commute could
drop him back onto that floor without warning. He began taking longer routes to avoid the hospital
district, rationalising this as a preference for less congested streets. When his cardiologist
scheduled a six-week follow-up, Kiet cancelled and did not reschedule. He told himself he felt fine.
What he could not tell himself — because it was too large and too shapeless — was that walking back
through those automatic doors felt like confirmation of something he was not ready to confirm. He
has not had a physical examination since discharge.

At work, where Kiet spends long hours building user interfaces that are meant to feel intuitive and
frictionless, the environment has become its own kind of minefield. When a design file takes longer
than usual to load, or when a client delays feedback beyond the agreed window, something in him
shifts immediately into a register that has nothing to do with the task at hand. He had come to
believe, in a way that felt utterly rational to him, that delays meant something had gone wrong —
that the pause before a response was the pause before bad news. He would refresh the screen, check
his email, message the client, and be unable to explain to his colleagues why he seemed so agitated
over a slow server. Even when his project lead assured him that the timeline was fine, Kiet found it
impossible to absorb that reassurance. Safety, as others described it, felt like a story told about
someone else's life.

His body remained in a state of low-grade readiness that exhausted him. He had stopped going to the
gym, where the elevation of his heart rate now frightened him in a way he could not fully articulate
but would not risk. He and his wife had stopped hiking, something they had done most weekends before
his illness. She had not complained. He was grateful for that, and also aware that her patience was
its own kind of weight.

What concerned Kiet most, and what he found hardest to say aloud, was that he no longer felt like
himself in any way he could point to. He described feeling hollow in the sessions — present in rooms
but not quite in them, going through the motions of a life that used to feel like his. He had come
to believe there was something fundamentally broken in him, not medically but essentially, and that
his inability to simply move on from an illness that had been treated and resolved was evidence of a
weakness he found humiliating. He had survived. He believed he should be fine. The gap between those
two facts was where he lived now, and what finally brought him to treatment was the quiet fear,
shared with no one until now, that the gap was getting wider.
