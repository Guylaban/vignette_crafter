Tariq Mansour is a forty-year-old Lebanese-American software engineer who presents for an initial
psychological evaluation approximately fourteen months after surviving a catastrophic tornado that
struck the suburban Tennessee neighborhood where he lives with his wife. He reports a PCL-5 score of
37, consistent with clinically significant post-traumatic stress, and describes a life that has
narrowed considerably since the storm.

The tornado made landfall on a Tuesday afternoon in early spring. Tariq was working from home,
seated at his desk in the study, when the sky outside his window turned a color he later struggled
to describe. The warning sirens had sounded only seconds before the structure began to shake. What
followed was perhaps forty seconds of total sensory obliteration: the freight-train roar that seemed
to come from inside the walls, the concussive pressure change that popped his ears, the sound of
glass exploding inward, and then the particular crack of the roof separating from the house above
him. He was thrown from his chair and found himself pinned beneath a collapsed bookcase as the storm
passed. He lay there in silence broken only by settling debris and a distant car alarm, unable to
fully reconstruct, even now, the exact sequence of what happened between the moment the sirens
sounded and the moment he found himself on the floor. The memories arrive not as a coherent story
but as fragments — the green light, the roar, a sensation of weightlessness, cold air on his face —
and when they surface, they do not feel like recollections. They feel like the present moment. He
can hear the crack of the roof as clearly as if he were still beneath it.

In the weeks after the storm, Tariq returned to remote work quickly, telling his wife and colleagues
that staying busy was the healthiest thing he could do. He made a deliberate practice of redirecting
his thoughts toward gratitude — the house was repairable, no one had died, he still had his job. He
repeated these facts to himself with something close to discipline. What he could not explain, and
did not share with anyone, was that this effort of positive redirection required nearly constant
vigilance and left him exhausted by midday.

His work as a software engineer means he spends long hours at a standing desk in a rebuilt home
office, and it is here that the physical residue of the tornado is most present. A colleague joining
a video call and leaning suddenly toward the camera, a coworker's hand landing on his shoulder
during an in-person sprint review at the office — these moments produce a sharp, full-body alarm
response that he finds deeply embarrassing. He has begun positioning himself near the exit in
conference rooms before any meeting begins, scanning the layout before he can settle. He tells
himself this is practical. What frightens him most is the private conviction that he would not be
fast enough if something happened again, and that this is, in some fundamental way, his own fault.
Someone more capable, he has come to believe, would not have been caught unprepared in their own
home.

He has not been to a doctor since the storm, canceling two follow-up appointments for a minor hand
laceration he sustained that day. The sight of an ambulance on the highway causes him to change
lanes and, if possible, take an alternate route. He cannot fully articulate why, only that proximity
to medical settings feels like an acknowledgment he is not ready to make.

His wife has noticed that he is different — quieter, quicker to irritate, and absent in a way she
cannot name. He knows she has noticed. He finds himself spending long mental hours rehearsing
grievances toward the local emergency management system, turning over questions of negligence and
accountability that have no practical outlet. He is aware these thoughts are unproductive. He cannot
stop them.

What finally brought Tariq to seek help was a conversation with his wife in which she told him,
gently, that she missed him. He had no answer for her. He had come to believe, with a quiet
certainty that frightened him, that the person she missed was simply gone — that happiness was
something that had belonged to a previous version of himself and that nothing waiting ahead of him
would restore it. He arrives at intake hoping, with some ambivalence, to be proven wrong.
