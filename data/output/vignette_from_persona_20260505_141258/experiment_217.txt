Priya Subramaniam is a 66-year-old South Asian woman who works as a security guard at a mid-sized
shopping complex in the suburbs of Houston. She has been married for over forty years, and until
approximately eighteen months ago, she would have described her life as quietly full. That changed
on a Tuesday afternoon when her son, Kiran, was struck by a driver who ran a red light at an
intersection Priya knew well — one she had suggested he use as a shortcut when he called her from
the road that day. He was airlifted to the hospital and survived, but sustained a traumatic brain
injury that left him with lasting cognitive impairment. Priya was the last person who spoke to him
before the crash. She had told him to take that route.

In the weeks that followed the accident, Priya sat at Kiran's bedside through the acute phase of his
hospitalization, and it was there that something shifted in her that she could not name at the time.
She began replaying the phone call obsessively — her own voice, the specific words she used, the
confident way she had directed him. She had come to believe, with a certainty that felt unshakeable,
that she was the reason this had happened. Not the driver, not the light, not chance — her. She had
failed to protect her child, and the evidence, as she experienced it, was irrefutable.

When Kiran was eventually discharged to a rehabilitation facility, Priya returned to work. The
shopping complex where she patrols has a large car park, and several times each shift she watches
vehicles maneuver through tight spaces, hears the occasional screech of brakes, or passes colleagues
talking about fender-benders on the morning commute. Each of these moments arrives in her body
before it arrives in her mind — a sudden surge of heat, her heart slamming against her chest, her
hands going rigid on the radio at her hip. The memory of Kiran's voice cutting out mid-sentence does
not feel like something that happened eighteen months ago; it feels like it is happening at the
exact moment she hears tires on asphalt, immediate and total, pulling her out of the present
entirely. When it comes on fully she has to step into a stairwell and press her back against the
wall until it passes, because the emotion that accompanies it is so overwhelming she cannot continue
to function. She has told no one at work why she disappears.

Outside of work, Priya has quietly reorganized her life around what she cannot bear. She stopped
attending the temple events she once looked forward to, because a close family friend lost her
husband last spring and Priya cannot be near grief — flowers arranged for mourning, the particular
stillness of a room where someone is being remembered — without her body responding as though she
herself is standing at the edge of something catastrophic. She declined the invitation to the
friend's husband's memorial without explanation. Her husband has noticed that she deflects any
conversation that carries even the faint possibility of disagreement, retreating from the room or
changing the subject before tension can gather. He has begun to move around her carefully, and the
distance between them has grown in ways neither of them has yet spoken aloud.

Priya keeps her body in a state of continuous readiness. At the security desk, in the car park, in
the supermarket — her eyes move constantly, cataloguing exits, watching for anything that might
signal something about to go wrong. She tries, with considerable effort, not to think about the
accident directly, pushing the images away when they surface. What frightens her most is not the
intrusions themselves but what she has concluded from them: that she is no longer capable of feeling
ordinary warmth or ease, that something in her that used to be available to her family has been
permanently closed off.

She came to seek help after Kiran asked her, during one of her visits, why she seemed so far away.
She could not answer him. What she hopes for, though she can barely articulate it, is some way back
to being present in her own life. What she fears is that she has already proven she does not deserve
it.
