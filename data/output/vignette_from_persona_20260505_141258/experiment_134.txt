Layla Mansouri is a 52-year-old married woman of Iranian descent who works as a project manager for
a mid-sized construction firm in the greater Houston area. She presented for an initial intake
following a terrorist attack she survived approximately fourteen months ago, during which she was
present at a public civic event when a vehicle drove deliberately into a crowded pedestrian area.
Layla had been standing near a barrier at the edge of the gathering when the vehicle accelerated
through the crowd ahead of her. She witnessed people struck and thrown, heard screaming and the
sustained roar of the engine, and within seconds found herself surrounded by chaos — emergency
responders converging from every direction, police officers shouting commands, and, within minutes,
the heavy rhythmic thudding of a news helicopter circling overhead. She was physically uninjured,
but she remained at the scene for nearly two hours while law enforcement cordoned off the area and
conducted initial witness interviews. Officers moved through the crowd repeatedly, directing people,
separating witnesses, and at one point a uniformed officer stood directly beside her for an extended
period while she gave her account. The combination of sounds, uniforms, and the press of authority
in those hours became woven into the fabric of the experience in ways she did not fully understand
until weeks later.

In the months that followed, Layla found that certain ordinary moments had become unbearable. She
works on active construction sites, and police vehicles are a routine presence — at traffic
diversions, at permit inspections, occasionally when incidents occur on or near a site. Each time
she rounds a corner and encounters an officer in uniform, something seizes in her chest and she is
no longer entirely in Houston; she is back at the barrier, the engine still roaring. Similarly, the
construction environment is not without its sounds of machinery and overhead air traffic, and the
deep rotary thrum of a helicopter — whether a news aircraft, a medical transport, or a private
charter passing over a site — has become one of the most reliable and unwanted of her daily
experiences. She had come to believe, in a way she could not fully articulate but felt with complete
conviction, that these sounds meant something terrible was still unfolding, that the danger had not
passed. Footage of violence — even brief clips glimpsed on a colleague's phone during a lunch break,
even the word "attack" in a headline visible on someone else's screen — produced the same sudden,
total sense of being back inside the event.

Over the months, Layla began arriving at job sites earlier than necessary and leaving before the
afternoon news cycle, when traffic helicopters are most active. She rerouted her site walk-throughs
to avoid areas where police were stationed. Her team noticed she had become less present in
meetings, quicker to delegate site visits she would previously have led herself. A junior project
manager quietly absorbed responsibilities that had been hers. Layla told herself this was efficient
delegation, but she knew it was not.

What has troubled her most, in a way she finds harder to name than the fear, is what she has come to
feel about herself. She had always understood herself as resilient — a woman who had built a career
in a demanding industry, who prided herself on steadiness. Now she looks in the mirror and does not
entirely recognise the person looking back. She was certain, in a quiet and persistent way, that
something essential had been lost and might not return. She described herself in the intake session
as having been a happy person — not naively so, but genuinely — and said that happiness now felt
like a quality that belonged to someone else's life. Equally distressing was her sense that her
internal state was somehow visible to others: that colleagues, her husband, strangers in a meeting
could look at her and see, without her saying a word, that something had happened to her, that she
was marked.

It was her husband who finally asked her directly whether she had spoken to anyone. She had not.
What brought her in was a site inspection last month during which a police cruiser pulled up
unexpectedly and a helicopter passed overhead at almost the same moment — and she had to walk away
from her own team and sit in her car for twenty minutes before she could breathe normally. She came
to therapy hoping, above all, to feel like herself again.
