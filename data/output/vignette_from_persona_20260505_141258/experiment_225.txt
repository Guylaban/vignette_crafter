Mateo Reyes is a 25-year-old Colombian American data scientist who presented for an initial intake
following what he described, with some difficulty, as "a year of not being able to hold it
together." He is single, lives alone in a one-bedroom apartment, and works remotely for a mid-sized
analytics firm. The event that brought him to therapy occurred approximately fourteen months ago,
when his four-year-old niece, Valentina, was rushed to the emergency room in septic shock following
what her parents had initially mistaken for a stomach bug. Mateo had been babysitting her that
afternoon.

He had been the one to call 911. He remembers sitting on the kitchen floor with Valentina limp
against his chest, her skin mottled and her breathing shallow, while the dispatcher kept asking him
questions he could barely process. The ambulance arrived within minutes, but those minutes felt, in
his words, "like time had broken." The paramedics worked quickly, and the smell of the room — the
particular closeness of sweat and fear, his own and hers — stayed with him long after the hospital
corridors had faded. Valentina survived, after five days in the pediatric intensive care unit. Mateo
was told she had come very close to dying. He stood in the waiting room and did not cry. He has not
cried since.

In the weeks that followed, Mateo found that the memory of that afternoon did not behave like a
normal memory. It did not sit in order. He could not reliably reconstruct whether he had called 911
before or after he tried to wake her, whether the dispatcher had spoken first or whether he had. The
sequence felt scrambled, and this confused him deeply, because he had always trusted his mind to be
precise. What troubled him more was a feeling he struggled to articulate: that the event was so
extreme, so outside anything he had known, that it did not feel as though it had genuinely happened
to him. Yet when it surfaced — uninvited, often at his desk — the terror returned in full, not as a
thought but as a bodily fact, his heart accelerating, his hands going cold, exactly as they had on
that kitchen floor.

Mateo works from home, which has offered him a degree of control over his environment, though that
control has come at a cost. He keeps his apartment windows closed because the sound of sirens from
the street below — common in his urban neighborhood — produces an immediate and overwhelming
physical reaction that leaves him unable to concentrate for the rest of the morning. He has
structured his workday around this: he begins his most demanding analyses before the city fully
wakes. His colleagues have noticed that he declines in-person meetings at the company's downtown
office, and his manager recently flagged his increasing isolation from the team as a performance
concern. What his manager does not know is that the office building is two blocks from a hospital,
and Mateo has not been able to walk past an ambulance bay, schedule a routine physical, or enter any
medical facility since the night he stood in that PICU waiting room.

He has come to believe that the safest course is to keep the memory at arm's length. He works long
hours, fills his evenings with data, and when something edges toward the surface, he redirects with
a deliberateness that has become automatic. He does not allow himself to be angry — not at the
situation, not at himself, not at anyone — because he has come to feel that anger, if it were ever
released, would be something he could not contain or come back from. When a colleague's child came
up in conversation and he felt something sharp move through him, he excused himself and sat in the
bathroom until it passed.

What finally brought Mateo to seek help was a moment at his desk three weeks ago when a neighbor's
child was crying in the apartment above him. He sat frozen for forty minutes, unable to work, unable
to move, his body insisting it was still on that kitchen floor. He called the clinic the following
morning. What he is most afraid of, he said quietly at the end of the intake, is that the memory
will never make sense. What he is hoping for, he added after a long pause, is to feel like himself
again.
