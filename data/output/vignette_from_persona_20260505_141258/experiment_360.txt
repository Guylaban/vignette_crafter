Mackenzie Hollis is a 21-year-old electrician who presented for her intake appointment approximately
seven months after surviving a life-threatening motor vehicle collision. She arrived on time,
dressed in work clothes, and spoke with a directness that occasionally gave way to long pauses —
moments when she seemed to be deciding, carefully, how much to say.

The accident occurred on an interstate highway last winter, when Mackenzie was a passenger in a
coworker's truck on the way to a job site. The roads were clear, and she remembers the morning
feeling ordinary — coffee in a thermos, talk radio low in the background. A sedan in the adjacent
lane drifted without warning and struck the truck at speed, sending it into the median barrier. The
impact was violent and sudden. Mackenzie recalls the sound of the collision more than anything else
— a dense, compressive roar — and then a period of fragmented awareness in which she was not certain
she was alive. She sustained significant injuries and required hospitalization. The driver of the
sedan was cited but faced no meaningful legal consequences, a fact that Mackenzie returned to
repeatedly during her first sessions, her voice tightening each time.

In the weeks that followed her discharge, Mackenzie found herself unable to make sense of the gap
between how catastrophic the event had been and how little, in her view, had been acknowledged or
addressed. She had come to believe, with a quiet but corrosive certainty, that what had happened to
her was profoundly unfair and that the world had simply moved on without accounting for it. This
conviction sat alongside a more private one — that her body, which had once felt like something she
could rely on, had become untrustworthy. During the accident she had been unable to brace, unable to
react, unable to protect herself in any way that mattered, and she had not fully forgiven herself or
her body for that. When she experiences physical sensations she cannot immediately explain — a
sudden muscle twitch, a brief moment of dizziness on a ladder — she does not dismiss them. She
monitors them, waiting for evidence of something going wrong.

Mackenzie's work as an electrician has placed her in direct and daily contact with one of her most
persistent difficulties. She frequently travels to job sites with colleagues, and riding in a
vehicle with other people has become quietly agonizing. She positions herself near the door, watches
the mirrors, and tracks the speed of surrounding cars in a way that leaves her exhausted by the time
she arrives. When a vehicle nearby changes lanes abruptly or when she glimpses a car that has been
visibly damaged in a collision — a crumpled hood, a shattered rear panel — she experiences an
intrusive rush of imagery that she describes as the accident reasserting itself, uninvited, into the
present moment. Over the past several months she has begun arriving at job sites by other means when
she can manage it, citing schedule differences or errands, though her supervisor has noticed the
pattern and commented on it twice.

What has proved more difficult to explain to those around her is her relationship to the future.
Conversations about plans — where she might be in five years, whether she wants to take on an
apprenticeship, what she imagines her life looking like — produce a kind of blankness that she finds
hard to articulate. She has come to feel that she cannot trust herself to do the right thing when it
counts, and that making plans assumes a stability she is no longer confident she possesses. A
friend's casual question about whether she had thought about buying a car of her own prompted a
response Mackenzie later described as "shutting down completely."

She sought help now, she said, because she had recently turned down an apprenticeship opportunity —
something she had wanted for years — and found herself unable to explain why, even to herself. What
she most fears is that the person she was before the accident is no longer available to her. What
she most hopes, though she stated it tentatively, is that she might learn to trust herself again.
