Cassandra Whitfield is a 38-year-old bartender who has been in a relationship for the past four
years. She presented for an initial intake following what she described as months of "just white-
knuckling it" — a phrase that, given everything, carried more weight than she seemed to realize.

a red light while Marcus was crossing a street outside the hospital where he worked as a nurse.
Cassandra was with him. She had been the one to suggest they grab coffee from the café across the
road, and she had stepped off the curb a half-second before him, which meant she had already cleared
the intersection when the car came through. She turned at the sound of impact. What she saw — Marcus
airborne, then still — is now stored in her mind as a sequence of freeze-frames: the angle of his
body against the hood, the colour of his jacket, the way his shoes came off. Other moments are
simply absent. She cannot recall who called 911. She has no memory of the ambulance arriving, though
she was told she spoke to the paramedics. What remains is vivid and visual, a set of images that
surface unbidden with a sharpness she finds almost insulting — as though her mind has chosen to
preserve exactly the parts she would most like to forget.

Marcus survived, but required months of rehabilitation. In the weeks following the accident,
Cassandra found herself replaying the decision to cross the street. She had wanted coffee. She had
been the one to say, let's go. She had come to understand the accident as something she had set in
motion, a conclusion she held with the quiet, immovable certainty of someone who has already
deliberated and reached a verdict. Alongside this sat a darker shift in how she viewed the people
around her — the driver had seemed, by all accounts, an ordinary man, someone who looked like
anyone. She had come to believe that people were fundamentally opaque, that the surface others
presented concealed something unreliable or dangerous. This extended even to those close to her. She
had grown reluctant to lean on her partner, Dominic, not because she doubted his care but because
she had become convinced, in some wordless way, that depending on another person was a structural
error — one she had already made once.

In her work behind the bar, the television mounted in the corner of the room became a source of
particular vigilance. Local news segments, traffic reports, anything that showed a street
intersection or emergency response footage — she would feel her attention narrow and her jaw
tighten. She began requesting that the channel be changed when certain segments aired, and when
colleagues asked why, she offered vague explanations. Over several months, she stopped watching any
news at home as well. What had started as a reasonable preference became a firm boundary she
enforced without examining.

She did not seek medical or psychological help in the immediate aftermath of the accident, and she
has not done so since — until now. She had attended one follow-up appointment with her GP and found
herself unable to speak about what she was experiencing, describing her sleep as "a bit off" and
leaving with a pamphlet she threw away in the car park. She had come to feel that disclosing the
full extent of her distress would require her to lose control of it, and maintaining control of her
emotions had become something she worked at constantly, a second job she performed in parallel with
every interaction. She described this effort not as suppression but as professionalism — she was
good at keeping it together, she said, and she needed to be.

What finally brought Cassandra in was a conversation with Dominic, who told her quietly one evening
that he felt like she was somewhere else even when she was in the room. She had not argued with him.
She came to the intake session hoping, in her words, to "figure out if there's something actually
wrong or if I just need to get over it" — and frightened, beneath that, that the answer might
confirm what she already suspected about herself.
