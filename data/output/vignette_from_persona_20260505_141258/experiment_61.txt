Marisol Vega is a 66-year-old divorced Latina woman who works as a senior editor at a mid-sized
publishing house in Chicago. She was born in El Salvador and emigrated to the United States in her
early thirties, having survived the civil conflict that consumed much of her young adulthood. It is
the specific afternoon of a government military sweep through her neighborhood in San Salvador —
when she was twenty-eight years old — that she identifies as the event from which her current
suffering most directly flows. Soldiers in olive-green uniforms moved door to door along her street,
and when they reached the house two doors down, a concussive explosion tore through the block. She
did not know at the time whether it was a grenade, a planted device, or something else; she
remembers only that the sound seemed to erase all other sound for several seconds, and that when she
looked up from the floor where she had thrown herself, one of the uniformed men was standing in her
doorway, backlit, staring at her. He said nothing. She said nothing. Then he turned and left. She
has never been certain what he intended, and that uncertainty has never resolved itself into
something she could file away as finished.

She arrived in the United States the following year, built a career in publishing, raised a daughter
largely alone after her divorce in her mid-forties, and for many years managed to keep the memories
at a functional distance. The symptoms she now presents with did not announce themselves all at
once. They accumulated quietly across the past several years, tightening around her life in ways she
initially attributed to aging, to stress at work, to the ordinary anxieties of living alone.

Marisol's editorial work requires her to read manuscripts, correspond with authors, and occasionally
attend industry events in downtown Chicago. One of the building's security staff wears a dark
uniform with a badge and a utility belt, and on more than one occasion she has rounded a corridor
corner and encountered him unexpectedly. Each time, she has felt something close to paralysis — a
locking of the chest, a sudden inability to speak naturally. She has begun timing her arrivals and
departures to avoid the lobby when she knows he is likely to be stationed there, which has meant
leaving earlier than her schedule requires and sometimes missing conversations with colleagues she
would otherwise have joined. She has not explained this to anyone at the office.

At home, the nights have become the most difficult terrain. Marisol had come to believe, in a way
she could not entirely articulate but felt as a physical certainty, that darkness itself reduced the
margin between safety and harm. She checks the locks on her apartment door and windows before bed,
and then checks them again. When she wakes in the early hours — which she does most nights — she
lies still and listens before she allows herself to move, scanning for sounds she cannot name but
would recognize. Even when her daughter has told her the apartment is secure, even when friends have
reminded her that she is in Chicago and not in San Salvador, Marisol finds that she cannot absorb
the reassurance. She understands it intellectually and dismisses it emotionally in the same moment,
as though the part of her that assesses danger has decided that other people's perceptions of safety
are simply not relevant data.

The trigger that finally brought her to seek help was a film trailer that played before a
documentary she attended with a colleague. The trailer contained the sound of an explosion — brief,
digitally enhanced, unremarkable to everyone else in the theater. Marisol left the screening and sat
in the lobby for forty minutes before she felt able to take the train home. She told her colleague
she had a headache. Sitting in that lobby, she recognized that she had been managing something for a
long time that she could no longer manage alone.

What she is most afraid of, she said in her first session, is that the fear has become permanent —
that at sixty-six she has simply run out of time to be otherwise. What she is most hoping for, said
more quietly, is to sleep through the night without waking to listen for something that is no longer
there.
