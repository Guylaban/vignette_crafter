Fatima Khalil is a 34-year-old divorced woman of Lebanese descent who works as an agricultural
laborer on a large produce farm in California's Central Valley. She was referred for psychological
evaluation following a conversation with a farmworker health advocate who noticed she had become
increasingly withdrawn and was struggling to complete her shifts. She presents with a PCL-5 score of
65, consistent with severe posttraumatic stress.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Fatima was present when a young woman in her residential labor
camp — a neighbor she had known only a few weeks, who had recently given birth — was subjected to a
prolonged and violent assault by a man who had been circling the camp for several days, asking
questions, watching. The man had seemed off to Fatima in the days before: too attentive, too still,
positioning himself near doorways and watching people without quite meeting their eyes. She had
noticed him but had told herself she was being paranoid. When the attack occurred, Fatima was close
enough to hear everything. She heard the infant crying in the adjacent room throughout, a sound that
did not stop for a very long time. She did not intervene — she has never fully explained to herself
why — and by the time others responded, severe harm had already been done. The woman survived.
Fatima has not spoken to her since.

In the weeks that followed, Fatima found herself unable to account for large portions of her memory
of that evening. What she could recall came back not as a coherent sequence but in fragments — the
sound of the baby, the particular quality of the silence that preceded the attack, the way the man
had been standing earlier that day with his arms slightly away from his body. These fragments
returned without warning, and when they did, they carried none of the distance of the past. The
baby's crying was the most relentless of these intrusions. On the farm, where she now spends her
days pruning and harvesting in the open rows, a coworker's phone notification, a distant sound from
a passing car, something as unremarkable as a high-pitched mechanical whine can pull her back to
that room. She stops working. Her hands go still. The other women around her notice, and she has had
to offer explanations she does not believe herself.

What has settled most heavily over the past year is not the fear but the understanding — the cold,
clarifying certainty — that she had been wrong about everything. She had looked at that man and
hesitated. She had assumed the best, or at least the neutral, and someone had paid for that. She had
come to believe that people are not what they present themselves to be, that surfaces conceal
intentions, and that her own judgment cannot be trusted to read the difference. On the farm, when a
new worker arrives and moves through the rows in a way she cannot immediately interpret, or lingers
near the water station without obvious purpose, she feels her attention narrow to a point. She
watches. She catalogs. She does not relax until she has decided she understands what they are doing
and why, and even then the certainty is thin.

At home in her small apartment, she has developed a habit of cleaning that she understands, on some
level, is excessive. The kitchen, the bathroom, the windowsills — she returns to them in the
evenings in a sequence that has become fixed, and if she is interrupted she begins again. She knows
it is not dirt she is managing. When memories surface during the day, she works to replace them
deliberately with other images — her family in Beirut, a meal she once enjoyed, anything — and when
that fails she simply pushes, hard, against the thought itself, holding it at a distance through
sheer effort. She has stopped attending the informal gatherings the farmworkers organize on
weekends, partly because conversations turn unpredictably and sometimes land on topics — violence,
children, the safety of women living alone — that she cannot navigate without feeling the floor drop
away. She has lost her sense of where she fits. She was not the victim. She does not feel she has
the right to be this undone. She belongs, she has concluded, nowhere in particular.

What finally brought her to the advocate's attention was a shift supervisor asking why she had been
leaving rows unfinished. What brought her to this office was the fear that she is becoming someone
she does not recognize, and the smaller, more fragile hope that this might not be permanent.
