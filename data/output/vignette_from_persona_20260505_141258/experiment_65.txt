Leilani Fono is a 67-year-old widowed woman of Samoan descent who works as a database administrator
for a regional logistics company, a role she has held for nearly a decade and performs almost
entirely from her home office. She presented for psychological assessment approximately three months
after leaving a relationship in which she had been repeatedly physically abused by her partner of
four years, a man she had met through her church community following the death of her husband. The
abuse had escalated gradually, but the incident that finally ended the relationship occurred on an
otherwise unremarkable evening when, during an argument, her partner grabbed her by the shoulders,
shook her violently, and struck her across the face. What stayed with her — more than the pain — was
the moment just before the blow landed: his eyes meeting hers, steady and cold, as though he had
made a calm decision. She had looked directly into his face and seen something she could not name,
and that image had not left her.

In the weeks that followed, Leilani noticed changes in herself that she struggled to explain. She
began avoiding eye contact in almost every interaction — with the delivery driver who brought her
groceries, with her adult daughter during video calls, with her own reflection. At work, her
database role required occasional video conferences with colleagues, and she had taken to
positioning her camera slightly off-centre and keeping her gaze fixed on her keyboard, offering
terse, technical responses before ending calls as quickly as possible. Her manager had commented,
gently, that she seemed "somewhere else." She did not know how to respond.

Physical contact became something she arranged her life around avoiding. She declined her daughter's
offer to visit, citing a cold that did not exist. When a colleague placed a hand briefly on her arm
during an in-person site visit she could not avoid, she felt her body go rigid in a way that
embarrassed and confused her. She had come to believe that the rigidity itself was evidence of
something broken in her — that a person of ordinary resilience would have recovered by now, would
have moved on, would not still be flinching at a colleague's casual touch months after leaving.

Sleep had become its own ordeal. Leilani found herself staying up until two or three in the morning,
scrolling through work logs or reorganising database schemas that did not need reorganising. She
told herself she was simply catching up on tasks, but what she was really doing, though she did not
frame it this way, was postponing the stillness that came with lying down in the dark. The late
hours were hers in a way the daylight hours no longer felt.

What troubled Leilani most deeply, and what she returned to again and again in her intake sessions,
was her sense that she no longer recognised herself. She had been, she said, a capable woman —
someone who raised three children largely alone after her first husband's illness, who taught
herself database administration in her fifties, who served on the welcoming committee at her church.
She could not reconcile that woman with the one who now angled her face away from people and
manufactured excuses to avoid her daughter's embrace. She was certain that something was wrong with
her as a person — not with what had happened to her, but with her, fundamentally — and this
certainty sat at the centre of her distress like a weight she carried without being able to set
down. She described feeling like a stranger inhabiting her own life.

It was her daughter who finally insisted she seek help, after noticing during a video call that
Leilani had not looked at the screen once in forty minutes. Leilani agreed, though she came to the
first session with considerable ambivalence. What she feared most was confirmation of what she
already suspected — that her difficulty recovering proved something irreparable about her character.
What she hoped for, expressed quietly and almost as an afterthought at the end of the intake, was to
feel like herself again, though she acknowledged she was no longer entirely sure who that was.
