Raymond had spent nearly three decades building a career around the idea that spaces could be
transformed — that the right light, the right arrangement, the right palette could make a person
feel entirely at home in the world. He was good at it, known in his city for an eye that balanced
warmth with precision, and the work had sustained him through a difficult divorce in his late
forties and the quieter loneliness that followed. Then, fourteen months ago, a delivery truck ran a
red light on a rain-slicked intersection and struck the driver's side of Raymond's sedan at highway
speed. The impact spun the car across two lanes. He remembers the sound before anything else — a
compression of metal so total it seemed to come from inside his own skull — and then the sensation
of the world rotating, the windshield filling with grey sky and then asphalt and then sky again,
before everything stopped and there was only the ticking of the cooling engine and the distant sound
of someone screaming his name.

He was hospitalised for eleven days with a fractured pelvis, three broken ribs, and a concussion
that left him with weeks of cognitive fog. The physical recovery was long and humiliating in ways he
had not anticipated — the dependency, the slowness, the body that would not do what he asked of it.
What stayed with him, and what brought him finally to a psychologist's office more than a year
later, was not the pain but something harder to name: a sense that the man who had walked into that
intersection no longer existed, and that whoever had been pulled from the wreckage was someone he
did not recognise or trust.

In the months after the accident, Raymond found that certain things had become almost unbearable.
Driving was the first to go. He told himself it was practical — he was still healing — but long
after his pelvis had mended he continued to take rideshares, and even then he sat rigid in the back
seat, watching every intersection with a vigilance that left him exhausted. When another car moved
too quickly into an adjacent lane, or when a truck merged close, the sound of it — tyres on wet
pavement, the rush of a large vehicle passing — would hit him like a physical thing, and for a
moment he was back in the rotation, the sky and the asphalt trading places. He began wearing earbuds
on every ride, filling his ears with music, though he could not have said whether this helped or
simply gave him something to focus on while his hands gripped his knees.

At work, the difficulty became concrete in ways that affected his livelihood. Client meetings
sometimes required site visits in unfamiliar parts of the city, and Raymond began declining projects
that involved locations he could not reach on foot or by a route he had pre-approved in his mind. A
colleague covered for him twice, then a third time, and eventually Raymond lost a significant
commercial contract because he could not bring himself to attend a series of walkthroughs near a
highway interchange where the sound of traffic was constant and unavoidable. When a client
mentioned, in passing, that they had been in a minor fender-bender that week, Raymond felt the
conversation close around him like a fist. He made an excuse and ended the call.

He had come to believe, with a quiet and terrible certainty, that his body was no longer a reliable
instrument. Before the accident he had trusted his physical intuitions — his sense of space, his
ability to move through the world with confidence. Now he interpreted ordinary sensations as
warnings he could not decode. He was certain, too, that something essential had been permanently
altered in him, not just functionally but fundamentally, and this frightened him more than any
specific symptom. The man who had once walked into a room and immediately understood how to make it
feel like somewhere worth being could no longer recognise himself in the mirror.

What finally brought Raymond to seek help was a conversation with his adult daughter, who told him
quietly that she was worried he was disappearing. He had not been able to articulate what he was
afraid of finding in therapy, only what he was hoping for: some evidence that he still existed, that
the damage was not, as he feared, permanent.
