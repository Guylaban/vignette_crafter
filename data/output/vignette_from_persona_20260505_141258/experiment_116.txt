Marcus Holt is a 34-year-old Navy officer, married, who presented for an initial psychological
evaluation following a referral from his unit's medical officer. He carries himself with the
composed bearing one might expect from someone who has spent years operating in high-stakes
environments, yet there is something effortful about that composure now — a quality of maintenance
rather than ease. He is soft-spoken in session, choosing his words carefully, as though he is still
not entirely certain that what happened to him is something worth discussing.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Marcus was on approved leave visiting extended family in a
coastal community in the Gulf South when a rapidly intensifying hurricane made landfall with little
warning. He and several family members, including his wife's younger brother, were sheltering in a
single-story home when the storm surge overwhelmed the structure. The water rose faster than anyone
had anticipated. In the chaos that followed — darkness, the roar of wind and water, the press of
debris — Marcus found himself separated from the others, pinned briefly beneath a collapsed interior
wall in chest-high water, unable to determine who was around him or whether anyone was calling out.
The smell in the air was a sour, fetid mixture of floodwater and the contents of a nearby liquor
cabinet that had shattered in the surge. His wife's brother did not survive. Marcus did, pulled free
by a neighbor who had heard him. He has described the hours that followed — waiting in a crowded
emergency shelter, pressed against strangers, unable to account for everyone — as worse in some ways
than the water itself.

In the weeks after returning to duty, Marcus noticed that his memories of the event did not behave
like other memories. They came as images rather than narrative — a flash of dark water, the angle of
a ceiling beam, a particular quality of light through a broken window — and when they arrived, he
felt his body respond as though it were still pinned: his chest would compress, his arms would
brace. What unsettled him most was the sense that the memory felt cinematically extreme, almost
impossible to locate in his own biography. He has said more than once, with a kind of bewildered
flatness, that it doesn't feel like something that actually happened to him. And yet his body
insists otherwise.

He had come to believe, quietly and with some conviction, that he deserved whatever suffering came
to him. His reasoning, never fully articulated until recently, was shaped by his survival — by the
fact that he, a trained officer, had not been able to do more. This belief has not taken the form of
dramatic self-recrimination but of something quieter and more corrosive: a sense that comfort or
relief would be somehow dishonest, and that he ought not seek either. He began telling himself this
in the shelter, and he has not stopped.

The practical consequences have accumulated over months. Marcus stopped accepting invitations from
friends in his unit — dinners, social gatherings — until the invitations stopped coming. His wife
has noticed the withdrawal but has interpreted it as grief, which is not entirely wrong. He avoids
the commissary during peak hours when it is crowded, routing his errands to early morning slots, and
he has begun timing his movements around the base to minimize the press of bodies. He keeps clear of
any situation that might replicate the sensory texture of that night: he will not sit in dim rooms,
will not enter spaces where he cannot see the exits clearly, and the smell of alcohol — present at
any number of officer social functions — has become something he physically removes himself from,
sometimes without explanation. His supervisor has noted his absences from unit social events, and
there has been an informal conversation about whether Marcus is "still himself."

He has come to feel, with a certainty that surprises him when he examines it, that he no longer
belongs anywhere in particular — not fully among his colleagues, not fully at home, not anywhere he
inhabited before the storm. He describes himself as standing slightly outside whatever room he is
in. What finally brought him to seek help was his wife asking him, plainly and without anger,
whether he thought things would ever feel normal again. He did not have an answer. He is most afraid
that the answer is no.
