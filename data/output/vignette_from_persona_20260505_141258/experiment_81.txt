GP, who had grown concerned after Callum mentioned, almost in passing, that he had not been sleeping
well since returning from a military deployment eight months ago. His PCL-5 score of 53 places him

a young man quietly drowning in an experience he cannot yet fully name.

Callum enlisted at eighteen, drawn by a sense of purpose and a family tradition of service. He was
deployed as part of a small infantry unit, and it was during a patrol through a village that his
world fractured. The unit came under fire, and in the chaos that followed, a vehicle ahead of his
was struck by an improvised explosive device. The blast was enormous — a wall of pressure and heat
that threw him sideways — and what followed was a scene of burning metal, thick black smoke, and the
screaming of men he had trained alongside for months. The smell of burning material and flesh
saturated everything. Helicopters arrived to extract the casualties, their rotors cutting through
the smoke-filled air. Callum survived physically intact, but something in how he processed that
afternoon did not hold together. He has described the event in fragments across sessions — a flash
of orange, the particular pitch of a man's voice, the texture of the ground beneath his hands — but
the pieces do not cohere into a continuous account. He knows what happened in the way one knows a
fact, but when he tries to speak it, the words dissolve. He has sat in silence for long minutes,
hands pressed flat on his thighs, unable to continue.

Since returning home, the memories have not receded so much as reorganised themselves into a loop he
cannot switch off. They arrive unbidden — a film, as he put it, that plays whether or not he has
chosen to watch it. Driving his bus route through the city, Callum has found that the job he took
partly for its straightforwardness has become its own minefield. A truck backfiring in traffic can
stop him cold, his hands tightening on the wheel as his body responds to a threat that is not there.
On two occasions, the sound of a news helicopter passing low over the route has made him pull the
bus to the kerb and sit, breathing, until the sound faded. The smell of something burning — a
passenger's takeaway bag, a mechanical fault in another vehicle — arrives in his cab without warning
and takes him somewhere else entirely.

He has come to believe, with a quiet but immovable certainty, that the world is genuinely dangerous
and that his survival depends on remaining alert to that danger at all times. He scans his mirrors
constantly, watches passengers with a vigilance that exhausts him by the end of a shift, and has
stopped attending the church his family has gone to for three generations, unable to tolerate the
enclosed crowd, the incense, or the unpredictability of the congregation around him. He avoids
shopping centres entirely and has not attended a single social event since his discharge. In the
evenings, he retreats into his phone — hours of games, scrolling, streaming — not because it brings
him pleasure, which he notes it no longer does, but because it holds his mind just occupied enough
to keep the film from starting. His mother has commented on how much he has withdrawn from the
family. He has not been able to explain it to her, partly because he cannot find the words, and
partly because he has begun to wonder, in his darker moments, whether any of it is worth explaining
at all.

What finally brought Callum to seek help was a near-miss on his route: distracted by the sound of a
distant explosion at a construction site, he braked hard and unnecessarily, frightening his
passengers. He was written up by his supervisor. Losing this job — the one structure still holding
his days together — felt like a line he could not afford to cross. He arrived at his first session
guarded and precise, saying only that he needed to get his head right. What he has not yet said
aloud, but what sits just beneath the surface of every session, is that he is terrified he no longer
knows how.
