Pacific goods and grocery shop in a mid-sized California city for nearly two decades. She came to
the clinic in a state of visible exhaustion, her speech careful and halting, as though she were
choosing each word from a diminishing supply. She had resisted seeking help for a long time, she
said, because she was not the kind of person who fell apart. The fact that she was here at all felt,
to her, like a confession of failure.

Approximately fourteen months before the intake, Mere had been closing her shop alone on a weekday
evening — the light outside already deep orange, the street quieting — when two police officers
entered in response to a neighbour's call about a reported disturbance in the building. What
followed escalated with a speed that Mere still cannot fully account for. She was pushed against the
shelving behind her counter, her arms wrenched behind her back, her face pressed into the wooden
edge of a display case. She remembers the fluorescent hum of the shop lights, the smell of cardboard
and dried fish, and then a sensation of dizziness so severe she thought she was losing
consciousness. She remembers one officer's face close to hers, the feeling of being pinned and
watched with cold indifference. And then there are gaps — stretches of the event she cannot retrieve
no matter how deliberately she tries, as though the recording simply stopped. What she does remember
carries a quality she finds profoundly disorienting: the memory is so extreme, so unlike anything
she had ever imagined happening to her in her own shop, that she sometimes cannot fully believe it
belongs to her life. She has no language for the worst of it. When her adult daughter has gently
asked what happened, Mere finds herself unable to form a coherent account — not because she refuses,
but because the words do not come. She gestures, she falls silent, she changes the subject.

In the months that followed, certain things became unbearable. The late afternoon light — that
particular amber quality that arrives around five o'clock — began to fill her with a dread she could
not explain to customers. She started closing the shop an hour earlier, telling herself it was about
her knees, her tiredness, her age. The sensation of dizziness, which occasionally returned with no
apparent cause, would stop her mid-task, gripping the counter, certain something catastrophic was
about to happen again. She had come to believe that her body was unreliable, that it would betray
her without warning. And the feeling of being watched — which in a shop is, of course, constant —
had become something she could no longer manage neutrally. Customers browsing near the counter, a
delivery driver lingering at the door, even the security camera monitor she herself had installed:
all of it now registered as surveillance, as threat.

She began avoiding the anniversary of the incident with a rigidity that surprised even her,
rearranging her schedule weeks in advance so she would not have to open the shop that day, spending
the hours instead in a kind of restless, joyless movement through shopping centres, buying things
she did not need. The compulsive spending had become a pattern she recognised and was ashamed of —
bags of items returned weeks later, or simply accumulated in a back room. She knew it was not
helping. She was certain it meant something was deeply wrong with her.

What frightened Mere most, sitting across from the clinician in the intake room, was not the memory
itself but her own response to it. She had raised children, buried a husband, rebuilt a business
after a difficult divorce. She was not, she insisted, someone who went to pieces. The fact that she
was still struggling more than a year later had led her to a conclusion she stated quietly but with
absolute conviction: that her reactions meant she was losing her mind, that she was fundamentally
inadequate, that she was coping badly in a way that revealed something broken at her core. She
hoped, she said, that someone could tell her she was wrong about that. She was not entirely sure she
believed it was possible.
