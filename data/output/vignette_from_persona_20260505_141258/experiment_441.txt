Rajan Mehta is a 41-year-old South Asian architect who presented for treatment following a period of
escalating distress that he described, with some difficulty, as having "finally become impossible to
manage alone." He is married, and it was his wife who first noticed that the man she had known for
over a decade had become someone she no longer fully recognized — withdrawn, rigid in his routines,
and prone to sudden, inexplicable alarm. What Rajan had not told her, until very recently, was that
the source of his distress predated their marriage by several years, rooted in a relationship he had
spent a long time trying to bury in professional achievement and deliberate silence.

In his early thirties, Rajan was involved with a woman he had met through a mutual social circle.
The relationship was volatile in ways he had initially attributed to passion, then to stress, and
finally to something he could not name without implicating himself in a story he found deeply
shameful. She drank heavily, and when she drank, the apartment they shared would fill with a
particular quality of noise — slurred, looping speech that collapsed into something almost phonemic,
words losing their edges, her voice cycling through registers he could no longer parse. On several
occasions, during these episodes, she became physically violent. The worst incident occurred on a
winter evening when Rajan had come home unwell, feverish and nauseated, and had lain down on the
couch. He had not heard her come in. He had not processed, in any sequential way, what happened next
— only that his body had understood before his mind did, the shadow crossing the room, the shift in
air pressure, and then impact. He remembered it now not as a continuous event but as a series of
arrested frames: the grain of the couch fabric against his cheek, the smell of alcohol very close to
his face, a particular angle of lamplight, the ceiling. Still photographs. Each one carrying the
full emotional weight of the original moment, so that to remember any one of them was, for Rajan,
not recollection but re-entry.

He ended the relationship within months of that night and told no one. He built his career with
considerable discipline, joined a respected firm, married a woman he loved and trusted, and
understood himself, for a time, to have moved on. The first sign that this was not true came at an
office gathering where a colleague spilled wine nearby and the smell reached him before the sound of
breaking glass did. His body had already moved — chair pushed back, hands braced — before he had any
conscious thought. He stood there, heart hammering, in a room full of colleagues, and had no
explanation to offer anyone, including himself.

In his work as an architect, Rajan regularly attends client consultations and site meetings,
environments where overlapping conversations are common. When multiple voices blur into
simultaneous, unintelligible sound — the particular acoustic texture of a crowded conference room —
he finds himself scanning the space with an alertness disproportionate to any identifiable threat.
He sleeps poorly, lying awake in a state of readiness he cannot entirely account for, as though some
part of him has not accepted that the night is safe. He startles at sounds his wife does not even
register.

What has accumulated alongside these responses is a set of beliefs about himself that Rajan finds
more distressing than the symptoms themselves. He had come to believe, with a quiet and persistent
certainty, that he had failed in the most fundamental way a person could — that he had not protected
himself, had not acted rightly, had not been the person he was supposed to be. He felt that the
trust he had extended in that relationship had been turned against him in a way that still colored
how he understood his own judgment. He was not sure, even now, that he could trust himself to
respond correctly when it mattered.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment at his daughter's school, when a cluster of
parents talking over one another in the hallway sent him out of the building and into the parking
lot, where he sat in his car for twenty minutes unable to go back inside. He had missed the
performance. His daughter had looked for him in the audience. He arrived for the last session of
treatment hoping, above everything else, to be someone his family could count on — and afraid, in
equal measure, that he had already proved he was not.
