Dorothea is an 80-year-old woman of European descent who has worked as a social worker for most of
her adult life, spending decades advocating for vulnerable clients in crisis — people whose
suffering she understood, she once thought, because she had been spared it herself. She presents now
as a single woman living alone, referred through her own agency's employee assistance programme
after a colleague noticed she had begun refusing to conduct home visits and had stopped attending
team meetings held in small conference rooms. She is soft-spoken in session, precise with language
in the way that long professional training produces, and visibly startled by any sound from the
corridor outside the office.

The abuse took place over approximately three years with a man she had been involved with since her
late seventies, a widower she had met through a mutual friend. The relationship began warmly, and
Dorothea had permitted herself, after decades of independence, to believe that companionship at this
stage of life was both possible and safe. The violence escalated gradually. What she returns to most
often — and what she returns to involuntarily, in fragments she cannot fully assemble — are several
specific moments: the sound of his key turning in the lock, or the door being thrown open, which
meant that what followed would be bad; the sight of his face across a small room; the feeling of
being unable to move toward an exit. She cannot reconstruct the sequence of events across those
years into a coherent story. What she has instead are discrete images, each one vivid and still: his
shadow in a doorway, the pattern of a rug she stared at, the particular quality of afternoon light
on a certain day. When these images surface, what overtakes her is not fear alone but something she
describes as grief so total it feels physical — a mourning she cannot name the object of, though the
therapist understands it as the loss of the self she had been before.

In the months since leaving the relationship, Dorothea has reorganised her life around a set of
quiet but far-reaching adjustments. She no longer works with male clients and has constructed a
plausible professional rationale for this that has so far gone unchallenged. When she enters any
room — the office kitchen, a waiting area, a community centre where she facilitates a support group
for elderly women — she locates the exits before she does anything else, her eyes moving to the
doors before she has consciously decided to look. She does not sleep well. She lies awake for long
stretches, and on the nights when she has learned that sleep is more likely to bring disturbance,
she keeps a lamp on and reads until nearly dawn, accepting exhaustion as the lesser cost. She has
also, in ways she finds difficult to articulate, tried simply not to think about what happened — to
press the memories down and away — though she acknowledges this has not worked and that the images
return regardless.

What has perhaps changed most visibly is her relationship to the professional identity she spent her
life building. Dorothea has always understood people, has always believed, in some foundational way,
that human beings are essentially knowable and that the world, while imperfect, operates according
to principles she could navigate. She had come to believe none of that was true anymore. People, she
now felt with quiet certainty, could not be trusted — a conviction that sat uneasily against fifty
years of social work practice and that she had not yet spoken aloud to anyone before these sessions.
She also carries something she calls a feeling of being soiled, a sense she cannot fully rationalise
but cannot dismiss, that what was done to her has left a mark on her that others, if they looked
carefully, might see.

She has also spent considerable time asking why this happened to her, turning the question over
without resolution, as though the right answer might restore something. She came to seek help not
after any single crisis but after realising, during a supervision session with a young colleague,
that she could not be alone in a room with that colleague's male supervisor without her heart
hammering and her attention fixed on the door. What she most hopes for, she says carefully, is to be
able to do her work again. What she most fears is that the person who could do that work no longer
exists.
