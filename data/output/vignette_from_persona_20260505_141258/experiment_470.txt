Priya Anand is a 32-year-old South Asian woman, divorced, who works as a hospital chaplain in a mid-
sized American city. She presented for psychological assessment with a PCL-5 score of 68, describing
a period of suffering that has quietly dismantled much of the life she had rebuilt for herself over
the past several years.

Approximately two years before seeking help, Priya was travelling in a region experiencing civil
unrest when she was detained by uniformed security forces at a checkpoint. What followed over the
next several days was a sustained experience of torture. She was held in a small room, her wrists
and ankles bound for long periods, unable to move or shift position without pain. Her captors wore
military-style uniforms — dark, insignia-marked, indistinguishable from one another in her memory as
a collective mass of authority and threat. Throughout her detention she was subjected to sounds she
has never been able to name aloud: a specific metallic scraping, the rhythm of boots on a concrete
floor, a door being struck from the outside. She was eventually released without explanation and
made her way back to the United States, where she told almost no one what had happened.

In the months that followed, the experience did not settle into the past the way Priya had hoped it
would. When she tried to tell her then-husband what she had been through, something collapsed inside
the attempt — she would begin a sentence and find herself stranded in the middle of it, the thread
gone, unable to locate the words that should have followed. She described it later to her intake
clinician as knowing the shape of something she could not hold. The experience existed in her body
and in flashes of sensation rather than in any narrative she could construct or share. She had come
to believe this was not ordinary difficulty but evidence that something had broken in her — that the
part of her capable of coherent thought or speech had been fundamentally damaged.

Her work as a chaplain placed her in a hospital environment where the clinical world pressed against
her own wounds in particular ways. Security officers and police liaisons moved through the corridors
in uniform, and on the days she encountered them, Priya found herself rerouting through longer
hallways, taking stairwells she had no practical reason to use. She had not fully articulated this
to herself as avoidance; she experienced it simply as not being able to breathe properly when they
were nearby. On one occasion she was asked to accompany a patient to a procedure that required brief
physical restraint for a scan, and she had to leave the room. She told the nursing staff she felt
unwell, which was true in every sense that mattered.

Over time she stopped attending the informal staff gatherings she had once looked forward to — the
small lunches, the after-shift conversations with colleagues who had known her before her trip
abroad. She had come to feel that being around people who remembered her earlier self made the
distance between that person and who she was now unbearably visible. The friendships thinned, and
she let them. Her divorce, finalised eight months ago, was not unrelated to this withdrawal, though
she did not frame it that way to herself.

On several occasions, when the intrusive material became most acute, Priya had hurt herself
physically — pressing her nails into her forearm, or striking her hand against a hard surface — not
from a wish to cause lasting harm but because the sharp, immediate sensation of it pulled her
attention entirely into the present moment and away from wherever her mind had gone. She was aware
this was not a solution. She was not sure what a solution would look like.

What finally brought her to seek help was a conversation with a dying patient who asked her, gently,
whether she herself was all right. She had said yes. Driving home, she understood for the first time
that she was not. What she feared most, walking into the intake appointment, was being asked to
explain what had happened and failing again — losing the thread, going silent, confirming what she
already suspected about herself. What she hoped for, more quietly, was that someone might be able to
sit with what she could not yet say.
