Miriam Taufa is a 58-year-old Tongan-American woman, divorced, who has served for nearly two decades
as a rabbi at a mid-sized Reform congregation in the Pacific Northwest. She came to therapy
following a life-threatening car accident fourteen months ago, presenting with a PCL-5 score of 78
and a quiet, contained desperation that she described as "exhausting to maintain."

The accident occurred on a rain-slicked highway in November, when a semi-trailer drifted across the
median and struck Miriam's vehicle head-on. Her car was spun into the guardrail and caught fire at
the rear. She was conscious throughout — trapped by a buckled door, smelling the acrid bite of
burning plastic and insulation spreading from behind her seat, unable to free herself for what felt,
she said, like an eternity before a passing motorist wrenched the door open. She was not seriously
injured in the physical sense that required extended hospitalization, and that fact had become its
own source of confusion for her. She had walked away. And yet she had not walked away.

In the weeks that followed, Miriam found that sleep brought no relief. She would wake from
nightmares in a state of acute alarm — heart hammering, sheets damp — and the residue of those
dreams would cling to the morning in ways she could not fully shake. She began dreading the
transition back to consciousness itself, because waking felt less like escape than like re-entry. On
those mornings she would arrive at the synagogue distracted and short-tempered, and she noticed,
with shame, that she had snapped at her cantor twice over minor liturgical disagreements — outbursts
that were entirely unlike the person she believed herself to be. She had also begun startling
violently when a congregant touched her arm during a greeting, a physical reflex she could not
suppress and could not explain to people who knew her as warm and steady.

The smell was the most disabling trigger in her daily life. Miriam's congregation hosts a weekly
community dinner, and the kitchen adjoins the sanctuary corridor she walks every Shabbat. On two
occasions when something was left too long on the stove, the faint smell of scorching drifted into
the hallway and Miriam froze mid-step, her body flooding with a response she could neither reason
away nor control. She had come to believe that her nervous system was simply broken — that it had
been rewired to read safety as danger, and that no amount of conscious reassurance could override
it. She described covering her face with her hands in those moments, standing in the corridor of her
own synagogue, unable to move until the smell dissipated.

Over the following months, Miriam developed habits she recognized, on some level, as harmful. She
began eating heavily in the evenings — not from hunger, she said, but because it was the only thing
that reliably blunted the agitation that built through the day. She also found herself driving
faster than she should, taking highway on-ramps at speeds that alarmed her own passengers, and she
could not fully account for why. She forced herself, especially in public and especially on the
bimah, to hold only positive thoughts — to mentally narrate gratitude and resilience over whatever
was actually moving through her — and the effort of that had become its own form of exhaustion.

What frightened Miriam most, sitting in the intake session, was not any single symptom but the
aggregate sense of self-estrangement. She had spent her adult life as a person who held others'
grief, who stood at the edge of loss and did not flinch. The woman who now covered her face in
hallways, who ate alone in her kitchen at midnight, who could not be touched without flinching — she
did not recognize her. She felt set apart from her congregation in a way she could not articulate
from the pulpit, and she had not told anyone what was happening. What finally brought her to seek
help was a bat mitzvah ceremony two weeks prior, during which the smell of the Shabbat candles — not
burnt, merely present — had made her grip the lectern and force her way through the service by sheer
will. She came to therapy hoping, she said carefully, "to find out if I am still in there
somewhere."
