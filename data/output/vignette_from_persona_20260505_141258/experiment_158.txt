Meredith Calloway is a 58-year-old single woman of European descent who currently serves as a city
council member in a mid-sized American city. She presented for psychological assessment
approximately fourteen months after returning from a six-week humanitarian deployment to a conflict
zone in Eastern Europe, where she had volunteered with a relief organization coordinating medical
supply distribution to civilian populations near active front lines. She had no prior mental health
history and described herself, before the deployment, as someone who had spent three decades in
public service without significant psychological difficulty.

The incident that precipitated her current difficulties occurred during her fourth week in the
field. Meredith and two local colleagues were unloading supply crates inside a partially damaged
community center when a shell struck a building on the adjacent street. The blast wave shattered the
remaining windows and threw one of her colleagues against a concrete wall. In the seconds that
followed, Meredith found herself crouched on the floor beside the injured man, pressing her hands
against a wound on his side while dust and smoke filled the room. What she remembers most sharply
from those minutes is the sound of his breathing — a labored, wet, rhythmic pulling of air — and the
helplessness of not knowing whether he would survive. He did survive, but Meredith flew home ten
days later without having spoken to anyone about what she had witnessed.

In the months that followed her return, she noticed that her memory of the event had taken on a
quality she found deeply unsettling. When she tried to recall what had happened, it came back to her
not as a coherent story she owned but as a series of still images — the man's face, her own blood-
covered hands, a particular angle of grey light through broken glass — that felt as though they
belonged to footage of a stranger's life. Yet despite this sense of distance, the memory did not
stay where she tried to keep it. Images and fragments forced themselves into her awareness without
invitation: during morning briefings at city hall, while drafting budget proposals, while sitting in
silence before bed. She had come to believe that if she allowed herself to think about the event
deliberately, something inside her would give way entirely — that she would lose her composure in a
way she could not recover from — and so she worked to hold it at arm's length, pushing it back
whenever it surfaced.

Her professional environment has made this harder than she anticipated. City council sessions
occasionally include public testimony about infrastructure failures, emergency response gaps, or
construction accidents, and the acoustics of the council chamber mean that a slammed door or a
microphone feedback burst can arrive with the sudden, percussive quality of a distant explosion. On
three separate occasions in the past year, such sounds have stopped her mid-sentence. Colleagues
have noticed, and she has offered explanations about fatigue. She has also begun arriving at
sessions earlier than necessary to survey the room before it fills — a habit she has not named to
herself but which has quietly consumed an additional hour of most working days.

What has frightened Meredith most, however, is not the intrusions or the sounds but what she has
come to believe about her inner life. She had always understood herself as a person of feeling —
moved by constituent stories, energized by community work. Over the past year she has noticed that
emotional responses she once took for granted seem to have gone quiet. She sat through her niece's
wedding last spring and felt, she said, like a person watching it through glass. She has come to
believe this is permanent — that whatever capacity she once had for ordinary emotional life was used
up or broken in that community center — and this conviction has settled into her as a kind of quiet
grief. She also carries a broader sense that the world she returned to is not the world she left:
that danger is closer and more arbitrary than she had previously understood, and that this knowledge
cannot be unknowing.

What finally brought her to seek help was a council vote on an emergency preparedness resolution. As
she read aloud a passage describing mass casualty scenarios, she heard, beneath her own voice, the
sound of her colleague's breathing. She stopped, collected herself, and finished the session. That
evening she made the appointment. She told the intake coordinator she was not sure therapy could
give her back what she had lost, but that she needed to find out.
