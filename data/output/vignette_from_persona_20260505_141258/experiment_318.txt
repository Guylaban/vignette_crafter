Marguerite Calloway is a sixty-six-year-old widowed woman who has worked as a professional model for
most of her adult life, a career that has taken her from regional catalogue shoots in her thirties
to, more recently, editorial work for brands targeting older consumers. She presents for an initial
psychological evaluation approximately fourteen months after an incident in which she accidentally
struck a pedestrian with her vehicle, causing the woman serious and lasting physical injury.
Marguerite's PCL-5 score of 41 places her within the clinical range for PTSD, and the picture that
emerges across intake and early sessions is one of a woman who has not had a single uncomplicated
day since the afternoon the accident occurred.

It happened on a Tuesday in late autumn, just after dusk. Marguerite had been leaving a studio
fitting on the outskirts of the city, running slightly behind, distracted by a phone call she had
just ended. The light was poor and the pedestrian — a middle-aged woman — had stepped from between
two parked delivery vans into the road. The impact was immediate and the sound, Marguerite has said
more than once, was something she cannot locate language for. She stayed at the scene. She called
for help. She held the woman's hand until the ambulance arrived. The woman survived but sustained a
fractured pelvis and spinal damage that required months of rehabilitation and left her with a
permanent limp.

In the weeks that followed, Marguerite found herself returning obsessively to a single question: why
had she not slowed down sooner? She had been on a familiar stretch of road. She had driven it dozens
of times. What frightened her most was not the memory of the impact itself but the conviction, which
had settled into her like something calcified, that another person — a more careful person, a more
attentive person — would never have been in that situation at all. She had come to believe that the
accident revealed something essential and damning about her character, a conclusion she held with a
certainty that no reassurance from friends or her attorney had been able to touch. She felt
permanently humiliated, not only by the public nature of the incident — there had been a brief local
news item — but by what she understood it to say about who she was. She was certain she would never
forgive herself for not having done more in those seconds before impact, even as she acknowledged,
in a quieter part of herself, that there had been almost no time at all.

Her body has remained in a state of readiness that exhausts her. She cannot sit still through a meal
or a television programme. Her muscles carry a tension she notices most acutely in her shoulders and
jaw, and she has described lying in bed at night feeling as though her body is braced for something
that has not yet arrived. Concentration has become unreliable in ways that have begun to affect her
professionally. During shoots — which she has continued, in part because work has always been a
source of structure — she loses the thread of a director's instruction mid-sentence, has to ask for
things to be repeated, and has noticed the barely concealed impatience of colleagues who knew her
previously as composed and precise. Last spring, a booking she had held for several months was
quietly reassigned to another model. No formal explanation was given, but Marguerite understood. She
has said nothing about this to anyone.

What finally brought her to seek help was a conversation with her daughter, who visited in the
autumn and told her, plainly and with evident worry, that she seemed like a different person.
Marguerite did not disagree. She made the referral call the following week, though she spent several
days beforehand rehearsing reasons not to. She arrives at sessions composed in the way of someone
who has long been accustomed to being looked at, but beneath that composure she is carrying a grief
she has not yet named — for the woman she injured, yes, but also for the version of herself she has
not been able to find her way back to. What she hopes for, she has said, is to stop feeling like
someone who is waiting to be punished. What she fears is that she deserves to wait.
