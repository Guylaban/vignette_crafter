Marta Brennan is a 35-year-old married woman of Irish-Polish descent who works as a counselor at a
community mental health center in a mid-sized Midwestern city. She presented for treatment
approximately eight months after an incident that she describes with considerable difficulty — not
because she is unwilling to speak, but because, as she has explained in early sessions, the event
itself does not come back to her in any orderly way. What she can access arrives in pieces: the
smell of gasoline, the sound of a car door, a color she cannot quite place, a silence that followed
something loud. She cannot, even now, give a clear account of what happened in sequence, and this
inability distresses her as much as the memories themselves.

The incident occurred on a rainy evening in early autumn. Marta had been driving home from a long
shift, tired in the way that accumulates over weeks of absorbing other people's pain. She misjudged
a turn in a residential neighborhood and struck a cyclist — a young man, as it turned out, who
sustained a serious spinal injury. He survived, but the nature of his recovery remains uncertain.
Marta stayed at the scene, called emergency services, knelt beside him while waiting. She remembers
fragments: his jacket, the way his helmet had shifted, the sound of the ambulance arriving. She does
not remember what she said to him, and this gap haunts her.

In the weeks that followed, Marta found herself unable to sleep with any consistency, lying awake in
the dark with a vigilance she could not explain rationally but could not switch off — scanning
sounds in the house, her husband's breathing, the particular quality of silence that preceded
nothing at all. She began trying, with considerable effort, not to think about the accident at all,
pushing thoughts away the moment they surfaced, which meant that much of her day was organized
around a kind of internal surveillance.

She has come to believe, with a certainty that feels unshakeable to her, that what happened was
entirely a result of how she was that evening — her fatigue, her inattention, a choice she made. She
does not frame it as an accident in any meaningful sense. In her understanding, she failed to
protect someone who had every reason to expect safety on a public road, and that failure belongs to
her alone. What frightens her most is not the legal aftermath, which has been resolved, but the
moral one. She has told her therapist that she no longer understands what her work means, that
sitting with clients in distress feels hollow in a way she cannot articulate — as though the
permission she once felt to help others has been revoked.

This has had concrete consequences at the clinic. Marta avoids sustained eye contact with clients
now, something she once considered a cornerstone of her practice. Colleagues have noticed a flatness
in her manner. She declined to co-facilitate a psychoeducation group she had run for two years,
citing scheduling, though the real reason was that the group met in a room adjacent to the
building's medical consultation suite, and she cannot walk past examination rooms or hear the sound
of an ambulance without a wave of feeling she describes as overwhelming and formless — grief, she
thinks, though also something closer to horror.

She has not visited a doctor since the incident, has rescheduled two routine appointments, and has
asked her husband to drive a different route to avoid passing the hospital where the young man was
initially treated. Her husband has been patient, but Marta is aware that she has become difficult to
reach — that she deflects his questions, that she is present in their home in only the most
functional sense.

What finally brought her to seek help was a session with one of her own clients, a man processing
survivor's guilt, who described feeling that he no longer deserved to take up space in the world.
Marta recognized the feeling precisely. She made her own referral that afternoon. She is hoping, she
says carefully, that she might eventually be able to do her job again. What she is most afraid of is
that she will be told she cannot.
