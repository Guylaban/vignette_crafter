Marisol is a 78-year-old widowed Salvadoran American woman who works part-time as a call center
agent from her apartment in a mid-sized Texas city. She was referred for psychological evaluation
following a routine check-in with her primary care physician, who noted her elevated distress and
persistent somatic complaints. Her PCL-5 score of 73 places her in a range of severe posttraumatic
stress, and the picture that emerged across the intake and first two sessions was one of a woman
quietly carrying an enormous weight, largely alone.

Several years ago, Marisol was present during a terrorist attack at a public market she visited
regularly — a place she had gone to since arriving in the United States decades earlier, a place
that smelled like the markets of her childhood. The attack unfolded without warning. There was an
explosion, then chaos, then a period she describes only in fragments: a ringing in her ears, the
sensation of something striking the back of her neck, the sight of strangers on the ground. What she
cannot account for — and what distresses her profoundly — is a stretch of time she simply cannot
reconstruct. She knows she was there. She knows she survived. But there is a gap, and no amount of
effort has filled it. When she tries to speak about what happened, she finds herself unable to
locate the words, as though language itself has been stripped from the experience. She will start a
sentence and stop, her hands moving slightly, reaching for something that isn't there. She has
described this to the evaluator as feeling like she is trying to explain a colour that has no name.

In the months following the attack, Marisol quietly reorganized her life around what she could not
bear to approach. She has not returned to that part of the city. The anniversary of the attack
passes each year with her curtains drawn and her phone turned off. What she has come to believe,
with a certainty that feels absolute to her, is that she failed the people she loved most — that a
woman of her age and experience should have done something, helped someone, been more than a
survivor. Her late husband, she says, would have acted. She has also come to believe that there is
simply no future left for her, that the arc of her life ended at that market and what remains is a
kind of aftermath. What frightens her most is not death but the suspicion that she cannot protect
anyone she loves from harm — that bad things will find their way to her and to those around her, and
she will be powerless each time.

This sense of helplessness has shaped how she moves through her days. She works her call center
shifts from home, and it is there that her triggers arrive uninvited. Callers sometimes describe
losses — a family member's illness, a bereavement, a sudden tragedy — and when they do, Marisol
feels something shift in her body before she is fully aware of what she is hearing. The pain in her
neck, a dull and persistent ache she has had since the attack, sharpens in those moments. She has
learned to mute herself briefly on calls when this happens, to breathe, to wait for it to pass. Her
supervisors have noted that her call times have lengthened and that she occasionally disconnects
before resolution. She has not explained why.

She has also withdrawn from her daughter and her two grandchildren, keeping visits short and
conversation light. Being emotionally close to them, she has found, opens something in her she
cannot close again easily. It is safer, she has decided, to love them from a careful distance.

What finally brought Marisol to seek help was not a crisis but an accumulation — a grandchild's
offhand question about why she never talks about the past, and the realisation that she had no
answer. She arrived at the intake quietly, dressed carefully, and said very little at first. What
she hoped for, she eventually offered, was simply to find the words.
