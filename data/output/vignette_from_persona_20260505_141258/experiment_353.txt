medicine at a mid-sized community hospital in the Chicago area for over three decades. He is a
careful, methodical clinician, known among colleagues for his composure under pressure. It was that
reputation — and perhaps the private pride he took in it — that made what happened to his son two
years ago so devastating, and so difficult to speak about even now.

His son Mateo, then 34, collapsed without warning during a family gathering at Rodrigo's apartment.
What followed were several hours in the emergency department of the very hospital where Rodrigo
works, watching a team of physicians — his colleagues, his trainees — work on his child. Rodrigo
stood at the periphery of the resuscitation bay, stripped of every professional role he had ever
occupied, unable to do anything except watch monitors and read faces. At certain points the team
asked him to step outside. He complied, standing in the corridor under fluorescent lights, and it
was in that corridor — with its particular smell of antiseptic and floor wax, its particular quality
of sound — that some of the worst moments occurred. Mateo survived, after a prolonged ICU course,
but the outcome was uncertain for many days, and the period of not knowing lodged itself in Rodrigo
with a force that surprised him.

In the weeks that followed, Rodrigo noticed that his memory of those hours was strangely incomplete.
He could recall fragments — the color of Mateo's shirt, a nurse's expression, the sound of an alarm
— but they did not connect into anything he could narrate, even to himself. When he tried to
reconstruct a clear sequence of events, the effort dissolved. He knew, in an abstract sense, what
had happened, but when he reached for language to describe it, words failed him entirely. He would
sit at his desk after a long shift, attempting to write something in his private journal, and find
himself staring at a blank page, the pen motionless.

He returned to clinical work within two weeks, partly because he believed staying busy was the
responsible thing to do, and partly because keeping his mind occupied had become something close to
a necessity. He began scheduling himself for extra shifts, filling every available hour, accepting
consultations he might once have declined. In the evenings he watched television for hours without
registering what he saw, anything to prevent his thoughts from turning back toward those hospital
corridors. When thoughts of Mateo's illness surfaced anyway, he pushed them aside with a deliberate,
almost muscular effort, and for a time this seemed to work.

But the hospital itself had become treacherous ground. Walking past the ICU on rounds, catching the
particular smell of the corridor where he had waited, hearing a certain pattern of monitor alarms —
any of these could produce a moment in which Rodrigo was no longer in the present. He would be back
in that corridor, certain in his body that it was happening again, that Mateo was dying behind a
door he could not open. These episodes were brief but left him shaken and, he felt, dangerously
distracted during patient care. He had come to believe that he was no longer fully reliable as a

monthly critical care case conferences he had once helped organize. Rodrigo offered a vague answer
about scheduling.
