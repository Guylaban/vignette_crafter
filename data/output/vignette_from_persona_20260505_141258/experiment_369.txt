house. Marcus had been the one to receive the call from the hospital, and he arrived before Daniel
was pronounced dead — close enough to see him on the gurney, close enough to hold his hand in the
final minutes. The image of his son's face in that hospital bay, pale and still in a way Marcus had
never seen, became the center of a memory that would not recede.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus moved through the expected rituals of grief — the funeral
arrangements, the burial, the casseroles from neighbors — but something shifted in him that he could
not name at the time. He began to notice that the memory of the hospital did not behave the way he
expected a memory to behave. It did not soften or recede. Instead, it returned with a vividness that
felt less like recollection and more like re-entry: the antiseptic smell, the sound of the monitor
flatlining, the weight of Daniel's hand going slack. When these episodes came, Marcus was certain he
was back in that room. The present moment — his shop counter, his kitchen table, his wife beside him
— dissolved entirely. He had come to believe that something in his mind had broken in a way that
could not be repaired, that he was condemned to relive that afternoon on a loop he could not switch
off.
