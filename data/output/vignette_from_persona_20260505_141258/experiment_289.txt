Valentina Reyes is a 26-year-old Colombian American graduate student who presented for psychological
assessment approximately eight months after a toxic chemical exposure that occurred in the
laboratory where she worked as a research assistant. She had been alone in the building late on a
winter evening, finishing a preparation protocol that required handling an industrial solvent, when
a faulty ventilation hood failed without warning. The fumes accumulated rapidly in the enclosed
space. Valentina recalls the moment the smell hit her — sharp, synthetic, coating the back of her
throat — and then the disorientation that followed: her vision narrowing, her legs losing
coordination, her chest tightening as she tried to reach the door. She spent three days in the
hospital, and it was there, in the antiseptic-scented corridors and under the fluorescent monitoring
lights, that much of her acute distress was processed — or, more precisely, not processed. She was
discharged with a clean bill of physical health and returned to her apartment.

In the weeks that followed, the experience did not resolve into a coherent story she could tell
herself or others. When she tried to reconstruct what had happened, the sequence came apart in her
hands — she could not say with any certainty whether she had called for help before or after she
fell, whether she had been conscious when the paramedics arrived, whether minutes or seconds had
passed between the first smell and the loss of control. What she was left with were fragments: the
chemical odour, the darkness of the lab after the emergency lighting flickered out, the sensation of
her body no longer obeying her. When she tried to explain any of this to her mother or her cohort
advisor, she found that the words simply were not there. She knew, intellectually, what had
happened. She could not speak it.

The triggers that emerged in the months following her discharge were precise and unforgiving. The
smell of antiseptic — in a campus clinic, in a friend's bathroom, once in a grocery store cleaning
aisle — produced immediate and overwhelming physical reactions: a sudden constriction in her chest,
a burning sensation in her throat that mirrored what she had felt on the night itself, as though her
body were reliving the exposure rather than remembering it. She had come to understand this as
evidence that something in her was fundamentally broken. Her body, she believed, could no longer be
trusted to distinguish past from present. She was certain there was something wrong with her at a
level deeper than symptoms — that this was not an injury but a revelation of a defect that had
always been there.

Darkness became another terrain she could not safely occupy. She began sleeping with every light in
her apartment on, and waking in the night — disoriented, heart already hammering before she was
fully conscious — had become a near-nightly occurrence. She had stopped going into the university
library's basement stacks, where she had previously spent hours working. Her research output
declined noticeably, and her supervisor had commented on it, which Valentina experienced not as
concern but as confirmation.

She avoided the building where the exposure occurred entirely, taking a longer route across campus
that added fifteen minutes to her commute. She scanned every room she entered — noting the exits,
the ventilation, the proximity of other people — in a way that felt involuntary and exhausting. When
a classmate touched her shoulder from behind during a seminar, she flinched so violently that the
room went quiet. She laughed it off, but she did not return to that seminar.

She had tried, methodically and unsuccessfully, to simply not think about it — to overwrite the
memory with other content, to refuse it when it surfaced. This had not worked, but she continued
trying because the alternative seemed worse.

What finally brought her to seek help was a conversation with her mother, who told her she seemed
like a different person. Valentina did not disagree. What she most feared, sitting in the intake
session, was that the therapist would confirm what she already suspected: that the person she had
been before that evening in the laboratory was gone, and that what remained was a body she could no
longer trust, living in a danger that never ended.
