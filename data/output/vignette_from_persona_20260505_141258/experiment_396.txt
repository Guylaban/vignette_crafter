Rajan Mehta is a 41-year-old South Asian American graphic designer who lives with his wife in a mid-
sized city in the Pacific Northwest. He came to the clinic after more than a year of what he
described, haltingly, as "not being able to get back to normal." He struggled to say much more than
that in the first session, not because he lacked intelligence or willingness, but because whenever
he tried to recount what had happened, the thread of the story seemed to dissolve before he could
finish a sentence. He would begin, pause, look at his hands, and say only that it was "complicated"
— that he knew what had happened, he simply could not seem to find the words to make it land.

Approximately eighteen months ago, Rajan was detained by police following what he later learned was
a tip provided by a colleague — someone he had considered a close friend, a man he had mentored and
trusted for several years at the small design studio where he worked. The tip alleged, falsely, that
Rajan had been involved in financial misconduct at the firm. Officers arrived at his home on a
weekday morning. He remembers the smell most vividly: something acrid and burnt, which he later
understood came from a flare or device used during the initial approach to his property. That smell
embedded itself in the encounter with a precision that nothing else has matched. He was handcuffed
in his front doorway while his wife watched from the hallway. He was held for several hours,
questioned, and ultimately released without charge. No formal apology was issued. The colleague who
had made the report was never disciplined.

In the weeks that followed, Rajan found himself replaying the morning with an intensity that felt
less like memory and more like re-entry. When he tries to recall the event, what rises most
immediately is not fear but guilt — a grinding, sourceless guilt that he has not been able to
explain or argue himself out of. He had come to believe, somewhere beneath rational thought, that a
person who was truly innocent would not have been treated that way. This belief has not left him.

He began noticing, gradually, that certain things in his daily environment had become unbearable.
The burnt smell from a colleague's lunch in the break room microwave, or from a candle in a nearby
shop, would stop him cold. He began routing his commute to avoid the stretch of road where a police
substation sits, and on the occasions when he encounters an officer or a security guard — anyone in
uniform — he feels his body reorganize itself around vigilance, scanning for intention, for danger,
for the moment things might turn. He had come to believe that the world contains threats he simply
had not been alert enough to see before, and that most people, given the right circumstances, would
not protect him.

The rehearsing began quietly. Before any workplace meeting, before calling his bank, before speaking
to a landlord or a contractor, Rajan would spend considerable time mentally scripting what he would
say, anticipating every turn the conversation might take, preparing for the moment authority might
assert itself. At the design studio, this has meant that what used to be easy creative collaboration
has become effortful and slow. He has missed two client pitches by over-preparing to the point of
paralysis, and his manager has noted the change in his output without knowing why.

He stopped making plans. Not dramatically, not all at once, but steadily — holidays that were never
booked, a home renovation left unscheduled, a conversation with his wife about starting a family
that has simply not been resumed. The future had come to feel like a space he could not trust,
because he had once trusted it and had been wrong.

What finally brought him to seek help was his wife. She told him, quietly and without accusation,
that she felt she had lost him — that she was living with someone who was present in body but absent
in some essential way. Rajan said he was afraid that she was right. What he hopes for, he said at
the end of the intake, is to feel like a person who can be known again — not just managed, not just
survived around, but actually known.
