Marta Kowalski is a 41-year-old Polish-American woman who has served as a firefighter for sixteen
years. She presents for psychological assessment with a PCL-5 score of 68, referred by her
department's employee assistance program after colleagues noticed significant changes in her
functioning over the past year.

Fourteen months ago, Marta was admitted to the emergency department following a sudden cardiac event
during a routine training exercise. What began as chest tightness became, within minutes, a full
arrest. She was resuscitated on the gymnasium floor by her own crewmates, then rushed to hospital
where she spent eleven days in the cardiac intensive care unit. During that time she was intubated,
connected to multiple drip lines, and subjected to repeated procedures — echocardiograms,
catheterisations, blood draws that came so frequently she lost count. She recalled lying immobile
under fluorescent lights, unable to speak around the ventilator tube, watching the IV lines run into
her arms and feeling entirely disconnected from her own body, as though she were watching someone
else be worked on. The smell of the unit — that particular antiseptic sharpness, somewhere between
bleach and plastic — was constant. She could not escape it. When she was finally extubated, her
first coherent thought was that she had been certain she would not survive.

In the weeks following her discharge, Marta found that the experience resisted being shaped into any
kind of story she could tell. When her husband, Darek, asked her what it had been like, she would
open her mouth and find nothing — not silence exactly, but an absence of language, as though the
event existed in a register that words could not reach. What did come back were physical things: a
sudden heaviness in her arms, a constriction across her chest, her body arranging itself into the
flat, helpless position she had occupied in that hospital bed. These returns arrived without warning
and brought with them the same terror she had felt under those lights — not a memory of terror, but
terror itself, present and total.

By autumn she had quietly reorganised her life around avoidance of anything that might open that
door. She declined a routine follow-up cardiology appointment, then a second one, telling Darek the
scheduling was difficult. She began driving longer routes to avoid passing the hospital where she
had been treated. At the station, she had always been the crew member who coordinated with
paramedics on scene, but she found she could no longer stand near the ambulance bay — the antiseptic
smell that clung to the rigs, the glimpse of an IV bag through open doors, was enough to produce a
physical reaction so immediate and overwhelming that she would have to step away and focus hard on
something else, deliberately redirecting her attention until the sensation passed. Over time she
stopped volunteering for medical-assist calls altogether, citing administrative reasons. Her
lieutenant has not yet questioned this directly, but the pattern has not gone unnoticed, and Marta
is aware that her credibility within the crew is quietly eroding.

At home she has become difficult to reach emotionally. She had come to believe, without quite
articulating it to herself, that allowing any strong feeling to surface risked losing control
entirely — that the feelings connected to the illness were too large to be contained once started.
So she keeps the emotional temperature low, deflecting Darek's attempts at closeness with
practicality and task-focus. He has told her he feels shut out. She does not disagree, but she does
not know how to explain what she is managing.

Throughout her days — at the station, at the grocery store, in her own kitchen — Marta moves with a
vigilance that exhausts her. She scans rooms without meaning to, tracking exits and anomalies, her
body held in a low-grade readiness that never fully releases. She does not feel safe in her own
physiology anymore, and that is perhaps the most frightening thing: that the danger, when it came,
had been internal.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment last month when she froze entirely during a
structure fire — not from fear of the fire, but because the medic on scene uncapped a syringe
nearby. She stood in full gear, unable to move, for several seconds. She has not told anyone what
stopped her. She comes to treatment hoping, above everything, to be able to do her job again — and
afraid that what happened to her body once might mean she can no longer trust it.
