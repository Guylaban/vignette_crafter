Boon-Nam Srisawat is a 66-year-old Thai-American man who has worked as a roofer for nearly four
decades, a trade he took up after immigrating to the United States in his late twenties. He lives
with his wife in a modest home in a suburb of Houston, and until recently he was known among his
crew as steady and capable — the kind of man who could read a roofline by feel and who rarely needed
to be told something twice. That version of himself is one he now thinks about with a grief he
cannot fully articulate.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Boon-Nam was part of a crew re-roofing a commercial building that
had been flagged for renovation but not yet fully cleared. Without adequate warning, he and two
colleagues spent the better part of a morning working in close proximity to improperly stored
industrial solvents — chemicals that had been sealed behind a ventilation panel and that, when
disturbed by the work above, began leaking fumes into the enclosed attic space where Boon-Nam was
laying underlayment. He noticed the smell first: sharp, almost sweet, but wrong in a way he could
not name. Within minutes his eyes were streaming and his chest felt as though a hand were pressing
from inside. By the time he understood what was happening, he was disoriented and struggling to find
the hatch he had climbed through. He remembers being on his hands and knees, unable to call out,
convinced in that moment that he was dying alone in the dark and the heat, with no one who could see
him. He was eventually pulled out by a colleague and transported by ambulance to a hospital, where
he was treated for acute chemical inhalation. He was discharged after two days, told his lung
function would likely recover, and sent home.

The recovery, however, has not followed the trajectory anyone described to him. In the weeks after
the incident, Boon-Nam began waking in the night, sometimes in complete darkness, and finding
himself back in that attic — not remembering it, but inhabiting it, his chest tight, his body
certain that the air was wrong. His memories of the event are extraordinarily vivid, detailed in
ways that feel almost photographic: the particular grain of the plywood beneath his palms, the
quality of the light through the gap in the eaves. These memories arrive without invitation,
replaying with a completeness that exhausts him. There are also moments when the emotional weight of
the event descends without any accompanying image — a sudden wave of dread or helplessness that he
cannot locate or explain, which leaves him feeling as though something is happening to him that he
cannot control or predict.

Smell has become a source of particular vigilance. When his wife uses certain cleaning products in
the house, Boon-Nam leaves the room without explanation. A visit to his physician last spring — the
antiseptic smell of the waiting room — sent him into a state of rigid, silent distress that he
managed by sitting near the exit and focusing on the floor. He has not told his doctor about this.
He has not told his wife the full extent of what he experiences, partly because he has come to
believe that disagreeing or asserting himself in any conversation risks something he cannot afford
to lose, and so he has learned to say little and concede much. He tries, with considerable effort,
to not think about the event at all, and yet the effort itself seems to keep him tethered to it.

He no longer takes jobs that involve enclosed or unfamiliar structures. He has turned down work that
would have paid well, offering his foreman vague explanations. He cannot fully relax even sitting in
his own living room, and his concentration — once reliable, once a point of quiet professional pride
— has become unreliable in ways that shame him. He finds himself dwelling on who he was before: a
man who could trust his own body and his own judgment. He has come to feel that he cannot rely on
that man anymore, that the world contains dangers that do not announce themselves, and that his
future has a quality of foreclosure he cannot see past.

What finally brought him to seek help was his wife asking him, quietly, whether he was still there.
He did not know how to answer her. He is hoping, tentatively, that someone can tell him whether he
can come back. What he fears most is that the answer is no.
