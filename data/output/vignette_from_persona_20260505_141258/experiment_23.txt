Dr. Aroha Ngata is a 58-year-old New Zealand-born psychiatrist, divorced, who has practiced in the
United States for over two decades. She came to her first appointment carrying the particular
discomfort of a clinician who knows exactly what she is experiencing and finds that knowledge offers
no relief whatsoever.

Fourteen months ago, Aroha was driving home from a late hospital consultation on a rain-slicked
interstate when a pickup truck ran a red light at speed and struck the driver's side of her car. The
impact spun her vehicle across two lanes. She remembers the headlights filling her window —
enormous, white, arriving without any warning interval she could process — and then a period she
cannot account for, followed by the sensation of being upside down and the smell of something
burning. She does not know how long she was suspended in the wreckage before paramedics arrived. She
sustained a fractured clavicle, three broken ribs, and a concussion. She was discharged after four
days. The physical injuries healed. The rest did not.

In the weeks after the accident, Aroha noticed that the memory refused to arrange itself into
anything she could hold steadily. When she tried to recount what had happened — even privately, even
just to herself — the sequence dissolved. She could not place the moment of impact in relation to
the skid; she could not establish whether she had lost consciousness before or after the car stopped
moving. The story had no beginning she could locate and no middle she could trust. What she had
instead were fragments that arrived without invitation: the white headlights, the smell, the
disorientation of the inverted cabin. These did not come as recollections. They came as
reinstatements — sudden, total, indistinguishable in their first instant from the present moment. A
colleague describing a patient's car accident in a case conference. A film trailer glimpsed on a
waiting-room television showing a collision. Any of these could return her, without transition, to
the interior of her spinning car.

Aroha had come to believe, with a quiet certainty she found difficult to examine, that she had been
permanently altered. She is a psychiatrist who has spent her career helping patients regulate affect
and tolerate uncertainty; she now found herself unable to trust her own clinical judgment, sitting
with patients and wondering whether she would do the right thing in a critical moment. This self-
doubt had begun to shape her practice in ways she had not fully acknowledged until recently.

She stopped driving at night almost immediately. Over the following months she stopped driving on
highways altogether. She arranged her consulting room so that her chair faced the door with her back
to the wall, telling herself it was a minor preference. She began waking in the small hours — not
from nightmares, but alert, listening, checking. She had developed the habit of going to bed with
the lamp on because lying in the dark felt intolerable in a way she could not fully articulate, and
she had not slept a full night since the accident. She worked hard to keep her face neutral and her
voice measured with patients, expending considerable effort on the management of her own internal
state during sessions. Intimacy had become impossible; she had ended a relationship of two years,
offering her former partner an explanation that satisfied neither of them.

The consequence that finally brought her to seek help was professional. Three months ago she made a
prescribing error — minor, caught quickly, no harm done — but it confirmed what she had been trying
not to believe: that the woman who had practiced psychiatry with confidence for twenty-five years
was no longer entirely present at work. She had sat in her car afterward, heart pounding at the
sound of traffic passing outside, and understood that she could not manage this alone.

What Aroha most fears is that the damage is exactly as permanent as it feels. What she most hopes,
though she states it with the careful hedging of someone who has learned not to want things too
openly, is that she might be wrong about that.
