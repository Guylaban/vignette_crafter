Harjit Sandhu is a 61-year-old South Asian man who has worked as a paramedic for nearly three
decades. He is married and has spent most of his adult life running toward emergencies that others
flee. He presented for psychological assessment approximately eight months after a chemical exposure
incident that he describes as unlike anything in his long career.

The incident occurred on a night shift. Harjit and his partner were dispatched to an industrial
facility where a worker had collapsed. The scene was poorly lit, and Harjit entered a storage area
in near darkness before any hazmat protocols had been established. Within seconds of entering, he
registered an acrid, chemical smell — sharp and unfamiliar — and almost simultaneously began to feel
his eyes burning, his chest tightening, and his legs weakening. He recalls the disorientation of not
knowing what he was breathing, of calling out to his partner and hearing his own voice sound
strange, of dropping to one knee on the concrete floor and being uncertain whether he would be able
to stand again. He was evacuated by colleagues and treated on site, then transported to the
emergency department. Physically, he recovered within days. What did not recover was his sense of
the world as a place where his training and experience were sufficient protection.

In the weeks that followed, Harjit found that the memory of those minutes in the dark did not behave
like other difficult memories from his career. It did not stay where he put it. A colleague opening
a bottle of rubbing alcohol in the ambulance bay — the sharp, solvent edge of the smell — could
return him instantly and completely to that storage room floor, the burning in his eyes, the terror
of not knowing whether he was dying. The transition was not gradual; it was total and without
warning. When the memory arrived in this way, Harjit described feeling so overwhelmed by emotion
that he had to physically stop what he was doing and grip something solid until it passed.
Attempting to recall the event deliberately produced the same terror he had experienced in the
moment — not a muted echo but the original fear, as immediate as the night itself.

Harjit had come to believe, with considerable conviction, that if he had simply waited for the
hazmat team, none of this would have happened — that his decision to enter early was a failure of
judgment that he should have known better than to make at his age, with his experience. He returned
to the sequence of that evening repeatedly, reconstructing it, testing alternative decisions, unable
to arrive at a version in which he was not culpable. This mental activity occupied long stretches of
his evenings and early mornings.

To prevent those stretches from becoming too long or too quiet, Harjit began filling every available
hour with company. He arranged family dinners on nights when none had been planned, lingered at the
station longer than his shifts required, and became, in his wife's words, unusually reluctant to be
alone in any room of the house. What he understood himself to be doing was keeping the thoughts at
bay; what accumulated over months was a kind of exhaustion in the people around him, a subtle but
growing friction with his wife, who felt simultaneously sought out and shut out — present in body
but not in the conversation she actually wanted to have with him.

At work, the difficulties became harder to conceal. Harjit began requesting reassignment away from
hospital handovers, citing administrative reasons. He delayed or avoided mandatory physical
examinations, rescheduling twice before his supervisor noted the pattern. The ambulance itself — the
smell of it, the equipment, the interior lighting that reminded him in some peripheral way of
industrial spaces — had become a place he entered with effortful deliberation rather than
professional ease.

What finally brought him to seek help was a near-miss: a moment at a handover bay when a nurse
opened an alcohol swab beside him and Harjit, a man who had worked trauma scenes without flinching
for twenty-eight years, had to excuse himself and stand outside in the cold for several minutes
before he could return. He told his wife that evening. He is most afraid that the version of himself
that walked confidently into emergencies is gone. He is hoping, cautiously, to be told that it is
not.
