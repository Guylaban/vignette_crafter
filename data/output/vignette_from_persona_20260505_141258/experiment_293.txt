Priya Chandrasekaran is a 73-year-old widowed South Asian woman who has spent the better part of
four decades building a respected interior design practice in the Atlanta metropolitan area. Her
work has always been tactile and sensory — she has made her living by running her hands across
fabric swatches, feeling the grain of upholstery, selecting textures that communicate warmth or
authority or calm. It is a bitter irony, then, that touch has become one of the things she most
fears.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Priya was present at a peaceful community demonstration in her
neighborhood, attending in solidarity with younger members of her local temple. What began as an
orderly afternoon gathering escalated when a line of officers in full riot gear moved into the
crowd. In the confusion, Priya was pushed to the ground, and as she attempted to rise, an officer
pressed a knee and a gloved hand against her back and held her there on the pavement for what she
estimates was several minutes. She recalls the coarse weave of her own jacket grinding against the
asphalt, the weight on her spine, voices shouting above her. Then her memory breaks apart. She knows
she was eventually released. She knows she walked to her car. The interval between those two facts
is largely missing, a gap she cannot account for no matter how carefully she reaches back toward it.
What she does have are pieces — a flash of a boot near her face, the particular scratch of riot-gear
fabric against her wrist, the dark navy of a uniform filling her entire field of vision — but these
pieces do not assemble into a coherent sequence. When she tries to tell the story, even to her
daughter, she finds herself stopped not by emotion but by an absence of language, as though the
experience exists in a register that words were not designed to reach.

In the months that followed, Priya's world contracted in ways that accumulated so gradually she did
not at first recognize them as a pattern. She stopped attending the temple's community events, then
stopped attending services altogether. She rerouted her daily errands to avoid the street where the
demonstration had taken place. When a police cruiser appeared in her rearview mirror, her hands went
cold on the steering wheel and her heart hammered in a way that felt wholly disproportionate and
wholly uncontrollable. She had come to believe that her body was simply no longer trustworthy — that
it would respond to the world with alarm regardless of what she consciously understood about her
safety, and that this represented a permanent change in who she was.

Her professional life has been affected in ways that distress her deeply. Priya works closely with
fabric samples, and on two separate occasions during client consultations she has encountered a
coarsely woven textile — a heavy linen, a nubbled upholstery — and found herself unable to continue
the meeting. She has not been able to explain this to clients in any way that preserves her
professional standing, and she lost one significant contract when she abruptly ended a presentation.
She has begun delegating the physical handling of fabric samples to her assistant, framing it as a
matter of her arthritis, and she is aware of the deception this requires.

What frightens Priya most now is not any single symptom but a broader conclusion she has drawn about
the remainder of her life. She is certain that nothing good can happen to her anymore — not in any
meaningful sense — and that this is simply the truth of her situation rather than a distortion of
it. When small difficulties arise, a delayed delivery, a miscommunication with a contractor, she
finds herself flooded and unable to manage in ways that would once have been trivial, and this
confirms her fear that whatever capacity she once had for resilience has been permanently spent.

It was her daughter who finally persuaded her to seek help, after Priya spent an evening unable to
stop weeping following a television news segment that showed officers in uniform. Priya agreed to an
intake appointment with considerable ambivalence. What she most hopes for, she said quietly at the
end of her first session, is simply to be able to do her work again — to touch fabric without fear.
What she most dreads is being told that she cannot.
