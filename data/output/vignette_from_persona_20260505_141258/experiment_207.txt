Marcus Holt is a 49-year-old plumber who has been married for eleven years and who, until recently,
managed to keep the worst of what had happened to him pressed down beneath the ordinary weight of a
working day. He presented for intake following what he described as "a breaking point" — a moment at
a job site three weeks prior that left him shaking in his truck for nearly an hour, unable to return
to work for the rest of the afternoon.

The events that brought Marcus to therapy occurred approximately two years ago, during a
relationship that preceded his current marriage. His partner at the time had a volatile temperament
that escalated over the course of eighteen months into repeated episodes of physical violence. The
incident that Marcus identifies as the most severe took place in her kitchen. An argument had begun
quietly — he remembers her voice staying almost controlled at first, measured in a way that, he now
understands, was its own kind of warning — before she struck him with a cast-iron pan that had been
left on the burner. The pan had been heating. The smell of scorched metal and the faint acrid trace
of burnt residue on the iron filled the room. He remembers that smell more precisely than he
remembers her face in that moment. He suffered a laceration to his forearm and a fractured wrist. He
did not call the police. He told his employer he had slipped on a wet floor.

In the months following, Marcus met his current wife, left the previous relationship entirely, and
attempted to move forward. For a time, this appeared to work. But the symptoms that have since
accumulated tell a different story. He finds himself unable to sleep at a normal hour, staying up
well past midnight most nights, sitting in front of the television or scrolling his phone until
exhaustion overtakes him. He cannot easily say why, only that going to bed earlier feels wrong in a
way he struggles to articulate. He has also worked hard not to think about what happened — not to
let his mind settle on the kitchen, the pan, the sequence of events — and has largely structured his
inner life around avoiding that territory. He has not returned to the neighborhood where the
apartment was located, rerouting his drives across that part of the city even when it adds twenty
minutes to a job.

At work, the triggers have become harder to manage. Plumbing frequently takes Marcus into
residential kitchens, and on the afternoon that finally brought him to seek help, he was repairing a
pipe beneath a sink when a homeowner left something on the stove and the faint smell of scorched
metal drifted through the room. He was back in that kitchen before he had any choice in the matter —
not as a memory he was reviewing but as something happening to him now, immediate and physical. He
made an excuse and left.

Confrontations on job sites have also become significant. When a general contractor raised his voice
at Marcus over a scheduling dispute last spring — not aggressively, Marcus acknowledged, just firmly
— Marcus found himself unable to respond, his chest tightening, his thoughts scattering. He had come
to believe that any expression of anger in his vicinity, even calm displeasure, carries within it
the possibility of sudden escalation. A colleague who snapped irritably at a supplier. A customer
who expressed frustration about a delayed repair. Each instance lands in Marcus with a force that
seems to him disproportionate but also, privately, entirely logical. He had learned, in that
relationship, that quiet anger was simply loud anger with the volume turned down.

His wife has noticed that he seems unreachable at times, and that he flinches at things she cannot
identify. He has told her very little. He had come to believe that speaking about what happened
would mean confirming something about himself that he prefers to leave unexamined — that he was a
man to whom this had been done. What he hopes for, sitting in the waiting room before his first
session, is less clear to him than what he fears: that the telling of it will make it more real, not
less.
