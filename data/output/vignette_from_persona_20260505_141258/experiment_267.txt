Rohan is a 20-year-old South Asian American writer who presented for treatment approximately seven
months after the sudden death of his younger brother, Arjun, who collapsed without warning from an
undetected cardiac arrhythmia during a family gathering. Arjun was seventeen. There had been no
illness, no warning, no moment in which the family could have prepared themselves. One moment Arjun
was laughing at the dinner table; the next, Rohan was on the floor beside him, calling his name
while their mother screamed for someone to dial 911. Rohan held his brother's hand until the
paramedics arrived. Arjun was pronounced dead at the hospital that evening.

In the weeks that followed, Rohan described feeling as though the architecture of his understanding
of the world had simply collapsed. He had grown up believing, in the way that most young people do
without examining it, that catastrophe announces itself — that there are signs, that youth is a kind
of protection, that the people sitting across from you at dinner will still be there tomorrow.
Arjun's death had not simply taken his brother; it had dismantled every quiet assumption Rohan had
ever made about how life worked. He had come to believe, with a certainty that felt less like a
thought and more like a physical fact, that the world was genuinely and randomly dangerous — that
safety was an illusion people told themselves because they had not yet seen what he had seen. When
friends described feeling safe or content, he found their confidence bewildering, almost naïve.

Rohan had always identified as a warm, curious, and essentially happy person. Writing had come
easily to him; he had been working on a novel and contributing essays to a small literary magazine.
In the months after Arjun's death, he found himself certain that the person who had written those
pages no longer existed. He did not experience this as grief in any form he recognized — he
experienced it as a permanent alteration, a before and after with no bridge between them. He was not
sad in a way that felt temporary. He was miserable in a way that felt like the truth about him now.

His sleep had deteriorated significantly. He lay awake for hours, and when he did sleep, he woke
easily. Ordinary sounds — a door closing in another room, a notification chime from his phone, a car
backfiring outside his apartment building — would jolt him upright, his heart pounding, his body
certain something had gone wrong. He was unable to settle even in environments he knew to be safe.
Sitting at his writing desk, which had once been a place of absorption and pleasure, he found
himself scanning, waiting, unable to release the vigilance that had taken up residence in him since
the night he knelt on the floor beside his brother.

His writing had effectively stopped. He could not make plans — not for the novel, not for
submissions, not even for the following week. What frightened him most about planning was the
implicit assumption that the future would arrive, that the people he loved would be in it. He had
learned, in the most visceral way possible, that this assumption could be shattered in a single
moment, and he was not willing to make it again. He had withdrawn from a writing residency he had
been accepted to before Arjun's death, citing personal reasons without elaborating. His editor at
the literary magazine had stopped reaching out after several unanswered emails.

He had also begun seeking reassurance from his parents and his closest friend with a frequency that
had begun to strain those relationships — asking, repeatedly, whether they thought he was handling
things normally, whether they believed he would be all right. He avoided anything that carried
Arjun's presence: a particular playlist, the restaurant where they used to eat together, the
neighborhood where they grew up.

What finally brought Rohan to seek help was a conversation with his mother, who told him quietly
that she was worried she was losing him too. He came to the first session saying very little about
what he hoped for, except that he wanted to know whether it was possible to live in the world again
without being afraid of it.
