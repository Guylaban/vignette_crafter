Minh Tran is a 60-year-old Vietnamese-American man who has worked in hospitality for over three
decades, most recently as the general manager of a mid-sized hotel in the greater Houston area. He
presents with a PCL-5 score of 68 following a toxic chemical exposure incident that occurred
approximately fourteen months ago, and he has only recently agreed to seek professional help after
considerable encouragement from his long-term partner, Linh.

The incident took place during a routine inspection of the hotel's basement mechanical room, where a
contractor had been servicing the industrial HVAC system. Minh entered the space without warning and
walked directly into a dense, invisible cloud of refrigerant gas that had been venting from a
ruptured line. Within seconds he felt a burning sensation coating the back of his throat and tongue
— a sharp, synthetic sweetness that seemed to cling to his taste buds — and then his chest tightened
as though something were pressing down on his sternum. He tried to call out but found his voice
reduced to a whisper. He could not locate the door he had come through. For what he later learned
was roughly four minutes, but experienced as something far longer, he believed he was going to die
alone in that room, unable to move, unable to summon help, the chemical taste filling his mouth
while his body refused to obey him.

In the weeks that followed, Minh noticed that the memory of those four minutes did not stay in the
past where he expected it to. It arrived without invitation — during a morning briefing with staff,
while reviewing invoices at his desk, once during dinner with Linh — and when it came, it came with
the taste. The synthetic sweetness would suddenly coat his tongue exactly as it had in the basement,
and for those moments he was not in his office or at his kitchen table but back in the dark, chest
tight, door invisible. He had come to understand these returns not as memories but as repetitions,
and the distinction frightened him in a way he found difficult to articulate.

His body had also changed in ways that felt like a betrayal. He had always prided himself on
physical steadiness — on being the calm presence in a crisis — but now he was certain his body was
no longer reliable. A mild headache became, in his interpretation, evidence of neurological damage.
An irregular heartbeat during exercise confirmed, to his mind, that the exposure had done something
permanent and undetected. He checked his pulse at his wrist several times each day and had begun
keeping a written log of physical sensations, convinced that if he stopped monitoring he would miss
the sign that arrived too late to act on. He had come to believe that bad things happened to him
precisely when he was not watching, and that vigilance was the only partial protection available to
him, even as he doubted its adequacy.

This same watchfulness extended outward. Walking the hotel floors — the central task of his working
day — Minh now moved through the building scanning for chemical smells, for contractors carrying
equipment, for any door that led to a mechanical or utility space. When a guest complaint took
longer than usual to resolve, or when a maintenance call went unanswered for more than a few
minutes, his body registered the delay as confirmation that something was already wrong. He had
reassigned the basement inspections to his deputy manager without explanation, and the staff had
begun to notice his altered presence — the way he hovered near exits during vendor meetings, the
sharpness that entered his voice when procedures deviated from schedule.

He had gradually withdrawn from the informal social rhythms of hotel life — the after-shift drinks,
the industry dinners — telling Linh it was fatigue, though he was aware the explanation was
incomplete. He had come to feel that he no longer quite fit into the world he had occupied
comfortably for thirty years, as though the basement had marked a before and after that others could
not see and he could not explain. What finally brought him to the clinic was a moment three weeks
ago when Linh found his symptom log and sat with him quietly until he agreed to make the call. What
he most fears is being told that the damage is real and permanent. What he most hopes is that the
taste will one day leave his mouth.
