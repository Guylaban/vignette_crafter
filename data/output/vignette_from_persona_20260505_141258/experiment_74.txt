Priya is a 23-year-old South Asian American woman who works as a junior auditor at a mid-sized
accounting firm in the Pacific Northwest. She presents to therapy approximately fourteen months
after witnessing a roadside explosion while visiting extended family in a region experiencing active
civil conflict. She had been staying with her aunt and uncle for what was meant to be a three-week
trip, her first time returning to her family's country of origin since childhood. On a quiet
afternoon, she had stepped outside to watch her younger cousin ride his bicycle along the narrow
street in front of the house. The explosion happened without warning. The blast came from somewhere
to the left of where she was standing, close enough that the pressure moved through her chest. When
the sound cleared and her vision returned, she saw her cousin's bicycle on its side in the road, and
she saw parts of people — a hand, the curve of a shoulder — that she understood immediately but
could not process. She does not know how long she stood there. She does not know exactly what she
did next. What she carries are not sequences but images: the bicycle wheel still spinning, a
specific angle of light, a fragment of a face she could not identify. The rest is absence.

In the weeks following her return to the United States, Priya found herself unable to assemble what
had happened into a coherent account. She knew the facts in the way one knows something written in a
language one cannot speak — she had been there, something terrible had occurred, people had died —
but when she tried to explain it to her mother or to a friend, the words simply were not available.
She would open her mouth and find nothing. This did not feel like grief or shock to her; it felt
like a fundamental failure, as though the part of her that was capable of understanding and
communicating had been permanently switched off.

At work, where Priya spends long days reviewing financial documents and sitting through team
meetings, the difficulty became concrete and consequential. She had always been precise, detail-
oriented, someone her supervisors described as unusually composed for her age. But after returning,
she found she could not sustain the concentration that auditing demands. She had come to believe
that she was no longer the person who could do this work — that whatever capacity had made her good
at her job had been left behind on that street. She missed two filing deadlines in the same quarter,
something that had never happened before, and her manager scheduled a formal check-in that Priya sat
through feeling, as she later described it, like a piece of furniture in the room — present in shape
but not in substance. She had begun to feel like an object moving through her own life, acted upon
rather than acting, and what frightened her most was that this did not seem temporary. It seemed
like the truth of what she had become.

Outside the office, ordinary sounds had acquired a new and unbearable weight. A car backfiring in
the parking garage, a door slamming in her apartment building, a truck's air brakes on the street
below — each one arrived in her body before her mind could name it, and for a moment she was back in
that afternoon light. She had stopped using the bike path that ran alongside her commute route,
crossing to the opposite sidewalk whenever she saw a bicycle, though she could not have explained
this to anyone without it sounding unreasonable. Certain images — a bare arm, a particular
configuration of a shoulder — seen in a magazine or on a passing stranger, could stop her mid-step.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was not a single event but a slow recognition that she had
been waiting to feel like herself again and that the waiting had started to feel permanent. She is
most afraid that the therapist will confirm what she already suspects — that the deadness she feels
is not a symptom but a verdict. What she hopes for, though she articulates it tentatively, is to
find words for what happened, because she has come to believe that until she can say it, she cannot
begin to put it anywhere.
