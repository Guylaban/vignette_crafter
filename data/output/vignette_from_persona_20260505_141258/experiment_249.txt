Maly Phommasack is a 52-year-old Lao-American woman who works the morning shift at a fast food
restaurant in a mid-sized city in the Pacific Northwest. She came to the clinic accompanied by her
adult daughter, who sat quietly in the waiting room while Maly, speaking in careful, measured
English, described what had happened to her nearly two years earlier and what her life had become
since.

In the late summer of two years ago, Maly was visiting extended family in a rural coastal community
when a major tropical storm made sudden and catastrophic landfall. The warning came too late. Water
entered the house within minutes, rising from ankle height to chest height in what felt, to Maly,
like a single breath. She was separated from her relatives in the darkness and the noise — a sound
she still struggles to describe, a roaring that seemed to come from everywhere at once — and spent
several hours clinging to a roof beam in the flooded structure, alone, unable to see or hear anyone
she knew, certain that she was going to drown. The water eventually receded enough for her to be
pulled out by emergency workers, but not before she had watched the roof of a neighboring structure
collapse. She returned to the United States physically unharmed. Her relatives survived. On paper,
she was told, she had been lucky.

In the weeks that followed, Maly found that the event had not stayed where it belonged. Fragments of
it returned without invitation — not as a coherent story she could tell herself and set aside, but
as broken pieces that arrived out of sequence: the texture of the beam under her hands, a flash of
brown water, the sound before the neighbor's roof fell. When these pieces surfaced, she felt so
flooded with emotion that she would have to stop whatever she was doing — set down the mop, step
away from the fryer, press her back against the wall of the break room — until the feeling passed.
She had come to believe that these returns were not something she could manage or predict, that the
memory had a will of its own that she could not override.

Night had become its own problem. Maly lay awake long after her husband fell asleep, listening. She
was not sure what she was listening for, only that darkness made the world feel uncontained in a way
that daylight did not. When she did sleep, she woke at small sounds — a car passing, the house
settling — and would lie rigid until she had accounted for every noise. At the restaurant, the
industrial sounds of the kitchen had become a source of sustained distress. The clatter of a dropped
tray, the sudden hiss of the fryer, a door banging shut in the back — each unexpected sound brought
her instantly upright, her body braced. Her manager had commented on it twice, gently, and a
coworker had begun warning her before turning on the blender. Maly was grateful and ashamed in equal
measure.

What frightened her most, in the quieter moments of reflection, was not any single symptom but a
conclusion she had slowly and reluctantly reached about herself: that she was permanently altered.
She no longer felt the ordinary textures of daily life — the satisfaction of a good meal, warmth
when her grandchildren visited, ease in her husband's company. She was certain these capacities had
been taken from her and would not return. She had also come to feel that she did not belong anywhere
in a way she could not fully articulate — not in her American life, not in the community she had
visited, not in the self she had inhabited before the storm. She had come to believe, with quiet
conviction, that bad things would continue to find her, that there was nothing she could do to
prevent them, and that this was simply the truth of her life now.

What finally brought her to the clinic was her daughter's persistence and, underneath it, Maly's own
exhaustion — not with the symptoms themselves, but with the effort of carrying them alone. She
arrived hoping, tentatively, to feel like herself again, and afraid, more than anything, that a
therapist would confirm what she already suspected: that the woman she had been before the storm was
simply gone.
