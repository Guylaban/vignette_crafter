Marisol is a 47-year-old Salvadoran American woman who has been married for nineteen years and works
as a social ministry worker at a Catholic community center in her city's largely immigrant
neighborhood. She came to the clinic three months after being raped by a man she knew through her
parish — a volunteer coordinator who had asked her to stay late one evening to review program
materials. The attack happened in a small storage room off the main corridor, a room she had entered
dozens of times before. What she remembers is not a continuous sequence but a series of frozen
images: the overhead fluorescent light flickering once before he pushed her against the metal
shelving, the particular silence of the building after the last staff member had left, the texture
of a cardboard box against her shoulder. Each image is vivid and complete in itself, like a
photograph, but the moments between them are gone. When she tries to speak about what happened — to
her therapist, or in the single halting conversation she attempted with her husband — she finds that
the words simply are not there. She knows what occurred. She cannot say it. What she can say, and
has said more than once, is that she deserved it, that something she had done or failed to do had
made this inevitable.

In the weeks after the assault, Marisol continued going to work. She had no language for what had
happened and no framework that felt safe enough to hold it. She had spent twenty years organizing
food drives, coordinating refugee resettlement, sitting with women in crisis, and she had come to
believe, quietly and with gathering certainty, that she was now permanently separated from the
person who had done that work. That woman had been happy, or something close to it. This woman was
not. She had also lost something she could not easily name — a sense that the world was held
together by something trustworthy, whether God or community or ordinary human decency. The faith
that had been the foundation of both her vocation and her marriage now felt like a story she had
told herself.

At the community center, the storage room remains off limits to her. She routes her path through the
building to avoid it, which her colleagues have noticed without understanding. More disruptively,
the silence that sometimes falls over the office — between phone calls, after a meeting ends — has
become unbearable. That particular quality of quiet, the same quiet that preceded the attack, now
registers in her body as warning. She will find a reason to speak, to move, to create noise. When a
colleague is late returning from a home visit, Marisol's certainty that something terrible is
happening mounts with each passing minute until she is calling the colleague's cell phone, her hands
unsteady. Her supervisor has gently asked whether she is managing her caseload.

At home, Marisol has stopped eating dinner with her husband most evenings. She controls what she
eats with a precision that she experiences as the one domain in which she remains competent and
intact. She has also stopped allowing herself the ordinary comforts she once relied on — a bath in
the evening, sitting still with a book — because stillness itself feels like exposure, like leaving
a door unguarded. Her husband is confused and grieving the distance between them, though she has
told him almost nothing. She had not found the words.

What finally brought her to the clinic was a moment at work when a teenage girl she was counseling
described feeling that her body no longer belonged to her. Marisol recognized the sentence as her
own. She made the appointment that afternoon. She arrives carrying a shame she has not been able to
articulate — a deep conviction that what happened to her body has made her body wrong, contaminated
in some way she cannot explain — and a fear that speaking will make everything worse rather than
better. What she hopes for, tentatively, is to find the words. What she is most afraid of is that
finding them will confirm what she already suspects: that she will not recover the person she was
before that evening.
