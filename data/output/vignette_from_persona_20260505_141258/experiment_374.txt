Mere Tuilagi is a 35-year-old Fijian-American woman who works as an accountant at a mid-sized firm
in suburban Atlanta. She is married, describes her husband as supportive, and until approximately
eight months ago would have characterised herself as someone who moved through the world with quiet
confidence. That changed on an evening in late autumn when she stayed late at the office to close
out a quarterly report. Walking to her car in the parking garage shortly after nine o'clock, she
heard footsteps accelerating behind her. Before she could turn fully, a man grabbed her by the
shoulder, spun her around, pressed a knife against her ribs, and demanded her bag and phone. She
remembers the cold concrete smell of the garage, the metallic taste of fear that flooded her mouth,
and the way her body locked — arms pinned back, torso twisted at an angle she could not correct. The
encounter lasted perhaps ninety seconds. He took her bag and ran. She stood motionless for some time
before she could move toward the elevator.

In the weeks that followed, Mere found herself returning compulsively to the sequence of decisions
that had led her there: she had chosen to stay late, had declined a colleague's offer to walk with
her, had taken the quieter stairwell exit rather than the main lobby. She had come to believe, with
a certainty that felt almost logical, that the robbery had happened not because of random misfortune
but because of the kind of person she was — someone careless, someone who had acted wrongly, someone
who had, in some fundamental way, invited it. She dwelt on this for hours at a time, reconstructing
alternative evenings in which she left at six, took a different route, said yes to the colleague.
The rumination brought no resolution, only a deepening sense that the event had been preventable and
that the failure was hers.

When memories of the attack returned — sometimes without warning, sometimes triggered by the sound
of footsteps in the parking structure she still uses each day for work — they did not arrive as
ordinary recollections. Mere described them as total: she could taste the same metallic flood in her
mouth, and her body would find itself twisted into that same locked posture, arms pulled back, torso
angled, as though the event were still in progress. What disturbed her almost as much as the
vividness was the opposite quality — the sense that something so extreme could not truly have
happened to her, that the memory felt too extreme to be real, belonging to someone else's life
rather than her own. She held both experiences simultaneously: a body that remembered everything and
a mind that struggled to accept any of it.

At work, the parking garage became the most concrete arena of her distress. Each morning, walking
from her car to the elevator, any footsteps behind her — a colleague arriving late, a delivery
driver — produced an immediate, full-body alertness that no amount of reassurance could dissolve.
Her husband had taken to accompanying her to the garage on weekends, telling her she was safe, and
she believed him intellectually while finding it entirely impossible to feel it. Even in her own
office, with the door closed and familiar spreadsheets open on her screen, she could not settle.
When a client call ran longer than scheduled or a file took longer to load than expected, she was
certain something was wrong — that the delay was a signal of something bad unfolding just outside
her awareness. She had begun leaving her desk less, eating lunch at her computer, and declining
after-hours client events, which her manager had noticed and gently raised with her.

When an unfamiliar colleague touched her arm in the break room to get her attention, Mere left the
building and sat in her car for twenty minutes, deliberately trying to detach from herself, to go
somewhere inward and blank until the feeling passed. She had developed this habit in the months
since the attack and found it the only thing that offered any relief, however temporary.

What finally brought her to seek help was a conversation with her husband in which she could not
explain why she was crying. She hopes, tentatively, to feel safe again. What she fears most is that
she will not.
