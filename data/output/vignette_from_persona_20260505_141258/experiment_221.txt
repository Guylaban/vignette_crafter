Aleksander Novak is a 29-year-old Polish-American man who works as a salesperson for a building
materials company, a job that requires him to drive regularly between construction sites and
supplier warehouses across the rural outskirts of the city. He presents to therapy approximately
fourteen months after surviving a catastrophic flash flood that swept through a campground in the
Appalachian foothills where he had been spending a long weekend with two colleagues. The flood
arrived in the early hours of the morning with almost no warning — a wall of cold, churning water
that tore through the tree line and submerged the lower campground in minutes. Aleksander was woken
by shouting, scrambled from his tent into complete darkness, and found himself immediately waist-
deep in fast-moving water, unable to see either of his companions, unable to orient himself among
the trees he had walked through casually the evening before. He clung to a fence post for what he
later estimated was close to two hours before the water receded enough for him to move. One of his
colleagues sustained a serious leg injury; the other was unaccounted for for nearly six hours before
being found downstream, frightened but physically unharmed. Aleksander himself was not physically
injured, a fact he has since found difficult to reconcile with the severity of what he carries.

In the weeks that followed, Aleksander noticed that certain moments would collapse without warning
into something that felt less like memory and more like re-entry. He would be standing in a hardware
showroom mid-pitch and suddenly be back in the dark water — not picturing it, but inhabiting it, his
body braced, his breath short, the fluorescent lights of the warehouse briefly meaningless. What
made these episodes particularly disorienting was that they did not always arrive with images.
Sometimes there was no picture at all, only the terror itself, arriving full and sourceless, as
though the fear had been untethered from its origin and now moved freely through his days. There are
stretches of that night he cannot retrieve no matter how deliberately he tries — a gap of perhaps
forty minutes between entering the water and reaching the fence post — and this blankness troubles
him deeply. When he does attempt to recall the event directly, the terror returns at its original
intensity, unchanged by time, and so he has largely stopped trying.

Aleksander now wakes most nights somewhere between two and four in the morning, sitting up and
scanning the room before he is fully conscious, checking the window, listening for sounds he cannot
name. He moves through his days in a state of low-grade watchfulness that he finds exhausting but
cannot switch off. His sales territory includes several routes that pass through wooded, low-lying
areas, and on overcast days or after rain, when the ditches alongside the road fill with standing
water and the tree canopy closes overhead, he finds the drive nearly intolerable. He has rerouted
two of his regular client runs to avoid a particular stretch of highway that passes through a river
valley, adding nearly forty minutes to his day. His manager has noticed the scheduling
inefficiencies and raised them twice. Aleksander has not explained why.

He had come to believe, with a quiet and settled certainty, that other people could not actually be
counted on when things went wrong — that the shouting in the dark, the hours of not knowing, had
revealed something true about the fundamental aloneness of crisis. He has declined several social
invitations from the colleagues who were present that night and has not spoken to either of them
about what happened. He describes feeling like a stranger to himself, as though the person who drove
to that campground no longer exists and what remained is someone he does not fully recognise.
Nothing he used to find satisfying — the competitiveness of sales, weekend football, his plans to
save for a first apartment — seems to carry any weight.

What finally brought Aleksander to seek help was a sales visit two weeks ago to a client whose yard
backs onto a creek. He had to pull over on the way home and sit in his car for thirty minutes,
unable to account for where the previous hour had gone. He came to therapy not entirely sure what he
was hoping for, but most afraid, he said quietly, that this was simply who he was now.
