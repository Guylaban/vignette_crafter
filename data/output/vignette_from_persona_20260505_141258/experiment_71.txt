Rodrigo is a 70-year-old widowed Latino man who has worked as a welder for most of his adult life,
immigrating to the United States from Central America decades ago and building a quiet, self-
sufficient existence around his trade. He presented for treatment with a PCL-5 score of 80,
describing a level of daily suffering that he had, until recently, tried to manage entirely on his
own.

Approximately three years ago, while visiting family in his country of origin, Rodrigo was detained
by a group of armed men who accused him of political affiliations he did not hold. He was taken to a
location he did not recognize, bound, and subjected to prolonged physical violence over what he
believes was two days, though he cannot account for all of the time. At several points during his
captivity, a firearm was discharged in close proximity to him — sometimes as a threat, sometimes
without apparent reason. He was given no information about whether he would be released or killed,
and there were moments when every door, every silence, every shift in his captors' tone felt like it
might be the last thing he experienced. He was eventually released without explanation and made his
way back across the border. He told no one the full account of what had happened.

In the months that followed, Rodrigo returned to his welding work at a fabrication shop, where the
environment — loud, industrial, punctuated by the sharp percussive sounds of pneumatic tools and
metal striking metal — became a daily ordeal he navigated without anyone around him understanding
why. When a nail gun fired in the adjacent bay or a steel beam dropped unexpectedly, Rodrigo's body
responded as though he were still bound in that room. The memory, in those moments, did not feel
like something that had ended. It arrived fully, without distance, as though the intervening years
had not occurred at all. He found he could not explain this to his supervisor when his work slowed,
when he began arriving late to avoid the noisiest parts of the morning shift. He attempted once to
describe what had happened to a younger colleague and lost the thread entirely mid-sentence, the
account dissolving before he could reach anything coherent, leaving him ashamed and silent.

Rodrigo had come to believe, with a certainty that felt like knowledge rather than fear, that there
was no reliable way to identify who might harm him. Strangers, authority figures, even men who
reminded him in posture or tone of his captors became objects of quiet, exhaustive vigilance. At the
same time, when he sensed any interpersonal tension — a foreman's impatience, a neighbor's
irritation — he became accommodating to a degree that felt involuntary, agreeable in ways that
afterward left him feeling hollowed out. He worked hard to keep the memories from surfacing, pushing
against them deliberately throughout the day, and when they broke through anyway, what rose with
them was not grief but rage — a fury so intense it frightened him, directed at the men who had done
this and, with equal force, at himself.

What tormented Rodrigo most was not the violence itself but his own conduct during it. He had come
to believe that something in the way he had behaved — words he had said, the way he had presented
himself in the days before the detention — had made him a target. Worse, he was convinced he had
failed to act during his captivity in ways that might have changed the outcome, and he carried this
as a permanent verdict on his character. He would never forgive himself for it, he said plainly, as
though this were simply a fact.

It was the death of his wife two years prior, and the subsequent absence of the one person to whom
he had been even partially known, that finally made the isolation unbearable. His adult children had
noticed his withdrawal, his flinching at ordinary sounds, the way he sometimes stopped mid-
conversation and seemed to go somewhere else entirely. It was his daughter who made the appointment.
Rodrigo came because he was tired — not of living, he was careful to say, but of carrying something
this heavy entirely alone. What he hoped for, tentatively, was that some part of it might become
possible to set down.
