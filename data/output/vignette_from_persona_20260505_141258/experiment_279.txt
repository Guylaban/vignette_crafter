Kiet Pham is a 51-year-old Vietnamese American policy analyst who lives with his wife in a mid-sized
Pacific Northwest city. He presents following a wildfire that swept through his rural county
approximately fourteen months ago, an event that has left a persistent mark on nearly every
dimension of his daily functioning. His PCL-5 score of 55 places him firmly within the severe range,
and the clinical picture that has emerged over the course of intake and early sessions is one of a
man who once prided himself on careful, evidence-based thinking and who now finds that his own mind
has become a source of profound distrust.

The fire came on a late August evening with almost no warning. Kiet and his wife had been monitoring
reports throughout the day, but the wind shifted suddenly and the fire crested a ridge that
emergency services had publicly designated as a natural containment line. Within forty minutes,
evacuation orders reached their neighborhood. Kiet has described the next several hours in
fragmented terms: the orange sky pressing down like a ceiling, the smell of smoke that was no longer
distant but immediate and acrid, the way his chest tightened and his hands shook as he tried to load
documents and medications into the car while his wife called out to him from the driveway. He lost
track of time. He could not recall whether he had locked the door. He drove the wrong direction
twice before finding the evacuation route. Their home survived, but three houses on their street did
not, and Kiet spent six days in a community shelter before returning.

In the months that followed, the world began to feel constitutionally unreliable in ways it had not
before. Kiet works primarily from home, drafting policy briefs and regulatory analyses, and the
professional environment that once suited his methodical temperament has become a setting in which
the symptoms find him with particular ease. He will be seated at his desk, deep in a document, when
a neighbor lights a backyard fire or a nearby restaurant vents its kitchen, and the smell reaches
him through an open window. What happens next is not, in his description, a simple startle — it is a
full-body mobilization, a certainty that something has already gone wrong. He has begun keeping the
windows closed even in summer, which his wife has noticed and which has become a source of quiet
friction between them.

Kiet has also developed a vigilance around physical sensation that colors his days in a more diffuse
way. When he feels even mildly unwell — a headache, a tight chest from poor posture, the mild nausea
that sometimes accompanies long hours in front of a screen — he cannot dismiss it as ordinary. He
had come to believe that his body's signals were the earliest warning system for something
catastrophic, and so each twinge demanded attention and interpretation. He monitors himself
throughout the workday in a way that makes sustained concentration increasingly difficult, and his
output, once reliably strong, has slowed noticeably. His supervisor has commented on missed
deadlines, which Kiet attributes to his difficulty concentrating but has not yet disclosed as
connected to anything psychological.

What frightens him most, and what he returned to repeatedly in early sessions, is not the fire
itself but his own responses since. He had always understood himself as someone who could assess
risk accurately and act deliberately under pressure. The evacuation had shown him something
different, and he had not been able to reconcile it. He was certain that the confusion, the
intrusive sensory memories, the way any unscheduled or unpredictable event — a sudden change in
plans, an unexpected phone call, a power flicker — could send his heart rate climbing, all pointed
toward something fundamentally broken in him. He had begun to use the phrase "going crazy" in
session not as hyperbole but as a genuine diagnostic hypothesis he was advancing about himself.

Kiet sought help after his wife found him sitting in the car in the garage at two in the morning,
unable to explain why he had gone there. He told her he did not know what was happening to him. He
came to the first appointment hoping, above everything, to be told that there was a name for this
and that the name was not the one he feared.
