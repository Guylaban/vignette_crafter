Minh Tran is an eighteen-year-old Vietnamese-American photographer who presented for an initial
intake following referral from a veterans' outreach program. He enlisted at seventeen with parental
consent, deployed to a forward operating base in an active combat zone, and was medically separated
after approximately eight months of service. He now lives alone in a studio apartment in a mid-sized
city and works freelance, shooting events and portraits to cover his rent.

The incident that brought Minh to this point occurred during a routine patrol escort he had joined
as an embedded unit photographer. The convoy was moving through a narrow village corridor when an
IED detonated beneath the lead vehicle. In the seconds that followed, Minh was close enough to feel
the pressure wave move through his chest. He raised his camera by reflex — a detail he has returned
to many times since — and through the viewfinder he watched two soldiers he knew by name burn inside
the vehicle while a third, still alive, called out from beneath the wreckage. The extraction attempt
was chaotic and ultimately failed. Minh was physically uninjured. He walked away. The third soldier
did not.

In the weeks after his medical separation, the memories did not behave like memories. They did not
recede with time the way Minh expected them to. Instead they arrived with a texture and immediacy
that ordinary recollections — his childhood, his mother's kitchen, his first camera — entirely
lacked. He described them in session as feeling realer than real, as though the patrol corridor
existed in higher resolution than the room he was currently sitting in. When those images returned,
what rose with them was not grief exactly but a fury he could not direct anywhere useful. He would
find himself rigid in his apartment, hands clenched, certain that his rage had nowhere to land.

The triggers arrived without negotiation. At a wedding shoot three months after discharge, a car
backfired in the parking structure below the venue and Minh was back in the corridor before he
registered that he had moved. He gripped a bridesmaid's arm hard enough to leave a mark. He did not
finish the job. After that, he began turning down indoor venue work where ambient noise was
unpredictable — banquet halls, clubs, crowded galleries — and his income contracted accordingly. He
told himself this was a scheduling preference. His bookings dropped by roughly half over the
following two months.

Even in quiet settings, Minh could not locate anything that felt like calm. He described lying awake
in his apartment at three in the morning with every light on, scanning the room for nothing he could
name, his body maintaining a readiness that his mind knew was unnecessary but could not override. He
had begun accepting assignments that took him into genuinely dangerous neighborhoods late at night,
working alone, carrying expensive equipment in ways that invited theft. He was not unaware of the
risk. What frightened the people around him — his older sister, the one contact from his unit who
still texted him — was that the risk appeared to be part of the point.

Minh had come to believe, with a certainty that felt structural rather than emotional, that he had
failed the soldier under that wreckage. He had the camera. He had stood there. He had not pulled
hard enough, or called loudly enough, or done whatever the unnamed thing was that would have changed
the outcome. This conviction sat beneath everything else like a load-bearing wall. Alongside it was
a quieter but equally firm sense that he no longer belonged in any of the spaces available to him —
not in the veterans' community, whose grief he felt he had forfeited by surviving intact, and not
among civilians, who moved through the world with an ease he experienced as a kind of offense. He
trusted almost no one, and the few people who reached toward him he met with a flat, watchful
distance that he could see confused them but could not bring himself to change.

What finally moved Minh to make the call was a near-miss on a night shoot: a stranger approached him
from behind and Minh's response was physical and immediate enough to frighten them both. He sat in
the parking lot afterward for a long time. He is not sure what he hopes for from treatment. What he
is most afraid of is being asked to look directly at the moment he raised the camera.
