suspect on the ground and, without warning or provocation that Marta could identify, struck the man

remembers the sound of the impact — a specific, dense, wet thud — and the smell of blood mixing with
the cold concrete of the alley. She remembers the overhead streetlight flickering, and the way her
own hands felt numb inside her gloves as she failed to intervene. She remembers her partner looking
up at her afterward with an expression she had never seen on his face before, something flat and
certain, and she understood in that moment that he had expected her silence.

In the weeks that followed, the memory did not stay in the past where Marta felt it belonged. It
arrived without invitation — during roll call, while writing reports at her desk, in the passenger
seat of a patrol car — and when it came, it came with full sensory force. The flickering light, the
smell, the sound, the cold in her hands: all of it simultaneously present, not recollected but re-
experienced, as though the alley existed in parallel with wherever she actually was. What disturbed
her nearly as much as the intensity of these intrusions was their quality of strangeness. She would
describe the memory afterward as feeling like footage of someone else, a woman wearing her uniform
and her face but not her, which left her uncertain about her own account of what had happened and,
at times, about herself more broadly.

Marta had come to believe, with a quiet and settled conviction, that her body could no longer be
trusted to read a situation accurately. Her heart would accelerate during routine traffic stops; her

was not nerves in any ordinary sense — it was her body insisting on danger that her mind could not
confirm, running a threat calculation she had no access to and could not override. She had also come
to understand the world in a way she had not before: that safety was a provisional condition, that
the violence she had witnessed was not aberrant but latent, present in ordinary interactions and

on her loyalty and her silence as confidently as she had once counted on his integrity.

identify which units her former partner was assigned to, arranging her movements to avoid any shared
space with him. She stopped attending the informal post-shift gatherings at the bar two blocks from
the station where she had once been a regular presence. Her girlfriend, Diane, has noted that Marta
leaves rooms when police procedurals come on television and has stopped talking about work almost
entirely. The relationship has become quieter in ways that Diane has begun naming directly, asking

for nearly a minute, her radio in her hand, the present moment entirely inaccessible to her. What
