Gerald Marsh is a 73-year-old divorced journalist who spent the better part of four decades covering
industrial and environmental catastrophes across North America. He came to his first intake
appointment wearing a pressed oxford shirt and carrying a reporter's notebook out of what appeared
to be old habit, though he admitted he had not written a publishable word in nearly two years. He
sat down only after positioning his chair so that his back was flush against the wall of the
consulting room, scanning the space briefly before settling. He did not appear to notice he had done
it.

Approximately twenty-six months before seeking treatment, Gerald was embedded with a response team
during a catastrophic chemical plant explosion in the Gulf Coast region — an event he had arranged
access to through contacts cultivated over a long career. He arrived on-site expecting the
controlled aftermath typical of industrial incidents. What he encountered instead was ongoing,
cascading failure: secondary explosions, the collapse of a storage structure while workers were
still inside, and the release of a dense chemical plume that moved faster than anyone had predicted.
Gerald was in a press staging area when the second explosion occurred without warning. The
concussive force knocked him from his feet. In the chaos that followed, he lay partially pinned
beneath debris for approximately forty minutes, unable to move, surrounded by the screams of injured
workers and the acrid, sweetish chemical smell that had saturated the air and coated the back of his
throat. It was a taste unlike anything he had encountered before — viscous, almost metallic beneath
its sweetness — and it stayed with him long after the physical exposure had passed.

In the weeks following his evacuation and medical clearance, Gerald found himself unable to file his
account of the event. He told his editor it was a question of verification. He told himself the same
thing. What was actually happening was that any attempt to sit with the material — reviewing
photographs from the scene, reading wire copy from other outlets — produced a quality of experience
he struggled to articulate: the memory did not feel like remembering. It felt like being returned.
The images played with a continuity and physical immediacy that made the present room dissolve. He
had come to believe that if he allowed himself to go there fully, something in him would not come
back intact.

The unpredictability of the second explosion had lodged itself as a permanent feature of his world.
Gerald had always prided himself on reading situations, on knowing where the danger was. Now he
understood, in a way that felt cellular rather than intellectual, that danger did not announce
itself. In restaurants and waiting rooms and the lobby of his apartment building, he began
cataloguing exits, noting the weight and reach of objects near other people's hands — a habit he was
certain was simply reasonable given what he now knew. He listened each night for sounds at his front
door. He could not remember the last time his body had felt at rest.

His spending had escalated quietly. Small purchases at first — books he did not read, equipment for
a kitchen he barely used — then larger ones, a pattern he recognised intellectually as purposeless
but that provided, in the moment of transaction, a few minutes of something that approximated
relief. His ex-wife, with whom he had maintained a cordial post-divorce friendship for nearly a
decade, had stopped calling after he cancelled three consecutive plans without explanation. He had
not tried to repair it. The effort required felt disproportionate to what he believed he could offer
anyone.

What finally brought Gerald in was a dinner with a former colleague at which a dish arrived that
carried, faintly but unmistakably, that same sweetish chemical undertone — likely nothing more than
a particular reduction sauce. He froze completely, fork in hand, unable to speak or move for what
his colleague later told him was nearly a full minute. He drove home and sat in his car in the
parking garage for two hours. He had come to believe he would never feel anything the way he used to
— not curiosity, not pleasure, not the particular satisfaction that had once made the work feel
worth the cost. He was not certain therapy could return any of that. What he said, quietly, at the
end of the intake session, was that he wanted to find out whether it was still possible to live in
the present tense.
