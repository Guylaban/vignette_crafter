Gerald Whitmore is a 68-year-old married school counselor who presented for an initial psychological
assessment following a referral from his primary care physician. He is soft-spoken and deliberate in
his speech, choosing his words carefully — a habit that, in the counseling office where he has spent
three decades helping adolescents, has always served him well. In recent months, however, that
careful composure has begun to cost him considerably more effort than it once did.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Gerald was assaulted in the parking garage beneath the community
center where he volunteers on weekend evenings. A man he did not recognize approached him from
behind, smelling strongly of alcohol, and attacked him without warning or apparent provocation. What
followed lasted only minutes, but Gerald describes those minutes as a series of frozen images: the
concrete pillar his face was pressed against, the yellow fluorescent light flickering overhead, the
feeling of his own breath leaving him. He was found by a passerby, transported by ambulance, and
treated for a fractured orbital socket and two broken ribs. The hospital corridor where he was
stabilized — bright, antiseptic, humming with clinical urgency — was the last environment he was
fully conscious of before he lost track of time.

In the weeks after the assault, Gerald returned to work as quickly as his physician permitted. He
told his wife, Donna, that staying busy was the only sensible thing to do. But something had already
begun to shift. When he remembered the event — and he found that he did, without choosing to — it
did not feel like remembering. The terror he had felt in that parking garage arrived again whole, as
if the interval between then and now had collapsed entirely. The memory did not come as a narrative
but as a sequence of snapshots, each one vivid and self-contained: the smell of alcohol on the man's
breath, the flicker of that light, the cold of the floor. Fragments of these images surfaced without
warning during ordinary moments — while reviewing a student's file, while eating dinner — and then
receded just as abruptly, leaving Gerald disoriented and ashamed.

He had come to believe that if he could keep enough distance between himself and the world around
him, these intrusions might lose their grip. He began arriving at work earlier and leaving later,
structuring his day to minimize unplanned encounters in the hallways. When students came to him in
emotional distress — which, in his role, they did regularly — he found himself doing something he
could not entirely account for: a kind of internal retreat, a deliberate effort to go somewhere else
inside himself, to blank out just enough that their anguish did not reach him. He was certain this
made him a worse counselor, and the certainty troubled him deeply, but the alternative felt
untenable.

At home, the distance had widened in ways Donna had begun to name. Gerald had always been a
physically affectionate husband; he was no longer. When she reached for him — not with desire,
simply with the ordinary tenderness of a long marriage — he found himself withdrawing before he
understood why. What frightened him most was not the withdrawal itself but his inability to explain
it to her in terms that made sense, because it did not fully make sense to him. He knew only that
closeness, emotional proximity of any kind, had come to feel like exposure to something he could not
manage.

The smell of antiseptic became its own problem. Gerald accompanies students to the school nurse's
office several times a week, and the sharp, clinical scent of that small room had begun to stop him
at the threshold in a way he could not disguise indefinitely. A colleague noticed. Gerald said he
had a headache. He said it again the following week.

It was Donna who finally asked him directly whether he had spoken to anyone about what had happened
in that garage. He told her he was fine. Two weeks later, he made the appointment. He came to the
first session holding his hands very still in his lap, and what he said he most hoped for was to be
able to sit with a student who was crying without feeling the need to disappear.
