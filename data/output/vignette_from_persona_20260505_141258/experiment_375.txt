Valentina Reyes is a nineteen-year-old Latina woman who works as a train conductor on a commuter
line in the greater Chicago area. She presented for treatment following what she described as a
period of "not being able to keep it together anymore," though she had been living with the
consequences of her childhood for most of her life.

Valentina grew up in a two-bedroom apartment on the southwest side of the city, the eldest of three
children. From the time she was approximately seven until she was fourteen, she witnessed her
stepfather assault her mother on a near-nightly basis. The violence followed a pattern she came to
know with terrible precision: the apartment would grow quiet after dark, then would come the sound
of the front door slamming, followed by shouting that escalated quickly into something physical. She
would lie in the darkness of the room she shared with her younger siblings, her heart hammering
against her ribs, pressing herself against the wall as though she could disappear into it. She
remembers one particular night when she was ten — a night that has never fully become the past for
her — when she crept to the doorway and watched her stepfather throw her mother against the kitchen
cabinets. She could taste the metallic fear in her mouth, the same taste that returns to her now
without warning. She did not call for help. She did not step into the kitchen. She stood frozen, and
then she went back to bed.

In the years that followed, Valentina developed a way of moving through the world that was organized
almost entirely around not stopping. She graduated high school early, earned her conductor's
certification at eighteen, and threw herself into shift work with a focus that her supervisors
admired. Keeping busy had always felt like the only reliable way to keep the past from catching up
with her. She also began restricting what she ate, in a quiet, controlled way that she did not
discuss with anyone — a way of managing something internal that felt otherwise unmanageable. When
she had time to herself, she found her mind returning again and again to the question of what would
have happened if her stepfather had never entered their lives, cycling through alternative histories
that offered no resolution.

At work, Valentina operates the evening and overnight commuter runs, which means she regularly moves
through tunnels and unlit stretches of track where the cab goes dark. In those moments, her heart
begins to race, and the sensation of her own pulse is enough to pull her back to that bedroom — not
as a memory she is recalling, but as something happening now, disordered and immediate, the sequence
of events scrambled so that she cannot always tell what came before what. She cannot always say
whether she is nineteen or ten. She has developed a habit of checking the cab door lock multiple
times before a run begins and again at each stop, and she sleeps during the day with every window
latch confirmed before she will lie down. Nights, even in her own apartment, do not feel safe to
her. When a train on an adjacent platform brakes suddenly and the sound cracks through the station,
she goes rigid, her body scanning for what is coming.

What frightens Valentina most, in a way she has never said aloud to anyone, is her certainty that
she is the reason the violence happened — that something in how she behaved as a child invited or
prolonged it. Alongside this sits the conviction that she failed her mother and her siblings in that
kitchen doorway, and the broader belief that bad things will continue to find her because she is the
kind of person they find. She has come to understand her own helplessness as a fixed feature of her
life rather than a response to an impossible childhood situation.

She sought help after a night run during which she sat frozen at the controls for nearly a minute
following an unexpected loud noise from the track, unable to distinguish where she was in time. A
colleague had to speak to her twice before she responded. She came to the intake appointment hoping,
she said quietly, to find out whether she was still fixable — and afraid, above everything else, of
what the answer might be.
