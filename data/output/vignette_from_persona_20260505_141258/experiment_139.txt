Valentina Reyes is a 22-year-old Salvadoran American woman who works as a junior accountant at a
mid-sized firm in Houston. She came to therapy eight months after the acute crisis had passed,
describing herself as someone who had been waiting to feel normal again and had finally accepted
that waiting was not working.

Approximately a year ago, Valentina's four-year-old niece, Camila, whom she had helped raise since
infancy while her sister worked double shifts, was admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit
following a sudden and severe asthma attack that progressed to respiratory failure. Valentina had
been the one home with Camila that afternoon. She remembered the particular quality of the silence
that fell over the apartment just before she found her — the cartoons had been playing, and then
they weren't, and the absence of sound was the first thing that told her something was wrong. She
ran to the back bedroom and found Camila motionless on the floor, lips tinged blue, making no sound
at all. In the ambulance and through the hours that followed in the waiting room, Valentina sat with
the taste of the coffee she had drunk that morning still on her tongue — a thin, burnt bitterness
she has never been able to separate from those hours. Camila survived, and recovered fully over
several weeks. Valentina told everyone she was relieved, and she was, but something had not resolved
the way the rest of the family's distress seemed to.

In the months that followed, Valentina found herself unable to give coherent shape to what had
happened. When her sister or mother tried to talk through the day, she would begin to speak and find
that the experience existed somewhere beneath language — not as a story she could tell, but as
something physical and immediate that resisted narration. She would push the memory back before it
could fully surface, not because she chose to, but because the attempt to recall it seemed to
produce a sensation she could not tolerate. What she was left with instead were fragments: the
taste, the silence, the colour of Camila's lips. These arrived without warning and without context.

At work, the fragments arrived in particular ways. Valentina's office is quiet — the kind of
sustained, carpeted silence that a small accounting firm produces on ordinary afternoons. Several
times a week, she would register that silence and feel her body respond before her mind caught up:
her chest would tighten, her hands would go cold, and she would find herself scanning the room
without knowing what she was looking for. When a colleague was late returning from a meeting, or
when a file took longer than expected to load, she would feel certain in a way she could not reason
herself out of that something had already gone wrong somewhere and she had not yet been told. She
had begun staying later than necessary, rechecking completed work, not because she doubted her
competence but because the alternative — leaving something unverified — felt connected in some
unexaminable way to the idea that she had not done enough.

She had come to believe, with quiet and persistent conviction, that she was someone to whom bad
things happened and that there was nothing she could do to prevent them. This belief sat alongside a
sense of being separated from the people around her by something she could not name. Her colleagues
went to happy hours she declined. Her friends from college sent messages she read and did not
answer. She was not angry at them. She simply could not locate the version of herself that had once
moved through those spaces easily. She used to think of herself as someone who was fundamentally
cheerful — her family had called her the sunny one — and the loss of that self-understanding was
something she grieved privately and without resolution.

What finally brought her to seek help was a Sunday afternoon when Camila, now healthy and loud and
entirely herself, fell asleep on Valentina's lap, and the silence of the child's breathing made
Valentina's heart begin to pound. She sat there for twenty minutes, rigid, unable to enjoy what she
knew was ordinary. She made the appointment the next morning. What she most hoped for, she said
carefully, was to be able to sit with a sleeping child and feel nothing but love.
