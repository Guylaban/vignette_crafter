Amara Osei is a 33-year-old Ghanaian-American woman who works as a park ranger at a large state
nature reserve in the Pacific Northwest. She came to her first session eight weeks after the sudden
death of her younger brother, Kofi, who collapsed from an undetected cardiac arrhythmia while the
two of them were hiking together on a trail she had personally recommended. She had been leading the
way, perhaps thirty yards ahead, when she heard him fall. By the time she reached him and radioed
for emergency services, Kofi was unresponsive. She performed CPR until the rescue team arrived, but
he could not be revived. He was twenty-nine years old.

In the weeks that followed, Amara described the event as something that had lodged itself inside her
not as a continuous memory but as a series of arrested images — the particular angle of afternoon
light through the Douglas firs, the sound of his body hitting the trail, the colour of his jacket
against the dirt. These fragments surfaced without invitation: in the middle of a conversation with
her husband, while filling out an incident report at the ranger station, while standing at the
kitchen sink. Each image arrived with the quality of a photograph rather than a moving scene, vivid
and fixed, and when she tried to place them in sequence they resisted her. Alongside the intrusions
came a grief so acute during those moments of recall that she described it as physically winding her
— a sensation she found both overwhelming and bewildering, as though the loss were happening again
each time.

Her body had not settled since the day Kofi died. She lay awake most nights, alert to every sound in
the house, her muscles tense in a way that had become so habitual she no longer noticed it until her
husband pointed out that she was clenching her jaw at dinner. The sleeplessness compounded a broader
emotional flatness that frightened her perhaps more than anything else. She had come to believe that
she was no longer the person she had been before — that something essential had been altered beyond
repair. What she described as feeling dead inside was not depression in the way she understood the
word; it was more like a disconnection from her own interior life, a silence where there used to be
feeling.

This sense of permanent damage was sharpened by her work. Amara had spent nine years as a ranger,
and her competence in the field had been central to how she understood herself. She knew trails,
weather, risk, terrain. She had guided hundreds of visitors safely. Yet she had been unable to save
her brother on a trail she knew by heart, and she had come to believe, with a certainty she could
not argue herself out of, that she was no longer capable of the things that had defined her. She
began leaving patrol routes early, citing administrative tasks, and delegating field
responsibilities to junior colleagues. Her supervisor had noticed. A formal check-in had been
scheduled.

At home, she kept herself occupied at all times — podcasts while cooking, television she did not
watch but needed on, her phone in her hand during any unstructured moment. She had come to avoid
stillness with a deliberateness she recognised as effortful but could not relinquish, because quiet
felt like an opening through which the images and the grief could rush in unchecked. She had stopped
the evening walks she and her husband used to take, stopped the Saturday morning calls with her
mother in Accra, let her garden go unattended. Her husband, patient and worried in equal measure,
had begun to ask questions she did not know how to answer.

What finally brought Amara to seek help was a conversation with her husband in which he told her,
gently, that he felt he had lost her too. She sat with that for several days before calling a
therapist. She arrived at the first session carrying a guilt she could barely articulate and a shame
she had not yet named, and what she said she most feared was that the woman she had been before that
afternoon on the trail was simply gone. What she most hoped, though she said it quietly and without
much conviction, was that someone might tell her otherwise.
