Rohan Mehta is a 26-year-old South Asian American man who works as an editor at a mid-sized
publishing house in Chicago. He is married and, until recently, was regarded by colleagues as
meticulous and warm. He presented for therapy approximately eight months after an encounter with
police that he describes, quietly and with long pauses, as the event that divided his life into a
before and an after.

The incident occurred on a winter evening when Rohan was walking home from a late shift. Officers
stopped him in an alley shortcut he had used dozens of times. What followed was prolonged: he was
pressed face-down against cold concrete, his cheek against pavement that he later estimated was near
freezing, his arms wrenched behind his back. A female officer knelt beside him for much of the
encounter, speaking in clipped, commanding tones while her partner searched his bag. He was
eventually released without charge. The whole episode lasted perhaps twenty minutes. He has not been
able to account for those twenty minutes in any straightforward way since.

In the weeks that followed, Rohan noticed that memory of the event did not behave the way he
expected memory to behave. It did not recede. When it surfaced — triggered by something as ordinary
as stepping outside on a cold morning and feeling the winter air against his face — it arrived with
his body already arranged in the posture of that alley: arms drawn inward, shoulders curled, cheek
tilted as though pressed against something hard. He would find himself at his desk at work,
manuscripts open in front of him, and realise his body had contracted around a memory he had not
consciously chosen to revisit. Sometimes the smell came with it — cold concrete and something
chemical, like cleaning solvent — arriving uninvited in the middle of an editorial meeting. And when
the memory came in its fullest form, what Rohan felt was not fear but grief, a grief so total it
surprised him each time, as though he were mourning something he could not yet name.

He began to avoid the cold. In practical terms this meant refusing to step outside without layering
so heavily that no air could reach his skin, even in early autumn. His wife, Priya, noticed he had
stopped accompanying her on evening walks. At work, the consequences became harder to conceal.
Rohan's office is open-plan, and he shares a small conference room with two female colleagues for
weekly editorial reviews. After the incident, being alone in that room with either of them — or with
any woman he did not know well — produced a vigilance he could not explain or suppress. He began
rescheduling those meetings, citing workload, then simply stopped attending some of them. His
managing editor flagged his absences in a performance review three months ago, noting a pattern of
avoidance that was affecting the team. Rohan said nothing in response because he had no language for
what was happening that did not sound, to him, like the language of someone falling apart.

That sense of falling apart had itself become a source of dread. He had come to believe, with a
certainty that felt almost logical, that his responses since the incident were evidence of a
fundamental inadequacy — that other people would have moved through this, that his inability to do
so confirmed something broken at his core. Alongside this sat a flattening of the future: he had
stopped making plans with Priya, stopped pitching book projects he had been excited about, stopped
imagining his life at thirty or thirty-five. Nothing in the forward direction felt real or
reachable. The life he had been building felt like it belonged to someone else.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment two weeks ago when he began to feel unreal mid-
conversation with Priya — a dissociative loosening, as though the room were slightly wrong — and he
recognised it as the same feeling he had learned to dread, the leading edge of something he could no
longer manage alone. He made the appointment the following morning. What he is most afraid of is
that nothing will restore the sense that his life contains possibility. What he is most hoping for,
though he struggles to say it directly, is to feel like himself again.
