Margaret Calloway, a 60-year-old divorced politician of Irish-American descent, presents with a
PCL-5 score of 68 following a motor vehicle accident in which she was the driver and in which a
pedestrian sustained serious injuries. She has been serving in a state legislative role for over a
decade, a position that has always demanded a high tolerance for public pressure and interpersonal
conflict. She sought help only after her office staff raised concerns about her performance, and she
arrived at the intake appointment visibly composed but effortfully so — the manner of a woman long
practiced at projecting control.

The incident occurred approximately fourteen months ago on a wet November evening. Margaret was
driving herself home from a late committee session, fatigued and preoccupied with the day's
proceedings. At an intersection she knew well, she failed to see a man stepping from between parked
cars and struck him at low speed. The impact was not catastrophic by some measures — he survived —
but the sound was. A sharp, dense thud followed immediately by the man's cry, high and animal, and
then her own scream as she braked. She remembers the particular way her neck snapped forward against
the seatbelt and the radiating ache that followed. She remembers the shouting — her own,
bystanders', eventually emergency responders — layered and disorienting against the hiss of rain.
She remained at the scene, shaking, until the ambulance came. The man suffered a fractured pelvis
and spent months in rehabilitation. Margaret was not charged. This, she has come to believe, made
everything worse.

In the weeks that followed, Margaret found herself unable to account for what she had done. She had
been driving that road for years. She had been careful, she had always been careful, and yet she was
certain the accident had happened because of some essential carelessness in her character — an
inattention she had simply never been caught in before. She would never forgive herself for not
having braked sooner, for not having seen him, for not having taken a different route. The public
nature of her role compounded her sense of exposure: a brief local news item had named her, and she
had come to feel permanently marked by it, humiliated in a way that no professional achievement
could reach.

Her avoidance developed gradually, in the way of habits that seem reasonable at the time. She
stopped driving within the first month, arranging for staff to transport her to legislative
sessions. Within a few months she had stopped being a passenger in cars whenever she could avoid it,
citing scheduling flexibility to colleagues who asked. The consequence has been significant: a
politician who once prided herself on constituent visibility now declines town hall events in
distant districts, citing workload. Her chief of staff has quietly noted that she has missed three
regional appearances in the past quarter. Margaret has told herself this is temporary.

Inside the chamber and in committee rooms, the difficulties are subtler but accumulating. When
debate grows heated and voices rise — as they reliably do in her line of work — Margaret feels her
body respond before her mind catches up: a flush of heat, a tightening across her chest, a sudden
difficulty tracking what is being said. She has found herself losing the thread of arguments she has
made dozens of times, sitting through votes with a concentration that feels like it is being held
together by will alone. She avoids any situation that might generate strong feeling, including, she
admits quietly, the celebratory dinners her party holds after legislative wins. She had come to
believe that if she allowed herself to feel anything at full volume, something would break that she
would not be able to repair.

When she tries to deliberately recall the accident in order to account for it, the memory resists
her — it will not come in an ordered way when she reaches for it. But it arrives unbidden: the sound
of the impact, heard again from inside her skull; the ache in her neck reanimating without warning.
What finally brought her to seek help was not the avoidance or the sleeplessness but a moment in
committee when a colleague shouted across the floor and Margaret's hands began to shake visibly. She
is most afraid, she says, that she is no longer fit for her role. What she hopes for, though she
offers this carefully, is to stop feeling like a person who causes harm simply by moving through the
world.
