Kofi Mensah is a 65-year-old Ghanaian-born man who has lived in the United States for nearly three
decades, working for the past eleven years as a floor associate at a large home-improvement retail
store in suburban Georgia. He came to the intake appointment accompanied by his wife, Abena, who sat
quietly in the waiting room while Kofi spoke with measured, careful words about what had happened to
him — and about what had not stopped happening since.

In the late summer of two years ago, Kofi was visiting family in coastal Louisiana when Hurricane
Ida made landfall. He had stayed behind with his brother-in-law to protect the property, both men
certain the storm would weaken before it arrived. It did not. The hours that followed were unlike
anything Kofi had encountered in sixty-three years of living: the walls of his brother-in-law's
house groaning and then partially giving way, floodwater rising past his knees and then his waist in
near-total darkness, the roar of wind so absolute it swallowed every other sound. For several hours
he clung to an interior door frame, unable to see more than inches in front of him, uncertain
whether the structure above him would hold or collapse entirely. When the water finally receded
enough for him to move, he found his brother-in-law injured but alive on the second floor. Both men
were rescued the following morning. Kofi flew home to Georgia four days later with dry clothes and,
as he described it to his therapist, "something broken inside that I cannot find to fix."

In the weeks after his return, Kofi noticed that the memories of those hours would not stay in the
past where he placed them. They arrived without warning — not as recollections but as re-immersions,
a film that did not merely play but pulled him back into the dark water, the groaning walls, the
sensation of not knowing whether the next moment would be his last. During these episodes he was
certain, in a way that no amount of reassurance could reach, that it was happening right now. Abena
would find him rigid at the kitchen table, his hands gripping the edge, and he would be unable to
explain to her that he was not in Georgia at all.

He had come to believe, with a quiet but unshakeable conviction, that the world had revealed its
true nature to him that night — that it was irreducibly dangerous, that safety was an arrangement
that could dissolve in hours, and that the man he had been before the storm was gone. He did not say
this dramatically; he said it the way a person states a fact they have verified repeatedly. He was
also certain that he had to remain alert at all times, that dropping his guard even briefly was an
invitation to catastrophe.

This certainty reorganised his daily life in ways that accumulated over months. At the retail store
where he works, Kofi now positions himself instinctively near the emergency exit at the far end of
the lumber aisle — his assigned section — and he has quietly rearranged his work patterns so that he
is rarely more than a few steps from that door. When customers approach him, he finds himself
scanning the high ceiling, the wide concrete floor, the loading bay doors, cataloguing what might
give way and what might hold. He falls asleep with difficulty most nights, feeling that
unconsciousness itself is a form of exposure, a dropping of the watch he believes he must keep. He
has begun declining invitations to family gatherings held in unfamiliar buildings, and Abena has
noticed that he will not sit with his back to a room. A younger colleague at the store mentioned to
a supervisor that Kofi seemed distracted and difficult to reach; Kofi received a quiet word about
customer engagement that he found humiliating and that deepened his sense that the damage in him was
permanent and visible to others.

What finally brought him to seek help was Abena's direct and tearful request one evening, after she
found him standing at their bedroom window at two in the morning, scanning the yard. He came, he
said, for her. What he feared most was that therapy would confirm what he already suspected — that
there was no going back. What he hoped for, almost against his own belief, was to be wrong about
that.
