Valentina Reyes is a 26-year-old Salvadoran American woman who works as a school counselor at a
middle school in the same city where she grew up. She presented for treatment describing a pattern
of distress that she had, until recently, managed to attribute to stress or personality — something
she had simply always been. It was only after a student disclosed a situation of domestic violence
at home that Valentina found herself unable to function for the remainder of that school day,
sitting in her car in the parking lot for over an hour, and she began to accept that what she was
carrying was something older and heavier than work fatigue.

Valentina grew up in a two-bedroom apartment in a dense urban neighborhood, the eldest of three
children. From approximately age seven through twelve, she witnessed her father's repeated violence
toward her mother — eruptions that were sudden, physical, and loud, often occurring in the kitchen
or living room with no warning that Valentina could reliably detect in advance. She described one
incident with particular clarity: she had been sitting at the kitchen table doing homework when her
father entered, and within seconds the room transformed. She remembered the sound of her mother's
back hitting the refrigerator, the way the metal surface rang out, and her own paralysis — she had
not moved, had not called for help, had not done anything. Her mother eventually left the
relationship when Valentina was twelve, but by then the family had cycled through a brief period of
contact with child protective services that resulted in no sustained intervention. Valentina had
told a teacher once, obliquely. Nothing changed.

In the years that followed, Valentina constructed a life oriented around competence and care for
others. She studied hard, earned a graduate degree, and chose a profession in which she could be the
adult who actually listened to children. But the architecture of her daily life had quietly
organized itself around the residue of those years. She had never returned to the neighborhood where
she grew up, routing her commute to avoid even passing the street where the apartment building
stood. The avoidance had become so automatic that she no longer consciously registered it as a
choice.

At work, Valentina is warm and skilled with students, but colleagues have noticed that she tends to
position herself near the wall in staff meetings, angling her chair so that the door remains in her
sightline. She has come to feel that sitting with her back exposed is genuinely dangerous, not
metaphorically uncomfortable but concretely unsafe, and she arranges herself accordingly in every
room she enters, including her own apartment. Even there, she described a persistent sense of being
on guard — checking the lock twice before bed, lying awake listening to the building settle. When a
colleague placed a hand briefly on her shoulder during a difficult conversation about a student,
Valentina's body responded before her mind could intervene: she startled sharply, knocking her
coffee cup from the table, and spent the rest of the afternoon ashamed and over-explaining.

She scans people carefully, reading for signs of volatility — a raised voice in the hallway, a man
who moves with a certain energy. She had come to believe, as a settled and rarely examined
conviction, that people cannot be trusted to remain safe, that their surfaces are unreliable. This
vigilance extended inward as well. She returned repeatedly, in quiet moments, to the night she sat
frozen at the kitchen table, and she was certain that if she had acted differently — screamed, run,
called someone — the years of violence might have unfolded otherwise. The logic was circular and she
knew it intellectually, but the feeling of culpability did not yield to logic. She also carried a
corrosive sense that the systems meant to protect her and her mother had simply looked away, and she
understood her own career partly as a correction of that failure — though the knowledge that she now
sat inside one of those same institutions complicated things.

When Valentina tries to explain what happened to her as a child, she loses the thread partway
through, the narrative fragmenting into sensation and silence. The memory does not arrive in
sequence; it arrives whole and without invitation, triggered by the ring of metal, by a raised voice
in a classroom corridor, by the smell of a particular cleaning product. What finally brought her to
seek help was not just the parking lot afternoon, but her recognition that she was becoming less
present with her students — the very thing she had built her life to prevent. What she feared most,
sitting across from the intake clinician, was that telling it would make it more real. What she
hoped for, though she could barely say it aloud, was that it might finally become the past.
