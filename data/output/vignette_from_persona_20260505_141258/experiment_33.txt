Marisol Reyes is a 39-year-old Salvadoran American woman, currently serving as an active-duty
soldier, who was referred for psychological evaluation following persistent functional difficulties
that have intensified over the past eighteen months. She is married and describes her husband as
supportive, though she acknowledges that her behavior at home has become increasingly difficult for
both of them to understand.

Approximately two years ago, Marisol was on approved leave visiting extended family in a rural
region of Central America when a catastrophic mudslide struck the hillside community where she was
staying. The event unfolded in the early morning hours, preceded by several days of heavy,
relentless rain. She described waking to a sound she initially mistook for distant artillery — a
deep, grinding roar — before the structure around her began to shift. She was partially buried in
debris for nearly four hours before emergency responders and local residents were able to extract
her. During those hours, she could hear others calling out and then going silent. She could not
move. Rescue workers and local police officers were among the first faces she saw when she was
finally pulled free, their voices loud and overlapping, their commands urgent and unintelligible
through the noise of machinery and continued rainfall. She sustained physical injuries but
recovered. Several members of her extended family did not.

In the weeks that followed her return to the United States and to active duty, Marisol began
noticing changes she initially attributed to normal readjustment. But the changes did not resolve.
On days when the sky turned a particular shade of grey and the air carried that specific heaviness
before rain, she found herself stopping mid-task, her body going rigid, her attention narrowing to
the sound of water against windows or pavement. During a training exercise involving simulated
explosive sounds, she dropped to the floor before she had consciously registered what she was doing.
Loud noises of any kind — a door slamming in the barracks, a vehicle backfiring — produced the same
involuntary lurch, a full-body startle that left her shaking and embarrassed. The presence of police
officers, whose uniforms and radios and raised voices had been woven into the chaos of her rescue,
had come to feel not like safety but like the imminence of catastrophe.

What has accumulated over these months is a way of moving through the world that Marisol herself
describes as exhausting. She enters every room — the mess hall, the briefing room, her own living
room — and before she sits down she has already noted the exits, assessed which path would be
fastest, calculated what might block her. She scans crowds, parking lots, and open fields with the
same vigilance she would bring to a hostile environment, and she cannot turn it off even when she
knows, intellectually, that she is safe. Ordinary sounds that her colleagues absorb without reaction
pull her attention like an alarm. She had come to believe that these responses were evidence of
something fundamentally broken in her — that a trained soldier, someone who had been through
deployments and hardship, should not be undone by rain. She was certain that her inability to simply
manage herself meant she was a lousy coper, weaker than she had ever allowed herself to be. More
troubling to her was a feeling she struggled to articulate: a sense that she had become something
less than a person, that she moved through her days as though she were a body being acted upon
rather than a woman making choices. She felt that bad things would find her regardless of what she
did, that the mudslide had confirmed something she now could not unknow.

The occupational consequences have been concrete. Marisol has requested reassignment away from
outdoor training exercises on overcast days, citing minor physical complaints — a pattern her
commanding officer eventually flagged. Her husband has described her as absent even when present,
and the two have stopped spending time with friends because public spaces require too much of her
vigilance to feel worth it.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment in which she realized she had mapped the exits of
her own home. She is most afraid, she says, that this is simply who she is now. What she hopes,
quietly, is that she is wrong.
