Matthias Brenner is a 49-year-old European-American fashion designer who has been married for
twenty-two years and has built a career around precision, beauty, and the careful construction of
things that last. He presents for treatment approximately fourteen months after the event that, by
his own account, divided his life into two irreconcilable halves.

On a Sunday afternoon in early autumn, Matthias's seventeen-year-old son, Luca, was struck by a
vehicle while cycling near their home in a suburb outside Chicago. Matthias was not present at the
moment of impact, but he arrived at the scene within minutes, having received a call from a
neighbor. What he found — Luca unconscious on the asphalt, a spreading dark stain beneath his
helmet, the bicycle wheel still spinning — became the material around which his inner life has since
reorganized itself entirely. Luca survived, but spent eleven days in intensive care and sustained a
traumatic brain injury that has permanently altered his speech and fine motor function. Matthias
stood at the roadside that afternoon, spoke to the paramedics, held his son's hand in the ambulance,
and then, at some point he cannot fully account for, he was in a hospital waiting room. The gap
between those two moments remains one of the most distressing features of his recall. Some images
from that day are engraved with terrible clarity — the spinning wheel, the particular gray of the
pavement, the paramedic's orange gloves — while other stretches are simply absent, and he cannot
determine whether he said the right things, did the right things, or whether there was a moment in
which he could have acted differently. He had come to believe, with a conviction that resisted all
counter-evidence, that there was something he had failed to do or say, some intervention he had
missed, and that this failure was the defining moral fact of his life.

In the months that followed, Matthias found that certain features of his ordinary working
environment had become unbearable. He is a designer whose studio is located in the city's garment
district, and his work regularly involves courier deliveries, fabric shipments arriving by van, and
the constant movement of vehicles on the street below his studio windows. On several occasions, the
sound of a delivery truck braking sharply outside, or the sight of a cyclist passing on the street,
produced in him an immediate and violent physical response — his heart would seize, his hands would
shake, and he would find himself gripping the edge of his drafting table, unable to speak to the
assistants around him. He began scheduling his most important client meetings for rooms at the back
of the building, away from street-facing windows, and over several months his staff came to assume
this was simply a preference. It was not. He had also stopped attending the regular industry events
and seasonal planning meetings that had previously anchored his professional calendar. When
colleagues proposed collaborative projects or asked him to commit to collections twelve months out,
he found himself unable to respond. The idea of making plans — of imagining a future in which he was
present and functional — felt to him like a kind of presumption he no longer had the right to make.

At home, he described keeping himself relentlessly occupied: working late, filling evenings with
administrative tasks, watching television programs he had no interest in, anything to prevent the
stillness in which the memory could surface. When it did surface, the emotional force of it was such
that he could not remain with it for more than a few seconds before needing to stop entirely — to
leave the room, to splash water on his face, to find some task. He slept poorly, lying awake in the
early hours with a heaviness he could not name as anything other than shame.

What finally brought Matthias to seek help was a conversation with his wife, who told him quietly
one evening that she felt she had lost him too. He wept, which surprised him. He came to the first
session saying he was not sure therapy could help someone who had simply not done enough. What he
hoped for, he said, was to be able to look at his son without feeling that he was the reason Luca's
life had been diminished.
