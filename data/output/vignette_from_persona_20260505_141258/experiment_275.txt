Linh Tran is a 57-year-old Vietnamese American interior designer who has been married for nearly
thirty years. She presents with a PCL-5 score of 72, seeking help for the first time following what
she describes as a "breaking point" she can no longer manage alone.

Approximately two years ago, Linh was attending a design trade exposition at a convention center
when a coordinated terrorist attack unfolded in the building. She was in a crowded exhibition hall
when the first explosion occurred. In the chaos that followed, she was pressed against a display
wall by the surging crowd, unable to move, surrounded by screaming and the acrid smell of smoke
mixed with the spilled alcohol from a nearby hospitality bar that had been overturned in the
stampede. She watched a man near her sustain a serious injury. She remained trapped in that corner
for what she later learned was forty minutes before emergency personnel reached her. She was
physically unharmed.

In the weeks after the attack, Linh found herself unable to look at the photographs she had taken at
the exposition before the event occurred. She had loaded them onto her laptop intending to delete
them, but the moment the thumbnails appeared on the screen, something shifted violently inside her —
the hall, the heat, the noise — and she slammed the laptop shut. Since then, old photos of any kind,
even family albums her husband leaves on the coffee table, have become difficult to be near. She has
come to understand, in her own wordless way, that images carry the past too close to the surface.
Similarly, the smell of alcohol — at dinner parties, in restaurants, even from her husband's
occasional glass of wine — lands on her with a physical force she cannot explain to others. She has
quietly stopped attending social events where drinking is likely, telling colleagues she is simply
busy.

Talking about the attack remains impossible. When a close friend raised it gently over lunch eight
months ago, Linh felt the room tilt. She excused herself, said she was fine, and has not had lunch
with that friend since. In her work as an interior designer, client meetings occasionally involve
discussing high-profile public venues — hotels, event spaces — and she has begun declining those
commissions without explanation, a pattern that has started to cost her professionally and has left
her business partner confused and quietly frustrated.

There are moments during the day when Linh is no longer in her studio but back inside the convention
center — not remembering it, but inhabiting it, the crush of bodies, the smell. These episodes
arrive without warning, and she has come to dread them as evidence that something in her mind has
broken permanently. She has come to believe that she is simply not built to withstand ordinary
difficulty anymore, that the slightest disruption — a delayed delivery, a disagreement with a client
— undoes her entirely. When someone touches her shoulder unexpectedly, even her husband reaching for
her in the kitchen, she startles so sharply that she has knocked things from countertops. She
watches strangers in public with a quiet, exhausting vigilance, scanning for something she cannot
name but is certain she would recognize. The world, she has concluded, is not safe and never was;
she simply had not been paying close enough attention before.

To manage what she cannot articulate, Linh has developed a set of private rituals. She runs — far
more than is healthy for her joints, which now ache chronically — because the physical effort quiets
something. She eats in ways that comfort and distract her, often alone after her husband has gone to
bed. And on the worst nights, she has hurt herself physically, pressing her nails into her forearm
or striking her thigh, not from a wish to die but because the sharp sensation seems to interrupt the
unbearable pressure building inside her. She has told no one about this.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was her husband finding her one evening on the kitchen floor,
unable to explain why she was crying. She saw his face — frightened, helpless — and felt a shame she
could not bear. She hopes, more than anything, that she is not beyond help. What she fears most is
that she is.
