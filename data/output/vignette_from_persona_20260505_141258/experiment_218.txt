Linh Tran is a 36-year-old Vietnamese-American urban planner who presented for psychological
assessment reporting significant distress that she traced back to a deployment she undertook four
years ago as a relief worker in a conflict zone in the Middle East. She had volunteered with an
international humanitarian organization during what was described to her as a stabilization phase,
but within weeks of her arrival the situation deteriorated sharply. The incident that she identifies
as the core of her current suffering occurred on a morning when her team drove a supply convoy
through a rural corridor flanked by open scrubland. The landscape was oddly peaceful — pale grass,
low hills, a wide sky — and Linh remembers noticing how the light looked almost golden. Then,
without warning, there was shouting from the vehicle behind her, a rapid escalation of voices she
could not immediately parse, and within seconds the convoy was caught in crossfire. Two of her
colleagues were killed. Linh survived by lying flat in a ditch at the roadside for what she later
learned was nearly forty minutes, listening to men's voices shouting commands in a language she did
not speak, unable to move, certain she was about to die. She returned home six weeks later, having
told almost no one what had happened.

In the months that followed, Linh continued working, and for a time her professional competence
seemed to hold. But the world around her had quietly reorganized itself into a landscape of threat.
Because her work as an urban planner frequently takes her to site visits — open lots, parks under
development, green corridors along rivers — she began encountering, repeatedly, terrain that
recalled the scrubland of that morning. Standing in a meadow to assess a proposed greenway, or
walking the edge of a new park, she would feel the ground shift beneath her, the present dissolving,
and find herself back in that ditch, the shouting still in her ears, the golden light still on the
grass. These episodes were not brief. They consumed her entirely. She had come to believe that being
outdoors in any natural setting was genuinely unsafe, that her body knew something her rational mind
refused to accept. She began scheduling site visits only when she could bring a colleague, and
eventually began delegating them altogether, an accommodation that her supervisor had noticed and
that had generated a quiet but accumulating professional tension.

Shouting of any kind — a raised voice in a meeting, a contractor's argument overheard on a
construction site, even a cheer from a neighboring apartment — produced an immediate physical
response: her body braced, her attention narrowed, and she scanned for exits. Unexpected movement
nearby, a colleague stepping suddenly into her peripheral vision, a car door opening as she passed,
produced the same reaction. At night she lay awake with a vigilance she experienced not as anxiety
but as necessity, as though sleep itself was a position of exposure she could not afford. She had
found a box of photographs from the deployment in a storage drawer several months ago and had not
been able to open it since; even knowing it was there felt like a weight in the apartment.

When she tried to talk about what had happened — to her partner, Dani, or once to a friend — she
found that the account collapsed on itself. She would begin, lose the thread entirely, and emerge
from the attempt feeling that the memory belonged to someone else, a woman she had once been and
could no longer access. What she could access was rage: a sudden, flooding anger that arrived
whenever the memory surfaced, an anger she could not direct or explain. To manage the feelings that
had no other outlet, she had begun hurting herself physically, pressing her nails into her forearm
or striking her own leg, describing it as the only thing that returned her to her body. She kept her
mind occupied constantly — podcasts, work, scrolling — because stillness felt like an invitation.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was a conversation with Dani, who told her quietly that she
seemed to be disappearing. Linh did not argue. What she feared most, sitting in the intake room, was
that if she opened the memory fully it would confirm what she already half-believed: that the person
who lay in that ditch had not entirely come back.
