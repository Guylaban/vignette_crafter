Caleb is a 25-year-old single man of North American background who works as a housekeeper at a mid-
sized hotel in his city. He presented for psychological assessment reporting a cluster of
distressing symptoms that, as he described them in the intake session, had been quietly dismantling
his life for years before finally becoming impossible to manage.

Caleb grew up in a household where his primary caregiver — a man he describes with a flat, careful
voice — would come into his room at night. The room would already be dark. Caleb remembers the
particular texture of the worn cotton bedsheet pulled up to his chin, the fabric rough and pilled
against his knuckles, and the sound that would sometimes precede or follow these intrusions: a
younger sibling crying in the next room, audible through the thin wall. The abuse was physical and
repeated across several years of his childhood. By the time it ended — through circumstance rather
than any intervention — Caleb had learned to associate darkness, that specific tactile roughness,
and the sound of children crying with the particular quality of helplessness and exposure he had
felt as a boy.

In the years that followed, Caleb managed well enough on the surface. He finished school, found
work, maintained a modest independence. But in his early twenties the symptoms intensified in ways
he could no longer explain away. At night, waking in the dark of his apartment, he would find
himself not merely startled but somewhere else entirely — heart hammering, skin crawling, the room
feeling not like his room but like that childhood bedroom. These were not dreams he could shake off.
His memories of the abuse return to him like a film that plays without his consent, and when they
come, they come in full — the smell, the weight, the texture of that sheet — so completely real
across his senses that stopping whatever he is doing becomes the only option. He has learned that
trying to push through the memory only makes the overwhelm worse, so he stops, waits, breathes, and
tries to return.

At work, the triggers find him in unexpected places. Changing bed linens — the central task of his
job — means handling sheets of varying textures, and certain fabrics, rougher or more worn, produce
an immediate and violent physical reaction: his chest tightens, his hands go cold, and on several
occasions he has had to leave a guest room abruptly and stand in the corridor until the sensation
passes. He has begun requesting assignments to public spaces — lobbies, hallways — rather than guest
rooms, telling his supervisor only that he is having some health issues. The consequence has been a
quiet demotion in practice if not in title, and a growing awareness among his colleagues that
something is off. When children are staying at the hotel and he can hear them crying in the
corridors, he has found himself frozen at the housekeeping cart, unable to move.

Caleb has come to believe, with a certainty that feels unshakeable, that other people can read what
happened to him simply by looking at him — that his history is somehow visible, written into his
face or his posture. He feels, in moments he finds difficult to articulate, less like a person than
like something acted upon, something without interior life. He is certain that harm can come from
anyone, that there is no reliable way to identify who is safe, and so he has arranged his life
accordingly: he makes sure never to be alone with another person if he can avoid it, always
positioning himself near exits or in shared spaces. When the weight of daily demands becomes too
much — the triggers at work, the nights — he finds himself drifting, retreating into an internal
world where nothing can reach him. Over the past year he has let several responsibilities lapse,
including a community college enrollment he had been proud of.

What finally brought Caleb to seek help was a night last month when he woke in the dark and, for
several minutes, genuinely could not locate himself in time. He was most afraid, he said quietly at
the end of the intake, that this was simply who he was now. What he hoped, with some difficulty, was
that it didn't have to be.
