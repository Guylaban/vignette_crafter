Kofi Mensah is a 63-year-old Ghanaian-American journalist, married for thirty-four years, who has
spent the better part of his career translating complexity into language — finding the precise word,
the clarifying phrase, the sentence that makes the unbearable comprehensible to a reader. It is a
particular cruelty, then, that the event he most needs to describe is the one that has robbed him of
exactly that capacity.

Fourteen months ago, Kofi's wife Abena suffered a catastrophic stroke while the two of them were
having dinner at home on a quiet Tuesday evening. He remembers the meal with an intimacy that
disturbs him: the groundnut soup she had made from her mother's recipe, its rich, oily warmth still
coating his tongue in a way that has nothing to do with appetite. When the memory surfaces — and it
surfaces without warning — he can taste it again, fully and completely, that specific sweetness and
heat, and the taste arrives before any conscious thought, before he has chosen to remember anything
at all. Abena had been speaking, laughing at something she'd seen on her phone, and then her words
became something else: a sliding, garbled sound that didn't belong to her, syllables that had come
loose from meaning. Kofi had sat very still for a moment, certain he had misheard, and then he
understood that he had not misheard at all. He called emergency services and held her hand on the
kitchen floor until the paramedics arrived, and during those minutes her voice continued — a soft,
confused murmur that he could not decode and could not answer.

Abena survived. She is home, she is recovering, and her speech has largely returned. But Kofi has
not returned to where he was before that evening.

In the weeks that followed, colleagues and family noticed that he had become hesitant in
conversation, sometimes stopping mid-sentence and pressing his fingers to his mouth as though
physically searching for the words he needed. When asked directly what had happened that night, he
would begin the account and then lose it — the thread simply dropped, the sequence dissolving,
leaving him standing in the middle of a story he could not finish. He has come to dread this
particular failure more than almost anything else, because language is not incidental to who Kofi
is; it is the instrument of his professional identity. In editorial meetings at the newspaper where
he works, he has twice been unable to complete a verbal summary of a story he had written himself,
and he has watched his younger colleagues exchange glances he pretends not to see.

The triggers that have reorganised his daily life are specific and, to him, feel entirely rational.
When conversations turn to the future — plans, hopes, what someone intends to do next year — Kofi
becomes acutely uncomfortable in a way he struggles to name. He had come to feel that the future is
a country he no longer has reliable access to, and talk of it presses on something raw. More
disorienting still is any sound that resembles incomprehensible chatter: a television left on in
another room, a crowded restaurant where voices blur together, a phone call with poor reception.
These sounds do not merely annoy him; they relocate him. He finds himself back on the kitchen floor,
hearing Abena's voice come apart. He has quietly stopped attending the staff gatherings his editor
holds at a local bar, where noise accumulates in exactly that way, and has offered scheduling
conflicts as the explanation.

He is also unable to sit with conversations in which someone has lost a person they love. As a
journalist he has conducted hundreds of such interviews, and he has always considered this capacity
— to witness grief without flinching — one of his professional strengths. He has now declined two
assignments that would have required it, telling his editor his schedule was full.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was a conversation with Abena herself, who told him gently
that she felt she could not speak freely about her own fears for the future in front of him, and
that this silence was its own kind of loneliness. He arrived at the intake appointment holding a
small notebook, as if he might need it to find the words. What he most hopes for, he said quietly,
is to be able to finish a sentence about that night — just once, all the way to the end.
