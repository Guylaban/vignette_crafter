Miriam Calloway is a 72-year-old married woman of North American background who works as a network
engineer — a career she has sustained well into her eighth decade through a combination of technical
precision and professional discipline. She presented for psychological assessment approximately
eighteen months after witnessing a violent incident at a downtown bus terminal, describing a pattern
of distress that had quietly reshaped her daily life before she finally acknowledged she needed
help.

On the afternoon in question, Miriam had been waiting at a crowded transit hub to catch a bus home
after a client meeting. The terminal was dense with commuters, the kind of shoulder-to-shoulder
press she had navigated unremarkably for decades. She was standing near the boarding queue when a
physical altercation broke out several feet ahead of her — sudden, explosive, and close enough that
she felt the shift in the crowd before she fully understood what she was seeing. A man was beaten
with sustained and deliberate violence while passengers scattered around her. Miriam did not run
immediately. She stood frozen as the crowd surged, people pushing against her from every direction,
the noise rising and collapsing in waves. She remembers the specific quality of the crowd's movement
— not a single direction but a panicked, churning compression — and the way no one seemed to know
where safety was. By the time she reached the street, her legs were shaking. She rode home in a taxi
in silence and told her husband only that there had been "some trouble."

In the weeks that followed, Miriam found herself reorganising her commute. She stopped taking the
bus entirely, telling colleagues it was simply more efficient to drive. She began avoiding the
transit hub itself, rerouting her drives so she would not pass within a block of it. These felt like
reasonable adjustments at first — practical, even sensible. What frightened her most was not the
memory of the violence itself but the crowd: she had come to believe that compressed, unpredictable
gatherings of people represented a specific and unmanageable danger, that something could erupt
without warning and she would again be unable to move. Shopping centres, which she had once visited
without a second thought, became places she declined to enter. Her husband began doing the grocery
shopping alone, and when he asked why, Miriam said she simply preferred it that way.

At work, where her role involves occasional on-site visits to data centres and client offices, she
found herself scanning rooms when she arrived, positioning herself near exits, noting how many
people were present. She was certain that vigilance was not anxiety but wisdom — that what she had
learned at the terminal was simply the truth about the world, which is that it is dangerous and that
most people are not paying adequate attention. When younger colleagues talked casually about transit
or mentioned crowded venues in conversation, she would redirect the topic or fall quiet, finding she
could not engage with such subjects without a tightening in her chest that she did not want to
examine too closely.

Miriam also developed what she described as a deliberate mental discipline: she would not allow
herself to dwell on anything distressing, actively pushing her thoughts toward gratitude,
competence, the concrete satisfactions of her work. She framed this to herself as resilience. What
she had not anticipated was a growing sense of estrangement — from her husband, from colleagues,
from the ordinary rhythms of a life she recognised but no longer felt fully part of. She had come to
believe, without quite putting it into words until her intake session, that she was permanently on
the outside of something she could not name: a kind of ordinary ease that other people seemed to
carry and that she no longer had access to.

It was her husband who finally said, gently and with evident worry, that she seemed to have
disappeared inside herself. Miriam sat with that for several weeks before making the appointment.
She arrived composed and articulate, minimising nothing but volunteering little. What she said she
hoped for was modest: to ride a bus again without dreading it. What she was most afraid of, she
admitted after a long pause, was that the person she had been before that afternoon at the terminal
was simply gone.
