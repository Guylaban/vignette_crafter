Ren Thongchai is a 40-year-old Thai-American journalist, divorced, who presents for his initial
intake following a referral from his primary care physician. He had spent the better part of his
career covering political unrest across Southeast Asia, a vocation he had chosen out of genuine
conviction that bearing witness mattered. That conviction was tested irrevocably three years ago
when, while reporting on a crackdown near the Thai-Myanmar border, he was detained by paramilitary
forces and held for eleven days. During that time he was subjected to repeated sessions of physical
and psychological torture — beaten, deprived of sleep, subjected to prolonged stress positions, and
at intervals made to listen to the sounds of other detainees, including children separated from
their families in the same facility, crying in adjacent rooms. He was eventually released without
explanation and made his way back to Bangkok, then to the United States. He has told almost no one
the full account of what happened.

In the months that followed his return, Ren found that the memory of those eleven days did not
arrange itself into anything he could hold steadily in his mind. When he tried to deliberately
recall the sequence of events — to write about it, to explain it to a therapist he briefly saw — he
found the memory strangely inaccessible, as though it had been placed behind frosted glass. There
were entire periods he simply could not retrieve, gaps that troubled him not because he feared what
they contained but because their blankness felt like evidence of something broken in him. What he
could access felt so extreme, so discontinuous from the person he had been before, that he had come
to half-believe it had not happened to him at all — or had happened to someone who no longer
existed.

He had returned to journalism in a diminished capacity, writing domestic policy pieces from a desk
in Chicago. The newsroom, which had once felt like home, became a place of vigilance. He found
himself watching colleagues closely — tracking the shift of a body, the tone of a voice — certain in
a way he could not fully articulate that harm could arrive without warning from almost anyone.
Ordinary sounds in the open-plan office made him flinch visibly, and he had begun positioning
himself near exits. One afternoon, a junior reporter's young daughter was brought in during a school
holiday; the child began crying when she scraped her knee, and Ren left the building without a word
and did not return that day. His editor noticed the pattern of absences. A story he had been
assigned on immigration detention — work that would once have felt urgent and meaningful — sat
unfinished on his desktop for four months.

Outside work, Ren had progressively withdrawn from the friends and colleagues he had known before
the detention. He had come to believe, with a quiet but absolute certainty, that people who had
known him before could see something in him now — some mark of what had been done to him, or of what
he had done or failed to do — and that their company confirmed it. He was certain there was
something fundamentally wrong with him as a person, not merely something that had happened to him.
He had acted in ways during those eleven days that he measured against the man he had believed
himself to be and found the distance unbearable. He did not specify the details in intake, but the
guilt and shame he described were not abstract. He had also largely stopped eating regular meals,
managing on very little, in a pattern he could not entirely explain except that appetite had come to
feel irrelevant or undeserved.

He spent considerable time, particularly at night, turning over the question of what he could have
taken differently — a different route, a different source, an earlier departure — dwelling on the
architecture of prevention until the early hours. Occasionally, hearing a name phonetically similar
to one of the guards, spoken casually in a café or on the radio, produced a sudden wave of dizziness
that he found deeply disorienting and that he had come to dread.

What finally brought Ren to seek help was his divorce being finalised last month — a quiet
administrative event that nonetheless made the isolation feel total. He arrives hoping, tentatively,
to understand why he cannot simply move forward. What he is most afraid of is being seen clearly.
