Priya Sharma is a 32-year-old South Asian woman who works as a school counselor at a mid-sized
public elementary school in the suburbs of Minneapolis. She is married, college-educated, and until
approximately eighteen months ago was regarded by colleagues and family alike as someone of unusual
steadiness and warmth. She is now presenting for psychological assessment at the insistence of her
husband, who has watched her withdraw from nearly every aspect of the life they built together.

Two years ago, Priya traveled to her mother's home country of Sri Lanka to visit extended family and
to volunteer for two weeks with an educational nonprofit working in a region that had experienced
intermittent civil unrest. On her third day in a rural district, the convoy of vehicles she was
traveling in was caught in the middle of a sudden escalation of violence between armed factions. The
vehicles stopped on an exposed road. There was gunfire. Priya was crouched in the back of a minivan
alongside four other volunteers and a local translator named Amara, a young woman in her mid-
twenties. When the shooting intensified, Priya froze. She did not move toward Amara, who was nearest
the door and visibly panicking. Amara opened the door and was struck by debris from an explosion in
the seconds that followed. She survived but sustained serious injuries. Priya was physically
unharmed. The convoy eventually reached safety, and Priya was evacuated from the country within
forty-eight hours.

In the weeks that followed her return to Minneapolis, Priya found herself replaying the moment she
froze — not as a coherent recollection but as something that broke through without warning, vivid
and disorienting, arriving without context or clear beginning. The memories carried a quality of
immediacy that she struggled to articulate, as though time had collapsed and she was still on that
road. She began to notice that the sound of a bus engine idling outside her school building, or the
hydraulic hiss of its doors, would bring on a sudden, overwhelming return of that feeling — the
cramped interior of the van, the noise, the stillness in her own body. She stopped taking the city
bus she had relied on for years and began driving to work, adding forty minutes to her commute each
way rather than step onto public transit.

What had settled most heavily into Priya's inner life was not the fear itself but the meaning she
had made of what she did and did not do. She had come to believe, with a certainty that felt
unshakeable, that she had failed Amara in a way that revealed something essential and damning about
her character. She was certain she was weak — not situationally overwhelmed, but fundamentally,
constitutionally incapable of rising to a moment that mattered. This belief had migrated into her
professional identity in ways that were now affecting her work. As a school counselor, Priya had
always been the person students came to in crisis — the adult in the room who stayed calm and knew
what to do. She no longer trusted herself to be that person. She had begun deflecting the more acute
cases to her supervisor, citing administrative reasons, quietly certain that she was no longer
capable of the very thing that had defined her vocation.

necklines regardless of the season, preferring to move through public spaces as inconspicuously as
possible. She avoided the television in the evenings, and when her husband watched the news she
would leave the room, unwilling to encounter footage of conflict zones or civil unrest that might
press against the edges of what she carried. The news had come to feel like an exposure she could
not afford.

came to her office after witnessing a fight on the playground and was visibly shaken. She sat across

the session early. She drove home and told her husband she thought something was seriously wrong.

gone. What she hopes, though she can barely say it aloud, is that someone will tell her she is not
the person she has decided she is.
