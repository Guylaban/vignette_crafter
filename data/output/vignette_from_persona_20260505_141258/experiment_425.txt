Boon-Nam Srisawat is a 69-year-old widowed Thai-American man who presents for an initial
psychological consultation after more than two years of what he describes, haltingly, as "not being
myself anymore." He works as a nanny for a family in suburban Minneapolis, caring for two school-
aged children, a role he took up after retiring from restaurant work and one he had come to regard
as a source of quiet purpose in the years following his wife's death. It is a role that has become,
without his fully understanding why, almost unbearable to him.

The event that preceded his current difficulties occurred approximately twenty-six months ago, when
the younger of the two children in his care, a four-year-old boy named Marcus, collapsed without
warning while Boon-Nam was preparing lunch. The child had been running through the kitchen moments
before, laughing, and then he was on the floor, his lips darkening, his small body rigid. Boon-Nam
called emergency services and stayed on the line, following instructions he could not fully process,
until paramedics arrived. Marcus was rushed to hospital and diagnosed with a severe cardiac
arrhythmia. He survived, and after several weeks of treatment he returned home. By any external
measure, the outcome was as good as it could have been. But for Boon-Nam, something did not resolve.

In the weeks that followed, he found that he could not construct a coherent account of what had
happened, even in the privacy of his own mind. When he tried to recall the sequence of events, the
images came not as a story with a beginning and an end but as fragments — the sound of the child's
laughter cutting off, the colour of the kitchen tiles, the dispatcher's voice, the weight of
waiting. These fragments did not feel like memories of something past. They felt immediate, present-
tense, as though the kitchen floor were still beneath his knees. He described his recollections of
that afternoon as more vivid and more real than anything else in his life, more real than his wife's
face, more real than his own reflection in the mirror. This quality disturbed him deeply.

He began to try hard not to think about it. He kept the television on from the moment he woke until
he fell asleep. He filled every quiet moment with tasks, errands, anything that required his
attention. At work, this meant an almost compulsive busyness — reorganising cupboards, preparing
elaborate snacks, keeping the children in motion — because stillness was when the fragments
returned. The children's laughter, which had once been the texture of a good day, now sometimes
stopped him mid-movement, not because it reminded him of Marcus's laughter before the collapse, but
because it preceded it. The joy and the silence that followed had become inseparable in his
experience of that day.

He had not seen a doctor since the event, despite his own age and the various aches he had begun to
notice. The thought of a waiting room, of clinical smells, of someone asking him questions about his
health and wellbeing, felt intolerable in a way he could not fully articulate. He had come to
believe that seeking help would require him to say aloud what he had witnessed, and he was not
certain he could do that without something in him breaking permanently. He had already come to feel
that he was permanently changed — that whatever steadiness and warmth had defined him as a man, as a
caregiver, as a grandfather figure to the children he tended, had been altered in a way that could
not be undone. He was certain that the person looking back at him from the mirror was not the man he
had been. He had also grown quietly suspicious of the family he worked for, of colleagues, of
neighbours — not for any specific reason he could name, but with a settled sense that people were
not fully what they presented themselves to be, that surfaces concealed things.

What finally brought him to seek help was not a crisis but a conversation. The mother of the
children told him gently that Marcus had asked why Boon-Nam never smiled anymore. He sat with that
for several days before making the call. What he most fears is that the man Marcus remembers — the
one who smiled — no longer exists. What he most hopes is that someone can tell him otherwise.
