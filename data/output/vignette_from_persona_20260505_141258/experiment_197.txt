the door before the intake session had formally begun.

Ernesto served in the United States Army during a period of active combat deployment in Central
America in his early twenties. The event he identified as the origin of his current difficulties
occurred during a patrol ambush in which his unit came under sustained fire in dense jungle terrain.
Several men were wounded, and Ernesto, separated briefly from his squad, found himself pinned behind
cover for what he estimates was close to an hour. What he remembers most vividly is not the sound of
gunfire but the moment it stopped — the sudden, disorienting silence, and the way the world seemed
to tilt beneath him as he rose to his feet, so dizzy he had to brace against a tree trunk to stay
upright. He had eaten field rations just before the patrol, and the particular metallic, processed
taste of that meal sat in his mouth through the entire ordeal and for hours afterward as he helped
carry a wounded colleague to the evacuation point. Those two sensory details — the dizziness and
that taste — became, without his choosing it, the body's private archive of that afternoon.

For many years after his discharge, Ernesto managed. He married, raised two children, and built a
life that kept the memories at a manageable distance. His divorce in his mid-fifties, which he
describes without elaboration as "a long time coming," left him living alone, and it was in the
quieter years that followed that the symptoms began to sharpen. He noticed that certain moments
would return him, without warning, to the jungle: a bout of vertigo from standing up too quickly, or
the taste of a particular canned soup he'd eaten at work, would produce something more than a memory
— a sudden, full-bodied sense that something terrible was about to happen. He came to dread these
moments. Talking about the deployment, even in the most casual terms, produced the same effect, and
so he stopped. When a colleague at school asked about his service during a Veterans Day assembly,
Ernesto gave a brief, deflecting answer and excused himself.

What frightened him most, he explained during the intake, was not the memories themselves but the
conviction that his body was signaling something real. He had come to believe, with quiet certainty,
that the dizziness and the alertness he felt in unfamiliar spaces were accurate readings of genuine
danger — not echoes of the past but warnings about the present. Before entering any room, including
his own classroom, he identified the exits. He sat with his back to the wall during lunch. He
scanned the hallways between periods with a focused, systematic attention that his colleagues likely
mistook for attentiveness. When unexpected sounds occurred — a locker slamming, a student dropping a
tray in the cafeteria — his body responded before his mind could intervene, and the residue of that
response would linger through the rest of the school day.

Over the past two years, Ernesto had quietly restructured his life around these habits. He declined
invitations to staff social events, citing tiredness, because the emotional unpredictability of
group settings felt unmanageable. He requested a ground-floor classroom closer to the building's
main exit, telling the administration it was for accessibility reasons. His adult children have
noticed that he seems more withdrawn, and one of them recently told him he seemed "far away." That
comment, more than anything else, was what brought him in. He is not hoping, he said carefully, to
talk about what happened. What he is hoping for is to feel less like a man who is always about to
run.
