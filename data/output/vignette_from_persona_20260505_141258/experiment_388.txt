been quietly struggling for nearly two years before anyone outside her immediate circle noticed that
something was wrong.

The crisis that reorganised Aroha's inner life began when her daughter, Mia, then four years old,
was diagnosed with bacterial meningitis. What had started as what Aroha assumed was an ordinary
fever escalated with terrifying speed over a single night. She remembers the exact texture of the
hospital's fluorescent lighting, the particular pitch of the monitors, and the way a nurse gently
but firmly moved her aside to insert a lumbar puncture needle into her daughter's small curved
spine. Aroha stood at the edge of the room, unable to look away, unable to help, watching Mia's body
flinch against the restraint of gloved hands. There were hours in which the medical team could not
tell her whether Mia would survive, and then further hours in which they could not tell her whether
Mia would be left with permanent neurological damage. Mia recovered fully, which was the outcome
every consultant had hoped for. But Aroha did not recover in the same way.

In the months that followed, she found that her body seemed to be living on a different timeline
than the rest of her life. At home in the evenings, when the house was quiet and she should have
felt safe, she remained watchful in a way that exhausted her — scanning the hallway, tensing at
sounds from the kitchen, unable to fully settle into any room. She had come to believe, without
being able to articulate it precisely, that safety was something that could be revoked without
warning, and that relaxing her attention was the condition under which bad things happened. When a
colleague at work crossed her peripheral vision unexpectedly in the server room, she would spin
around with her heart already pounding, the physical response arriving before any conscious thought.
She was embarrassed by this and said nothing about it.

Her body's reactions to reminders were among the most distressing features of her experience. When
Mia's school sent home a routine form requiring a medical history update, Aroha sat at her kitchen
table for forty minutes unable to complete it, her hands damp, her chest tight in a way that felt
indistinguishable from the night in the hospital. When her own GP suggested a cervical screening was
overdue, she cancelled the appointment twice. She had not yet rescheduled it. What frightened her
most was not the procedure itself but the particular quality of helplessness she associated with
medical settings — the sense of a body being acted upon, of outcomes outside her control — and she
was certain that she could not tolerate that feeling without something in her coming apart.

Her divorce from Mia's father had been finalised eight months before the illness, and their co-
parenting relationship remained tense. Arguments with him — over scheduling, over Mia's schooling,
over small logistical disagreements — had become something her body treated as emergencies. Her
heart rate would climb, her vision would narrow, and she would find herself speaking in a voice she
did not quite recognise. Afterwards she felt hollowed out and ashamed. She had begun declining calls
from him and communicating only by text, which had introduced new friction into an already difficult
arrangement.

At work, Aroha's technical competence remained intact and her performance reviews were positive, but
she had quietly withdrawn from the informal social fabric of the office. She took lunch alone and
avoided the break room when it was crowded. A senior colleague who had known her before Mia's
illness remarked to her manager that she seemed like a different person, and it was this
observation, eventually relayed to Aroha herself, that prompted the occupational health referral.

She arrived at her first session composed and precise, describing her symptoms with the measured
language of someone accustomed to diagnosing systems rather than feelings. What she most hoped for,
she said, was to stop feeling like the emergency was still happening. What she most feared was being
told that she would always feel this way.
