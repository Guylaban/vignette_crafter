Linh Tran is a 38-year-old Vietnamese American woman who works as a welder at a mid-sized
fabrication shop in the outskirts of Houston. She came to the clinic six weeks ago, referred by her
employee assistance program after her supervisor noticed she had become unreliable at work —
arriving late, leaving early, and on two occasions sitting motionless at her station for stretches
her colleagues found alarming. She is single, lives alone in a small apartment, and until
approximately eight months ago had described herself, in her own words, as someone who "kept to
herself but was fine with that."

The incident that brought her to treatment occurred during a routine afternoon shift last spring.
Linh was operating a MIG welder on a structural steel project when a junior colleague, a twenty-two-
year-old named Darius, moved into her peripheral workspace to retrieve a piece of stock material.
She had not heard him approach over the ambient noise of the shop floor. When she repositioned her
torch, the arc contacted his forearm and part of his neck. Darius sustained third-degree burns
requiring multiple surgeries and a prolonged hospital stay. He survived, but the injuries were
severe. In the immediate aftermath, Linh remembered the smell of burning — and something she has
never been able to fully articulate to others — a faint, acrid taste at the back of her throat that
she now understands was the air in that moment, the particulate and heat together. She remembers the
sound he made. She remembers dropping the torch. After that, her recollection becomes fragmented and
unreliable; she knows she was present for what followed but cannot reconstruct a coherent sequence,
and this incompleteness disturbs her deeply. She has come to believe that the missing pieces contain
something worse than what she can recall, and that not being able to see the full picture means she
has not yet fully reckoned with what she did.

In the months since, Linh has struggled to sleep. She lies awake replaying partial images, and when
she does fall asleep she often wakes before dawn without knowing why, already tense. During her
waking hours, concentration has become difficult in ways that feel alien to her — she is a precise,
technically skilled worker, and the erosion of that focus has frightened her. At the shop, certain
moments carry disproportionate weight: the particular smell of flux and hot metal, the sound of a
torch igniting, the sight of a younger colleague moving near her station. When these things happen,
her body responds before her mind catches up — her chest tightens, her hands begin to shake, and on
one occasion she experienced a wave of nausea so intense she had to leave the floor. What frightened
her most in those moments was not the reaction itself but the taste that sometimes accompanied it —
the same acrid sensation from that afternoon, arriving without warning, making the past feel
physically present in a way she could not explain or dismiss.

She had come to believe that being around other people made things worse. She stopped joining her
coworkers for lunch. She declined a birthday gathering for a colleague she genuinely liked. Outside
work, she has reduced contact with her older sister, who has been trying to check in on her, and she
has stopped attending the Vietnamese community events she used to find grounding. When she is alone,
she tries not to think about the incident directly, redirecting herself with television or household
tasks, though this has become increasingly effortful and less reliable. When the memory does
surface, she is flooded with guilt — not as an abstract moral judgment but as something somatic and
immediate, a conviction that she is someone who harms people and that this was not an accident so
much as a failure of the person she should have been. She dwells on specific moments before the
incident: whether she should have checked her surroundings more carefully, whether she had been
distracted, whether there was a version of that afternoon in which she had simply been more careful.

What finally brought her to the clinic was a near-miss at work — she had again failed to notice a
colleague approaching her station, and this time caught herself in time, but the recognition that it
could happen again was something she could no longer set aside. She came to the first session quiet
and watchful, saying very little about what she hoped for except that she wanted, somehow, to be
able to do her job again without feeling like a danger to everyone around her.
