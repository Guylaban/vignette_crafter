Leilani Fono is a 66-year-old widowed Samoan-American woman who works as a network engineer for a
regional telecommunications firm in suburban Oregon. She was referred for psychological assessment
following a disclosure she made to her primary care physician during a routine appointment, at which
point her PCL-5 score of 75 indicated severe posttraumatic stress. She had not spoken about what
happened to her in nearly two years.

Approximately twenty-six months ago, Leilani was assaulted by a man she had been seeing for several
months following the death of her husband. The relationship had developed slowly and she had allowed
herself, cautiously, to feel something again. On the evening of the assault, he had been drinking
heavily — she had noticed the sharp, sweet-sour smell of alcohol on him the moment he arrived at her
door — and what began as an embrace escalated rapidly into violence. He pinned her, and she found
herself unable to move, unable to stop what was happening to her body. What stayed with her most
viscerally afterward was not only the pain but a particular helplessness that felt total: her arms
held, her voice gone, her strength useless. She had always considered herself capable, self-reliant,
a woman who had raised children alone after her husband's illness and built a technical career in a
field that rarely welcomed people who looked like her. That night dismantled something she had
quietly relied upon.

In the weeks that followed, Leilani did not contact police. She had come to believe, in a way she
could not fully articulate but felt with great certainty, that she had somehow permitted this — that
she had opened a door she should have kept closed, and that in doing so she had acted against
everything she believed herself to be. The shame she carried was not abstract. It lived in her body.
She began avoiding mirrors. She stopped wearing the clothes she had liked. She ate very little, and
then began controlling her meals with a precision that had nothing to do with nutrition — counting,
restricting, scheduling — because it was the one domain in which she felt she could determine an
outcome. She gave up the evening walks she had taken for years along the river path near her home,
and she stopped attending her church community's monthly dinners, which had been a source of genuine
pleasure since her husband's passing.

At work, Leilani sits in an open-plan office where colleagues occasionally pass behind her chair or
place a hand briefly on her shoulder to get her attention. Each time this happens, her body responds
before her mind can intervene — she startles violently, her chair scraping back, her breath gone.
She has begun positioning herself with her back to the wall when possible and has developed a habit
of scanning the room when she enters it, her eyes moving to the letter opener on a nearby desk, the
heavy stapler, the glass partition she has calculated could shatter. She is aware that this is not
rational, and the awareness itself distresses her. When colleagues have noticed her flinching and
told her she is safe, that this is just the office, she has nodded and said nothing, because she has
found it genuinely impossible to feel what they are describing. Safety, to Leilani, has become a
word that belongs to other people.

She has not permitted anyone to hug her since the assault. When her adult daughter visited last
Christmas and reached for her at the door, Leilani stepped back before she could stop herself. Her
daughter looked hurt. Leilani said she had a cold. The distance between them has quietly widened
since then, and Leilani knows it, and has not known how to close it — nor, she admits, has she been
certain she wanted to.

What finally brought her to her physician was a moment at work when she sat in a server room alone
and realised she could not remember the last time she had felt anything she would call pleasure. She
is most afraid, she says, that this is simply who she is now. What she is hoping for, though she
offers it tentatively, is some evidence that it isn't.
