Mere Tuilagi is a 77-year-old Fijian-American surgeon, divorced, who presented for psychological
support following a life-threatening illness that struck her approximately fourteen months prior to
intake. She had spent nearly five decades in medicine — the last thirty of them as a cardiothoracic
surgeon at a large academic medical centre — and had long understood the body as a system she could
read, interpret, and repair. That understanding did not prepare her for what happened when her own
body became the site of crisis.

The illness came on quickly. Mere had noticed fatigue she attributed to overwork, then a
constellation of symptoms that escalated within days into a medical emergency requiring intensive
hospitalisation. She was intubated. There were procedures — she knows this because she has been told
— but her memory of the central period of her illness is not a continuous narrative. It is, instead,
a series of fragments that refuse to cohere: the particular quality of fluorescent light above a
gurney, a voice she did not recognise saying her name with an urgency that frightened her, the
sensation of not being able to move her hands. Other stretches are simply absent. No matter how many
times she has tried to reconstruct the timeline, whole days remain blank, and certain moments that
she can recall arrive with a sharpness that feels almost violent — a monitor alarming, a face masked
and leaning close — while what came immediately before or after remains inaccessible. She has
described sitting with a legal pad trying to write it out in sequence, as a surgeon would chart a
case, only to find the chronology collapsing into disconnected pieces that do not fit together.

What has unsettled Mere most profoundly in the months since her recovery is not the physical
sequelae but the person she has found herself to be. She had come to believe, across a long career
defined by precision and authority, that she knew exactly who she was. The woman who emerged from
that hospitalisation did not match that knowledge. She had lost her appetite for the operating
theatre not because of physical limitation but because something in her orientation toward the work
had shifted in a way she could not name or reverse. She was certain, in a way that distressed her,
that she no longer recognised herself. The faith she had carried — not strictly religious, but a
working trust in the intelligibility of the world, in the idea that careful people living careful
lives could expect a measure of protection — had been quietly evacuated. She had lost her faith in
the orderliness of things, and with it a sense of herself as someone anchored in a coherent life.

She returned to the hospital three months after discharge, believing that re-entering the
environment would restore her. Instead, the smell of the surgical suite, the sight of an IV line
being prepared, even the sound of an ambulance bay door opening onto the street below her office
window, produced a quality of distress she found difficult to describe and impossible to dismiss.
She began scheduling her clinical rounds to avoid the floors where she had been treated. She
declined to perform physical examinations she could reasonably delegate. Over the following months,
this reorganisation of her working life became more elaborate — she stopped attending grand rounds
held in the building's east wing, rerouted her morning walk to avoid the ambulance bay — until
colleagues noticed her absence from spaces she had once occupied with complete ease. A junior
surgeon she had mentored for years asked, carefully, whether she was all right. Mere told her she
was fine and then went home and sat in her kitchen for two hours, asking herself, over and over, why
this had happened to her, what meaning it was supposed to carry, whether she had somehow earned it.
She forced herself, in those moments, to list reasons for gratitude, to insist to herself that she
had survived and that survival was enough. The insistence rarely held.

What finally brought her to seek help was a scheduled physical examination — routine, required for
her hospital privileges — that she cancelled three times before acknowledging she could not walk
back into a consultation room as a patient. She arrived at intake hoping, above all, to understand
what had happened to the person she used to be, and afraid that the answer might be that that person
was simply gone.
