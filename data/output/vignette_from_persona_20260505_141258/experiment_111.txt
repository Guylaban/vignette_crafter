before the assault that brought them to the present moment of seeking help. They present with a

is one of profound disorientation — not only about what happened to them, but about who they have
become in the months since.

The assault occurred approximately eight months ago, in the late evening, when Rangi remained alone
in their office to finish a quarterly report. A man who had been contracted as a building

sequential. There is the particular industrial smell of cleaning solvent that the man carried. There
is the sound of a radio playing from somewhere down the corridor — a country song with a woman's
voice — and the sound of the man's breathing close to their ear. There is the taste of blood where
they had bitten the inside of their cheek. Then there is a gap. Then fluorescent light. These
fragments do not assemble into a story Rangi can tell, even to themselves. When asked in session to
describe what happened, they stop and restart, lose the thread, and look genuinely bewildered — not
evasive, but lost. They have described it as trying to read a document where whole paragraphs have
been torn out.

When the memory does return unbidden, it arrives in pieces that feel more present than past. The
country song — or anything with a similar female vocal timbre — can bring the sound of that corridor
radio flooding back with a physical immediacy that Rangi finds terrifying. The taste of blood, which
they now notice whenever they accidentally bite their cheek or eat something iron-rich, carries with
it the full sensory weight of that office floor. These intrusions are not experienced as
remembering. Rangi has come to believe that their mind is no longer functioning as a mind should,
that something has broken in the machinery of their cognition. The fear of going crazy is not
metaphorical for them — they are genuinely uncertain whether these experiences mark the beginning of
a mental deterioration they cannot stop.

In the weeks following the assault, Rangi encountered the police twice: once when the incident was
reported, and once for a follow-up interview. Both encounters left them shaking for hours afterward,
though they cannot articulate precisely why the presence of officers produces such visceral alarm.
What they know is that being in the presence of any authority figure now carries a weight it did not
before. At work, this has had concrete consequences. Rangi is a senior analyst whose role involves
regular review sessions with a managing director who is known for blunt feedback. Where Rangi once
received correction as routine professional friction, they now find that being criticised or
corrected — even mildly, even kindly — produces a flooding sensation of shame and helplessness that
they struggle to contain in professional settings. They have begun submitting work late rather than
face review, and twice have left meetings abruptly, citing illness. Colleagues have noticed. Rangi
is aware they have noticed, and this awareness compounds their distress.

They have also, gradually and almost without deciding to, withdrawn from the small social world that
sustained them after their spouse's death. The grief of widowhood had already narrowed their life;
the assault has narrowed it further. Nothing in that remaining life — the work they once found

feels as though it means anything. Rangi does not say this dramatically. They say it flatly, which

What finally brought Rangi to seek help was a moment at work when their managing director gently

frightened enough by their own unrecognisability to try.
