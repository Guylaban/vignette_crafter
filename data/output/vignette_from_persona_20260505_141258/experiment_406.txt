Fatima Nassar is a 46-year-old single Lebanese-American pharmacist who presented for psychological
assessment following a traumatic period that began approximately two years ago, when her then-nine-
year-old son, Kareem, was admitted to the intensive care unit with acute bacterial meningitis. What
had begun as what Fatima dismissed as a winter fever escalated within eighteen hours into a seizure
in her living room, an ambulance ride during which paramedics worked over her son's rigid,
unresponsive body while she sat inches away, unable to touch him, and a forty-eight-hour vigil in a
pediatric ICU waiting room in which physicians told her, more than once, that they were not certain
Kareem would survive the night. She remembers the particular pitch of the monitor alarms, the smell
of the unit's antiseptic air, and the moment a nurse emerged through double doors with an expression
Fatima could not immediately read. Kareem did survive, and after a long recovery he returned to
school with no lasting neurological deficits. By most external measures, the story had a good
ending. Fatima has never quite been able to locate herself inside that ending.

In the weeks after Kareem's discharge, Fatima found herself replaying the ambulance ride with a
vividness she had not invited — the sound of the siren, the texture of the seat beneath her, the
particular helplessness of being a medically trained person who could do nothing. These intrusive
moments arrived without warning and carried the sensory weight of the original event rather than the
quality of ordinary memory. She began trying, with considerable effort, to push them away whenever
they surfaced, redirecting her attention, filling silences with tasks, keeping the television on
through the night. The effort was exhausting and rarely succeeded for long.

Her occupation placed her at the intersection of medicine every working day, and it was there that
the strain became most visible. The sound of an ambulance passing the strip mall where her pharmacy
was located could stop her mid-sentence with a customer. She began scheduling her lunch breaks to
coincide with moments when she could step into the back dispensary and avoid the front windows. When
her own annual physical examination came due, she cancelled it, and then cancelled the rescheduled
appointment, telling herself she would go when things settled down. She had not been seen by a
physician in over eighteen months. For a woman whose professional identity was built on medical
knowledge and precision, she had come to experience anything that carried the texture of a hospital
— the smell of latex gloves, the sight of an IV bag in a patient's prescription history, the wail of
an emergency vehicle — as something to be moved away from rather than through.

Fatima lived alone; she and Kareem's father had separated years before the illness, and her social
world had contracted steadily since the ICU. She was unable to relax even on evenings when Kareem
was safe at home doing homework in the next room. Ordinary sounds — a door closing sharply, a car
backfiring in the parking lot outside — produced a physical startle response that embarrassed and
frightened her in equal measure. Sleep was rarely restorative; she lay awake rehearsing scenarios
she could not stop generating, and when she did sleep she woke easily and could not return.

What troubled Fatima perhaps as much as any individual symptom was the interpretation she had built
around all of it. She had come to believe that her inability to simply move on — given that her son
was alive and well — was evidence of a fundamental deficiency in her character. She was certain that
a stronger person, a better mother, a more competent woman would have processed this and recovered.
She found herself returning, cyclically, to the question of why this had happened to her
specifically, what she might have failed to notice or failed to do. And beneath these questions sat
a quieter, more corrosive conviction: that nothing genuinely good was available to her anymore, that
the version of her life in which ordinary happiness was possible had closed the night Kareem was
loaded into that ambulance.

What finally brought Fatima to seek help was a conversation with her son. Kareem, now eleven, asked
her why she always looked scared. She had no answer she was willing to give him, and the absence of
one frightened her more than anything else. She came to the first session hoping, cautiously, to
become someone her son did not need to worry about.
