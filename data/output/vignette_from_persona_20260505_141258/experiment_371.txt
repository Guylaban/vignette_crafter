Mateo is a 22-year-old Latino HVAC technician who presented for an initial assessment reporting
significant distress following an incident at a worksite approximately fourteen months ago. He was
working alongside a senior colleague on a commercial rooftop unit when, in the course of
repositioning a heavy refrigerant compressor, his grip failed. The equipment shifted and struck his
colleague, a man in his forties named Derek, with enough force to send him over the low parapet
edge. Derek survived but sustained a severe spinal injury that left him partially paralysed. Mateo
had been the one directing the lift. In the seconds before the equipment moved, there had been an
unusual stillness on the roof — the ambient noise of the city seemed to drop away — and then the
sound of the impact, and then nothing but that same terrible quiet before Derek began to scream.

In the weeks that followed, Mateo continued working because stopping felt worse than moving. He told
himself he needed to understand exactly what had gone wrong, and so he would lie awake
reconstructing the sequence of events in minute detail — the angle of his hands, the precise moment
his grip shifted, whether he had said something that distracted Derek at the wrong instant. The
analysis never resolved into clarity. It only circled back on itself, each pass producing a slightly
different version of his own culpability. He had come to believe, with a conviction that felt
immovable, that he had not done enough to stop what happened — that there had been a window, however
narrow, in which a better version of himself would have acted differently and Derek would still be
walking.

That belief began to reorganise his life quietly and then all at once. On rooftop jobs, when the
ambient noise of traffic and ventilation systems would momentarily fall away, Mateo's body would
respond before his mind caught up — heart hammering, hands going cold, a wave of nausea that he
could not reason himself out of. He started requesting ground-level assignments and found reasons to
avoid rooftop work entirely, which his supervisor eventually noticed and questioned. He told the
supervisor it was an equipment preference. What he did not say was that the smell of hot roofing
membrane — a smell present on that afternoon — could arrive without warning and place him back on
that roof with a vividness that felt less like memory and more like dislocation. The memory, when it
came, did not feel like his own. It had a quality of watching someone else's footage, a figure who
wore his face but whose catastrophic failure he could observe from a remove that offered no comfort.

Outside of work, Mateo narrowed his world considerably. He felt genuinely safe in only two places:
his mother's kitchen and his own bedroom. Anywhere else carried a low-grade vigilance that was
exhausting to maintain. Silence, in particular, had become threatening in a way he struggled to
articulate — the quiet before something terrible, he called it. He began avoiding social gatherings,
family events, and eventually the anniversary of the accident entirely, spending that day in his
room with the television on at volume, filling the silence with noise.

What frightened him most, beneath all of this, was the sense that his body could no longer be
trusted. His hands — the hands that had failed — felt like instruments of potential harm rather than
tools he commanded. He had stopped playing guitar, something he had done since adolescence, because
holding the neck felt wrong in a way he could not explain. He looked at photographs of himself from
before the accident and recognised the face but not the person. The young man who had been proud of
learning a trade, who had wanted to become a foreman by thirty, felt entirely remote.

Mateo sought help after Derek's wife contacted him through a mutual colleague. She did not write
anything accusatory. She simply said Derek was asking about him. Mateo could not bring himself to
respond, and the inability to do so — the paralysis of it — was what finally brought him to the
clinic. He arrived hoping, he said quietly, to become someone who could at least write that reply.
