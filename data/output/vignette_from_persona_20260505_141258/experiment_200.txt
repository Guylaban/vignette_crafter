Nadia Khalil is a 31-year-old Lebanese-American woman who works as a social media manager for a mid-
sized lifestyle brand. She presents for her first course of trauma-focused therapy approximately
fourteen months after an incident that she describes, with considerable difficulty, as the worst
thing she has ever done. She was driving home from a colleague's birthday dinner on a wet November
evening, sober but tired, when she made a left turn through what she had judged to be a clear
intersection and struck a cyclist who had been riding without lights in her blind spot. The impact
was immediate and disorienting — the sound of it, a dense, hollow thud against the front quarter
panel, and then the smell: rain on hot asphalt, and something metallic she later understood to be
blood. The cyclist, a man in his forties, survived but sustained a severe traumatic brain injury and
has required ongoing care. Nadia stayed at the scene, called 911, and knelt beside him in the road
until the paramedics arrived. She was not charged with any offence. None of that, she has said,
makes any difference to her.

In the weeks that followed, Nadia found herself unable to articulate what had happened to almost
anyone. She would open her mouth to explain it to her mother or to a friend and discover that the
words simply were not there — not the right ones, not any ones. She could hold the facts of the
event in her mind like objects in a sealed box, but language would not reach them. At other times
the opposite would happen: she would be sitting at her desk scheduling posts for the brand's
Instagram account, and the smell would arrive without warning — that same wet-metal-asphalt
combination — and for a moment she would be back in the intersection, kneeling on the road. These
moments arrived without any clear trigger she could name, and they left her shaken and disoriented
in ways she found hard to justify to herself or to her colleagues.

What frightened Nadia most, in the months that accumulated after the accident, was not the memory
itself but what she had concluded about herself from it. She had come to believe, with a certainty
that felt irrefutable, that she could not be trusted to do the right thing in any situation that
mattered. She had thought she was a careful driver. She had thought she was a responsible person.
The accident had, in her view, exposed something fundamental about her judgment that could not now
be unseen. When a colleague placed a hand on her shoulder to look at her screen, Nadia would flinch
sharply, her whole body registering danger before her mind had time to assess the situation. She
would apologise and laugh it off, but privately she was certain that something in her had been
permanently altered — that the woman she had been before November no longer existed, and that what
remained was someone she did not recognise and was not sure she wanted to know.

Her marriage, already under strain, ended six months after the accident. She does not describe the
divorce as a consequence of the trauma so much as another piece of evidence that she is not who she
thought she was. She stopped attending the monthly dinners her Lebanese community group holds,
partly because the drive across town in the dark felt impossible, and partly because she could not
bear to sit with people who remembered the version of her that predated all of this. Her world has
grown considerably smaller.

She continues to work, though the job has become its own kind of difficulty. Managing the brand's
cheerful, aspirational content while cycling through episodes of strong, formless guilt and shame
that arrive without apparent warning has left her feeling fraudulent in a way she cannot fully
describe. On days when a near-miss notification or a breaking-news alert triggers a sudden sense of
alarm — her body convinced, briefly, that something terrible is about to happen — she has learned to
close her office door and wait for it to pass.

What finally brought Nadia to seek help was a conversation with her younger sister, who told her
plainly that she seemed to have disappeared. Nadia could not argue with that. What she hopes for,
she says carefully, is to recognise herself again. What she is most afraid of is that there is
nothing left to recognise.
