Marama Tuilagi is a 43-year-old Fijian-American woman who has worked as a physical therapist for
nearly fifteen years, a career she chose, as she once explained to her intake clinician, because she
wanted to spend her professional life helping people recover. That conviction has become one of the
more painful ironies of her current situation. Marama presents with a PCL-5 score of 77 and is
seeking treatment approximately fourteen months after the event that she describes, with visible
difficulty, as the worst night of her life.

It was a Saturday evening when Marama, after a long week of back-to-back patient appointments at the
outpatient rehabilitation clinic where she works, attended a colleague's birthday gathering. She had
been drinking — more than she intended — when she offered to drive a friend home, insisting she was
fine. She was not fine. On a residential street less than two miles from the venue, she ran a stop
sign and struck a cyclist. The man, a father in his late thirties, sustained a severe traumatic
brain injury and spent weeks in intensive care. He survived, but with lasting neurological deficits.
Marama was not charged criminally, but that fact has offered her no relief. In the weeks that
followed, she told almost no one what had happened. She stopped attending social events. She stopped
returning calls from the colleague whose party it had been. She has come to believe, with a
certainty that feels immovable, that she will never forgive herself for not simply calling a cab,
for not listening to the small voice that told her to wait, for not doing more to stop what was
already in motion.

The smell of alcohol — at a restaurant, on a patient's breath, even from hand sanitiser with a
particular ethanol sharpness — produces an immediate and visceral shift in Marama's state. In those
moments the night returns not as a recollection but as something happening now: the jolt, the sound,
the stillness that followed. These intrusions arrive with a vividness she describes as more real
than anything she can ordinarily remember, and yet when she tries to construct a coherent account of
the sequence of events, even privately and to herself, the narrative fractures. She cannot hold the
whole story in order. What she is left with instead is shame — a wave of it that arrives reliably
whenever the memory surfaces, a shame so total that she has come to feel she cannot think about the
event at all without it overwhelming her completely.

At work, Marama moves through the rehabilitation gym with a vigilance that has become habitual. She
scans the room — the resistance bands looped on hooks, the weighted bars, the parallel rails —
registering each object with a quiet, automatic assessment she cannot fully explain but cannot stop.
She positions herself near the door during group sessions, a habit that has not gone unnoticed by
her supervisor, who has gently asked whether she is feeling well. Marama has deflected each time.
Outside the clinic, she has spent money she cannot account for — online purchases arriving at her
apartment in a steady stream, items she barely registers before they accumulate in corners — and she
has, on a small number of occasions she disclosed with evident shame, used prescription medication
obtained from sources she did not specify to get through particularly bad nights.

What has finally brought her to seek help is not any single event but an accumulation. Her partner
of four years, a patient and steady man named David, told her recently and quietly that he did not
recognise her anymore. The words landed because she had been thinking the same thing. The woman who
had spent her career restoring function and movement in others now feels, in her own words, like a
stranger inhabiting her own history. She is afraid, above all, that treatment will require her to
speak about what happened — that talking about the event will confirm what she already suspects,
that she will not be able to handle it. What she hopes for, tentatively and with no great
confidence, is that some version of herself from before that Saturday night might still be
recoverable.
