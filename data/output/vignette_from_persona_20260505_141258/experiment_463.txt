Fatima Al-Rashidi is a seventy-five-year-old Jordanian-American woman who has spent more than three
decades on the bench, presiding over cases with a composure that colleagues describe as almost
architectural in its solidity. She came to the United States in her late twenties, built a legal

where she still lives with her husband of forty-eight years. It was in that same suburb, on an
unremarkable Tuesday evening eleven months ago, that her youngest son, Tariq, died of a sudden
cardiac event at the age of forty-four. He had been sitting at her kitchen table.

Fatima had noticed, over the preceding months, that Tariq looked tired. He had gained weight,
complained occasionally of indigestion, and seemed short of breath after climbing stairs. She had
mentioned it once, lightly, and he had deflected with the easy humor she had always loved in him.
She did not press. She told herself he was busy, that he was a grown man, that she did not want to
be the kind of mother who hovered. That evening, while she was refilling his tea, he said something
she did not catch, and when she turned around he was slumped forward. By the time the paramedics
arrived, there was nothing to be done.

In the months that followed, Fatima continued to appear in court each morning, robed and composed,
because the alternative — the admission that she could not function — felt to her like a
confirmation of something she was not yet willing to name. But the courtroom had become its own kind
of ordeal. The sound of someone clearing their throat with effort, the sight of a middle-aged man
loosening his collar in the heat of the gallery, the particular stillness that falls over a room
when someone pauses unexpectedly — each of these had become unbearable. She would find herself
gripping the edge of the bench, certain for a half-second that she was watching something begin to
go wrong, that she was about to fail again to stop it.

She had come to believe, with a certainty she could not argue herself out of, that Tariq's death was
her fault. Not in a vague, generalised sense, but in a specific, evidentiary one — the kind of
reasoning she had spent a career applying to others. She had seen the signs. She had chosen not to
act on them. A woman of her training and intelligence, she told herself, should have known better.
She should have insisted he see a cardiologist. She should have said something more than once. What
frightened her most was not the grief itself but the conclusion she had drawn from her own inaction:
that when it mattered most, she had been exactly the wrong kind of person to have in the room.

his death — the sleeplessness, the moments of frozen panic on the bench, the evenings she spent
sitting in the kitchen unable to eat — proved something damning about her character. She had
presided over cases involving trauma survivors and had always, she now admitted to herself with some
shame, assumed a kind of internal toughness in herself that would hold. Its absence felt like
exposure. She was certain that she was coping badly, and she read each difficult day as further
evidence of that.

because she had come to believe it was her burden to carry alone — the consequence, as she saw it,
of her own failure.

something had to change. She arrived at the intake appointment hoping, above all, to understand
whether she deserved to feel this way — and afraid, in equal measure, that the answer might be yes.
