Aroha Tane is a 27-year-old non-binary Māori-American special education teacher who presented for an
initial psychological assessment following a period of sustained stalking by a former acquaintance
that ended approximately eight months prior. They came to the first session quietly watchful,
choosing a seat facing the door, and spoke in measured, careful sentences — as though each word
required checking before release.

The stalking had unfolded over nearly four months. What began as unwanted messages escalated into
the acquaintance appearing outside Aroha's apartment building, then outside their school, then in
the parking lot where they walked to their car each evening after their students had been collected
by their families. Aroha had never been certain how the person always knew where they were. That
uncertainty became its own wound. There were evenings when Aroha would hear footsteps on the
stairwell of their building and stand completely still inside their apartment, listening, trying to
calculate whether the rhythm belonged to a neighbour or to him. Sometimes the footsteps would stop
on their floor, and Aroha would not breathe. The police were involved twice; the situation
eventually resolved through a restraining order and the acquaintance relocating, but by then the
four months had left something restructured inside Aroha that legal paperwork could not reach.

What Aroha finds most disorienting now is that the memories of the stalking do not form a coherent
story they can hold and examine. Certain moments arrive with terrible precision — the particular
sound of footsteps slowing behind them on a quiet street, the sight of a familiar car idling near
the school entrance — while whole weeks of that period are simply absent, as though their mind had
been recording intermittently. Aroha described feeling like they were trying to assemble something
from pieces that don't belong to the same puzzle. This incompleteness troubles them deeply; they
find themselves turning the fragments over repeatedly, trying to reconstruct what they might have
missed, what signs they failed to read, what different decision might have made the whole thing
impossible. This mental rehearsal of prevention occupies long stretches of their evenings and
commutes, though it yields nothing except exhaustion.

At school, where Aroha works closely with students who require attentive, regulated adult presence,
the hypervigilance has become both professionally disruptive and personally isolating. The classroom
has large windows that face the school's main car park, and Aroha now positions themselves
throughout the day so that the windows remain in their peripheral vision. When a teaching assistant
crosses the room quickly to redirect a student, Aroha's body responds before their mind can
intervene — a sharp internal lurch, muscles tensing, attention fragmenting from whatever instruction
they were giving. Colleagues have noticed that Aroha seems distracted. One gently asked whether
everything was all right, and Aroha deflected, aware that explaining felt both impossible and
dangerous, as though naming it would confirm that the threat was still present.

At home, Aroha's partner has noticed that they no longer settle. Evenings that once involved cooking
together or watching something on the couch now find Aroha moving through the apartment in irregular
circuits, checking the locks, listening. Aroha had come to believe that relaxing — truly relaxing,
the kind that loosens attention — was something they could not afford. The feeling of being watched
had not departed with the restraining order; it had simply become untethered from any specific
person, free to attach itself to any stranger walking behind them on the footpath, any car that
seemed to slow, any set of footsteps in a public space. Aroha knows, intellectually, that the threat
has passed. But the body does not know this, and Aroha has stopped trusting the body's ignorance as
safe.

What finally brought Aroha to seek help was a moment at school three weeks ago when a student — a
child they adore, one who sometimes approaches from behind to take their hand — caused Aroha to spin
around with a gasp that frightened the child. Aroha spent the rest of that afternoon in a state of
quiet devastation. They came to the first session saying they were most afraid that something
essential had been permanently altered in them. What they most hoped for, offered carefully at the
end of the session, was to feel safe enough to stop watching.
