Mere Taufa is a thirty-year-old Tongan-American woman who works as a licensed plumber for a
residential contracting company in suburban Sacramento. She came to the clinic on the referral of
her general practitioner, who had grown concerned after Mere mentioned, almost in passing, that she
had not been eating properly for several months and that she felt "wired all the time, like
something bad is about to happen."

Mere grew up in a household shaped by her father's volatility. He was a large man whose moods
shifted without warning, and the most reliable signal that violence was coming was the sound of his
voice rising. He would shout before he struck, and the shouting itself became indistinguishable from
the blow — a kind of advance echo of what her body already knew. He also had a habit of watching her
from doorways, arms folded, saying nothing, a silence that carried its own threat because it meant
he was deciding. The physical abuse was most severe between the ages of seven and twelve, when Mere
was small enough that she could not predict where in the house she was safe, only that she was never
entirely safe anywhere. She describes the period with a strange flatness, noting that some of it is
"very sharp, like a photograph," while other stretches are simply gone — not blurry, but absent, as
though they happened to a child she once read about rather than a child she was. The memory, she
says, feels borrowed. She recognises that it is hers in the way one recognises a signature, but it
does not feel inhabited.

For many years Mere managed. She left home at eighteen, trained as a plumber, and built a life that
felt, in her words, "like proof I got out." She married her husband Sione four years ago, and by
most external measures the household is warm and stable. The symptoms she is now presenting with did
not arrive in a single wave; they accumulated quietly and then, over the past year, became
impossible to ignore.

Her work brings her into close, practical contact with several of her most distressing experiences.
Plumbing is a trade conducted in tight spaces — under sinks, in crawlways, inside the homes of
strangers — and clients often stand nearby watching her work, checking on progress, hovering. Mere
has come to dread this. She is certain, in a way she cannot fully articulate, that being observed
means she is about to be judged and found wanting, and her body responds before her mind catches up:
her shoulders lock, her jaw tightens, and she loses the thread of what she was doing. Concentration,
which she needs to read pipe configurations and diagnose faults accurately, deserts her in these
moments. She has begun scheduling jobs so that she can work alone whenever possible, turning down
the larger renovation contracts that would have advanced her career, telling her supervisor only
that she "works better without an audience."

At home, if Sione raises his voice during an argument — even when his tone is more exasperated than
angry — Mere finds herself somewhere else inside her own body, watching from a remove. She has asked
him repeatedly not to express frustration aloud, and while he has tried to accommodate her, the
restriction has introduced a careful, effortful quality to their communication that neither of them
fully understands. Mere knows this is not fair to him, and that knowledge sits alongside the fear
without resolving it.

She keeps her mind moving at all times — podcasts during the commute, television in the evenings,
any noise that fills the space where thinking might otherwise settle. She has also been eating very
little, skipping meals in a way she connects only vaguely to feeling that her body is not quite hers
to tend to. Her weight has dropped enough that her GP noticed.

What finally brought her to seek help was a job last autumn in which a client's teenage son slammed
a door in an adjacent room. The sudden movement of a shadow across the hallway, combined with the
bang, sent Mere to the floor behind the water heater before she had consciously registered what she
was doing. She drove home and sat in her car for an hour. She had come to believe she was managing.
That afternoon made it clear she was not.

What she is most afraid of, she says, is that there is more she cannot remember, and that whatever
is missing is the worst of it. What she is hoping for, more quietly, is to be able to work a full
day and come home and sit with her husband in an ordinary silence that does not feel dangerous.
