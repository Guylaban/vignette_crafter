Miriam Calloway is a 44-year-old single woman who works as a hospital chaplain in a mid-sized
Midwestern city. She came to her first appointment carrying a small leather-bound notebook, which
she held in her lap throughout the session and did not open. She had waited nearly two years before
seeking help, and she arrived with the quiet, careful manner of someone who has been managing
something very heavy for a very long time.

Two years ago, Miriam was attending an interfaith memorial service at a civic auditorium when a
coordinated bombing attack struck the building during the opening remarks. She was seated in the
third row when the first device detonated near the entrance. The sound was not like anything she had
words for — it arrived in her chest before she understood it as noise. What followed was a period of
minutes that she cannot now reconstruct in order: smoke, a man in a security uniform running past
her and then falling, a woman near her laughing in a high, strange way that made no sense, people
pushing toward exits that were blocked, and then a second explosion. She was not critically injured,
but she was thrown to the ground and lay still for what may have been several minutes before
emergency responders reached her. Several people around her died. The security personnel she had
trusted to manage the space had, in her assessment, failed catastrophically — the exits had not been
properly maintained, the response was slow, and she had since read reports suggesting that warnings
had been ignored by the organizing institutions. She had come to believe, with a certainty that felt
like knowledge rather than feeling, that the world does not hold together the way she had once
assumed, and that the structures meant to keep people safe are largely theatrical.

In the months that followed, the memories did not behave like memories. They arrived with the full
weight of the present — the pressure in her chest, the smell of burning, the particular quality of
the light through the smoke — and they did not feel like recollections of something that had
happened. They felt like something happening now. She could not place them in sequence. She knew the
second explosion came after the first, but when the memories surfaced she could not always be
certain which came first, or where the laughing woman fit, or at what point she had been on the
floor. The disorder of it frightened her almost as much as the content.

At work, Miriam continued to visit patients and families through the hospital's wards, but she began
arriving early each day to scout the chaplaincy common room and the chapel, choosing her seat before
anyone else arrived — always with her back to a wall and a clear line to the door. When she sat with
grieving families, she found herself watching their faces for something she could not name, a
tightening around the eyes or a shift in posture that might mean danger. When a food cart rattled
unexpectedly in a hallway, or a code alarm sounded on another floor, she would go rigid and have to
grip the arm of her chair. Her supervisor noticed that she had begun avoiding the hospital's
security office and would take a longer route through the building rather than pass the uniformed
staff stationed near the main entrance. She told herself this was practical.

At home, she had begun eating very little, finding that hunger gave her a sensation of control over
at least one thing. She had also developed a set of private mental practices — specific phrases she
repeated in a particular order before leaving the house, a sequence she believed, without quite
endorsing the belief, might prevent something terrible from happening again. She knew,
intellectually, that this was not how causality worked. She was a chaplain. She had sat with people
in the worst moments of their lives and spoken with some confidence about meaning and safety. What
frightened her most now was the gap between who she had been and what she had become — she had come
to see her own responses as evidence of a fundamental inadequacy, proof that she was not the person
she had believed herself to be. When a patient last month told her they felt genuinely happy for the
first time in years, she had felt something lurch in her chest that she could not identify as
anything good, only as wrong and confusing.

She came to therapy, she said, because she could no longer do her job the way it deserved to be
done. What she was most afraid of was that nothing she had believed before the bombing was
recoverable.
