Priya Nair is a 45-year-old South Asian woman who works as a licensed plumber, a trade she has
practiced for nearly two decades. She presents as self-reliant, practically minded, and accustomed
to solving problems with her hands. She has been single for several years and lives alone in a
modest apartment. She came to the intake session visibly reluctant, sitting forward in her chair as
though ready to leave, and described seeking help as "a last resort."

Approximately fourteen months ago, Priya collapsed at a job site while replacing corroded pipework
beneath a commercial kitchen. She had been feeling mildly unwell for several days — attributing her
fatigue to long hours — but dismissed it. That morning, she became acutely short of breath while
working in a confined space, felt the room tilt around her, and lost consciousness before she could
call for help. She was found by a coworker and rushed by ambulance to the emergency department,
where she was diagnosed with a severe cardiac arrhythmia that had gone undetected. She spent eleven
days in intensive care, several of them in a medically induced state, and was told by her
cardiologist that she had come very close to dying. She was discharged with medication and a follow-
up plan she has not adhered to.

In the weeks after discharge, ordinary sensations became unbearable. The moment Priya felt even
slightly lightheaded — standing up too quickly, turning her head in a warm room — her body flooded
with the certainty that she was collapsing again, back on that concrete floor beneath the kitchen
units. The same happened when she became breathless: climbing a flight of stairs, carrying pipe
fittings up to a second floor, or simply walking quickly to her van on a cold morning. Each episode
arrived not as a memory she could examine from a distance but as something immediate and physical,
indistinguishable in her body from what had happened that day. She began to describe herself as
"broken" — she had come to believe that her body was fundamentally unreliable, capable of betraying
her without warning and without reason, and that no amount of attention or care on her part could
change that.

This belief shaped her working life in concrete ways. Priya began refusing jobs that required
physical exertion — crawling under flooring, working in crawl spaces, anything that raised her heart
rate. She turned down a lucrative commercial contract that would have required sustained overhead
drilling, telling the client she was overbooked. Over several months, her income dropped
significantly. Colleagues and a former business partner noticed she was no longer taking the larger
jobs and stopped referring work to her. She has come to believe she cannot rely on them to
understand, and she has not explained her situation to anyone in her professional circle.

The smell of antiseptic is another matter entirely. Priya cannot enter a pharmacy, a clinic, or any
building with that particular institutional cleanliness without the sensation arriving — the beeping
monitors, the tube in her throat, the disorientation of those first conscious hours in the ICU. She
has not returned for any of her scheduled cardiac follow-up appointments. She has not filled one of
her prescriptions in four months. She told herself initially that she felt fine, that the doctors
had been alarmist, but what she has come to believe is something more entrenched: that she has to
watch herself constantly, that no one else — not a cardiologist, not a pharmacist, not a colleague —
will protect her, and that the only safety available to her is vigilance and avoidance of anything
that might push her body past its fragile limits.

What finally brought Priya to the clinic was not a crisis but an accumulation. She had a near-miss
while driving — a moment of dizziness at a junction — and sat in her van for forty minutes
afterward, unable to move. She told herself she was managing. She was not managing. She came to the
first session hoping, she said quietly, that someone could help her "stop waiting for it to happen
again." What she fears most is that it already will have started before she notices.
