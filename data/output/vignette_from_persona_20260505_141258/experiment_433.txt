Fatima Khalil is a 63-year-old single woman of Lebanese descent who works as a senior auditor for a
regional accounting firm in suburban Michigan. She presents with a PCL-5 score of 63, seeking help
for the first time approximately fourteen months after witnessing the violent assault of her younger
sister, Nadia, during what was intended to be a quiet afternoon hike in a wooded state park outside
the city.

The two sisters had driven out on a Saturday in early autumn, following a trail they had walked
together several times before. What Fatima remembers most vividly about the beginning of that
afternoon is the particular quality of the light through the tree canopy and the smell of damp earth
after recent rain — sensory details that have since become unbearable to her. Roughly a mile into
the trail, a man emerged from the tree line and attacked Nadia with sudden and ferocious violence.
Fatima was close enough to touch her sister but found herself unable to intervene effectively; she
screamed, she ran toward them, she pulled at him, and none of it stopped what was happening. Nadia
survived, but sustained serious injuries and spent weeks in hospital. Fatima was physically
unharmed. That disproportion — her own intact body, her sister's broken one — has sat at the centre
of her distress ever since. She had come to believe, with quiet but iron certainty, that she is
fundamentally unable to protect the people she loves, that bad things arrive without warning and
that there is nothing she can do to prevent them.

In the weeks that followed, Fatima found the memory returning with a completeness that frightened
her. It did not come back as a story she could narrate from a distance; it arrived as the full
sensory reality of that afternoon — the sounds, the smells, the cold air, the exact texture of her
sister's jacket under her hands — so that for those moments she was not in her kitchen or her office
but back on that trail, experiencing the same terror she had felt then. These episodes could be
triggered without any preparation: a photograph of autumn leaves on a colleague's desk, the smell of
wet soil through an open window, a news segment showing a victim's photograph. Any reminder of
Nadia's suffering, however indirect, could collapse the distance between then and now.

Fatima's working life has been significantly affected. As an auditor, she travels regularly to
client sites — offices she has not visited before, in buildings she does not know. She has begun
declining these assignments, telling her supervisor she is managing a family health matter. What she
cannot easily explain is that unfamiliar buildings feel genuinely dangerous to her now; she cannot
assess them, cannot know the exits, cannot trust that she understands what might happen inside them.
Conference rooms that lock from the outside, server rooms, archive spaces — any enclosed area where
the door is controlled by someone else produces a climbing dread that she has come to recognise as
her body refusing to believe she is safe, even when she knows intellectually that she is. She had
come to think of her own nervous system as something working against her, sounding alarms in places
where no alarm was warranted, and the helplessness of that — of being unable to simply tell herself
the danger had passed — had quietly eroded her confidence in her own judgment.

At home, Fatima sleeps poorly, waking repeatedly and lying in the dark running through possibilities
she cannot stop herself from imagining. She has stopped attending the mosque she visited for thirty
years, finding that the faith which once organised her understanding of suffering no longer holds.
She has grown more distant from friends, partly because she does not want to explain herself and
partly because she had lost something she could not quite name — a basic trust in the idea that the
world was navigable by ordinary care and attention.

What finally brought her to seek help was a phone call from Nadia, who told her gently that she
seemed to have disappeared. Fatima sat with that for several days before making the appointment.
What she is most afraid of is that the person she was before that afternoon on the trail is simply
gone. What she is most hoping for is some way to be in a room — any room — without her body
insisting that catastrophe is imminent.
