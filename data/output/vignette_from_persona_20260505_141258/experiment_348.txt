Mere Tuilagi is a 46-year-old Fijian-American musician, married and based in the Pacific Northwest,
who was referred for psychological assessment following what she describes as nearly two years of
unrelenting distress in the wake of a life-threatening respiratory illness. Her PCL-5 score of 58
places her firmly within the severe range, and the clinical picture that has emerged across the
intake and early sessions is one of a woman whose interior life has been profoundly reorganised by
what happened to her body — and by what she believes that experience revealed about who she is.

Approximately two and a half years ago, Mere was hospitalised for six days with a severe bilateral
pneumonia that progressed rapidly to respiratory failure. She recalls the specific moment the
illness overtook her: she was mid-rehearsal with her chamber ensemble, playing viola, when she felt
a tightening across her chest that she initially dismissed as performance anxiety. Within hours she
was in the emergency department, and by the following morning she was on supplemental oxygen, unable
to complete a sentence without pausing to breathe. What she returns to most often in session is not
the fear of dying in the abstract, but the particular helplessness of her own lungs refusing to work
— the sensation of drawing breath and finding almost nothing there, of her body becoming something
alien and unreliable. She also recalls, with striking precision, the smell of the ward: a layered
antiseptic odour that she associates with the hours when she was most uncertain whether she would
recover.

In the months after discharge, Mere found that ordinary physical sensations had become charged with
meaning. Any episode of breathlessness — climbing stairs, carrying her instrument case, the slight
constriction that comes with cold air — would send her into a state of alarm before she had
consciously registered what was happening. Her heart would be pounding and her hands already
trembling before her mind had formed the thought that something might be wrong. She began waking in
the night, sitting up in the dark to listen to her own breathing, to confirm she was safe, sometimes
lying awake for an hour before she could return to sleep. The world, she told her assessor, had come
to feel manageable only inside her own home — specifically the bedroom and the living room — and
even there, only when her husband was present.

Her work as a musician has been directly and concretely affected. She has not returned to ensemble
rehearsal. The studio where she recorded for much of the past decade shares a building with a
medical clinic, and the faint antiseptic smell that sometimes drifts into the corridor is enough to
end her session. She has declined two professional engagements in the past year, offering vague
explanations to colleagues who have grown visibly puzzled by her withdrawal. She has not attended a
routine medical appointment since her follow-up visit three months post-discharge, at which a
stethoscope placed against her back produced a surge of panic so complete that she left before the
examination was finished. She has not rescheduled.

What has proved most corrosive, however, is the narrative Mere has constructed around her own
response to the illness. She had come to believe that the fact she could not simply recover and move
on was evidence of something fundamentally deficient in her character. She told herself, with quiet
conviction, that she deserved to feel this way — that she had somehow failed to take her health
seriously enough before the illness, that a stronger or more self-aware person would have coped
better. She found herself returning again and again to the thought that if she had only noticed the
symptoms sooner, or taken better care of herself across the years, none of this would have happened.
She could not trust her own judgment — about her body, about her choices, about whether she was
reading a situation correctly — and she was certain that this distrust was warranted.

What finally brought Mere to seek help was a conversation with her husband, who told her quietly one
evening that he had watched her disappear over the past two years, and that he missed her. She came
to the first session saying she did not know whether she could be helped, but that she was afraid of
losing her music entirely — and, beneath that, afraid of remaining, as she put it, a person she no
longer recognised.
