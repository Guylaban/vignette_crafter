Linh Tran is a 24-year-old Vietnamese American woman who works as an urban planner for a mid-sized
municipal agency, a role that requires her to attend public meetings, collaborate with colleagues,
and present proposals to community stakeholders. She sought psychological support after a routine
workplace health screening left her unable to return to the office for three days — an absence she
attributed to a stomach bug but which she privately understood as something she could not yet name.

Linh grew up in a household shaped by her maternal grandmother's authority. Her grandmother, who had
raised her following her parents' separation, was unpredictable and physically severe. Punishment
was not occasional; it was woven into the fabric of daily life, administered without warning and
without explanation. What Linh remembers most vividly — though even this memory arrives in pieces —
is the quality of being watched before it happened. Her grandmother's gaze would fix on her, and
Linh would feel herself become something smaller than a person, an object awaiting handling. The
strikes often came when Linh was already unwell — once, memorably, while she was nauseated and bent
over the bathroom sink. The smell of that room, the fluorescent light, the sensation of her own body
failing her while her grandmother's hand came down — these details do not arrive to her as a
coherent narrative. More often they arrive as a wave of rage with no image attached, or as a surge
of nausea that seems to have no physical origin.

By the time Linh left for college, she had developed a set of habits she thought of simply as being
careful. She agreed readily with others, rarely expressed preferences, and arranged her life to
avoid situations that felt unpredictable. She told herself this was maturity. It was only in her
early twenties, as the demands of her professional life expanded, that the careful architecture she
had built began to show its costs.

At work, Linh is required to attend community planning sessions in city hall conference rooms —
large, fluorescent-lit spaces where she often finds herself at the front of the room, visible to a
crowd. Being seen in this way, particularly when she cannot predict who is watching or what their
faces mean, produces a vigilance she cannot suppress. A colleague shifting suddenly in a chair, a
supervisor turning quickly toward her — these movements register in her body before her mind can
assess them, and she finds herself unable to hold the thread of what she was saying. Her difficulty
concentrating during these moments has led her supervisor to note, with some concern, that her
presentations have become less assured. Linh has interpreted this feedback as confirmation of
something she has long suspected: that she is fundamentally weak, that others can see it, and that
it is only a matter of time before this is made fully visible.

Outside of a small number of settings — her apartment, a particular coffee shop two blocks from her
office — Linh does not feel safe. She has declined two invitations to colleagues' gatherings in the
past four months, each time offering a plausible excuse. When a close friend recently asked her
directly whether something had happened in her childhood, Linh ended the conversation and did not
contact her friend for two weeks. The idea that someone might name the event, might refer to it,
felt like an intrusion she was not equipped to survive.

The workplace health screening that finally brought her to treatment involved a nurse practitioner
conducting a brief physical examination. The clinical setting, the overhead light, the sensation of
being observed and handled, and a wave of nausea during the blood draw converged in a way Linh had
not anticipated. She sat in the parking lot afterward for forty minutes. She has not been able to
stop thinking, in the weeks since, about how different everything might have been — her confidence,
her relationships, her sense of herself as a person rather than an object — if her childhood had
been otherwise.

What Linh most fears, entering therapy, is that speaking about what happened will confirm that it
has permanently marked her. What she most hopes is that someone will agree, without requiring her to
justify it, that what was done to her was genuinely wrong.
