Adaeze Okonkwo is a 21-year-old Nigerian-American woman who, by almost any measure, had built
something extraordinary. She had completed her undergraduate degree in three years, earned early
admission to a combined MD program, and was, at the time of her trauma, completing a surgical
residency rotation — the youngest person in her cohort to do so. She had grown up understanding that
excellence was both a gift and an obligation, and she had met that understanding with discipline
that impressed even her most demanding attendings. None of that prepared her for the night a tornado
tore through the mid-southern city where she was training.

It was late April, and Adaeze had finished a long shift and was walking the three blocks from the
hospital to her apartment when the sky turned a particular shade of greenish-grey she had never seen
before. The sirens came seconds later. She did not make it inside. The tornado touched down two
streets over, and the pressure change was so violent it knocked her off her feet before the debris
reached her. She spent what she estimates was forty minutes pinned beneath a collapsed section of
wooden fencing and a parked car that had been pushed against it, unable to move her left arm, unable
to see anything in the darkness except flashes of lightning, unable to hear anything except the roar
and then the terrible silence after. When emergency responders finally reached her, she was certain
she was dying. She had not called for help because she had believed, in those forty minutes, that
calling out was pointless — that no one was coming, that nothing she did would change what was going
to happen to her.

In the weeks that followed, Adaeze returned to her rotation because she did not know what else to
do. But the hospital had become a different kind of place. When a trauma case came through the bay —
a car accident, a fall, anything that carried the particular charge of sudden bodily catastrophe —
her chest would seize and her hands would shake in a way she could not account for clinically. She
began arriving earlier and leaving later, telling herself it was professionalism, though she had
come to believe that staying busy was the only way to stay in control of something. She stopped
running, which had been her primary means of managing stress through medical school; the physical
exertion produced a racing heart and shortness of breath that her body no longer distinguished from
the moments under that fence. She stopped exercising entirely.

Nights became their own problem. Adaeze would wake in the dark of her apartment and be, for several
seconds, completely unable to locate herself in time. The darkness itself had become
indistinguishable, in those first moments of waking, from the darkness under the debris. She began
sleeping with every light in her apartment on, running up an electricity bill she could not afford
on a resident's stipend. When storms rolled through — and in that part of the country they rolled
through often — she would monitor the weather radar compulsively for hours before the rain arrived,
standing at her window, watching.

She had come to believe, with a quiet and terrible certainty, that what happened to her had exposed
something true about the nature of her life: that she had never actually been in control of
anything, that the competence she had spent twenty-one years building was a kind of performance that
the universe had seen through. She was certain she could not rely on her own judgment. In the
operating theatre, she had begun deferring to senior colleagues on decisions she was qualified to
make herself, apologising for assessments before she had finished giving them, shrinking in ways
that confused the attendings who had previously flagged her as exceptional. She did not experience
this as weakness. She experienced it as accuracy — as finally seeing herself clearly.

What brought her to the clinic was a supervising surgeon who had noticed the change and, carefully,
suggested she speak with someone. Adaeze had resisted for two months. What finally moved her was a
night storm that sent her crouching in her bathroom for three hours, unable to convince herself the
walls around her were real. She came to her first appointment exhausted and precise, describing her
symptoms the way she would present a case — but what she most feared, she admitted quietly near the
end of the session, was that the person she had been before that April night no longer existed, and
that no amount of treatment would bring her back.
