Callum Marsh is a 32-year-old European American man who works as a private tutor, primarily teaching
high school students in their homes and occasionally at a local library. He is married and was
referred for psychological assessment following a prolonged stalking ordeal that ended approximately
fourteen months ago, though he describes his life since then as anything but resolved.

The stalking began two years prior, initiated by a former tutoring client — a young adult male who
had developed what Callum now describes as a "fixation" following several months of academic
sessions. At first the contact seemed merely excessive: repeated messages, a few unexpected
appearances near Callum's car. Within weeks, however, it escalated. The man began following Callum
on his commute, sometimes on a motorcycle that would idle at the end of his street or rev past the
library windows where Callum held sessions. There were evenings when Callum would hear that engine
note — a particular mid-range mechanical growl — and look up to find the man sitting outside on the
kerb, watching. The stalking continued for nearly eight months before a restraining order was
obtained, and for much of that time Callum felt acutely that no space was safe, that any unfamiliar
face in a parking lot or any slow-moving vehicle behind him on the road could be the beginning of
something terrible. The perpetrator made no explicit physical threats, which Callum found,
paradoxically, more frightening — there was never a clear line he could point to and say: this is
where it ends.

Since the restraining order, the man has not reappeared. Callum knows this intellectually. Yet when
he drives to a new student's home — a routine part of his work — he finds himself scanning every
street before he parks, circling the block, checking mirrors with a precision that has made him run
consistently late. He has turned down several tutoring referrals in unfamiliar neighbourhoods,
telling the families he is fully booked, because he cannot bring himself to walk into an environment
he has not already assessed. His income has quietly contracted as a result, and his wife has begun
asking questions he doesn't know how to answer.

The motorcycle noise is perhaps the sharpest intrusion into his present life. He cannot hear one
pass on the road without his body responding before his mind catches up — a sudden cold alertness, a
scan of windows and doors. He is certain, in those moments, that something is wrong, even when he
can identify no actual threat. The same thing happens when a student's parent raises their voice in
frustration during a session, or when a neighbour argues in the driveway next door. The anger
doesn't have to be directed at him; its mere presence in the air is enough to put him on full alert.
He has begun scheduling sessions at times when he believes the house will be quieter, reasoning this
through to himself as simple professionalism.

Sleep has become thin and unreliable. He lies awake running through the day's encounters,
cataloguing faces that seemed too attentive, cars that passed more than once. He has stopped
allowing himself the kind of settled, absorbed rest that used to come naturally in the evenings,
because something in him equates that softness with danger — as though the moment he stops watching,
the world will move against him.

What troubles Callum most, and what he finds hardest to articulate, is the way the experience has
rewritten his understanding of other people. He had come to believe that people are not what they
present themselves to be, that surface friendliness conceals agendas he cannot read. This has made
it nearly impossible to let his wife close to him in the ways she used to be. He pulls back from
tenderness without fully meaning to, and when she reaches for him emotionally, he finds himself
waiting for something to go wrong. He has also turned a particular cruelty inward: he tells himself,
with a regularity that frightens him, that he deserves what happened — that he missed signs he
should have caught, that a more competent or perceptive person would have stopped it sooner. He
cannot trust his own judgment.

It was his wife who finally insisted he seek help, after a family gathering at which he spent the
entire evening positioned near the exit, barely speaking. What Callum says he most hopes for is to
stop living as though the threat never ended. What he is most afraid of is that it hasn't.
