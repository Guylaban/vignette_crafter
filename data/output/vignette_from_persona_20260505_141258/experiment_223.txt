Kwame is a 68-year-old widowed Ghanaian-American man who works as a senior policy analyst for a
federal health agency in Washington, D.C. He came to the clinic after months of what he described,
with characteristic understatement, as "not being quite right." His PCL-5 score of 77 places him in
a range of severe symptom burden, and the picture that emerged across the first several sessions was
one of a man whose relationship with his own body had been fundamentally and catastrophically
altered.

Approximately fourteen months before his intake appointment, Kwame was admitted to the intensive
care unit following a sudden cardiac arrest. He had been at his desk reviewing a legislative brief
when the chest pain began — a sensation he initially dismissed as indigestion before it became
something else entirely, something vast and crushing that dropped him to the floor of his office.
Colleagues found him unresponsive. He was resuscitated twice in the ambulance. In the ICU, he was
intubated and sedated for four days, and during the fragmented periods of partial consciousness he
retained a visceral, disordered sense of being unable to breathe, of alarms sounding, of hands
pressing on his chest, of not knowing whether he was dying or already dead. His wife of thirty-nine
years had passed two years prior, and it was his adult daughter who sat at his bedside not knowing
whether her father would survive.

He did survive. He was discharged after eleven days, with a defibrillator implanted in his chest and
a cardiologist's cautious optimism. For the first several weeks, Kwame moved through his recovery
with what appeared to those around him to be composure. He returned calls, attended telehealth
appointments, and even submitted a policy brief from home. But something beneath that surface had
shifted in a way he could not name and did not yet have language for.

The memory of those hours did not behave like other memories. At times it came back not as something
that had happened but as something that was happening — the weight on his sternum, the
disorientation, the alarms — arriving with a freshness that made the fourteen months between then
and now feel like an illusion. At other times he could think about the hospitalization and feel
nothing at all, as though he were reading a case file about a stranger, which disturbed him in a
different way. And then there were moments when the memory returned with such emotional force that
he had to set down whatever he was doing and simply wait for it to pass.

What frightened Kwame most, he eventually disclosed, was not the memory itself but what the event
had revealed about his body. He had come to believe that his body was no longer a reliable
instrument — that it had betrayed him once without warning and could do so again at any moment, and
that there was nothing he could do to anticipate or prevent it. The defibrillator, which was meant
to reassure him, had instead become a symbol of this unreliability. He was certain that the tension
he felt throughout his body, the constant low-level readiness that never fully released, was
evidence that something remained wrong — that his body was still registering a danger his doctors
could not see. He felt this even at home, in his apartment where he had lived for twenty years,
where there was no rational threat he could identify. He remained watchful anyway.

At work, the consequences had accumulated quietly. Kwame's role requires him to read dense, complex
documents and synthesise them into actionable recommendations, but concentration had become
genuinely effortful in a way it had never been before. He had missed two internal deadlines —
unprecedented in his career — and had begun arriving earlier and staying later simply to produce the
same output, a compensation his colleagues had not yet noticed but that he tracked with quiet shame.

He had also stopped discussing his health with his daughter, deflecting her questions with
reassurances he did not believe. He had come to fear that whatever capacity he once had for feeling
— the warmth he used to feel talking to her, the satisfaction he used to find in his work — had been
damaged in a way that was permanent. What finally brought him to seek help was a moment at his desk
when he realised he could not remember the last time he had felt anything he would call ordinary. He
came hoping, with great caution, that someone might tell him he was wrong about that.
