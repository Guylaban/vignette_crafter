Marta Brzenkowski is a 79-year-old widowed woman of Polish descent who works part-time as a tailor
from the spare room of her home in a small Pennsylvania town. She was referred for psychological
assessment after her primary care physician noted significant deterioration in her mood and social
functioning over the past several years. What emerged across the intake and first sessions was a
clinical picture rooted in experiences she had carried, largely in silence, for most of her adult
life.

Marta was a young girl in postwar Poland when her father, a conscripted soldier, returned from the
front in a condition she has never been able to fully describe. She was present the night he came
home with severe wounds that had been inadequately treated — she remembers the kitchen table, the
smell, her mother's voice, and blood, a great deal of blood, spreading across cloth that her mother
pressed uselessly against his side. Her father died before morning. Marta was seven years old. What
she witnessed that night did not resolve into a coherent narrative over the decades that followed.
Even now, when she attempts to speak about it, the words do not come. She can produce fragments —
the table, the cloth, the dark — but she cannot place them in order, cannot say what happened first
or last, and the effort to do so leaves her agitated and exhausted. The event seems to live in her
body more than in language.

For most of her adult life Marta managed. She married, raised children, built a quiet competence
around her tailoring work, and did not speak of her father's death. But since her husband's passing
four years ago, something has loosened in the structure that kept those early memories contained.
She now finds that unwanted thoughts surface without warning — not full recollections but fragments,
impressions, a quality of dread that arrives uninvited while she is working at her sewing table or
lying awake at night. When a customer comes in with a cut finger, or when blood appears anywhere in
her environment, she experiences what she can only describe as a sudden internal collapse — her
hands stop, her chest tightens, and she is briefly somewhere else entirely. She has stopped watching
any television that might carry medical content for this reason. In her tailoring work, the sight of
a pin-pricked finger — her own or a customer's — has twice sent her from the room without
explanation, something she finds deeply humiliating.

Certain conversations have also become intolerable. When neighbours or acquaintances speak about
war, about soldiers, about fathers who did not come home, Marta finds ways to end the exchange
quickly and leave. She has told herself this is simply a matter of privacy, but the avoidance has
widened considerably. She has stopped attending her church's weekly lunch, where such topics arise
unpredictably. She no longer visits her closest friend of forty years, a woman who lost a son to
military service and speaks of him often. The friendship has effectively ended, and Marta has
offered no explanation.

What troubles her therapist, and what Marta herself articulates with quiet precision, is the way she
has come to regard positive experience as something dangerous. When she notices a moment of pleasure
— sunlight through her workroom window, a garment coming together well — she finds herself pulling
away from it, as though happiness were a surface that could not hold her weight. She had come to
believe, without ever quite stating it to herself, that feeling good only made the fall worse. She
pushes warmth away before it can establish itself. She had once thought of herself as a
fundamentally cheerful person; that self-understanding is now entirely gone. She describes feeling
dead inside, and says this with neither distress nor drama, simply as a fact she has accepted. When
she recalls her father's death directly, what rises in her is not grief but rage — at whom or what
she cannot say — and this surprises and frightens her each time.

What brought her finally to seek help was a grandchild's wedding invitation. She wants to attend.
She is afraid she will not be able to feel anything if she does, and more afraid still that she will
feel too much. She hopes, tentatively, that something can be recovered — though she is not certain
she believes it.
