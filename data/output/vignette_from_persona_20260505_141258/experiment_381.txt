Rangi Taufa is a 55-year-old Tongan-American man who works as a nanny for a family in suburban
Portland, Oregon. He is divorced and lives alone in a small apartment not far from the home where he
works. He came to the clinic carrying a PCL-5 score of 65, and in the intake session he sat with his
hands folded tightly in his lap, speaking in careful, measured sentences until, at certain moments,
he simply stopped mid-sentence and stared at the floor.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Rangi was caring for his younger brother Sione, who had been
living with him following a serious cancer diagnosis. Rangi had taken on the role of primary
caregiver with the quiet, determined devotion that had always characterised his relationships. On
the evening that changed everything, Sione suffered a catastrophic medical event — a haemorrhage —
while Rangi was in the room. What followed was a period of frantic effort: Rangi pressing his hands
against his brother's body trying to slow the bleeding, shouting into the phone for an ambulance,
hearing Sione's breathing change into something laboured and wet and then, finally, into nothing.
The paramedics arrived too late. Rangi was still kneeling on the floor beside his brother when they
came through the door.

In the weeks that followed, Rangi found himself unable to give a coherent account of what had
happened, even when he tried to tell his adult daughter over the phone. He would begin the story and
then the thread would simply dissolve — words stopped arriving, the sequence collapsed, and he would
trail off into silence. He had come to believe this was evidence of something broken in him, proof
that he was not the strong, capable man he had always tried to be. What frightened him most was the
conviction that he had failed at the one thing that mattered: he had been right there, in the room,
and Sione had still died. He was certain that a better man, a stronger man, would have done
something differently.

The memories, when they came, were not silent. He could hear the particular quality of Sione's
breathing in those final minutes — a sound that now arrived unbidden, playing in his mind with a
fidelity that felt almost physical. Along with the sound came grief so acute it was difficult to
distinguish from physical pain, a wave that could arrive in the middle of an ordinary afternoon and
leave him unable to move for several minutes.

His work as a nanny placed him in close, daily proximity to the children he cared for, and he found
that tending to their scraped knees, their small injuries — any moment that required him to look
carefully at a child's hands or arms or face in the context of hurt — could stop him cold. The sight
of a child's bruised shin, a cut that needed cleaning, any concentrated attention on a body part in
distress, carried with it an associative pull he could not fully explain and could not prevent. He
began deflecting those moments to the children's parents whenever possible, which the family had
noticed and quietly questioned.

When conversations arose at the family dinner table — the parents discussing a news story about
loss, a neighbour who had recently been bereaved — Rangi would excuse himself to the kitchen. He had
developed a habit of turning on the tap so that the sound of water covered whatever was being said
in the next room. Over months, this had created a subtle but perceptible distance between him and
the family he worked for, a warmth that had cooled without either side being able to name why.

Perhaps most distressing to Rangi was a creeping sense that he had become somehow less than a person
— that he moved through his days as a kind of functional object, present in body but absent in some
essential way he could not articulate. He did not share this with many people. He had shared almost
nothing with anyone.

What finally brought him to the clinic was his daughter's visit last month. She told him, gently but
directly, that she barely recognised him. He sat with that for three weeks before making the call.
He came hoping, he said quietly, to find a way to remember Sione without the sound.
