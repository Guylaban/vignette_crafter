Malee Srisawat is a 61-year-old Thai-American sculptor, married and living in a mid-sized Pacific
Northwest city, who presented for an initial psychological evaluation following what she described,
haltingly, as "something I saw that I cannot get out of my body." Her PCL-5 score of 41 places her
in the clinically significant range, and the picture that emerged across the first several sessions
was one of a woman whose interior life had been quietly reorganized around a single evening she
witnessed approximately fourteen months ago.

Malee had been working late in the shared studio complex where she rents space, finishing the
surface treatment on a large ceramic installation. The building is old, with long corridors and
uneven lighting, and on that evening she was among the last people remaining. Walking toward the
exit, she rounded a corner and came upon a man assaulting a younger woman in the hallway — a
sustained, brutal attack that Malee watched, frozen, from a distance of perhaps twenty feet before
the attacker fled. She has never been able to describe the sequence of events in full. When she
tries to narrate it — to her husband, to her primary care physician, and now in the consulting room
— she loses the thread somewhere in the middle, her speech slowing and then stopping, as though the
account itself has a wall in it. She cannot construct a clear story of what happened even to
herself, and this troubles her deeply. What she does have are impressions that arrive with a force
she finds hard to convey: she has said that the memory of that hallway feels more present and more
real than her recollection of her own wedding, more vivid than the faces of her children when they
were small.

In the months after the attack, certain things became unbearable without Malee being entirely able
to say why. The man who had committed the assault had been wearing a heavy, sweet cologne — she had
caught it in the air as she stood there — and now any similar fragrance, encountered in a grocery
store or on a passing stranger, produces in her an immediate and overwhelming physical reaction: her
chest tightens, her hands go cold, and she has had to leave public spaces abruptly on several
occasions. Footsteps behind her in a corridor produce the same flooding response, and the studio
complex where she has worked for eleven years has become a site of near-constant alertness. She now
arrives only in daylight, leaves before other tenants, and has rearranged her workspace so that her
back is never to the door. The large installation she was completing that night has remained
unfinished. Colleagues have noticed her early departures and her unwillingness to participate in the
communal evening openings that once formed the social fabric of her professional life, and two
collaborative commissions have quietly dissolved because she could not commit to the shared studio
hours they required.

Nights at home have brought their own difficulty. Malee has come to feel that darkness itself
carries a quality of exposure, that she is less able to trust the safety of her own house once the
light goes. She startles at sounds her husband makes moving through the kitchen, and she has
described lying awake listening in a way she recognizes as purposeless but cannot stop. She is
certain, in a way she finds shameful, that something is fundamentally wrong with her — that another
person, a stronger or more rational person, would have acted rather than frozen, would have
recovered by now, would not be derailed by a smell. She has come to hold people at a cautious
distance, finding trust difficult in ways she did not before, and she has arrived at a quiet but
settled conviction that the world is more dangerous than she had allowed herself to believe.

What finally brought Malee to seek help was a conversation with her husband, who told her gently
that he felt he had lost her. She sat with that for several weeks before calling. She is most afraid
that what she will be asked to do in therapy is tell the story all the way through — and most
hoping, she said, that someone might explain why she cannot.
