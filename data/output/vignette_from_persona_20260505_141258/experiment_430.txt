Rajan is a 50-year-old South Asian American animator, married, who has been referred for
psychological assessment following a period of escalating distress that his wife described as having
"quietly dismantled" the man she knew. The precipitating trauma occurred approximately fourteen
months ago, when Rajan's nine-year-old son was admitted to the paediatric intensive care unit
following a sudden and severe septic episode. What began as what Rajan assumed was an ordinary fever
became, within hours, a cascade of alarms, rushing nurses, and a physician pulling him aside in a
corridor that smelled sharply of antiseptic and floor cleaner to tell him that his son's organs were
under threat. Rajan spent four nights sleeping in a reclining chair beside his son's bed, waking
each time a monitor changed pitch, watching a small body connected to lines and tubes. On the third
night, the medical team performed an emergency procedure to place a central line, and Rajan stood
outside the curtain listening, unable to leave, unable to enter. His son recovered fully, was
discharged after eleven days, and is now, by every clinical measure, a healthy child. Rajan has not
recovered.

In the weeks after the discharge, Rajan began noticing that certain things pulled him back into the
PICU with a completeness that felt involuntary. The smell was the most disorienting — a routine
visit to his own GP for a blood pressure check filled the waiting room with that same antiseptic
note, and Rajan found himself standing at the reception desk, heart hammering, convinced on some
level that something catastrophic was unfolding. He rescheduled the appointment twice and has not
returned. At work, his studio is a largely solitary environment — he spends long hours alone at his
desk, and the company recently relocated to a building that requires him to pass through a medical
clinic on the ground floor to reach the elevator. The smell that drifts into the lobby has made him
use the stairwell exclusively, adding ten minutes to every journey and drawing puzzled comments from
colleagues. On the two occasions he has been required to attend the on-site occupational health
suite for a routine screen, he has sat rigidly in the examination chair, certain that the clinician
was about to deliver news that something was wrong with him, that illness had been quietly gathering
in his body as it had in his son's.

Sleeping alone has become its own ordeal. When his wife travels to visit her family — something she
does several times a year — Rajan finds the empty bedroom intolerable in a way he struggles to
articulate. He has taken to sleeping on the sofa with the television running, or calling his wife at
two in the morning, not to speak of anything in particular, but to hear her voice confirm that the
world is still intact. This pattern of reassurance-seeking has extended into daily life: he asks his
wife repeatedly whether she thinks he looks unwell, whether she noticed anything strange in his
behaviour, whether she believes the doctors truly checked everything when his son was discharged.
She answers patiently, but Rajan has noticed her patience acquiring a texture of exhaustion, and he
is frightened by what that might mean for the marriage.

In parallel, Rajan spends considerable mental energy rehearsing catastrophic futures — imagining the
precise sequence of events if his son were to fall ill again, working through what he would do
differently, what he failed to notice the first time, how he might hold the hospital accountable for
what he experienced as a failure of early warning. He is certain that someone should have caught the
infection sooner, and he returns to this conviction repeatedly, turning over what might be said,
what might be demanded. He also prepares himself by running through worst-case scenarios before any
medical appointment or family illness, believing that if he has already imagined the worst, it will
hurt less when it arrives.

What finally brought Rajan to seek help was a moment at his desk three weeks ago, when he was
animating a hospital scene for a children's series and found himself unable to continue working for
the remainder of the day. He sat with his headphones off, staring at the screen, and thought: this
is not going to stop on its own. What he hopes for, more than anything, is to stop waiting for the
next disaster. What he fears most is that the waiting has already become who he is.
