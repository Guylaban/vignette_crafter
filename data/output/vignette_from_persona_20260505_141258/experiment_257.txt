time before she believed it was safe to go back inside. It is that night — the cold earth, the
darkness, the aloneness — that has never fully left her.

In the months following her husband's departure, Adaeze had expected to feel relief, and in some
ways she did. But something else arrived instead. The memory of that night, and others like it,

— the particular smell of rain-soaked soil drifting in through a screen, or the faint earthy scent

the day, moving through her own home, she finds herself pausing at doorways, scanning rooms before
entering. She has come to believe that safety is something that exists only in a very small number
of places — her living room with the curtains drawn, her daughter's kitchen — and that anywhere else
carries an ambient threat she cannot name but cannot dismiss.

moves into the space that stillness creates. She has also found herself agreeing readily with
