Arjun is a 29-year-old South Asian man who owns and operates a small convenience store in a mid-
sized American city. He lives with his long-term partner and presents for treatment following a
period of physical abuse sustained at the hands of a previous romantic partner that ended
approximately fourteen months ago. His PCL-5 score of 80 places him in the severe range, and across
several intake sessions he has described a life that has quietly contracted around the aftermath of
what was done to him.

The abuse unfolded over the course of nearly a year and escalated in ways Arjun found difficult to
anticipate or understand at the time. The incident he returns to most often occurred late on a
winter night in the apartment they shared. His former partner came home after hours away, and Arjun
recalls the particular smell of sweat and stale heat that entered the room before any words were
exchanged — a smell that meant, he had learned by then, that something bad was about to happen. What
followed was a prolonged and severe physical assault during which Arjun did not fight back, did not
call for help, and did not leave. He stayed on the floor of the kitchen until it was over.

In the weeks after the relationship ended, Arjun found himself unable to make sense of that night in
any continuous way. The memory did not come to him as a story he could tell from beginning to end.
Instead it arrived without permission — a sudden, complete intrusion that could be triggered by
almost nothing he could predict. What he could predict, with terrible reliability, was the smell. At
the store, when a customer comes in after a long shift or a summer afternoon outside, the warmth and
body odour they carry with them can drop Arjun back into that kitchen before he has had a chance to
think. In those moments he does not see the memory so much as inhabit it — the full emotional weight
arrives without any clear image accompanying it, a wave of something he can only describe as dread
and fury arriving simultaneously. When the memory does surface with more clarity, what he feels is
not fear but rage, a rage so intense it frightens him and that he does not know what to do with.

He has come to believe that his body is working against him. His heart rate spikes when a stranger
walks toward him down an aisle of his own store, a space he has owned for three years and in which
he should, he knows, feel at ease. He has rearranged the layout of the front counter so that he can
see the door at all times. At night the sense of threat does not diminish when he locks up; if
anything it sharpens. He lies awake for hours, unable to settle, and when he does sleep it does not
feel restful. His partner has noticed that he flinches at being touched unexpectedly in the dark,
and the two of them have stopped talking easily about certain subjects — anything that edges toward
conflict, toward the dynamics of relationships, toward why someone would stay — because those
conversations can bring the whole thing rushing back in a way that leaves Arjun silent and
unreachable for the rest of the evening.

What weighs on him most heavily, and what he spoke about with the most visible difficulty during
intake, is not the fear but the self-condemnation. He was certain, in a way that felt immovable,
that he should have done something to stop what happened. He had not been helpless, he told himself;
he had made a choice, and the choice had been wrong, and that wrongness said something permanent
about who he was. He had come to believe that he could not trust his own judgment, his own
instincts, his own capacity to protect himself or anyone else. This belief had begun to affect how
he ran the store — he second-guessed hiring decisions, avoided confronting a supplier he suspected
was overcharging him, and felt a low-level paralysis around choices that once came naturally.

What finally brought him to seek help was his current partner's quiet insistence that he was
disappearing. Arjun arrived hoping, above all, to stop feeling like a stranger in his own body — and
terrified that the man he had been before that night in the kitchen was simply gone.
