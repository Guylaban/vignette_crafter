Aroha is a 20-year-old Māori woman who works as an interior designer and who, until recently, had
built what she described as a carefully ordered life. She married young, at nineteen, to a man she
had known since secondary school, and for several months the relationship appeared, at least from
the outside, to be stable. The abuse began gradually — a shove during an argument, a hand around her
wrist — before escalating into sustained physical violence that Aroha endured largely in silence, in
the apartment they shared, often late at night when the rooms were dark and the building around them
was quiet. On the night that brought her eventually to therapy, her husband struck her repeatedly in
the kitchen while shouting, and it was the combination of the sudden crack of sound against her face
and the enclosed darkness of the hallway she fled into that now live most vividly in her nervous
system. She left the apartment that night but did not leave the marriage legally for several more
months, a delay she has since turned over and over in her mind with something close to torment.

In the weeks that followed, Aroha moved into a small apartment on her own, which she had imagined
would feel like safety. Instead, the silence of being alone in a room became its own kind of
exposure. She began leaving lights on in every room at night and found herself unable to remain in
the apartment if she could not hear another person's voice nearby — a neighbour's television, a
phone call kept running in the background. At work, where her role required her to visit client
properties and conduct site consultations, she started declining meetings that would place her alone
in a building with a male client or contractor. She told her supervisor the scheduling no longer
worked for her. Over the following months, two significant client accounts were reassigned, and her
standing in the small firm quietly diminished. She has not explained the real reason to anyone
there, partly because she does not believe she could get through the explanation without losing the
thread entirely — when she has tried to describe what happened, even to her sister, the account
fragments and she falls silent, unable to finish, the sequence dissolving before she can reach the
end of it.

What has proved most destabilising is not always the full memory but something more formless: a wave
of dread and shame that arrives without any accompanying image, triggered by a car door slamming in
the street below or a male voice raised in conversation in the office. Her body responds before her
mind has named anything — shoulders drawn up, jaw clenched, a scanning alertness that does not
switch off. She checks behind her when she walks to the car park, certain at the level of the body
that someone is there, even when she knows rationally that he is not. When she did encounter her
former husband briefly outside a mutual friend's building three months ago, she stood frozen on the
footpath for several minutes after he had gone, unable to account for the time.

Aroha has come to believe, with a quiet and settled conviction, that she is responsible for what
happened — that she stayed too long, that she provoked him on the night it was worst, that a smarter
or stronger woman would have left sooner. The guilt arrives most forcefully when she remembers
specific moments, and she has not been able to dislodge it through reasoning. Alongside this she
carries a sense of profound dislocation: she is too young to feel this severed from ordinary life,
she thinks, and yet she does not feel she belongs in the social world her peers inhabit, nor in the
professional world she worked to enter, nor in any domestic arrangement she can imagine for herself.
She has come to see trust as something that was available to a version of herself that no longer
exists.

She sought help after her sister noticed she had not left the apartment on a weekend and sat with
her until she agreed to make a call. What Aroha says she is most afraid of is that the person she
was before cannot be recovered. What she is most hoping, tentatively, is that someone might tell her
she is not entirely to blame.
