Rohan is a 24-year-old South Asian man who works as a junior diplomat, currently stationed in
Washington D.C. following a posting abroad that ended abruptly under circumstances that have since
reorganised the entire architecture of his inner life. He presents with a PCL-5 score of 66, and he
arrives at the intake appointment dressed in a loose, long-sleeved shirt despite the warmth of the
season, sitting with his back to the wall and his eyes tracking the door at intervals throughout the
session.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Rohan was stationed at a diplomatic mission in a city that came
under sustained military assault over the course of several days. He had been attending a formal
reception at a colleague's residence when the first strikes landed several blocks away. The building
shook. Glasses fell. And then, in the minutes that followed, a portion of the outer wall collapsed
under secondary pressure, and Rohan found himself on the floor amid smoke, plaster dust, and the
smell of burning — a dense, particulate odour that mixed with the spilled wine and spirits from the
overturned table beside him. A man he did not recognise stumbled toward him through the debris, face
bloodied, arms outstretched, and Rohan, unable to move, lay still until colleagues reached him. He
was not gravely physically injured. That fact, he has since decided, is evidence of something
damning about him rather than something fortunate.

In the weeks that followed evacuation, Rohan began to notice that certain things undid him without
warning. The smell of alcohol — at a dinner, near a bar, even hand sanitiser with the wrong chemical
note — would return him without transition to the floor of that residence, the dust in his throat,
the man walking toward him. A car backfiring, a door slamming in an adjacent room, construction work
three streets over: any sharp, percussive sound could produce the same violent displacement, the
same certainty that it was happening again, that the walls were coming down. He found himself in
those moments not remembering the event but inhabiting it, speaking from inside it, and then
returning to the present disoriented and ashamed. What frightened him most in these episodes was not
the fear itself but his inability to accept reassurance. When his partner, Priya, held him and told
him he was safe, he could hear the words and find them meaningless — not because he doubted her
sincerity, but because he had come to believe that safety was simply not a category that applied to
him anymore.

He began to manage. He started running — long distances, at punishing hours, pushing past discomfort
into exhaustion, then further. He began using cannabis most evenings, and occasionally something
stronger when the nights were bad. He took to wearing long, layered clothing whenever he left the
apartment, operating on the quiet logic that if he was not visible, he was less of a target. He
stopped attending diplomatic receptions entirely, citing workload, and then stopped attending most
social functions associated with his posting. His supervisor has noted his withdrawal in two
informal conversations, and a formal performance review is pending. Priya has told him she feels she
is losing him, and he has not known what to say, in part because he knows what happened but finds
that when he tries to speak about it the words simply do not exist — there is the event, vivid and
sensory and present, but no language that reaches it. He can sometimes smell the smoke and spilled
spirits when he sits with the memory, but he cannot explain to anyone, including himself, what he
experienced.

What has settled most heavily over these fourteen months is not any single symptom but a set of
convictions that have quietly taken up residence. He is certain there is something about him — some
quality he cannot name — that made him a person to whom this would happen. He has lost whatever
faith he held in the coherence of the world and in the people who move through it. Strangers who
approach him in ordinary situations — a colleague crossing a room, a server leaning over to take his
order — produce a spike of alarm that he finds humiliating and cannot override. He does not believe
the future contains much for him. It was Priya who finally said the word therapy aloud, and it was
the look on her face when she said it that brought him here. What he is most afraid of is that
nothing will change. What he is most hoping, tentatively and almost against his will, is that
someone can help him find the words.
