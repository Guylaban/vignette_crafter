Tran Minh Duc is a 75-year-old Vietnamese American man, widowed, who works part-time as a
cybersecurity analyst from his home in the suburbs of San Jose. He presents with a PCL-5 score of
74, reflecting severe and pervasive posttraumatic stress symptoms following an event that occurred
more than four decades ago but which, in his own words, "never stopped happening."

When Duc was in his early thirties, his youngest son, Khoi, then eighteen months old, developed
bacterial meningitis. The illness progressed with terrifying speed. Within hours of Duc noticing the
child's stiff neck and unusual cry, Khoi was admitted to the intensive care unit, where medical
staff performed a lumbar puncture while Duc was made to wait outside a curtained bay. He remembers
the antiseptic smell of the ward with extraordinary precision — a sharp, chemical odour that seemed
to saturate his clothes, his hair, his skin. He remembers a nurse carrying a newborn past him in the
corridor as he waited, the sight of that healthy infant making the contrast with his own child
almost unbearable. He remembers the way a physician placed a hand on his shoulder and spoke slowly,
and how Duc understood from the cadence of the words before he understood their content that his son
might not survive the night. Khoi did survive, though he sustained partial hearing loss. Duc has
never fully absorbed that outcome as real.

In the months after Khoi's hospitalisation, Duc found himself returning compulsively to the hours of
that night. The memory did not behave like ordinary recollection. It arrived unbidden and played in
its entirety — the fluorescent corridor, the smell, the nurse with the newborn, the physician's
measured voice — as though it were footage on a loop that he had no means of stopping. More
disorienting still was the quality of presentness the memory carried. When it came, Duc did not feel
that he was remembering something that had ended; he felt that it was still occurring, that Khoi was
still behind that curtain, that the outcome was still undecided. He had come to believe that there
was something fundamentally wrong with his mind, that a healthy person would simply know the
difference between then and now.

Over the years his wife, Linh, had been the person who helped him navigate around the sharpest edges
of his daily life. After her death three years ago, those edges became unavoidable. His work in
cybersecurity, which he conducts remotely, requires occasional in-person consultations at client
offices, and one such client is a large hospital system. The first time Duc walked into that
building to review their network security protocols, the antiseptic smell hit him before he had
crossed the lobby threshold. His heart rate surged, his hands began to tremble, and he stood frozen
for nearly a minute before a colleague guided him to a chair. He has since declined every in-person
visit to that client, offering technical justifications that have begun to strain his professional
relationships. His supervisor has noted his reluctance in writing.

Outside work, Duc avoids his son Khoi's neighbourhood, where a maternity clinic recently opened. He
changes his route to the pharmacy to bypass it. When a colleague's pregnancy was announced in a
video call and someone held up a scan image to the camera, Duc left the call without explanation. He
has not been able to explain this to colleagues or to Khoi, in part because when he attempts to tell
the story of that night, something fractures in the telling. He loses the sequence, trails into
silence, and cannot find his way back to a conclusion. He has come to dread being asked about it,
and so he offers nothing.

What finally brought Duc to seek help was a routine cardiology appointment last month. The
examination room smelled exactly as that hospital ward had smelled. He experienced a flashback of
such intensity that the cardiologist paused the examination and asked whether he was all right. Duc
was not able to answer. He sat in his car for an hour afterward before he could drive. He made a
therapy referral the following week. What he is most afraid of is that the memory will never be
something that belongs to the past. What he most hopes for, quietly and with some difficulty
articulating it, is to be present with his son without being somewhere else at the same time.
