Harold Metzger is a 78-year-old divorced man who has worked for decades as a database administrator,
a career that has carried him well past conventional retirement age because, as he has put it, the
structure of the work keeps him anchored. He lives alone in a modest ranch house in suburban Ohio,
and it was there, eighteen months ago, that he collapsed in his kitchen while making coffee and was
rushed by ambulance to a cardiac intensive care unit, where he spent eleven days fighting septic
shock secondary to a ruptured abdominal abscess. The physicians told him afterward that he had been
within hours of dying. Harold does not dispute this. What he cannot seem to do is leave it in the
past.

The illness arrived without warning. He had felt mildly unwell for a day, attributed it to something
he had eaten, and then found himself on the kitchen floor, unable to rise, the smell of burnt coffee
filling the room as the pot continued to run. That smell — dark, slightly acrid, the particular
bitterness of coffee scorched past the point of drinking — became burned into the event in a way
Harold cannot fully explain. When he thinks about what happened, he can smell it again, and with it
comes the sensation that he is back on that floor, chest tight, the ceiling above him swimming. The
memory does not feel like something retrieved; it feels like something happening. He has described
it as more vivid and immediate than any other memory he possesses, sharper than recollections of his
wedding, his children's births, decades of ordinary life. He has quietly moved the coffee maker to
the garage.

In the months following his discharge, Harold returned to his remote database work, logging in from
home each morning to manage systems for a regional logistics firm. The work requires sustained
attention to error logs and system alerts — notifications that flag anomalies, warnings that
something in the infrastructure may be failing. Harold has noticed that when an alert tone sounds
from his workstation, his body responds before his mind can reason with it. His heart rate climbs,
his hands go cold, and a wave of what he can only describe as dread moves through him. He had come
to believe that his body had been permanently altered by what happened to it — that it no longer
distinguished between a server warning and a genuine emergency, and that this was evidence of
something broken and irreversible inside him. He is ashamed of this, and he has not told his
supervisor why his response times on certain tasks have lengthened.

Harold no longer sleeps easily in his own home. He checks the locks multiple times before bed, keeps
a light on in the hallway, and wakes in the early hours with a feeling he describes as wrongness — a
certainty that something is about to go wrong that he cannot locate or name. He is watchful in a way
that exhausts him. The world, he has said plainly, is not safe; he knows this now in a way he did
not before, and no amount of reassurance from his physicians has shifted that knowledge. He has lost
something he once had — a basic assumption that the body would give warning before betrayal, that
life operated with some underlying fairness or logic. His faith in that order is gone. He feels
anger at this loss and guilt that he cannot simply be grateful to be alive, as everyone keeps
telling him he should be.

His relationships have narrowed. He speaks to his adult children less frequently than before, partly
because their concern feels like confirmation of how diminished he has become, and partly because he
finds sustained conversation effortful in a way it never was. His ex-wife called after she heard he
had been ill; he did not call back.

What brought Harold to seek help was a moment three weeks ago when a system alert sounded and he
found himself crouched behind his desk chair, heart hammering, before he understood what he had
done. He sat on the floor of his home office for a long time afterward. He is most afraid, he said
at intake, that this is simply who he is now. What he is hoping for — though he offered this
quietly, as though uncertain he was entitled to it — is to be able to make a cup of coffee again
without it meaning anything at all.
