Valentina Reyes is a 24-year-old Salvadoran American woman who works the day shift at a large
distribution warehouse on the outskirts of the city. She came to the intake appointment wearing a
grey hoodie pulled low, and she spent much of the first session with her gaze directed at the floor
or the middle distance — rarely, and only briefly, meeting the clinician's eyes. She described
herself as someone who used to be social, someone her coworkers called *la que siempre ríe*, the one
who always laughs. That version of herself, she said quietly, felt like a stranger now.

Fourteen months ago, Valentina was present during a pipe-bomb detonation at an outdoor community
festival in her neighborhood — an event she had attended every summer since childhood. She had been
standing near a food stall when the device exploded approximately thirty feet away. The blast threw
her off her feet. In the seconds that followed, there was smoke, screaming, and a confusion of
bodies moving in every direction. She recalls the smell of burning and the sight of a man near her
falling, and then — nothing. A gap. She does not know how she got from the ground to the far side of
a chain-link fence, nor what she did in the minutes between the explosion and the arrival of
emergency services. No matter how many times she has tried to reconstruct that interval, the memory
simply is not there, and its absence frightens her in a way she finds difficult to articulate. She
knows what happened — a bomb went off, people were hurt, she survived — but when she tries to put
the experience into language, the words dissolve before she can reach them. She has attempted to
write about it twice and abandoned the page both times.

In the weeks after the attack, Valentina returned to the warehouse because she needed the income.
The warehouse floor became the first place where her changed nervous system made itself known in
daily life. The facility is enormous and loud: forklifts reverse with warning beeps, pallets drop
from height, coworkers shout across aisles. Any sudden movement in her peripheral vision — a
colleague stepping quickly around a corner, a hand raised to gesture — sends a jolt through her body
that she cannot suppress. She has come to believe that her body is no longer reliable, that it
responds to the world according to its own rules rather than hers, and this loss of confidence in
her own physical self has been quietly humiliating. A supervisor once asked why she flinched when he
tapped her shoulder, and she had no answer she was willing to give.

Valentina has developed a habit of arriving at her shift early enough to walk the warehouse floor
before others fill it, mapping the space so that nothing will surprise her. She positions herself
during breaks with her back to the wall. At night, in her apartment, she finds it nearly impossible
to feel safe — the darkness seems to carry a quality of exposure that daylight does not, and she
often lies awake running through sounds she cannot identify. She scans constantly: doorways,
windows, the street below. She had come to believe, in a way that felt less like a thought and more
like a fact, that the world is a dangerous place and that she is the only one paying sufficient
attention to it.

When memories of the festival surface without warning, what rises with them is not grief or fear but
a rage so sudden and consuming that it alarms her. She has learned to manage this by going somewhere
private and doing what she describes as "leaving" — deliberately detaching from her body, going
blank, waiting for the feeling to pass. It works, after a fashion, but she loses time, and afterward
she feels hollowed out.

What finally brought Valentina to seek help was a conversation with her younger sister, who told her
she had stopped seeming like herself. Valentina had not realised how visible the change had become.
She came to the first session hoping, in her words, to "get back inside my own body." What she fears
most is that the version of herself she is hoping to recover no longer exists.
