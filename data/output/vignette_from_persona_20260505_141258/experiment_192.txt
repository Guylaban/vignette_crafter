Marisol Vega is a 57-year-old divorced Latina woman who has worked as a physical therapist for
nearly three decades, most recently at an outpatient rehabilitation clinic in the Gulf Coast region
of Texas. She presents for an initial psychological evaluation approximately fourteen months after
surviving a major hurricane that struck her coastal community, reporting a constellation of
distressing symptoms that have progressively narrowed the boundaries of her daily life.

The storm made landfall on a Tuesday morning in late August. Marisol had chosen not to evacuate,
having ridden out lesser storms before and feeling a responsibility to her elderly neighbor,
Dolores, who lived alone next door. What she had not anticipated was the speed of the surge. Within
two hours of the storm's landfall, the water was at her windowsills. She remembers standing on her
kitchen counter, watching the brown water rise around her furniture, holding Dolores's hand as the
older woman wept. The surge eventually stabilized just below ceiling height in her ground floor, and
the two women spent eleven hours in the attic before rescue teams reached them by boat. Dolores
survived, but a family two streets over did not, and Marisol learned this only when she returned to
survey the damage weeks later. She had watched the water from her attic window for hours — that
particular quality of light on moving, opaque floodwater, the debris turning slowly in it — and that
image has never fully left her.

In the weeks that followed, Marisol noticed that something had shifted in the way she held the
memory of those hours. Rather than recalling it as something that had happened to her, she found
herself relating to it the way one might relate to a story heard about a stranger. The memory feels
like it belongs to someone else, she told her evaluator quietly, and yet the grief it carries does
not. There are moments — triggered by nothing she can name, or by a sound, or by a sudden wave of
nausea — when the full emotional weight of those eleven hours descends on her without any
accompanying image. She simply finds herself grieving, intensely and without context, in the middle
of a treatment session or while eating breakfast.

Water, in any form, has become difficult. Marisol drives a longer route to work to avoid a
particular overpass above a retention pond. At the clinic, she has quietly rearranged her schedule
to avoid the hydrotherapy pool, telling colleagues she prefers land-based techniques, which is true
now in a way it was not before. When a patient's name is Dolores, or when someone mentions a place
name associated with the storm — even tangentially, in passing conversation — she feels a jolt of
nausea and has learned to excuse herself briefly. The nausea itself, arriving without warning, has
become its own source of dread. She had come to believe that her body was no longer trustworthy,
that it carried information her mind refused to organize.

Her sleep has been poor since the storm. She lies awake listening to the sounds of the neighborhood,
and any sudden noise — a car backfiring, a door slamming somewhere down the hall — brings her fully
upright, heart pounding. She feels safe, she said, only in two places: her bedroom with the door
closed, and her office at the clinic with the blinds drawn. Everywhere else requires a vigilance
that exhausts her. She has largely stopped going out socially. A divorce finalized three years
before the storm had already reduced her social world, and the hurricane has contracted it further.
Friends have stopped calling as often. She knows this and dwells on it — on who she was before, the
woman who hosted dinner parties, who kayaked on weekends, who was known among her patients for her
steadiness and warmth. She spends considerable time in that comparison, and the distance between
that woman and her present self feels, on difficult days, unbridgeable.

What finally brought Marisol to seek help was a session last month in which she was guiding a
patient through gait exercises near the clinic's exterior windows during a rainstorm. She found
herself unable to continue and had to ask a colleague to cover. She had never before been unable to
do her job. What she most hopes for, she said, is to be able to stand near a window in the rain and
simply be a physical therapist again.
