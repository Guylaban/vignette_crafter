Miriam Caldwell is a 52-year-old divorced judge who has presided over a federal district court for
the past eleven years. She came to therapy quietly, having scheduled the appointment under a name
she rarely uses, arriving early and sitting in the corner of the waiting room with her back to the
wall. She described herself, with some difficulty, as a person who had always managed things — who
had, in her words, "never needed this before."

Approximately three years ago, Miriam volunteered during an extended leave of absence to serve with
an international legal aid organization operating in an active conflict zone in Eastern Europe. She
had done humanitarian work before, but nothing that had prepared her for what she encountered during
a particularly prolonged artillery assault on the town where her team was sheltered. For nearly
eighteen hours, she and a group of local aid workers and civilians took cover in the basement of a
damaged municipal building. The air tasted of concrete dust and something metallic she could not
name at the time — she later understood it to be blood from a colleague who had been wounded in the
initial blast. Throughout those hours, she heard the concussive percussion of shelling cycling in
and out, the sound of a child crying somewhere above them, and the particular silence that fell
between impacts — a silence she came to dread more than the noise itself. At one point, two soldiers
in full combat uniform entered the shelter to assess casualties, and Miriam could not determine, in
those first seconds, whether they were there to help or to harm. That moment of not knowing lasted
perhaps four seconds. It has not, in any functional sense, ended.

In the months following her return to the United States, Miriam resumed her position on the bench.
The work itself became an unexpected minefield. Courtroom security officers wear uniforms, and on
more than one occasion she has had to pause mid-proceeding, gripping the edge of the bench beneath
the sight line of the gallery, waiting for the wave of recognition — not of the officer, but of that
doorway in the basement — to pass. She has become meticulous about her composure in court, deploying
a controlled stillness that her clerks have come to read as authority but that she experiences as
containment, a continuous effort to hold something in place that strains against the effort. She has
not told anyone in her professional life what happened.

The anniversary of the assault arrives each year in late autumn, and in the weeks surrounding it
Miriam finds herself sleeping less, eating carefully, and avoiding the particular brand of instant
coffee that was all that was available in the shelter — a taste she encountered again accidentally
at a conference buffet table and that returned her, for a disorienting moment, to a basement floor
in a country far away. When the memories come, they do not arrive as recollections. She hears the
percussion. She smells the dust. The terror is not remembered; it is present, indistinguishable from
the original. She has described sitting in her chambers afterward, certain that her face must be
showing everything, that any clerk who looked at her directly would see what had happened — that she
had been reduced, in some permanent and visible way, by those eighteen hours.

Miriam has come to believe, with a quiet and settled conviction, that the world she now inhabits is
not the same world she lived in before she went. It is a world in which safety is a provisional
condition, revocable without notice. This belief has not made her dramatic or visibly fearful; it
has made her careful in a way that has narrowed her life incrementally. She no longer attends
certain events. She redirects her thoughts when they move toward the memories, sometimes

grown.

What finally brought Miriam to seek help was a moment in court three weeks ago when a uniformed

thread of the proceeding entirely. She is not afraid, she says, of falling apart. She is afraid that
she already has, and that no one has told her.
