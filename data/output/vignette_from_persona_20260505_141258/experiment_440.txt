Rangi Parata is a 56-year-old Māori man, divorced, who has worked as a detective in a mid-sized
American city for nearly two decades. He presented for treatment carrying a PCL-5 score of 62,
describing himself at intake as "finally out of excuses not to come."

The trauma Rangi carries is not from the streets he has worked for most of his adult life. It
originates in a small house in South Auckland, where he spent his childhood in the care of an uncle
who drank heavily and whose rages were unpredictable and methodical in equal measure. The beatings
followed a particular ritual: the smell of something burnt on the stove — scorched food, a forgotten
pan — was often the first signal that the night would turn violent. His uncle would move through the
kitchen with a deliberate, unhurried quality that Rangi came to understand as the most dangerous
kind of calm. Rangi was small, slight for his age, and he learned early that there was no reliable
way to stop what was coming. He was told, repeatedly and in explicit terms, that the violence was
his own fault — that something essential in his character invited it, deserved it, perhaps required
it.

He left New Zealand in his mid-twenties, joined law enforcement, and built a career on a capacity
for controlled vigilance that his colleagues read as professional excellence. For many years he
managed. Then, approximately three years ago, he was assigned to a series of domestic violence cases
involving child victims. The work required him to interview children, photograph injuries, and sit
across from families in cramped apartments where, more than once, something was burning on a stove
in the background. The first time he smelled it — that particular acrid, scorched note — he was in
the middle of taking a statement. The full weight of the Auckland house came back without
transition, without warning, as though the intervening decades had simply been lifted away. He
completed the interview. He did not tell anyone what had happened.

Since then, the smell of anything burnt — a neighbour's cooking, a colleague's forgotten coffee —
can return him to that kitchen with a completeness that leaves him gripping whatever surface is
nearby. He has removed the smoke alarm battery in his apartment twice, replacing it only after a day
or two, and he no longer cooks anything that requires sustained heat. At work, when he encounters
children or adults whose physical smallness or particular colouring recalls the victims from those
cases, something tightens in his chest and his hands begin to shake — a physical reactivity he finds
humiliating in a man of his profession. He has rearranged his caseload where possible to reduce such
encounters, and he has quietly stopped attending the team briefings where case photographs are
reviewed.

When he tries to explain any of this in conversation — to his ex-wife before their divorce was
finalised, to a department counsellor he saw briefly two years ago — he loses the thread entirely.
The account dissolves mid-sentence. What comes instead, when he sits alone with the memories, is not
grief but a rage so intense it frightens him. He has come to believe that the rage itself is
evidence of his deficiency as a person, confirmation of what his uncle told him. He had constructed,
over decades, a set of beliefs about effort and competence and the basic legibility of the world,
and he is certain those beliefs no longer hold. He is certain, too, that the event happened to him
because of who he fundamentally is — not what was done to him, but what he is.

He sleeps badly, keeping a light on and listening. He has withdrawn from the few friendships he
maintained after his divorce, finding the management of other people's company exhausting and, in
some way he cannot fully articulate, unsafe. He avoids wearing the dark, plain clothing his uncle
favoured, a habit so automatic he no longer consciously registers it. When something pleasant
happens — a good meal, a moment of professional satisfaction — he finds the feeling confusing and
briefly unbearable, as though it belongs to someone else.

What finally brought Rangi in was a near-miss: he came close to confronting a colleague who had done
nothing wrong, driven by a physical certainty of threat that he recognised, afterwards, as entirely
disconnected from the room he was standing in. He is most afraid that he will not be able to explain
himself clearly enough to be helped. He is most hoping, quietly and with considerable doubt, that
the person sitting across from him will not confirm what he has long suspected about himself.
