the precise moment a uniformed officer ordered everyone to move toward the platform, the sharp
command cutting through the ordinary noise of the evening commute. Within seconds of complying with
that instruction, the blast detonated near the platform entrance. Elliot was thrown to the ground,
surrounded by chaos, blood, and the screaming of other passengers. He witnessed severe injuries to
people standing only feet from him and, drawing on his medical training in a state of acute shock,
attempted to render aid to victims in the immediate aftermath. He was physically unharmed but
remained at the scene for nearly two hours before emergency services fully secured the area.

In the weeks that followed, Elliot continued working at his hospital, initially telling himself that
returning to routine was the rational response. But the hospital environment, which had once been a
domain of competence and calm, began to feel subtly threatening. When charge nurses issued
instructions during rounds or attending supervisors directed him to alter a treatment plan, Elliot
noticed a visceral resistance — a flash of muscle tension and barely suppressed anger that he could
not fully explain and that alarmed him. He had always been regarded as a measured, collegial
presence on the ward. Now a simple directive from a colleague could leave him irritable for hours,

comments. He began to understand that something had shifted in how he received authority — that
being given orders or instructions, however benign, now carried a charge that his body registered
before his mind could intervene.

to the sounds of the house settling, the distant movement of cars on the street, anything that might
signal an intrusion. His wife, Carolyn, noticed that he had begun checking the door locks multiple
times each evening and that he seemed unable to fully relax in the living room, frequently
positioning himself facing the entrance. He had come to believe, in some not entirely conscious way,
that the safety he had always assumed inside his own home was an illusion he had been naive to
trust. The world, as he now understood it, was genuinely and persistently dangerous — not in a
theoretical sense but in the immediate, physical sense that had been confirmed to him on the
platform that evening. He was also increasingly certain that people were not what they seemed, that
the ordinary social fabric of strangers on a street or colleagues in a corridor concealed intentions
that could not be reliably read. This conviction had made him withdraw from social engagements he
and Carolyn had previously shared, and she had grown quietly worried about the distance that had
opened between them.

Elliot had not ridden a bus since the attack. He drove to the hospital by a route that avoided the
transit district entirely, adding forty minutes to his commute each way. What frightened him most
about the idea of boarding public transport was not precisely the memory of the explosion but the
specific sequence that preceded it — the command to move, the compliance, the detonation — as though
obedience itself had been the mechanism of catastrophe. He had begun to feel that his life, as he
had built and understood it, had been fundamentally altered in a way that could not be undone. The
career he had devoted himself to, the marriage he valued, the sense of himself as a competent and
grounded man — all of it felt precarious in a way it had not before.

What finally brought Elliot to seek help was a conversation with Carolyn in which she told him,
gently but directly, that she felt she was losing him. He arrived at the intake session composed and
articulate, describing his symptoms with clinical precision, but acknowledged that he no longer
trusted his own read of the world — and that this, more than anything, was what he hoped to recover.
