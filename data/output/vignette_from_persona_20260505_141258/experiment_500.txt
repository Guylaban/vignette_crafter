Brigitte Halvorsen is a sixty-year-old midwife of Norwegian descent who has worked in a large
regional hospital in the Pacific Northwest for nearly three decades. She came to the clinic six
weeks ago, referred by her general practitioner after her partner of twelve years expressed alarm at
how profoundly she had withdrawn from ordinary life. Brigitte herself described the referral as
something that had been "forced" on her, though she acknowledged, quietly and with some shame, that
she no longer recognised the person she had become.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Brigitte was present at a chemical cleaning incident on the
maternity ward. A maintenance contractor had applied a concentrated industrial disinfectant to a
storage area adjacent to the birthing suites without adequate ventilation or warning. Brigitte was
midway through a delivery when the fumes began seeping under the door — acrid, sharp, and almost
immediately nauseating. Within minutes she was struggling to breathe, her eyes streaming, her hands
trembling as she tried to continue supporting the labouring mother. She recalled the blood on her
gloves catching the overhead light as she worked, her own vision beginning to blur, uncertain
whether the dizziness she felt would cause her to collapse before the birth was complete. She did
not collapse, but she vomited twice in the corridor immediately afterward and was taken to the
emergency department, where she was treated for respiratory irritation and kept for observation. She
returned to work two weeks later, believing herself to be recovered.

What followed was not recovery. In the months after the incident, Brigitte found that the sensory
landscape of her work had become treacherous. The smell of cleaning products could stop her in a
corridor. The sight of blood — unavoidable in her profession — no longer registered as the familiar,
manageable clinical reality it had been for thirty years, but arrived instead with a sudden,
vertiginous wrongness she could not explain or control. Any feeling of physical unwellness, however
minor — a headache, a light stomach — would spiral into something larger, as though her body were
announcing that something catastrophic was beginning again. She began arriving at work already
braced, scanning the ward before she removed her coat.

Outside the hospital, Brigitte had gradually reorganised her life around a principle she could not
quite articulate but acted on consistently: it was safer not to want anything. She had stopped
making plans — for holidays she and her partner had discussed, for a retirement gathering her
colleagues had proposed, for anything that required imagining a future she was no longer confident
would resemble anything she could bear. She had come to believe, in a way that felt less like a
thought than a settled fact, that nothing in her life held any real meaning anymore — not the work
she had once loved, not her relationship, not the garden she had tended for fifteen years and now
left largely untended. She described this not with grief but with a kind of flat certainty that
frightened her partner more than her distress would have.

She had also, without quite deciding to, become someone who agreed with everyone. In meetings at
work, with her partner at home, even with her GP, she found herself accommodating every preference
but her own, as though conflict of any kind had become a threat she needed to neutralise. She was
certain this was weakness — that a stronger woman, a better midwife, would have shaken off the
incident and continued. This conviction sat alongside a deeper disorientation: she no longer felt
she knew who she was. The Brigitte who had been confident, purposeful, and at home in a delivery
room seemed to belong to someone else's history.

In therapy, she has been careful and guarded. After the first session she spent several days
deliberately not thinking about anything that had been raised, a habit she now names without
apology. What finally brought her was a moment three months ago when she stood at the bedside of a
woman in labour, felt the now-familiar wave of nausea rise in her, and thought, with terrifying
clarity, that she could not do this anymore. What she hopes for, she says, is to feel like herself
again. What she fears most is that there is no longer a self to return to.
