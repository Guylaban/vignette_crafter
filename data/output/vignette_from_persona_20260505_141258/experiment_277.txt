Kwame Asante is a 47-year-old single African-American man who works as a park ranger in a large
national forest in the Pacific Northwest. He presents for his first formal psychological evaluation
reporting a PCL-5 score of 74, describing years of escalating distress that he has, until now,
managed largely in silence.

Kwame was born in Ghana and emigrated to the United States in his late twenties. In his mid-
thirties, he returned to West Africa to visit extended family during a period of civil unrest that
he had believed, based on news reports, was winding down. Within days of his arrival, the region he
was staying in was overtaken by militia activity. For approximately three weeks, Kwame was
effectively trapped in a village that came under repeated attack. The most catastrophic of these
attacks involved the deliberate burning of homes and a small community building where civilians had
gathered. Kwame survived by hiding in a drainage culvert while the fire consumed the structure
nearby. He could hear voices — some he recognised — and he did not emerge. He has never spoken
publicly about what he did or did not do in those hours. When he eventually reached safety, several
people he had known, including a cousin he had grown up with, had not.

In the months after returning to the United States, Kwame noticed changes he initially attributed to
exhaustion and grief. He took the park ranger position partly because it felt like a retreat — a
place of quiet and purpose, away from cities and crowds. For a time, it seemed to help. But the
forest itself began to work against him. The smell of smoke from controlled burns conducted by his
own agency, the sight of a campfire in a clearing, the orange light of late afternoon filtering
through pine trees — these sensations arrived not as reminders but as something more immediate and
disorienting, as though the boundary between the forest in Oregon and the village in Ghana had
become unreliable. He has found himself frozen on the trail, unable to account for lost minutes,
returning to his truck without completing his patrol. He has said nothing to his supervisors,
convinced that any acknowledgment of difficulty would be read as incompetence. He had come to
believe that being corrected — even minor feedback about a patrol report or a procedural matter —
confirmed something he feared was already true: that he was failing, that he was not the person he
had once been.

The uniform worn by a county sheriff's deputy who occasionally coordinates with the park service has
become something Kwame plans his schedule around avoiding. He cannot fully articulate why, except
that the sight of it produces a physical response he finds humiliating and uncontrollable. He has
reorganised his shift requests accordingly, and a colleague has noticed and made comments that Kwame
deflected with vague excuses.

His nights are largely sleepless. He lies in his cabin on the park grounds — a living situation that
once felt like a privilege — unable to let his body settle, listening to sounds in the trees with a
quality of attention that exhausts him. When a supply delivery runs late, or when a radio check-in
is not returned promptly, he is certain something has gone wrong in a way that cannot be recovered
from. He has begun drinking most evenings, not to feel better, he says, but to get his body to stop
waiting.

He has no close relationships remaining. Over the past two years, contact with friends and family —
both in the United States and in Ghana — has dwindled to nothing. He did not end these relationships
dramatically; he simply stopped responding, and eventually people stopped reaching out. He
experiences this not as relief but as confirmation of something he already suspected: that he exists
at a remove from ordinary human life that cannot be bridged. What troubles him most, and what he
returns to in the intake session with a kind of exhausted precision, is not the sleeplessness or the
drinking or even the fire — it is the memory of the culvert, and the question of what kind of man
stays hidden. He has come seeking help not because he believes recovery is possible, but because he
is no longer certain he can keep doing his job, and the forest — the one place that once felt like
his — has stopped feeling like his.
