Rajan is a 64-year-old South Asian man who emigrated from India in his late twenties and has spent
the better part of three decades working in a large distribution warehouse on the outskirts of a
mid-sized Midwestern city. He lives with his wife of thirty-eight years, and until recently their
life together had settled into the quiet rhythms of late middle age. He presented for psychological
assessment after his wife, frightened by months of watching him deteriorate, made the appointment on
his behalf and accompanied him to the intake session.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Rajan was working a closing shift at the warehouse — a shift that
typically ended around 10 p.m. — when he was ambushed in the parking lot by two men who forced him
at knifepoint into the back of a van. He was held for what he estimates was between six and eight
hours, blindfolded, his wrists bound, lying on the floor of the vehicle as it moved and stopped and
moved again. At one point his captors, apparently to intimidate him into silence, placed what he
later learned was a live snake across his chest. He could feel its weight and movement in the dark.
He could not see. He could not move his hands. He did not know whether he would be released or
killed. He was eventually left, still bound, in a field and found himself walking toward distant
lights in the early hours of the morning.

In the weeks that followed, Rajan returned to work because he believed he had no choice — the family
needed his income, and he told himself the incident was over. But the parking lot, the closing
shift, the moment each evening when the warehouse clock approached 10 p.m. — these became
unbearable. Something in him would lock into a state of heightened alertness as that hour neared,
his eyes scanning the loading bays and the rows of shelving, his body braced. He began turning
frequently to look behind him as he moved through the warehouse aisles, certain at some level that
someone was following. Colleagues noticed. His supervisor asked once, gently, if he was all right.
Rajan said yes, and then said it again, and then asked the supervisor to confirm it — was he okay?
Did he look okay? He found himself repeating these questions to his wife each evening, and to his
adult daughter on the phone, unable to hold onto any reassurance for more than an hour before the
doubt returned.

Nights became the worst of it. The blindfold, the darkness, the snake on his chest — these had all
belonged to the dark, and the dark had never released them. Rajan began delaying sleep for as long
as possible, sitting in the lit kitchen until two or three in the morning, telling his wife he was
not tired. When he did sleep, waking in the darkened bedroom produced an immediate, full-body alarm
that he could not reason his way out of. He began sleeping with the bathroom light on, then with the
overhead light on, a habit that disturbed his wife's sleep and introduced a quiet tension into their
marriage that neither of them had words for yet. He took to scanning his arms and hands each
morning, pressing his fingers along his torso, checking for something he could not name — some sign
that harm had already arrived without his noticing.

Rajan had come to believe, with a quiet and terrible certainty, that he had no power to prevent bad
things from happening to him. The memory of lying helpless in that van, unable to move, unable to
see, unable to do anything at all, had settled into something that felt less like a memory and more
like a permanent fact about who he was. He avoided thinking about the event directly because he was
certain that if he allowed it in fully, he would not survive it — that something in him would simply
give way. On the worst nights, he found himself wondering whether his life, diminished as it had
become, was worth continuing to live.

What finally brought him in was a photograph in a colleague's phone — a casual image of a snake
shared as a joke — that left him shaking and unable to speak for nearly an hour. His wife, who had
been patient and frightened in equal measure for fourteen months, told him she could not watch him
disappear any further. He came, he said, because she asked him to. What he hoped for, quietly, was
to feel safe in the dark again.
