Priya Krishnamurthy is an eighty-year-old divorced South Asian woman who has practiced dentistry for
over five decades, most recently running a part-time consulting practice in a mid-sized American
city. She presents for psychological support following a terrorist attack she survived approximately
fourteen months ago, and her PCL-5 score of 40 places her in the clinically significant range for
PTSD symptomatology.

The attack occurred at a crowded civic ceremony Priya had attended with colleagues from a local
dental association. A device was detonated in an adjacent building; Priya was inside the main hall
when the blast occurred, and the room rapidly filled with acrid smoke that burned her throat and
obscured the exits. In the chaos that followed, a security officer physically restrained her from
moving toward what she believed was the nearest door, holding her arm firmly and directing the crowd
in a different direction. She could hear sirens converging from all directions — first distant, then
overwhelming — while she stood immobilised, unable to see clearly through the smoke, uncertain
whether a second device might detonate. She was not physically injured, but she remained pinned
against a wall by the press of the crowd and the officer's grip for what felt, to her, like an
unbearable length of time before she was led to safety.

In the weeks that followed, Priya found that the world had reorganised itself around that afternoon.
The smell of smoke — from a neighbour's fireplace, from a patient's clothing, from the brief char of
a candle — would arrive without warning and carry her back into that hall with a vividness that felt
less like memory and more like recurrence. Sirens, which in a dental practice setting are not
uncommon given the clinic's proximity to a fire station, became nearly intolerable. On more than one
occasion she stopped mid-procedure, hands trembling, when an emergency vehicle passed outside. She
had come to believe, with quiet but firm conviction, that her body was detecting something real each
time — that the sirens and the smoke smell were signals, not echoes. What frightened her most was
not the memory itself but the certainty that the danger was not over.

Priya's consulting work brought its own complications. The examination chair places her patients in
a position of physical vulnerability, but it is Priya herself who has begun to feel trapped. She is
certain that rooms in which she cannot quickly orient herself toward an exit are unsafe, and she now
arrives early to every clinical session to identify the nearest door and position herself
accordingly. During patient appointments she works with one eye on the hallway. She has not returned
to the civic centre district where the attack occurred, rerouting her commute by nearly twenty
minutes each day to avoid passing within several blocks of the site. What began as a conscious
decision has solidified into something that feels less like a choice and more like a physical
impossibility — the idea of driving that route produces a tightening in her chest she interprets as
confirmation that the area remains dangerous.

The vigilance has accumulated costs. Colleagues have noticed that Priya seems distracted during case
consultations, and one junior associate gently asked whether she was well. She deflected the
question, but the exchange stayed with her. Over the months since the attack, she has declined two
professional invitations that would have required her to attend large indoor gatherings, and a
friendship with a longtime colleague has quietly attenuated because the colleague works near the
site she avoids. Priya has not named any of this as loss, exactly — she has framed each withdrawal
as reasonable precaution — but she is aware, in a way she finds difficult to articulate, that her
world has grown smaller.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment at her clinic when a patient's cologne carried a
faint smoky undertone and she found herself unable to complete the appointment, excusing herself to
the hallway and standing there for several minutes before she could re-enter the room. She is eighty
years old, she told the intake coordinator, and she has worked her entire life. She is not ready to
stop. What she hopes for, most simply, is to be able to walk back into her own operatory without
scanning the ceiling for smoke.
