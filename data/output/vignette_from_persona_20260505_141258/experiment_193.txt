Marguerite Calloway is a 68-year-old retired schoolteacher who presented for her initial intake
following the sudden death of her adult son, Daniel, fourteen months ago. Daniel, who was 44 at the
time, collapsed at a family barbecue in the backyard of the home he had shared with Marguerite and
her husband, Gerald, for the past two years while he managed a difficult divorce. He was gone before
the ambulance arrived. Marguerite had been the first to reach him, kneeling on the grass beside the
picnic table where he had been laughing only moments before, calling his name while the other guests
stood frozen. The cardiac event was entirely without warning. There had been no prior diagnosis, no
visible distress, nothing she could have read or acted on — and yet she had come to believe, with a
certainty that felt immovable, that she had failed him. She had been a mother. She should have known
something was wrong. She should have insisted he see a cardiologist after the mild chest complaint
he had mentioned in passing the previous winter. That she had not was, in her mind, an unforgivable
lapse.

In the weeks following Daniel's death, Marguerite's body seemed to have registered a threat that her
conscious mind could not fully articulate. She described a persistent physical tension — a tautness
across her shoulders and jaw, a readiness in her limbs — that did not ease even when she was sitting
quietly in her own living room. She had not been able to relax in the way she once could, not during
television programmes she used to enjoy, not during the long evenings with Gerald, not even during
sleep. Her body appeared to have settled into a state of vigilance that no amount of stillness could
interrupt, and strong surges of emotion — fear, horror, guilt, and something she named as shame —
arose unpredictably and with a force that frightened her.

What she had done with all of this, steadily and with considerable discipline, was keep moving. She
filled her days with tasks: reorganising cupboards, volunteering at the local library two mornings a
week, tending to a garden that had never been so meticulously kept. Busyness had become the
architecture of her survival. But alongside this relentless activity, she had quietly withdrawn from
the social world she and Gerald had occupied together for decades. The neighbours who had attended
that barbecue, the couples from their church group, the women she had met with monthly for a book
club — she had stopped seeing all of them. The thought of sitting across from someone who had been
in that backyard, who had witnessed what happened, who might look at her with pity or, worse, with
the same silent question she asked herself, was something she could not face. Over the months,
invitations had tapered off, as they do when they are consistently declined, and Marguerite had not
tried to reverse this. Gerald attended church alone now. He had stopped asking her to come.

What frightened her most was not the grief itself but what the withdrawal had confirmed to her about
her place in the world. She had come to feel that she no longer belonged anywhere — not among the
friends who had known her as Daniel's mother and a woman of warmth and competence, not among other
bereaved parents whose losses felt different from hers, not even fully within her marriage, where
Gerald's quiet steadiness seemed to belong to a version of life she could no longer access. She felt
set apart, as though the afternoon in the backyard had moved her permanently to a different side of
something, and the people on the other side could not see it.

It was Gerald who finally persuaded her to seek help, after she declined to attend their
granddaughter's birthday party — an event she had previously circled on the calendar with delight.
Sitting in the intake session, Marguerite was composed and precise, a woman long accustomed to
managing herself in front of others. What she said she most hoped for was to stop feeling as though
she no longer had a place among the living. What she most feared was that she was right.
