Tariq Mansour is a 37-year-old Lebanese American firefighter who has been married for nine years and
has two school-aged children. He presented for psychological assessment describing a level of
distress that had become, in his own words, "impossible to keep hidden anymore." His PCL-5 score of
62 places him well within the severe range, and the picture that emerged across the intake and first
two sessions was one of a man whose sense of himself had been profoundly destabilized by an
experience he had never anticipated and did not have a framework to understand.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Tariq began noticing the same man near his home — first outside
the grocery store, then parked across from his children's school, then on the street corner he
passed each morning when he rode his bicycle to the firehouse. The man never spoke to him. What made
it unbearable, Tariq explained, was the deliberateness of it: the way the figure would hold eye
contact just long enough before looking away, the way he seemed to know Tariq's routes and rhythms
with an intimacy that felt like a violation. Over several weeks the encounters escalated — a note
left under his bicycle seat, a photograph of his front door slipped beneath it, a phone call to the
firehouse in which the caller said nothing and hung up. Tariq reported the situation to police, but
the man was never identified, and the contact stopped as abruptly as it had begun. There was no
arrest, no explanation, no closure — only silence, which Tariq found he could not trust.

In the months that followed, the bicycle became something he could not bring himself to ride. It sat
in the garage where he had once kept it by the door, and he began driving to the firehouse instead,
telling his wife only that the bike needed repairs. The route he had taken for six years — past the
park, along the residential blocks where the man had appeared — became a stretch of road he avoided
entirely, adding fifteen minutes to his commute. His wife noticed the detour but accepted his vague
explanation. What he did not tell her was that riding the bicycle, or even walking those particular
streets, could collapse the distance between the present and those weeks entirely, without warning.
A glimpse of a man in a dark jacket standing near a parked car, or the particular quality of
footsteps behind him on pavement, could return him instantly and completely to the feeling of being
watched — not as a memory he could observe from a distance, but as something happening now, in his
body, with full force. When he tried to describe these episodes to himself or to his wife, the words
simply were not there. He knew what had occurred. He could not say it.

At the firehouse, the changes accumulated in ways that troubled him. He had always been someone his
crew relied on for composure in chaotic environments, but he now found himself scanning every new
building they entered for exits before anything else, cataloguing the positions of the other men on
scene, watching civilian faces for something he could not name but was certain he would recognize.
Loud sounds — a door slamming in the bay, the sudden activation of an alarm during a drill —
produced a spike of alertness that took minutes to subside. His lieutenant had not said anything
directly, but Tariq had intercepted the look exchanged between two colleagues after he flinched at a
backfire in the station parking lot, and the shame of that moment sat with him for days.

What frightened Tariq most was not the hypervigilance itself but what he had come to believe it
meant. He was certain that something had broken in him permanently, that the man who had ridden his
bicycle to work and felt easy in his own neighborhood was gone and would not return. A firefighter,
he reasoned privately, does not fall apart because someone followed him. He had walked into burning
buildings. He had carried people out. The fact that this had undone him in ways those experiences
had not led him to a conclusion he found deeply shameful: that he was weak, that his reactions were
evidence of a mind coming apart at its seams. It was this fear — not of the stalker, but of himself
— that finally brought him through the door.
