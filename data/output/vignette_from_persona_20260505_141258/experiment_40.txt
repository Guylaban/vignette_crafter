Priya Nair is a 66-year-old South Asian woman who has worked as a midwife for nearly four decades,
most recently in a busy urban hospital where she supervises a team of younger practitioners and
takes on more shifts than her contract requires. She is married, describes her husband as
supportive, and came to the clinic at his gentle but persistent urging after he told her, quietly,
that he no longer recognized the woman he had known before she deployed.

Priya served as a civilian medical contractor attached to a military installation overseas
approximately three years ago, providing obstetric care to the families of military personnel. The
posting lasted eight months. During the sixth month, she was working a night shift in the base
clinic when a group of armed men entered the facility. She was alone in a side corridor when two of
them found her. They were in uniform — not the uniforms she had come to recognize, but close enough
that for a fraction of a second she did not understand what was happening. They held her at gunpoint
for what she estimates was forty minutes, during which time she was interrogated about the layout of
the facility, watched continuously, and forbidden to move. One of the men stood directly behind her
for long stretches so that she could not see him. She was ultimately unharmed in the physical sense,
but two colleagues were injured elsewhere in the building, and she did not know this until it was
over. She was evacuated within days and returned to the United States.

In the weeks that followed, Priya told almost no one what had happened. She returned to her hospital
position within a month, reasoning that the work would steady her. And in some narrow sense it did —
the delivery suite was loud and purposeful and full of women, and when she was occupied she could
almost outrun what was gathering inside her. She began adding shifts, arriving before anyone else,
staying after handover, volunteering for weekend cover. Her colleagues noticed and attributed it to
dedication. What they did not see was that she was arranging her days so that there would always be
someone else in the room with her, always movement and noise and the particular busyness of a ward
that made it harder for her mind to settle into what it was trying to show her.

The sensation of being watched had become something she carried in her body. Walking down a hospital
corridor, she would feel it — a prickling certainty that someone behind her was tracking her
movements — and she would turn, or slow her pace, or find a reason to stop and let others pass. She
had come to believe, in a way she could not fully articulate, that the moment she stopped scanning
was the moment something would happen. In the maternity unit this vigilance was manageable; the
environment was familiar and predominantly female. But when a security guard in uniform appeared on
the ward — a routine presence she had never previously noticed — Priya found herself rerouting her
path through the unit to avoid passing him. She began doing this every shift, adding minutes to her
rounds, and eventually she requested that the hospital administration change which guard was
assigned to her floor. She gave a vague reason about staffing flow. No one questioned it.

At home, the inability to relax had become the texture of every evening. She sat with her husband
but could not settle into the chair. A sudden movement — his hand reaching across the table, the dog
shifting in its sleep — would bring her upright, her attention snapping toward it before she could
stop herself. She had not been alone with any man other than her husband in three years, arranging
arrivals and departures so that she was never the last person in a room, never in a lift with a male
colleague, never waiting alone in a space where a man might enter.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment at work two months ago. A junior male colleague
entered the break room while she was there alone, and Priya left so abruptly that she spilled her
tea and said nothing to explain herself. She saw the confusion on his face and, walking back to the
ward, understood that she was losing something she had spent her life building. She came to the
clinic hoping, above everything else, to be able to stand still again — to stop watching the door.
