Kofi Mensah is a 68-year-old widowed Ghanaian-American man who has spent the better part of five
decades moving through the world as a dancer and dance instructor, his body the instrument through
which he understood himself and communicated with others. He came to the clinic carrying a PCL-5
score of 67, referred by his primary care physician after months of what Kofi described, with
characteristic understatement, as "not being myself."

Fourteen months ago, Kofi was driving home from an evening rehearsal when a commercial delivery
truck ran a red light at speed and struck the driver's side of his sedan. The impact spun the car
across two lanes and into a concrete median. Kofi was conscious throughout — he remembers the shriek
of tires, the shattering of glass, and then a long, disorienting stillness in which he could not
determine whether the car was still moving or whether time itself had stopped. Emergency services
took nearly forty minutes to arrive. He waited alone, pinned at the hip by the crumpled door, unable
to reach his phone, watching other vehicles slow and then continue past him. He had come to believe,
in those forty minutes, that no one was coming — that the systems and people one is supposed to rely
on in a crisis simply would not show up. When paramedics finally reached him, he was told he was
fortunate. He did not feel fortunate.

The physical injuries healed. What did not resolve was Kofi's relationship to his own body and to
the world outside his apartment. He had sustained a pelvic fracture that required months of
rehabilitation, and even after his orthopedist cleared him to return to movement, he found he could
not trust what his body told him. A slight ache in his hip became, in his mind, evidence of
structural failure he could not see. He was certain, in a way he could not argue himself out of,
that his body would betray him without warning — that it had already done so once and would do so
again. For a man whose entire professional identity rested on physical precision and reliability,
this conviction was devastating. He has not returned to teaching. The studio where he has instructed
West African and contemporary dance for over two decades has been managed in his absence by a junior
colleague, and Kofi has not walked through its doors since the accident. His students have stopped
reaching out, assuming he has retired.

When he does venture to describe the collision — to his physician, briefly, or to himself in the
sleepless hours before dawn — the account is fragmentary and unsatisfying. He cannot construct a
coherent narrative of what happened; the event exists in his memory as a series of disconnected
impressions rather than a story with a beginning, middle, and end. When he tries deliberately to
recall the sequence, the details recede rather than sharpen, leaving him with the unnerving sense
that something important is missing and that he cannot locate it. What he does experience without
effort are sensory intrusions: the sound of tires on wet pavement, the particular quality of a truck
engine at speed. Unexpected loud sounds — a car backfiring, a door slamming in the corridor of his
building — send him into a state of acute alertness from which it takes him a long time to return.

Over the months, Kofi's world has contracted. He no longer goes out unless necessary, and he has
gradually stopped seeing the friends and fellow artists he once socialised with regularly, people
who now represent, in his mind, a version of himself he cannot access. At home, he positions himself
with his back to the wall when he sits, needing to see the full room before he can settle. When a
delivery runs late or an appointment time passes without contact, he becomes convinced that
something has gone wrong — that delay itself is a warning. He began a course of trauma-focused
therapy several weeks ago but has found himself unable to engage with the material between sessions,
steering his thoughts away from anything touched on in the room.

What finally brought him to seek help was simpler and more painful than any clinical threshold: a
former student sent him a video of a performance, and he watched it for thirty seconds before
turning it off. He is most afraid that the person he was — the dancer, the teacher, the man his late
wife knew — is no longer retrievable. He is hoping, cautiously, to be proven wrong.
