Linh Tran is a 25-year-old Vietnamese American woman who works as a database administrator at a mid-
sized logistics firm. She is married and was referred for psychological assessment following a
disclosure made to her primary care physician during a routine appointment. Her PCL-5 score of 74
places her well within the severe range, and the clinical picture that has emerged across the intake
and early sessions is one of a young woman whose interior life has been profoundly reorganised
around a single event that occurred fourteen months ago.

Linh was a passenger in a car driven by a close friend, Minh, when he ran a red light at speed on a
rain-slicked intersection. A delivery truck struck the passenger side with enough force to roll the
vehicle. Linh sustained a fractured collarbone and lacerations to her face and arms; Minh was
hospitalised for several weeks with more serious injuries. In the immediate chaos after the
collision, Linh was conscious but pinned, and she has described knowing — with terrible clarity —
that something was deeply wrong with Minh in the seat beside her. She could hear him but could not
reach him. She had urged him to drive that evening rather than call a rideshare, and she had not
insisted they slow down when she noticed his speed increasing in the rain. These details have
settled into her as facts of causation rather than circumstance. She has come to believe, without
qualification, that the crash happened because of choices she made and failed to make, and that she
will never fully forgive herself for not doing more in the moments before and during the collision.

In the weeks that followed, Linh found herself unable to trust the account anyone offered her — that
the accident was not her fault, that Minh did not blame her, that she had done what she could. She
had come to understand other people's reassurances as either ignorance or kindness masking judgment,
and she stopped confiding in friends. One by one, those friendships have thinned. She no longer sees
the small group of women she used to meet for dinner on weekends, partly because she does not
believe she deserves the comfort of their company, and partly because she cannot bear the
unpredictability of what conversation might surface. Arguments of any kind — even minor
disagreements overheard between colleagues in the open-plan office where she works — leave her
flooded and unable to concentrate for the remainder of the day. She has taken to wearing headphones
and keeping her screen angled away from the room, telling herself it improves her focus, though what
she is managing is the constant low-level vigilance against conflict.

At work, her role requires her to run regular system stress tests, processes that push the firm's
servers to capacity and generate alerts and error cascades across her monitors. She has begun
delegating these tasks to a junior colleague without explanation, because sitting through them —
watching systems strain toward failure — produces a physical tightening in her chest that she cannot
tolerate. The junior colleague has begun to notice, and Linh is aware that her professional
reputation for precision and reliability is quietly eroding.

When her husband's name, or any name that sounds like Minh's, is spoken aloud in her presence, she
experiences an intrusive return to the inside of the car. She has not told her husband the extent of
what she is experiencing, because she does not trust that he would not eventually use it against
her. The uncertainty of not knowing how people will respond has become one of the most disabling
features of her daily life — she rehearses conversations before having them and avoids initiating
any exchange whose outcome she cannot predict.

On several occasions over the past four months, Linh has hurt herself physically — pressing her
nails into her forearm or striking her hand against a hard surface — in order to interrupt the
intrusive thinking when it becomes overwhelming. She described this with matter-of-fact brevity in
session, as though it were a practical solution rather than a symptom.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was her husband finding marks on her arm and asking,
directly, what was happening. She could not produce a lie she believed he would accept, and the
uncertainty of what would happen if she said nothing frightened her more than the prospect of
disclosure. What she most hopes for, she said quietly, is to stop feeling responsible for something
she cannot change. What she most fears is that a therapist will confirm what she already suspects —
that she is.
