background noise of a toddler playing had simply stopped. By the time she reached the living room,
Minh was unresponsive on the floor. The ambulance ride, the fluorescent corridors, the sharp smell
of antiseptic that met her as she was ushered through the hospital doors, the sounds of monitors and
the squeal of wheels on linoleum — all of it compressed into a period of hours during which she was
told, more than once, that her grandson might not survive. He did survive, after several days in
intensive care, and he has since recovered fully. Linh has not.

In the weeks following Minh's hospitalization, Linh began to notice that certain moments pulled her
back into that afternoon with a force she could not resist. Driving to work, the sound of a car
engine idling or accelerating at a particular pitch would arrive in her chest before it arrived in
her mind, and she would find herself gripping the steering wheel with the same helpless urgency she
had felt waiting for the ambulance. Walking through the office building, the faint antiseptic smell

complete that she would have to stand still and wait for it to pass. Most disorienting of all was
silence. At home, if her husband stepped out and the house fell quiet, or at the office when the
open-plan floor emptied during lunch, the absence of sound became something almost physical — she
had come to believe, with a certainty she could not argue herself out of, that silence meant
something terrible was already happening just out of her sight.

garage, timing her entrance to avoid the smell in the corridors. She has rearranged her schedule so
that she attends fewer in-person meetings, telling colleagues she is managing her workload more
