she described as a tiredness she could no longer explain away — a weight that had been building
quietly for decades and had finally, in recent months, become impossible to set down.

remembered most vividly, when she allowed herself to remember it at all, was the particular quality
of not knowing where he was in the house — listening for footsteps, positioning herself near
doorways, keeping the wall at her back whenever she sat at the table so that no one could come up
behind her. She learned early that visibility meant survival: if she could see the room, she could
read it.

For much of her adult life, Marisol managed to keep those years at a careful distance. She built a
career that gave her structure and purpose. She raised two children largely on her own after her
marriage ended in her thirties. She was known at the clinic as exceptionally competent and quietly
self-contained. But in the past year, following the death of a close colleague and a subsequent
restructuring at work that required her to take on a larger caseload with unfamiliar staff,
something began to shift.

Fragments of memory started surfacing without invitation — a flash of a particular hallway, the
sound of a belt, the specific texture of linoleum under her palms — and then vanishing before she
could hold them. When she tried to sit with what had happened to her as a child, she found herself
able to narrate events in a flat, almost clinical voice, as though recounting something that had
befallen someone else entirely. The story felt so extreme, so outside the bounds of what she
believed her life had actually contained, that she sometimes doubted her own history. It was hard to
believe it actually happened to me, she told her therapist in their second session. She did not say
this with distress. She said it with the same careful neutrality she might use to describe a
patient's imaging results.

felt a surge of alarm so acute that she had to excuse herself to the corridor. She had come to
believe, without quite articulating it, that strangers approaching her in ordinary situations
represented a genuine and immediate threat. The belief was not rational to her in daylight; it was
simply true in her body.

her apartment with a thoroughness that felt necessary rather than optional, rearranging cupboards

What finally brought her to seek help was a conversation with her younger daughter, who told her

stop waking up afraid in a home she had spent thirty years making safe.
