Priya Mehta is a 23-year-old South Asian woman working as a UX designer at a mid-sized tech firm in
the Seattle area. She has been married for just over a year, and by most outward measures her life
had been moving in a direction she had worked hard to reach. That changed on a Tuesday evening in
late autumn, eight months before she first walked into a therapist's office.

Priya had been driving home from a client presentation, tired but satisfied, navigating a four-lane
road she used almost every day. At an intersection where the light had just turned green, she pulled
forward and struck a pedestrian — a young woman, perhaps a few years older than herself, who had
stepped off the curb against the signal. The impact was sudden and total. Priya remembers the sound
before she remembers anything visual: a dull, heavy thud against the front of her car, followed by
the sight of a figure crumpling onto the asphalt. The woman survived but sustained a serious leg
fracture and a traumatic brain injury that required months of rehabilitation. Priya was not cited —
witnesses confirmed the pedestrian had crossed against the light — but the legal exoneration did
nothing to settle what had taken root inside her.

In the weeks that followed, Priya stopped driving entirely. Her husband began dropping her at the
office each morning, and she arranged rides home through apps or asked colleagues for favours,
offering explanations about her car being in for repairs that eventually ran out of credibility.
Even as a passenger, she found herself gripping the door handle at intersections, scanning
crosswalks with an attention that exhausted her. The commute that had once been unremarkable became
something she had to survive.

At work, the difficulty compounded in ways she had not anticipated. As a UX designer, Priya spends
her days building interfaces meant to be intuitive and safe — systems where a single design flaw can
misdirect a user, sometimes with serious consequences. She had always found meaning in that
responsibility, but now the language of her own profession had become unbearable. Team discussions
about user error, about failure states, about what happens when someone does something unexpected —
all of it pressed against the same raw place. She had come to believe that her attentiveness at work
was not skill but proof of how dangerous inattention could be, and she moved through each project
with a vigilance that her colleagues had begun to notice, though they could not name what they were
seeing.

Outside the office, she found herself watching strangers. A woman with a similar build to the
pedestrian, a particular shade of dark coat, someone walking with a slight hesitation at a curb —
these would stop Priya mid-sentence, mid-breath. She was certain, in those moments, that she was
seeing the woman she had hit, even when she knew rationally that she was not. The certainty was not
a thought so much as a physical event, a lurch of recognition that left her shaken for the rest of
the day.

She had tried, with considerable effort, not to think about the accident at all. She had developed a
practised redirection — filling silences with podcasts, keeping her evenings scheduled so tightly
that there was no space for the images to surface. And when they did surface anyway, she would turn
her attention immediately and forcefully elsewhere, a habit her husband had begun to notice in the
way she would go quiet and then abruptly change the subject, or leave a room without explanation.
What she returned to, despite her efforts, was not the moment of impact but the moments before it —
the specific, iterative question of what she could have done differently. If she had left the office
ten minutes later. If she had taken the other route. If she had been less tired. She rehearsed these
alternatives not to find an answer but because she could not stop, and the rehearsal left her no
closer to anything that felt like resolution.

It was her husband who finally named what she had been unwilling to: that she was not managing, that
what had begun as shock had not lifted, and that the life they had been building together was
quietly contracting around her fear. Priya came to therapy not because she believed it would help,
but because she had run out of ways to convince herself she was fine. What she was most afraid of,
she said in their first session, was that she would never be able to get back behind the wheel — and
that this was simply what she deserved.
