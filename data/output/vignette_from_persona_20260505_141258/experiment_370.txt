Callum is a 19-year-old of Irish descent who works as a counselor at a summer residential camp in
rural Vermont, a role he had accepted with genuine enthusiasm the previous spring, before his world
contracted around a hospital bed. He is currently in a relationship, though he describes it with a
flatness that seems to pain him, and he came to the intake appointment wearing a hoodie despite the
warmth of the day, his eyes moving toward the door more than once before he settled into the chair.

Approximately eight months ago, Callum developed what he initially dismissed as a persistent flu.
Within days he was admitted to the ICU with bacterial meningitis. He was nineteen years old,
entirely healthy the week before, and suddenly he was lying beneath fluorescent lights while nurses
threaded IV lines into both arms and a physician performed a lumbar puncture that he was awake for,
unable to move, staring at a ceiling tile while he felt pressure and heard the clinical shorthand
passing over him. The smell in that room — antiseptic, recycled air, something underneath it he
could not name — surrounded him for eleven days. He was told at one point that the next forty-eight
hours were uncertain. He did not tell his parents that he had heard this. He lay still and waited.

He was discharged with a full recovery on paper. What followed was less legible. Back at home, and
then at camp when the summer began, Callum found that certain moments arrived without warning and
hit him with a force he could not account for. A fellow counselor received a flu shot at the camp
infirmary, and Callum, who happened to be passing the door, saw the needle and felt his body respond
before his mind could intervene — heart rate spiking, hands going cold, a wave of nausea that sent
him to sit outside for twenty minutes. He told no one. The camp infirmary, with its faint chemical
smell of antiseptic wipes and rubbing alcohol, became a building he routed his daily walks around.
When a camper needed to be examined by the visiting nurse and Callum was asked to accompany him, he
fabricated a scheduling conflict rather than step through that door.

He had come to believe, with a certainty that felt physiological rather than chosen, that his body
could not be trusted. It had collapsed once without warning, and it would do so again. What
frightened him most was not the memory of pain but the sense that the machinery of his own survival
was unreliable, operating on rules he had no access to. His body kept mounting alarms — the startle
when a camper ran up behind him on the trail, the full-body jolt that followed unexpected movement
nearby — and these reactions felt to him less like symptoms than like evidence. Evidence that
something was still wrong, that the danger had not passed the way the discharge papers claimed.

At night he stayed up long past the camp's quiet hours, scrolling or reading or simply sitting
outside, because lying in the dark meant the thoughts arrived unmediated. Sleep, when it finally
came, was shallow and broken. He had begun to notice, with something approaching dread, that he no
longer felt the satisfaction in the work he had loved. He had taken this job because he wanted to
help young people; now he moved through the days feeling as though meaning had been drained from the
activities that used to constitute his sense of himself. Nothing in his life felt like it mattered
in the way it once had.

He had tried, briefly, to speak to a counselor at his college's health center before the summer.
After one session in which the therapist asked him to describe what he remembered, he spent the
following week actively pushing away anything the session had surfaced, a habit that became
reflexive. He stopped scheduling appointments.

What finally brought him in was his girlfriend. She told him, quietly and without ultimatum, that
she felt she was losing him. He did not argue. He came to the intake hoping, he said, to feel like
himself again — and afraid, underneath that, that the self he was hoping to recover no longer
existed.
