Ernesto Villanueva is a 75-year-old married Latino man who has worked for decades as a QA Engineer,
a career built on the principle that careful, systematic attention to detail can prevent failure
before it occurs. He presented for psychological evaluation approximately eight months after a
wildfire swept through the rural foothills community where he and his wife have lived since his
retirement. He carries a PCL-5 score of 46, and the clinical picture that has emerged across intake
and early sessions is one of a man whose professional identity and personal history have become
deeply entangled with his suffering.

The fire came on a dry October afternoon when the Santa Ana winds were running hard and hot out of
the northeast. Ernesto had smelled smoke earlier that morning — a faint, acrid thread on the air —
and had told himself it was distant, someone's brush pile, nothing to worry about. By early
afternoon the sky had turned a sickening amber and the smoke was no longer a thread but a wall. He
and his wife had perhaps twenty minutes to leave. He remembered standing in their living room,
unable to decide what to take, watching orange light pulse through the window glass, hearing the
crackling that seemed to come from every direction at once. They escaped in their car with almost
nothing. Their neighbors across the road did not get out in time. The house burned to its
foundation.

In the weeks that followed, Ernesto and his wife stayed with their son's family while the insurance
process ground forward. It was during this period that the first signs of something more than grief
appeared. He found that he could not stop reviewing the morning of the fire — the smell he had
noticed and dismissed, the moment he had looked at the sky and decided to wait. He became convinced,
with a certainty that felt irrefutable to him, that he had failed a basic quality check on the most
important system he had ever been responsible for: the safety of his own home. He would lie awake
cataloguing the decision points, the variables he had not weighted correctly, the earlier evacuation
that would have changed everything. This was not ordinary regret; it was the methodical, recursive
logic of a man trained to find the flaw in the process, now turned entirely inward.

As autumn turned to winter, certain experiences became unbearable. The smell of smoke from a
neighbor's fireplace would stop him mid-sentence, his chest tightening, his hands going cold and
unsteady. At the QA firm where he still consults part-time — reviewing documentation remotely, a
role he had kept partly for structure — a colleague once burned microwave popcorn in the break room
during an in-person visit, and Ernesto had to leave the building. He told his colleague he felt
unwell, which was not untrue. On dry, windy days with a particular quality of light and heat, he
found himself unable to sit still, moving from window to window in whatever space he occupied,
scanning the horizon. He had come to believe that the conditions of that October afternoon were a
recognizable signature, one he could learn to detect early if he stayed vigilant enough, and so he
monitored weather forecasts with an intensity that consumed large portions of his day.

He also began avoiding the evening news, any documentary or film that might contain footage of fire,
and conversations about the disaster with his wife, who had her own grief and whom he did not want
to burden further. When intrusive images surfaced — the orange light in the window, the sound of the
fire — he tried to push them away, to redirect his attention, though he acknowledged in session that
this rarely worked for long.

What finally brought Ernesto to seek help was a moment at his grandson's birthday party, when
someone lit candles on the cake and he felt his heart hammering so hard he had to excuse himself to
the backyard. Standing there alone while his family celebrated inside, he understood that something
had narrowed around him in ways he could no longer manage quietly. He came to therapy hoping, above
all, to stop punishing himself for what he had not foreseen — and afraid, underneath that hope, that
he deserved to.
