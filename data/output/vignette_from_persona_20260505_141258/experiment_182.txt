Bounyong Phommasack is a 42-year-old Lao American farmer who lives with his wife on a small
vegetable operation in the Central Valley of California. He was referred for psychological
assessment after his wife contacted a community health worker, concerned that her husband had not
slept properly in nearly eight months and had become increasingly withdrawn from her, from their
children, and from the neighbors and extended family who form the social fabric of their rural
community.

The precipitating event occurred the previous winter, when Bounyong and his wife discovered their
seven-year-old son, Keo, unresponsive in his bed one morning. The boy had developed a severe
bacterial infection that had gone undetected for several days, and by the time the ambulance arrived
he was seizing. Bounyong rode with him to the hospital and spent the next eleven days in the
pediatric intensive care unit, watching his son connected to monitors and IV lines, his small body
swollen and mottled with the effort of fighting the infection. There was a night—the fourth or
fifth, Bounyong could no longer be certain—when a nurse called him urgently to the bedside and he
saw Keo's arm limp against the bedrail, the skin discolored at the crook of the elbow where the IV
had infiltrated. A physician spoke words about organ function and probabilities. Keo eventually
recovered fully, but something in Bounyong did not close behind him when they finally drove home.

In the weeks that followed, the memory refused to behave like something that had ended. Bounyong
described it in his first session as feeling less like remembering and more like being pulled back
without warning — a fragment of the PICU appearing suddenly and completely, the beeping of monitors,
the particular pallor of Keo's forearm, and then gone, leaving him standing in a field row or at the
kitchen table with his heart hammering. He was insistent on one point: it did not feel like the
past. It felt like now, like something still unfolding, and no amount of reminding himself that Keo
was currently outside kicking a soccer ball seemed to reach whatever part of him held the memory.

Certain things reliably pulled these fragments forward. The sight of a child's arm — or his own — in
a particular position, the inner elbow visible, was enough. He had begun wearing long sleeves on the
farm regardless of the heat, and he found himself looking away when his children reached across the
dinner table. A Lao pop song that had been playing softly from a phone in the waiting room during
one of the worst nights had become unbearable to him; he left a family gathering in March when a
relative played something that shared its tempo and key.

Night had become its own problem. The darkness of the bedroom carried a weight that Bounyong
struggled to articulate, but he associated the worst of those hospital nights with waking in the dim
ward, disoriented, and believing for several seconds each time that Keo had already died. He had
come to believe that falling asleep before exhaustion fully overtook him was not something he could
safely do, and so he stayed up well past midnight most nights, working on equipment in the barn or
watching television at low volume, waiting until he was certain sleep would take him immediately and
completely, before the dark could settle in around him. Even then he sometimes woke at three or four
in the morning and lay rigid, certain for a moment that something terrible was still happening
somewhere he could not reach.

The consequences had accumulated quietly. His wife handled the children's bedtime routines alone
now. Several neighboring farmers had stopped dropping by, put off by Bounyong's curtness on the
occasions when they did visit; he found conversation effortful and company intrusive in a way he
could not explain to himself or to them. His harvest this past season was smaller than it should
have been, because there were mornings he could not organize his thinking well enough to direct his
workers efficiently.

What finally brought him in was a conversation with Keo, who asked his father one evening why he
never laughed anymore. Bounyong could not answer the boy. What he most feared, sitting across from
the clinician at intake, was that something had permanently changed in him — and what he most hoped,
though he said it quietly and without much conviction, was that it had not.
