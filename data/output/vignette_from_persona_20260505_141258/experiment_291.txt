Marcus is a 55-year-old divorced fashion designer of Canadian descent who presented for
psychological assessment following what he described, with considerable difficulty, as a gradual
collapse of his ability to function both professionally and personally. He came to the first session
dressed with careful precision, speaking in measured sentences, and only occasionally allowing
silences to stretch long enough that the discomfort in his face became visible. The story he
eventually told was not a single, dateable event but a sustained childhood landscape of violence at
the hands of his father — a man whose moods governed every room he entered, and whose hands were the
primary instrument of discipline, correction, and rage. Marcus described years of beatings that were
unpredictable in their timing but entirely predictable in their logic: silence in the house often
preceded them. His father would go quiet, the television would be switched off, footsteps would
slow, and Marcus would know, with the certainty of a child who has learned to read threat the way
others read weather, that something was coming. He did not always know what he had done wrong.
Sometimes he was told; sometimes he was not. What he carried forward from those years was not a
single scene but a whole grammar of danger — one that his nervous system has never stopped speaking.

For most of his adult life, Marcus managed. He built a career in fashion that rewarded his eye for
detail and his capacity to work long, solitary hours. His marriage ended in his early forties, and
he has lived alone since then. He has always been, by his own account, someone who keeps people at a
measured distance — not unfriendly, but careful. He told the intake clinician that he has spent
decades believing that people, given enough time and proximity, will eventually reveal themselves to
be untrustworthy. This was not a new belief. It was one he had tested quietly across friendships,
professional relationships, and his marriage, and found consistently confirmed, at least in his
interpretation of events.

What changed in the past two years is harder to locate precisely. Marcus describes a kind of
accumulation — a threshold crossed without announcement. He began waking from nightmares and
reorganising his evenings in response: he stopped going to bed before midnight, avoided napping, and
gave up the long baths he used to take on Sunday evenings because the stillness and warmth had begun
to reliably precede disturbing sleep. At work, where he manages a small atelier with two junior
designers and a seamstress, he found himself flinching when a colleague touched his shoulder to get
his attention over a cutting table. He would recover quickly, laugh it off, but the alarm was real
and physical — a full-body jolt that left him embarrassed and watchful. He began positioning himself
near doorways during team meetings. The atelier, which had always been a place of controlled
creative noise, now felt different to him during quiet periods between collections. When the studio
fell silent — no machines, no music, no conversation — Marcus found himself scanning, waiting,
certain without any rational basis that the silence contained a threat he had not yet identified.

At home the vigilance intensified. He told the clinician that he checks the locks twice before
sitting down in the evening, not because he fears intruders in any articulable way, but because the
feeling of being unsafe inside his own apartment has become persistent and sourceless. When he tries
to remember specific incidents from his childhood in order to make sense of any of this, he finds
the memories arrive either stripped of feeling — as though he is reading a case file about someone
else — or accompanied by a wave of guilt so acute that he stops trying. He cannot construct a
coherent account of what happened to him; the story has no clean beginning, middle, or end, only
fragments and the emotional residue of fragments.

What frightened Marcus most, and what finally brought him to seek help, was his growing conviction
that he had been permanently altered — that whatever the person he might have been was, he had been
replaced by someone irreparably damaged. He has begun to wonder, with genuine fear rather than
metaphor, whether his reactions mean that his mind is no longer functioning as it should. He arrived
hoping, in his own words, to understand what is happening to him. He is not sure he believes it can
be changed.
