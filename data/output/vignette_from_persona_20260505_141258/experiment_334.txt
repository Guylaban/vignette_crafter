Priya Chandrasekaran is a 32-year-old South Asian woman who serves as a city council member in a
mid-sized American city. She came to the intake appointment dressed professionally but sat with her
chair angled slightly toward the door, her eyes moving across the room before she settled into
conversation. She described herself as someone who had always been driven and community-oriented,
qualities that had carried her into public office in her late twenties. What she had not anticipated
was that the same public life she had built would become the landscape in which her sense of safety
completely collapsed.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Priya was attending a city-sponsored outdoor community festival
in a downtown plaza — an event she had helped organize and was scheduled to speak at. Without
warning, a gas main beneath the plaza ruptured and ignited. The explosion and subsequent fire moved
through the crowd rapidly. Priya was close enough to feel the pressure wave knock her sideways and
to watch, in the seconds that followed, people running in every direction through smoke and flame.
She described being temporarily disoriented, unable to locate an exit, surrounded by screaming and
the smell of burning. She eventually made it out through a side street, physically unharmed but
having witnessed severe injuries to others around her. Several attendees were hospitalized. One did
not survive.

In the weeks that followed, Priya noticed that the world she returned to felt fundamentally altered.
She had come to believe, in a way that felt less like a thought and more like a bodily certainty,
that the world is a dangerous place and that safety is always provisional. She was certain that she
had to be on guard at all times, because what happened in that plaza — an ordinary afternoon, a
civic celebration — had demonstrated to her that catastrophe requires no warning and no logic. The
conviction that you never know what can happen next was not, for Priya, a philosophical abstraction;
it was something she had lived through in a public square on a Tuesday afternoon.

She began mapping every room she entered for exits. At council chambers, she knew which door opened
fastest and which hallway led to the street. At restaurants, she would not sit until she had
identified a clear path out. Unfamiliar venues presented a particular problem — she had stopped
attending events at locations she had not previously visited, because she could not assess the
danger in advance. This had consequences that extended well beyond her personal comfort. Council
members are expected to appear at ribbon cuttings, town halls, and community gatherings across the
district, and Priya had declined or delegated an increasing number of these engagements. Colleagues
had begun to notice. She had attributed her absences to scheduling conflicts, but she understood
that the explanation was wearing thin and that her effectiveness in office was being quietly
compromised.

She also reported becoming hypervigilant toward the people around her, particularly in crowds,
scanning faces and body language for signs of threat. She described this not as paranoia but as due
diligence — a reasonable response to having learned what crowds can become. At night, this vigilance
intensified; she found it genuinely harder to feel safe after dark, and her sleep had become
fragmented. She had stopped exercising in the way she once had, avoiding any situation that placed
her body under physical stress, because the elevated heart rate and shortness of breath felt too
close to what she had experienced during the explosion.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was a vote she had missed entirely — the first in her tenure
— because it was scheduled at a venue she had never visited and could not bring herself to enter.
She sat in her car outside the building for twenty minutes and then drove home. She described the
shame of that evening with more visible emotion than anything else she disclosed. She was not sure,
she said, whether she was hoping to feel safe again or whether she had simply accepted that she
never fully would. What she did know was that she could not continue to serve her constituents from
a distance, and that something had to change.
