Linh Tran is a 48-year-old Vietnamese-American woman who works as a private tutor, primarily
teaching mathematics and English to middle school students in their homes and occasionally at a
local library. She is married to her second husband, a man she describes as gentle and patient, and
she came to therapy after what she called a quiet crisis — a moment when she realized she had not
been able to sit with her back to a door for over two years without feeling that something terrible
was about to happen.

The abuse she suffered at the hands of her first husband unfolded over nearly seven years, but it
was a single evening four years ago that she returns to most often, and always in pieces. She had
come home late from a tutoring session to find him waiting in the kitchen. What followed involved a
ceramic serving dish pulled from the counter, a locked back door, and a period of time she cannot
fully account for. She remembers the overhead light flickering. She remembers the specific pattern
of the linoleum floor very close to her face. She remembers the sound of a cabinet drawer being
opened, though she cannot say what he took from it or what he did next. These fragments surface with
a quality she finds deeply unsettling — not like recollections but like still images, vivid and flat
at once, as though she is looking at photographs she did not consent to keep. And when they come,
the terror she felt that night returns with them in full, indistinguishable from the original.

In the months after she left the marriage and eventually remarried, Linh began to notice changes in
how she moved through the world. She started sitting at angles in rooms that gave her a view of
every entry point. At the library where she tutors on Thursday afternoons, she now arrives early
enough to claim the chair facing the main entrance, and she has quietly rearranged the furniture at
the table she uses with students so that nothing sits behind her. She checks the mirrors in her car
before getting in, then again once seated, then once more before pulling out — not for traffic, she
knows, but for something she cannot quite name. She scans the faces of parents who come to collect
their children, reading them for something she is watching for without being fully conscious of
what. Kitchen utensils at a client's home, a letter opener on a desk, a glass bottle left on a
counter — these things register in her awareness before anything else in a room does, catalogued and
located before she has made any deliberate decision to look.

What frightened Linh most, and what she had come to believe with a quiet certainty, was that the
harm had already been done in a way that could not be undone. She did not think of herself as
recovering. She thought of herself as permanently altered — a different woman from the one who had
existed before that kitchen, before that floor, before whatever was in that drawer. She had come to
feel that she existed slightly outside the ordinary fabric of her students' families, their easy
domesticity, their unlocked doors and unguarded kitchens. She watched them with something close to
tenderness and something close to grief, certain that she no longer belonged to the same world they
inhabited. And she had come to believe, in a way she found shameful to articulate, that you simply
could not know — not really — what another person was capable of. This belief had made her warmer
with her students and cooler with almost everyone else.

Her second husband had noticed. He had said, gently, that she flinched when he stood too quickly.
She had not known he had noticed. That conversation, more than anything else, was what brought her
to the intake appointment — not the flinching itself, but the look on his face when he said it,
careful and sad and trying very hard not to make her feel watched. She came to therapy hoping, she
said, to feel less like a stranger in rooms that should feel safe. She was not sure she believed
that was still possible.
