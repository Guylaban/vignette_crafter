Raymond Kowalski is a 65-year-old married man who has spent the better part of four decades as a
prosecutor in a mid-sized American city, a career built on the conviction that the law, applied
faithfully, is a reliable instrument of order and protection. That conviction was severely tested
approximately eighteen months ago when Raymond was present at the violent arrest of a bystander
outside a courthouse — a scene he had not anticipated and could not have prepared for.

Raymond had stepped out of the building during a recess, loosening his tie in the late afternoon
heat, when a confrontation erupted on the pavement no more than twenty feet away. Officers
responding to an unrelated call had converged on a man Raymond did not recognise, and within seconds
the encounter escalated into something Raymond could only describe, in his first session, as
"completely wrong." He watched officers pin the man to the ground, striking him repeatedly while
issuing a cascade of overlapping, shouted commands — a wall of sound so disordered and contradictory
that Raymond, a man trained to parse language precisely, could not make sense of what was being
demanded of whom. The man on the ground was screaming, the officers were screaming, and the noise
collapsed into something that felt, to Raymond, like the breakdown of all intelligible authority.
What disturbed him most in that moment, and has continued to disturb him since, was not only the
violence itself but the grotesque inversion it represented: he had spent his professional life
working alongside law enforcement, trusting the institutional logic that governed their conduct, and
what he witnessed felt like a fundamental moral rupture he had no framework to absorb.

In the weeks that followed, Raymond began to notice that ordinary moments could pull him back into
that pavement without warning. Passing a uniformed officer in the courthouse corridor — a routine
occurrence in his line of work — could arrest him mid-stride, his body stiffening as though bracing
for something. The sound of overlapping voices in a crowded room, or the unintelligible murmur of a
television in another room, could produce the same vertiginous sensation of meaning collapsing. He
found himself watching colleagues, opposing counsel, even strangers in the elevator with a careful,
measuring attention he could not entirely explain to himself, except that he had come to feel that
people were capable of turning in ways that were not always visible in advance, and that it was
better to know early. He had begun sleeping poorly — lying awake long after his wife had settled,
his mind conducting a kind of inventory of the day's encounters, alert to anything that had seemed
off.

Over the following months, these responses organised themselves into habits. Raymond started
arriving at work with earbuds in, filling the commute and any unoccupied moment with loud music —
not because he particularly wanted to listen, but because it covered over the ambient sounds that
could, without warning, become unbearable. He began asking his wife, with a frequency she found
troubling, whether she thought he seemed all right, whether his reactions to things seemed normal,
whether she was worried about him. She reassured him, every time, and every time the reassurance
held for only a little while. At work, the consequences became harder to conceal. Raymond found
himself scanning courtrooms and hallways with an alertness that had nothing to do with professional
preparation — clocking exits, noting where officers were stationed, tracking the body language of
anyone who raised their voice. A junior colleague remarked, gently, that he seemed distracted. He
had begun declining cases that required close coordination with police departments, rerouting them
to colleagues with explanations that were plausible but not entirely honest.

What finally brought Raymond to seek help was a morning several weeks ago when he stood outside a
courtroom, unable to make himself push open the door because he could hear, through it, a confusion
of raised voices he could not immediately interpret. He stood there for nearly four minutes. He is a
man who has argued before juries for thirty-eight years, and he stood in a hallway, unable to open a
door. He came to therapy, he said, because he was afraid he was losing his grip on the life he had
built — and because, beneath that fear, he was still trying to understand how the institution he had
served could have produced what he saw that afternoon.
