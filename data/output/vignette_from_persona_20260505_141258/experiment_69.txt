Kwame is a 62-year-old African American man who has spent the better part of four decades working in
bomb disposal, a career he describes with quiet pride as a calling rather than a job. He is married,
the father of grown children, and until approximately fourteen months ago, he was a man who, by his
own account, had learned to live comfortably inside danger. What he had not prepared for was the
particular kind of danger that came without a visible device, without a timer, without anything he
could disarm.

The incident occurred during a routine inspection of an abandoned industrial facility that had been
flagged as a potential site for an improvised explosive cache. Kwame and a colleague entered a sub-
basement storage room where, without warning, they were enveloped by a release of toxic chemical
vapour from corroded industrial containers. The smell hit first — acrid, synthetic, coating the back
of the throat — and within seconds Kwame's vision blurred and his legs gave out beneath him. He
remembers the concrete floor against his cheek, the sound of his colleague shouting somewhere above
him, and then the prolonged, disorienting hours that followed in hospital: the antiseptic sting of
the ward, the bright overhead lights, the sensation of his body having become something unreliable
and alien. He was discharged after several days, told the acute effects had resolved, and returned
to modified duties within weeks. What did not resolve was something harder to name.

In the months that followed, Kwame began to notice that certain things undid him in ways he could
neither predict nor explain to his satisfaction. Walking into any part of his workplace — the
equipment bay, the briefing room, even the car park — would bring on a wave of physical agitation
that he described as his body preparing for something terrible before his mind had caught up. Loud
noises, which his career had long since habituated him to, now landed differently; a door slamming
in the corridor would send him rigid, his hands braced against whatever surface was nearest. And the
smell of antiseptic — encountered in a supermarket cleaning aisle, in a colleague's first aid kit,
at a routine dental appointment — would stop him cold in a way he found deeply disorienting and, he
admitted with some difficulty, frightening.

Kwame had come to believe, in a way that felt less like a thought than a physical fact, that his
body was still compromised in ways the doctors had missed or had not told him. He was certain that
remaining still, remaining alone, remaining unoccupied was when the damage would make itself known.
And so he did not remain still. He began taking on additional shifts, volunteering for every
available tasking, filling his evenings with paperwork and equipment checks long after his
colleagues had gone home. His wife, Adaeze, told him she felt she was living with someone who was
always about to leave the room. He understood what she meant but could not stop. Being alone in the
house — even briefly, even in the afternoon — produced a vigilance in him that he described as
unbearable, a sense that something was converging on him from just outside his awareness.

In any new space — a restaurant, a waiting room, a relative's home — Kwame found himself conducting
a quiet, systematic survey before he could settle: where the exits were, which chairs faced the
door, whether the windows opened. He did this without deciding to. He also stopped scheduling the
annual medical review his role required, finding reasons to defer it, and he had not returned to the
hospital where he had been treated. The smell of that place, he felt, would confirm something he was
not ready to know.

What finally brought him to seek help was not a single event but an accumulation. Adaeze told him,
with a steadiness he recognised as the edge of her patience, that she was worried he had disappeared
inside himself. And his unit commander, noting the excessive hours and a recent incident in which
Kwame had reacted with visible alarm when a trainee placed a hand on his shoulder, had quietly
suggested he speak to someone. Kwame arrived at his first session guarded but present. What he said
he most feared was finding out that the exposure had changed him permanently. What he said he most
hoped for, in almost the same breath, was that it hadn't.
