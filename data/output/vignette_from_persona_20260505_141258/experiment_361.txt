activity. He had been treating civilians caught in the crossfire of the conflict and had spoken,
carefully but publicly, about the health consequences of state-sanctioned violence. One evening he
was taken from his home by men in military-style uniforms. What followed lasted three days, though
Rodrigo cannot account for all of them. Certain moments are etched with unbearable clarity: the cold
metal of a chair, the smell of damp concrete, voices issuing commands in a regional dialect he could
not fully parse, the sensation of not understanding what was being demanded of him as the sounds
blurred into a wall of incomprehensible noise. Other stretches are simply gone — blank spaces in the
timeline that he has never been able to recover and has stopped trying to. He was released without
charge, without explanation, and without any official acknowledgment that anything had happened at
all.

He left Colombia within months, carrying with him the conviction that the institutions he had
trusted — the medical establishment, the legal system, the state — had not only failed to protect
him but had, in some configurations, enabled what was done to him. The men responsible, as far as he
has ever been able to determine, faced no consequences. This knowledge sits in him like a stone.

In the years since, Rodrigo built a careful and outwardly functional life. But the careful life
required careful management. He stopped watching Spanish-language television, then news broadcasts

fundamental damage in him, something that set him permanently apart from the colleagues he
supervised and the patients he treated. A man who had spent his career helping others regulate their
nervous systems could not walk past a security desk without his heart rate spiking. He had begun
