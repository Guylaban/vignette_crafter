Linh Bùi is an eighty-year-old Vietnamese American woman who has been married for over fifty years
and works part-time as a religious educator at a Buddhist community center in the city where she has
lived since resettling in the United States decades ago. She was referred for psychological
assessment after her adult daughter noticed that Linh had become increasingly withdrawn, was
sleeping poorly, and had begun refusing to attend the outdoor ceremonies that had once been the
center of her spiritual life. Linh arrived at the intake appointment neatly dressed, soft-spoken,
and visibly reluctant to speak about the reason for her referral, saying only that she was not sure
any of it could be helped.

The event Linh eventually described took place when she was in her mid-twenties, during a period of
intense military conflict in the region of Vietnam where she lived with her young family. One
evening, without warning, the village on the edge of which her home stood was struck. She remembered
the smell first — the particular wet-earth and smoke smell of the ground being torn open around her
— and then the fire. A structure near her home caught and burned with a speed and brightness that
she said she could still see clearly, more clearly, she noted quietly, than she could see her
children's faces from that same period. She had been separated from members of her household in the
chaos, had lain flat against the ground as figures moved around and sometimes over her in the
darkness, and had at some point lost the thread of what was happening entirely. There is a span of
that night she cannot account for, no matter how many times she has returned to it in her mind. What
came before and what came after exist; the middle does not.

In the years immediately following resettlement, Linh managed the memories by filling her days
completely. She taught, she prayed, she cooked, she cared for her family. As long as her mind was
occupied, she told the clinician, it could not go back. This habit of constant mental occupation
became so ingrained that she grew uncomfortable with any unstructured silence, filling even short
car rides with recitation or audio recordings of sutras. But the strategy had limits she could not
always enforce.

At the community center where she teaches, outdoor gatherings often involve the lighting of incense
and ceremonial candles. On more than one occasion over the past several years, the sight of open
flame — even small, contained flame — has stopped Linh entirely, leaving her standing at a distance
from the group she is supposed to be leading, unable to explain to her students why she cannot
approach. She has begun arriving late to ceremonies and leaving before the candles are lit, and her
colleagues have noticed. One younger teacher gently asked if she was unwell, and Linh deflected, but
the exchange left her feeling exposed in a way she found difficult to name.

She checks the locks on her home each night before bed — the front door, the back door, the windows
— and then checks them again. She has done this for so long that her husband no longer remarks on
it. If someone touches her without warning, even a light hand on the shoulder in greeting, her body
responds before her mind can intervene, and she has come to believe that this reaction is evidence
of something broken and unreliable in her physical self. She had once trusted her body to know what
to do; she no longer does.

What troubles her most deeply, however, is not the fear but the shame. Linh has come to hold a
quiet, persistent conviction that something in her nature invited what happened — that a woman of
stronger character or greater spiritual merit would have been spared, or would at least have
remembered, would not have gaps. The memory itself feels strange to her, as though it belongs to a
woman she has read about rather than lived inside. And yet it arrives, when it arrives, with a
vividness that ordinary life cannot match.

She sought help now, she said, because she is afraid of dying with this still inside her, and
because she does not want her grandchildren to see her flinch from a candle flame and wonder what is
wrong with their grandmother.
