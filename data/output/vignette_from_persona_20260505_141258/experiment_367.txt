Arjun Mehta is a 42-year-old South Asian man who works as a tax inspector for a regional government
office. He is married and presents with a PCL-5 score of 72, reflecting severe and pervasive post-
traumatic stress following an accident approximately fourteen months ago in which he accidentally
struck a pedestrian while driving home from a late evening at work. The victim, a young man in his
early twenties, sustained catastrophic injuries and died in hospital three days later. Arjun was not
found to be at fault by the subsequent police investigation — the pedestrian had stepped from
between parked vehicles into an unlit stretch of road — but for Arjun, the legal outcome has carried
almost no weight. He had been behind the wheel. He had not stopped in time. The rest, in his mind,
belongs to him.

The event itself unfolded in seconds. What Arjun returns to, unbidden, is the sound: a dense, hollow
impact against the bonnet, followed almost immediately by a silence that felt absolute. He remembers
his own voice, though he cannot always remember what he said. He remembers the way the streetlight
caught the road surface. The sequence beyond that point becomes fractured — he knows he got out of
the car, knows an ambulance came, but stretches of those minutes are simply gone, as though his
memory was recording intermittently. Other moments are preserved with a sharpness that feels almost
punitive: the expression on a bystander's face, the specific pitch of a siren drawing closer. He has
described this to no one in full, partly because talking about the event produces the same auditory
fragments — the impact, his own voice — with an immediacy that makes the conversation feel less like
recollection and more like re-entry.

In the weeks following the accident, Arjun began organising his life around the avoidance of
anything that reopened that entry point. He stopped driving entirely for the first two months, then
resumed only for short, necessary journeys, always by routes that avoided the street where the
accident occurred. He found that images of damaged vehicles — glimpsed on the news, or in the car
park behind his office building — produced an almost physical recoil, and he began taking a longer
route from the staff entrance to his desk to avoid passing the window that overlooked the car park.
His colleagues noticed he had become quieter and less available, but attributed it to workload. His
wife knows something is wrong but not the shape of it, because Arjun has worked carefully to prevent
the conversation from forming.

He has also stopped attending any event that carries the register of grief. When a colleague's
father died last spring, Arjun sent flowers and a card and offered an excuse about a prior
commitment rather than attend the funeral. He had come to believe that being in a cemetery, or
standing in a room where loss was being formally acknowledged, would dissolve whatever composure he
had managed to construct. What frightened him most was not the sadness itself but the prospect of
breaking down in a way that would expose what he understood to be the truth about him: that he was
the kind of person who destroyed a young man's life through inattention, and that the suffering he
now lived with was not a disorder but a sentence he had earned. He told himself regularly that he
deserved to feel as he did. On other days, when the weight became almost unmanageable, he reminded
himself that the young man could have died at the scene, that things might have been worse — a
calculation that provided only momentary relief before the original verdict reasserted itself.

He has come to feel permanently humiliated in a way he cannot fully articulate, as though some
essential belief he held about himself as a careful, responsible man — a protector, in the quiet
sense that fathers and husbands understand themselves to be — had been not merely damaged but
disproved. The world, too, feels structurally different to him now: less predictable, less fair,
less legible.

What finally brought him to seek help was a conversation with his wife in which she told him,
quietly, that she felt she had lost him. He is most afraid that therapy will confirm what he already
suspects about himself. He is most hoping, though he has not yet found words for this, that it will
not.
