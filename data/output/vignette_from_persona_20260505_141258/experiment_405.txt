Kiet Phạm is a thirty-one-year-old Vietnamese American judge who has been married for three years
and who, until recently, was regarded by his colleagues as one of the most composed and methodical
people in his courthouse. He presented for psychological assessment approximately fourteen months
after surviving a catastrophic flash flood that struck the rural county where he had been attending
a judicial conference. What began as an overcast afternoon deteriorated within hours into a wall of
brown water that swept through the conference grounds, destroying two buildings and killing four
attendees. Kiet had been trapped on the second floor of a partially collapsed structure for nearly
six hours before emergency responders reached him. During that time he watched the water rise and
recede, carrying debris and, at one point, the body of a colleague past the window where he clung to
an interior doorframe. The air inside the building had taken on a thick, chemical smell from a
ruptured cleaning supply closet somewhere below — a smell that mixed with mud and standing water
into something he has never been able to name but has never forgotten.

In the weeks that followed, Kiet noticed that the memory did not behave the way he expected a memory
to behave. It did not arrive as a coherent narrative. Instead it came in discrete, frozen images —
the grey-green colour of the water at the window, the particular angle of a ceiling beam that had
dropped to shoulder height, the colleague's red jacket moving past. Each image was a photograph
rather than a film, and when any one of them surfaced it brought with it the full sensory weight of
the original moment: the cold of the water he had waded through, the chemical smell, the vibration
of the structure settling. He described these episodes to his intake clinician as feeling completely
real, as though the room he was currently sitting in had simply ceased to exist. He was certain,
during those moments, that something terrible was still happening.

The triggers that reliably produced these episodes became apparent to him gradually and with a kind
of grim logic. An overcast sky with a particular heaviness to it — the specific grey of that
afternoon — was enough. He began checking weather applications compulsively each morning, and on
days that matched the meteorological signature of the flood he found reasons to remain in his
chambers rather than travel between buildings. The sound of an ambulance siren passing the
courthouse, or the antiseptic smell that drifted from the first-aid station located near the
building's main entrance, produced the same effect. He began entering and exiting through a side
door to avoid the first-aid station entirely, a habit his clerk noticed but did not comment on for
several months.

Over time Kiet stopped going out beyond what his professional obligations strictly required.
Evenings and weekends that had once included dinners with his wife's family, walks along the river
near their neighbourhood, and occasional travel contracted into the apartment and the courthouse. He
had come to believe, without quite articulating it to himself, that the world outside contained too
many variables he could not control — weather that could shift, smells that could ambush him, sirens
that could appear without warning. His wife, Linh, initially attributed his withdrawal to the
demands of his caseload. By the eighth month she had stopped suggesting outings and had begun
spending weekend afternoons at her sister's house instead, a change that registered to Kiet as
evidence that something in their marriage had quietly altered.

He also worked hard not to think about the flood. He had developed a practice of immediately
redirecting his attention whenever an image began to surface — reviewing case files, doing
arithmetic in his head, turning on background noise. Rest itself had become something he avoided.
Lying still in a quiet room felt, to him, like removing the last barrier between himself and the
images, and so he had not genuinely relaxed in over a year. He stayed up late reading briefs,
telling himself he was simply industrious.

What finally brought Kiet to seek help was a moment in open court. An ambulance passed on the street
outside, its siren audible through the chamber windows, and he lost approximately thirty seconds —
long enough that the court reporter looked up. He was most afraid that he was becoming unreliable.
What he most hoped was that someone could explain to him why his own mind had stopped belonging to
him.
