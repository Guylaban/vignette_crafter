past eleven years as a state legislator, a role that places him in near-constant public contact —
constituent meetings, committee hearings, press galas, and the relentless social choreography of

symptoms that have persisted, and in recent months intensified, following his time as a volunteer
relief coordinator in a conflict zone in the Middle East approximately four years ago. He had

personal and cultural, to help organize medical supply distribution in a region where he still had
distant family. What he encountered over those six weeks reshaped something fundamental in how he
understands the world.

The incident that Karim returns to most — or rather, the incident that returns to him — occurred
during what had seemed like an ordinary morning run to a field clinic. The convoy he was traveling
with passed through a village that had fallen eerily quiet. He remembered noticing the silence
before he noticed anything else: no children, no market noise, no dogs. Then the smell reached him,
acrid and thick, the particular smell of things that had burned that should not burn. The vehicles
stopped. What the team found in the next hour was something Karim has never been able to assemble
into a coherent sequence. He remembers fragments — a collapsed wall, a man's shoe, a colleague's
face — but they arrive without order, without the connective tissue of narrative, as if the event
exists only as shards. He cannot reconstruct a beginning, middle, or end, and when he tries, the
terror of that morning floods back with a completeness that feels indistinguishable from the event
itself. More than once in session he has had to stop mid-sentence, pressing his hand flat on the
table, visibly overwhelmed.

In the months following his return, Karim noticed that the world had begun to feel structured
differently. Silence, in particular, had become menacing. In his legislative office, when a meeting
room goes quiet or a hallway empties unexpectedly, he feels the specific dread of that village
morning settle over him. He had come to believe, in some wordless but insistent way, that unusual
quiet was not neutral — that it was the interval before harm. He checks the locks on his office door
multiple times before leaving each evening, and he has developed a habit of positioning himself near
exits in public spaces, his gaze moving constantly across rooms in a way that his staff have noticed
and quietly remarked upon.

The occupational consequences have been significant and, for Karim, deeply disorienting. His work
requires him to be present, persuasive, and socially fluent, yet conversations with strangers —
constituents at town halls, lobbyists, journalists — now carry a low-grade alarm he cannot fully
suppress. When military topics arise in committee, as they frequently do in defense appropriations
discussions, he finds himself gripping the edge of the table. On one occasion, a colleague lit a
candle at a dinner event and the smell sent him somewhere he could not name, and he excused himself
for twenty minutes. He has told no one why.

What Karim has done instead is work. He has taken on more subcommittees, more constituent casework,
more evening calls than any of his colleagues, filling every hour with legislative tasks that demand
enough of his attention to crowd out what waits at the edges. He tries, with considerable effort, to
keep his face composed and his voice level, understanding that in his profession emotional display
carries professional cost. His divorce, finalized two years before the deployment, means there is no
domestic partner to notice what he carries home. He lives alone.

He sought help now because his chief of staff gently told him that he seemed, in her words,
"somewhere else" — and because Karim, who has spent his career reading rooms, recognized that she
was right. What frightens him most is that he cannot perform the version of himself his position
requires. What he hopes, with some uncertainty, is that the pieces might eventually be made to fit
together.
