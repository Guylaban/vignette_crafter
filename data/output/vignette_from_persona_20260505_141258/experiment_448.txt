Valentina Reyes is a 34-year-old Honduran-American woman who works as a corrections officer at a
medium-security state prison. She is married and has been living in the United States for twelve
years. She presented for intake following a referral from her employee assistance program, reporting
significant distress that she described as having "finally gotten too heavy to carry alone."

Valentina was seventeen when her family fled Honduras after her father, a local journalist, received
credible death threats from a cartel-affiliated group. The journey north was organized through a
network of guides whose reliability was never guaranteed. During the crossing into Mexico, the group
she traveled with — her mother, younger brother, and eleven others — was intercepted by armed men
who were never clearly identified as cartel members, corrupt officials, or both. The men smelled
heavily of alcohol. They separated the men from the women, issuing commands in sharp, clipped
Spanish that brooked no hesitation. Valentina remembers the precise sound of boots on gravel, the
bark of an order to get on her knees, the cold weight of a gun barrel pressed briefly to the back of
her head. Her brother, then thirteen, was beaten badly enough to lose consciousness. The ordeal
lasted several hours before the group was released — not rescued, simply let go, for reasons that
were never explained. They crossed the border the following night.

For years, Valentina managed. She learned English quickly, earned her GED, and eventually trained as
a corrections officer — a career she chose, she has said, because she wanted to be on the side of
people who held the keys. She married her husband, Marco, in her mid-twenties, and by most external
measures her life appeared stable and forward-moving. But the memories never fully receded. They
remained unusually intact: sharp, detailed, and sensory in a way that ordinary recollections are
not. When they return, she hears things — the crunch of gravel underfoot, the particular cadence of
commands shouted in the dark — and the emotional force of those moments arrives so completely that
she has to stop whatever she is doing, gripping the edge of a table or stepping outside until the
wave passes.

The daily environment of her job has made this increasingly difficult to manage. The prison is a
world organized entirely around authority, uniform, and command. Valentina is herself a figure of
authority, yet she has found that receiving orders from supervisors — even routine shift briefings —
produces a visceral wrongness she cannot easily name. She has begun positioning herself at the edges
of rooms during staff meetings, choosing seats near exits, and she has become hypervigilant to any
sudden movement from colleagues or inmates alike, reacting with a sharpness that has drawn comment.
A lieutenant she respects recently asked her privately whether she was "doing okay," and the
question unsettled her for days. When a new supervisor — a broad-shouldered man who favors a
particular aftershave that carries a faint alcoholic note — began overseeing her unit, Valentina
started requesting shift changes. She has not explained why.

At home, she keeps the television on at high volume in the evenings, and Marco has learned not to
ask about it. She has come to believe, with a quiet certainty that feels like simple realism, that
the world is genuinely dangerous and that people in positions of authority are not reliably what
they present themselves to be. She does not experience this as a symptom. She experiences it as
knowledge, hard-won and accurate. She has also begun to feel estranged from the young woman she was
before the crossing — someone she remembers but can no longer locate in herself — and this loss sits
alongside the other symptoms without clear connection to them, simply present and unresolved.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment three months ago when she drew her weapon during
a routine movement in the corridor — a colleague had stepped unexpectedly into her peripheral vision
— and stood for a full second with her hand on her holster before standing down. No one filed a
report. But Valentina filed it internally, and it frightened her. She came to the intake session
hoping, she said carefully, to "figure out how to stay in the job" — and afraid, beneath that
practical framing, that she no longer recognized the person doing it.
