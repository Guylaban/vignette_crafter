Tane Faleolo is a 24-year-old Samoan-American pharmacist who presents for his initial assessment
reporting a PCL-5 score of 58, describing the months since his younger brother Sione's death as a
period in which his life has become almost unrecognisable to him.

Sione was twenty when he died. On the night in question, Tane had been working a late shift and came
home to find his brother unconscious on the kitchen floor of the apartment they shared. The smell
reached him before anything else — a thick, sour wave of alcohol that clung to the air and to
Sione's clothes. Tane moved through what followed with a terrible mechanical efficiency: calling
911, performing CPR, speaking to the paramedics who arrived and who worked on Sione in the same
kitchen where they had eaten breakfast that morning. Sione had mixed alcohol with a prescription
medication he had not been prescribed. He did not survive. What Tane has never been able to fully
assemble into a coherent sequence is everything between finding Sione and the moment the paramedics
told him there was nothing more to do — he can retrieve fragments, images, the texture of the
linoleum under his knees, but he cannot narrate it to himself from start to finish, as though the
event exists only as shards rather than a story.

In the weeks that followed, Tane attended the funeral with his family, and something about standing
at the graveside — the formal grief of it, the flowers and the dark clothing and the sound of his
mother's crying — lodted itself in him in a way he did not anticipate. He has not attended a funeral
since, and when a colleague mentioned a family bereavement in passing, Tane found himself leaving
the break room abruptly, offering no explanation. He drives a longer route to work to avoid passing
the cemetery on Meridian Avenue.

The smell of alcohol is perhaps the most pervasive difficulty he encounters. Tane works in a retail
pharmacy adjacent to a supermarket, and the liquor aisle is visible from the dispensary window. On
several occasions he has had to ask a technician to cover the counter when customers arrive carrying
shopping bags containing wine or beer. He has not told his supervisor why. He had come to believe
that explaining himself would require telling the whole story, and he was not willing to do that —
not because he was protecting himself, he thought, but because he was not sure the story existed in
a form he could tell.

He has also become acutely attuned to people behaving badly in any register — a customer arguing
with the pharmacy tech, a driver cutting someone off in the car park, a stranger speaking harshly on
a phone call nearby. In these moments his body moves before he has formed a thought: his chest
tightens, his hands go cold, and he is already scanning the room for something he cannot name. He is
certain, in those seconds, that something is about to go very wrong, even when the moment passes
without incident. His body, he told the intake clinician, seems to have decided that the world is
permanently on the verge of catastrophe, and he cannot talk it out of that conclusion.

When he does allow himself to remember that night — and sometimes the memory arrives without his
permission — he is flooded with guilt so intense it is almost physical. He finds himself returning,
again and again, to the fact that he is a pharmacist, that medication safety is the substance of his
professional life, and that he was not there. At other times the memory comes back emptied of
feeling entirely, as though it happened to someone he read about once and barely remembers.

Tane describes himself as someone who used to find genuine pleasure in his work, in his family, in
the future he was building. He was certain that person was gone. Nothing he does now — not work, not
the occasional dinner with friends he has not yet stopped accepting invitations to — carries any
sense of weight or purpose. What finally brought him to seek help was a conversation with his
mother, who told him quietly that she was frightened for him. What he is most afraid of, he said at
the end of the intake, is that she is right.
