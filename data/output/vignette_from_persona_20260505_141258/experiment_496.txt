Leilani Fono is a 31-year-old Samoan-American woman who works as a professional dancer with a
contemporary company based in the Pacific Northwest. She came to the clinic following a period of
escalating distress that had begun to affect her ability to perform and rehearse, and she described

something just beneath the surface was pressing hard against a seal she had spent years reinforcing.

Leilani grew up in a household where her father's violence toward her mother was a recurring and
unpredictable feature of domestic life. She was the eldest of four children and described many
evenings spent listening from the hallway, watching through a door left slightly ajar, as her father
— a man whose name she now finds almost unbearable to hear spoken aloud — struck and berated her
mother in the kitchen. What she remembers most viscerally is the sound of her own heartbeat in those
moments: rapid and enormous, filling her ears while she stood frozen, certain that if she moved or
made a sound, the violence would turn toward her or her siblings. She recalls her mother's face
afterward — composed, explanatory, telling the children that everything was fine — and the
particular confusion of being looked at by a parent whose eyes communicated something entirely
different from her words. Leilani was perhaps nine or ten when the worst incidents occurred, though
the pattern persisted until her parents separated when she was fourteen.

She did not seek help in the years that followed. She threw herself into dance, which offered both a
structured outlet and, she felt, a domain in which she had some control over her body and its
expression. For much of her twenties she managed well enough, or believed she did. She married at
twenty-eight, a gentle and patient man named Daniel, and for a time the life she had built felt
genuinely stable. But in the past eighteen months, the symptoms she had long managed to contain
began to surface with increasing force.

Hearing a name that resembles her father's — even a partial phonetic echo, a character in a film, a
colleague introduced at a rehearsal — produces in Leilani an immediate and involuntary response she
cannot easily account for or dismiss. In the studio, where she spends most of her working hours, the
sensation of being watched during rehearsals has become acutely distressing. Dance, by its nature,
is a witnessed art, and Leilani now finds herself hyperaware of the director's gaze, of audience
eyes during run-throughs, of the mirrored walls that return her own image. The moment she registers
that quality of being observed, her heart begins to race — a sensation she recognises from the
hallway of her childhood home — and the full weight of those evenings can return to her without
transition or warning, as though no time has passed at all and she is once again small and still and
watching.

When the memories come, they carry with them a profound shame that she struggles to articulate. She
has come to believe, in a way she knows is not entirely rational but cannot dislodge, that there was
something in her — some quality she possessed as a child — that contributed to what happened in that
house. She also carries a deep and abiding sense that her mother's composed reassurances were a form
of betrayal, that the person she most needed to protect her had instead required protection herself
while asking Leilani to pretend. At other times the memories arrive entirely stripped of feeling, as
though she is reviewing footage of someone else's childhood — a numbness she finds almost more
unsettling than the shame, because it makes her feel that she cannot trust her own inner life. She
has grown increasingly guarded with people around her, including Daniel, whom she loves but watches
carefully. She has come to feel that people rarely are what they present themselves to be, and this
conviction has made the ordinary intimacies of marriage feel quietly exhausting.

What finally brought her to seek help was a rehearsal three weeks ago in which she froze entirely
mid-performance, unable to continue, in front of her full company. She told the therapist she was
not sure what she was hoping for, only that she could not keep losing pieces of her life to
something that happened in a hallway twenty years ago.
