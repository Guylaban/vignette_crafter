Amara Osei is a 47-year-old Ghanaian-American woman who works as a licensed plumber, married for
nineteen years and living in a mid-sized city in Ohio. She presented for psychological assessment
describing a cluster of symptoms that have intensified markedly over the past two years, though
their roots reach back to a childhood she has spent most of her adult life trying not to revisit.

Amara was raised in a household dominated by her maternal uncle, who became her primary caregiver
after her parents emigrated ahead of her and left her temporarily in his care — a temporary
arrangement that stretched into six formative years. During that time, her uncle subjected her to
repeated episodes of severe physical abuse. He was a large, loud man with a particular way of
calling her name — drawn out, low, with a flattening of the final vowel — that preceded many of the
worst incidents. He would summon her from another room, and the sound of his voice, even in ordinary
moments, carried the weight of what was likely to follow. The beatings were accompanied by
declarations that she was inherently flawed, difficult, and the source of every inconvenience in the
household. She was told, with the authority of someone who believed it entirely, that things
happened to her because of what she was.

Amara eventually reunited with her parents in the United States at age thirteen, and by most
external measures she built a stable life. She trained as a plumber, a trade she chose for its
concrete problem-solving and its solitude, and she has worked reliably for the same contracting
company for over a decade. She married a patient and steady man named Gerald. But the past did not
stay contained. Approximately two years ago, a new site supervisor joined her company — a man whose
voice carried the same low, unhurried cadence as her uncle's, and who one afternoon called her name
across a job site in a way that stopped her mid-motion. She stood holding a pipe wrench and could
not move for several seconds. Since that day, the job site has been a landscape of landmines.
Certain sounds — the clatter of a particular type of fitting being dropped on concrete, a radio
playing in the background — can pull her out of the present without warning. Hearing her own name
spoken in any context that carries even a faint auditory resemblance to her uncle's voice produces
an immediate, visceral shift in her body.

Over the months that followed, Amara began arriving at job sites earlier than her colleagues and
leaving before the supervisor made his rounds. She has declined two projects that would have
required close coordination with him, offering logistical excuses her company has so far accepted.
More broadly, she has developed a persistent wariness around anyone in a position of authority —
inspectors, permit officers, the building managers who oversee larger contracts. She finds reasons
to let colleagues handle those interactions, and when she cannot avoid them, she is tightly watchful
throughout, reading every shift in tone for signs of danger. The professional cost has been real: a
senior colleague recently remarked that she seemed to be stepping back from leadership opportunities
she had previously sought, and a promotion she was informally considered for did not materialise.

At home, Amara has grown quieter. She finds herself dwelling at length on what was done to her in
childhood — not in a way that brings resolution, but in a repetitive circling that leaves her
exhausted. She has come to believe, in some deep and largely unexamined way, that the abuse happened
because of the kind of person she fundamentally is — that there is something defective at her core
that invites harm and disqualifies her from ordinary happiness. Nothing she does at work or at home
feels as though it matters. Gerald has noticed her withdrawal and has asked, carefully, whether she
is all right. She tells him she is tired.

What finally brought Amara to seek help was a moment three weeks ago when she sat in her truck
outside a job site for forty minutes, unable to go in because she had heard, from the parking lot,
the supervisor calling another worker's name. She sat there and thought, with a clarity that
frightened her, that she could not keep living like this. What she most fears is that a therapist
will confirm what she already suspects about herself. What she most hopes, though she has not yet
found words for it, is that she is wrong.
