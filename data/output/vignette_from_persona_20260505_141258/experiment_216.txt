Valentina Reyes is a 39-year-old Salvadoran-American woman who has worked as a detective in a mid-
sized urban police department for eleven years. She came to therapy after a performance review
flagged her behaviour as erratic during a domestic violence callout — a category of case she had
been quietly requesting reassignment from for nearly two years. She presented as guarded, composed
in posture but watchful in the eyes, and she chose the chair nearest the door before the therapist
had finished gesturing toward the seating.

Valentina grew up in a household where her father's rages were a weather system the whole family
learned to read. The violence she witnessed was not occasional; it was seasonal, cyclical, and loud.
She remembers, with the quality of a photograph rather than a film, specific images: her mother
pressed against the kitchen wall, her father's shirt dark with sweat under the arms, the particular
smell of him when he came home that way — sour and close and wrong. She remembers the shouting most
of all, not the words but the volume, the way it seemed to occupy every room simultaneously so that
there was nowhere in the apartment that was safe. She was seven the first time she wedged herself
behind the sofa to wait it out. She was seventeen when she left.

She had carried these years quietly for a long time, or believed she had. The work of detecting, she
had always told herself, required exactly the kind of vigilance she had spent her childhood
developing. For years this felt true. Then, approximately eighteen months ago, she attended a
domestic disturbance call that ended with her physically restraining a man whose body odour — that
same sour, particular smell — was identical, as far as her body was concerned, to her father's. She
completed the arrest. She drove home. She sat in her car for two hours and did not go inside.

Since then, the images have returned in ways she cannot control and cannot, paradoxically, summon
when she tries. When she attempts to sit down and deliberately reconstruct what happened in her
childhood, the memory goes slippery and inaccessible, like trying to grip something underwater. But
when she is not trying — when a colleague raises his voice across the bullpen, or when someone moves
suddenly in her peripheral vision in the corridor — the images arrive without invitation: static,
vivid, grief-saturated. She described the grief as disproportionate and humiliating, a word she used
more than once. She had come to believe that this disproportionality was evidence of something
fundamentally broken in her, that her reactions since these episodes began meant she was losing her
grip on her own mind.

She wakes most nights between two and four in the morning and moves through her apartment checking
the locks, the windows, the door to the bedroom where her partner, Delia, is sleeping. Delia has
noticed. Valentina has not explained. Physical affection between them has become rare and then rarer
still; Valentina finds herself stiffening at any touch, even a hand on her shoulder, and has
developed the habit of angling her body away in crowded spaces, in the elevator at work, in any
situation where contact might be unavoidable. Delia has begun sleeping on her side of the bed with a
careful distance between them that neither of them has named aloud.

At work, Valentina selects her seat in briefings based on sightlines to the exit. She has told
herself this is professional habit, the occupational instinct of someone trained to assess
environments. She is certain, at some level she does not fully examine, that she cannot trust her
own judgment in high-stakes moments — that the event happened to her, that she was made for it
somehow by the kind of person she is, and that this same quality in her will eventually cause her to
fail someone who needs her.

What finally brought her to seek help was not the performance review but Delia asking, quietly,
whether Valentina still wanted her there. Valentina said yes. She was not sure she believed herself.
She came to therapy hoping to stop hurting herself during the worst nights, and afraid, more than
anything, that what the therapy would find is that there is nothing left underneath the vigilance to
work with.
