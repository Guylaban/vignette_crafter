Adaeze Okonkwo is a 63-year-old Nigerian-born woman who works as a senior financial analyst at a
mid-sized investment firm in Atlanta. She presents as composed and precise in her speech, though she
chooses her words with a carefulness that suggests long practice at concealing distress. She came to
therapy reluctantly, referred by her primary care physician after she disclosed, almost in passing
during a routine appointment, that she had not slept more than four hours at a stretch in longer
than she could remember.

Adaeze fled Nigeria in her late thirties during a period of severe political violence in her home
region. What she describes from that time is not a single discrete incident but a sustained ordeal
that culminated in one defining passage: a forced march through dense bush terrain, organized and
enforced by armed soldiers who had come to the village where she was sheltering. She and a group of
other civilians were ordered at gunpoint to move — where, they were not told. The soldiers decided
the pace, the direction, and whether anyone was permitted to stop. When an elderly man in the group
stumbled and sat down, he was struck with a rifle butt and dragged upright. Adaeze remembers the
particular quality of the light filtering through the canopy of trees, the red laterite soil
underfoot, the smell of vegetation after rain, and the absolute absence of any choice about what her
body was allowed to do. She eventually separated from the group during a moment of confusion at a
river crossing and made her way, over several weeks, to a refugee camp and eventually to the United
States.

She built a life here that, from the outside, looks like a story of resilience. She completed a
graduate degree, raised a daughter largely alone, and advanced steadily in her career. For years she
managed by simply not looking back. But in the last two years — since her daughter moved abroad and
Adaeze found herself living alone for the first time — the past has become harder to hold at a
distance.

She had come to believe, with a quiet and absolute certainty, that the people she loves are
perpetually on the edge of catastrophe. Since her daughter's move, she spends hours each day running
through scenarios of what might go wrong — illness, accidents, violence — and cannot fully release
these thoughts until she receives a text or call confirming her daughter is safe. The worry is not
proportionate to any specific threat; it is simply constant, a background frequency that
occasionally surges into something overwhelming.

At work, Adaeze is meticulous and well-regarded, but her environment creates its own difficulties.
The firm's building is in a district that is heavily patrolled, and there are security personnel
stationed in the lobby. She has rerouted her morning commute to enter through a side door that
bypasses the main security desk, telling colleagues she prefers the quieter entrance. When her firm
held a compliance training session led by a representative from a regulatory authority — a man in a
dark uniform-like blazer with a badge clipped to his lapel — she excused herself twice during the
session, citing a phone call. She had not planned to leave; the sight of him standing at the front
of the room simply made remaining there feel impossible.

She avoids any situation that carries even a faint possibility of disagreement. In team meetings she
does not challenge analyses she privately believes are flawed. She has declined two opportunities to
supervise junior analysts because supervision, she explained to herself, inevitably involves
correction, and correction might produce conflict. What frightened her most about confrontation was
not anger — she could not fully articulate it — but something closer to the sensation of not being
permitted to leave.

She does not walk in parks. The green corridors of Atlanta's trail system, which colleagues describe
as restorative, hold for Adaeze a quality of threat she cannot rationalize away. The smell of wet
earth and tree cover after rain is enough to make her turn back.

What finally brought her to therapy was a weekend her daughter visited. They walked briefly through
a wooded neighborhood, and Adaeze found herself unable to continue and unable to explain why. She
stood at the edge of the tree line, rigid, while her daughter waited. She is afraid, she says, that
she has no language for what happened to her. What she hopes for, more modestly, is simply to stop
organizing her entire life around what she is not willing to feel.
