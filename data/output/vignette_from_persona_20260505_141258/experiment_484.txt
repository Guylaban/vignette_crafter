Kwame is a 77-year-old Ghanaian-American man who has served as the principal of a mid-sized urban
elementary school for nearly two decades. He is married, well-regarded in his community, and had
built his professional identity around the conviction that calm authority and careful judgment could
navigate almost any situation. That conviction was shattered approximately fourteen months ago when
Kwame was assaulted in the parking structure adjacent to his school late on a Friday evening. Two
men approached him as he walked to his car; one pressed close enough that Kwame could smell the
alcohol on his breath with nauseating clarity. They demanded his keys, his wallet, and then — in the
detail that has lodged most deeply — ordered him to kneel on the concrete. He complied. He was
struck across the back of the head, losing consciousness briefly, and came to alone on the ground.
He lay there listening to the sound of his own car accelerating away, an engine sound that still
surfaces unbidden in quiet moments.

In the weeks that followed, Kwame attempted to resume his duties at the school, telling his wife and
colleagues that he was fine. He was not fine. Walking through the school's main entrance each
morning, he found himself pausing inside the doorway, eyes moving automatically to the fire exits,
the side corridor, the door behind the reception desk — cataloguing escape routes before he could
stop himself. Staff noticed that he flinched when a custodian rounded a corner quickly, or when a
child ran past him unexpectedly in the hallway. He had always been a composed presence; now
colleagues described him as watchful in a way that unsettled them. A vice-principal he trusted
eventually asked, carefully, whether something was wrong. Kwame said nothing was.

What he could not say was what he had come to believe about himself in the aftermath. He had been a
principal for nineteen years — a man others deferred to, a man who prided himself on reading
situations and people. And yet he had not seen them coming. He had not fought back. He had knelt. He
was certain, in a way that resisted argument, that something in how he had carried himself that
evening had invited what happened, that a different man — a more alert man, a less complacent man —
would have sensed the danger in time. Alongside this ran a quieter, equally corrosive conviction:
that he could not trust his own judgment anymore, and that he could not rely on the people around
him to keep him safe either. When his wife suggested walking to the car together in the evenings, he
declined, not because he doubted her care, but because he had concluded that no one else's presence
would actually protect him — and that believing otherwise would only make him weaker.

When the smell of alcohol reaches him — at a faculty event where wine is poured, in the corridor
outside a parent meeting where he suspects someone has been drinking — something shifts in him that
he cannot fully describe. Sometimes the memory arrives with a weight of grief so total that he has
to excuse himself. Other times he can recall the entire sequence of events with a strange,
dissociated flatness, as though reviewing something that happened to a man he once read about. He
has stopped attending the monthly principals' network dinners he used to chair. He told his district
supervisor they conflicted with other obligations.

What finally brought Kwame to seek help was a morning last month when a seventh-grade student burst
through his office door unannounced and Kwame, without thinking, pushed back from his desk and
pressed himself against the wall. The child stared at him. Kwame stared back. In that moment he
understood that whatever he had been managing in private was no longer private, and that it was
beginning to enter the institution he had spent two decades building.

He came to the intake appointment composed and precise in his speech. What he is most afraid of is
that the clinician will confirm what he already suspects — that he is, at the core of it, a man who
can no longer be trusted with authority. What he hopes for, though he has not yet found words for
it, is to feel like himself again.
