Leilani Fono is a twenty-year-old Samoan-American woman working as a junior auditor at a mid-sized
accounting firm in Seattle. She presented for therapy approximately eight months after the stalking
began, referred by her primary care physician following a marked deterioration in her functioning at
work and at home. Her PCL-5 score of 64 places her firmly in the severe range, and the picture that
emerged across the intake and first two sessions was of a young woman whose world had contracted
almost entirely around the effort to stay safe.

The stalking began during Leilani's first semester in her current role, when a former acquaintance
from her university cohort began appearing outside her office building. At first she told herself it
was coincidence. Then came the messages — dozens of them across multiple platforms — and the
photographs sent to her work email of her walking to the parking garage, images that made clear he
had been watching her for some time without her knowledge. One evening she left the office late
after finishing a quarterly audit and found him standing directly beside her car, close enough that
she could smell cigarette smoke on his jacket. He said nothing. He simply stood there, watching her,
until a security guard appeared. That moment — the stillness of him, the fluorescent light of the
garage, the sound of her own keys jangling in her hand — became the fixed point around which her
subsequent months arranged themselves.

In the weeks that followed, Leilani found herself scanning every environment she entered. The open-
plan floor of her office, which had once felt unremarkable, became a space she moved through with
her back angled toward walls. She began arriving before her colleagues and leaving only when others
were present, and she took to reviewing the parking garage via a security camera feed on her phone
before descending. Her supervisor has noted a change in her output — deadlines she once met without
difficulty now stretch past due, not because her competence has diminished but because concentration
in that building, in any building, requires an effort that leaves little room for the work itself.

What frightened Leilani most was not any single thing he might do, but the certainty that she had
been visible and unaware — that the world had always contained this kind of watching and she had
simply not known it. She had come to believe that safety was an illusion she had been naive enough
to trust, and that the appropriate response to what happened was to never trust it again. Going out
had become something she avoided almost entirely outside of work obligations. Groceries were ordered
online. Social invitations went unanswered. A close friend's birthday dinner, a concert she had
bought tickets for months before — she let both pass. It was not only that these situations felt
dangerous. It was that they felt wrong, as though enjoyment itself was no longer available to her,
or no longer deserved.

This last point surfaced with particular weight in session. Leilani described a quiet but persistent
conviction that what happened to her was, in some way she could not fully articulate, something she
had brought upon herself — that she should have seen it sooner, acted differently, been less
visible. She told herself, in her own words, that she deserved to sit with the suffering rather than
move past it. She had not shared this belief with anyone before the intake appointment.

She described her life before the stalking — the new job, her first apartment, the tentative
optimism of early adulthood — as something that belonged to a different person. My life has been
destroyed, she said, not dramatically but flatly, as a statement of fact. She was not sure what she
was hoping for from therapy so much as what she feared: that speaking about any of it would make the
danger feel more real, or confirm that there was no version of her life that could be recovered.
What finally brought her in was a morning when she sat in her car outside the office for forty
minutes, unable to make herself go inside, and understood that she could not continue managing
alone.
