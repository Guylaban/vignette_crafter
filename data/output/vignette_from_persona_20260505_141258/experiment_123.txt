Rajan is a 32-year-old South Asian man who works as a housekeeper at a mid-sized hotel in a mid-
Atlantic city. He has been married for four years, and until approximately eight months ago, his
wife described him as warm, talkative, and reliably optimistic. He presented for an initial intake
following a referral from his primary care physician, who had grown concerned about Rajan's
deteriorating sleep, social withdrawal, and a series of vague but persistent physical complaints
that had no identifiable medical cause.

The incident occurred on an evening in early autumn, when Rajan was walking home from a late shift.
Two men approached him from behind in a poorly lit side street, and within seconds one had him
pinned against a brick wall while the other drove a knee hard into his ribs and wrenched his arm
upward behind his back. They took his wallet and phone. Before they ran, one of them struck him
across the lower back with what Rajan believed was a fist or a hard object — he could not be sure,
because by then he had squeezed his eyes shut and was simply waiting for it to end. He was left on
the pavement, winded, with bruised ribs and a strained shoulder. Physically, he recovered within a
few weeks. In every other sense, he has not.

What returned most insistently in the weeks that followed were not images in the conventional sense
but sensations: a sudden tightening across his ribs, a hot pressure along his left shoulder, a dull
radiating ache in his lower back that arrived without warning and felt entirely indistinguishable
from the pain he had experienced against that wall. These episodes could be triggered by almost
nothing — shifting a loaded laundry cart at work, reaching overhead to strip a hotel bed, a
colleague brushing past him in a narrow service corridor. The pain was real enough that on three
occasions he stopped mid-task and had to sit down. His supervisor has noted the interruptions. Rajan
has offered no explanation.

He has also found, to his own bewilderment and shame, that he cannot speak about what happened with
any coherence. He knows the sequence of events with complete clarity — the footsteps behind him, the
wall, the knee, the waiting — but when he attempts to put it into language for his wife or his
doctor, something fails. The words dissolve before they reach his mouth. He has tried twice to tell
his wife the full story and both times trailed into silence, leaving her more worried than before.

The memories themselves, when they surface, are not vague or dreamlike. They are vivid and precise
in a way that Rajan finds deeply unsettling — the grain of the brick against his cheek, the exact
pitch of one man's voice, the cold of the pavement through his trousers. The clarity does not feel
like remembering. It feels like still being there.

What has changed most visibly, however, is what Rajan now believes about the world and the people in
it. He had come to understand the attack not as a random misfortune but as evidence of something he
had been naive to ignore: that people are not what they seem, that the friendly faces around him —
colleagues, neighbours, even strangers who hold doors — are simply individuals whose intentions he
cannot verify. At work, where his role requires him to move through occupied and unoccupied guest
rooms, to exchange pleasantries with strangers in hallways, and to accept direction from supervisors
he barely knows, this conviction has become a constant, exhausting undercurrent. He is certain that
the world is a dangerous place and that he had simply been fortunate not to have understood this
sooner. He trusts no one fully now — not the men he works alongside, and, in moments that frightened
him most, not entirely his wife either.

It was his wife who ultimately persuaded him to seek help. She told him quietly one evening that she
felt she was living with someone she no longer recognised. Rajan came to his first session hoping,
above all, to feel less alone inside his own experience — and afraid, most deeply, that what he has
become is simply what he now is.
