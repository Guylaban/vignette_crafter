him. Eleven months ago, Chanthon's youngest son, Dara, was beaten severely outside a convenience
store by a group of men who had followed him from a nearby bar. Dara survived, but sustained a
traumatic brain injury that left him with permanent cognitive impairment and partial paralysis on
his left side. Chanthon arrived at the hospital to find Dara unconscious, and what he heard in those
first moments — the flat, mechanical rhythm of the ventilator, a nurse's voice calling a code, the
sound of his own name being said by a doctor whose expression told him everything before the words
did — has never fully left him.

In the weeks that followed, Chanthon found that his memories of that night did not arrange
themselves into anything coherent. He could not hold the sequence of events in order; instead,
fragments surfaced without warning and without context — the fluorescent glare of the hospital
corridor, the specific timbre of the ventilator, a half-heard conversation between nurses that he
could not reconstruct but could not stop hearing. When these pieces came, they brought with them a
grief so total that he could not speak, could not move, could only sit with it until it passed. He
had come to believe that there was something wrong with the way his mind was working, that the
fractured quality of his memory meant he had failed even to properly witness what had happened to
his son.
