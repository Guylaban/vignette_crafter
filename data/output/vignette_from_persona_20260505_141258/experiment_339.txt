Dmitri Holbrook is a 55-year-old single man of Eastern European descent who works as a counselor at
a community mental health center in a mid-sized Midwestern city. He presents with a PCL-5 score of
74, seeking help approximately fourteen months after surviving a gas explosion and fire that tore
through the apartment building where he lived.

The event itself unfolded on an ordinary Tuesday evening. Dmitri had returned home from a late
shift, heated something on the stove, and was moving through his hallway when a pressure wave
knocked him off his feet. A gas main had ruptured in the unit below his, and the resulting explosion
ignited a fire that spread rapidly through the lower floors. He remembers the heat arriving before
the sound fully registered, and then — most vividly — the smell: a dense, acrid mixture of burning
insulation, scorched wood, and something chemical and sweet underneath it all. He made it out
through a stairwell that was already filling with smoke, pressing his sleeve to his face, and stood
on the street watching the building burn while his ears rang and his legs refused to stop shaking.
Two residents on the floor below him did not survive.

In the weeks that followed, Dmitri moved into a short-term rental and told himself he was managing.
He was not. The memory of that night did not come to him as a coherent story he could sit with;
instead it arrived sideways — as a wave of dread with no accompanying image, a visceral flooding of
fear whose source felt both undeniable and strangely unlocatable. More disorienting still was the
quality of the memory when it did surface with any clarity: it felt, he told his intake clinician,
as though it had happened to someone else, as though he were reading a case file about a man he did
not quite recognise. He had come to believe this disconnection meant something was fundamentally
wrong with him, that his mind was beginning to come apart in ways he could not name. He began
watching his own thoughts with a vigilance he had previously reserved for clients in crisis —
scanning for signs that he was losing his grip on reality, cataloguing each intrusion, each moment
of blankness, each unfamiliar emotional surge as potential evidence of deterioration.

The smell triggers were among the earliest and most reliable. A neighbor burning leaves two blocks
away, a colleague microwaving something in the break room, the faint trace of smoke from a passing
truck — any of these could stop Dmitri mid-sentence and flood him with the same physical alarm his
body had mounted on the night of the fire. He had come to regard his own physiology as something
unreliable, even treacherous; his heart rate and the tightness in his chest were, to him, his body
refusing to accept information his mind already knew. He found himself scanning every new
environment for exits, for the faint chemical trace of gas, for anything that might precede
catastrophe. What frightened him most was not that danger was present but that he could no longer
trust himself to distinguish between when it was and when it was not.

At work, the trouble became concrete and consequential. Dmitri's office shares a building with a
vocational training program, and when one of the workshop sessions involves any kind of heat tool —
a soldering iron, a heat gun — the faint smell drifts into the hallway. On three separate occasions
he had abruptly ended sessions with clients, offering vague explanations, and once he had asked a
colleague to cover the remainder of his afternoon without being able to say why. He had stopped
attending the informal Friday lunches his team maintained as a collegial ritual, finding that the
unpredictability of what someone might say or do — a lighter, a candle, an anecdote involving fire —
made the social setting feel unnavigable. The friendships that had sustained him for years were
growing thin through his absence, and he was aware of this, and found he could not change it.

What finally brought Dmitri to seek help was a session last month in which a client, in distress,
threw a folder of papers across the room. The sudden motion of the object through the air activated
something in Dmitri that he could not contain in the room; he had to excuse himself. He sat in the
men's room for ten minutes, waiting for his hands to stop trembling. He is a counselor. He knows
what this looks like from the outside. What he hopes, with considerable ambivalence, is that
understanding it professionally will eventually mean something to the part of him that does not feel
like his own.
