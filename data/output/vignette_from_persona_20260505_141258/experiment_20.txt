Priya Nair is a 44-year-old South Asian woman who works part-time at a clothing retail store in a
mid-sized New Jersey city. She is married, the mother of two teenage children, and until roughly
eighteen months ago was, by her own account, someone who rarely gave much thought to her own
emotional life. That changed on an afternoon when she was visiting a local park during her lunch
break and witnessed a man collapse and die in front of her following what bystanders later confirmed
was a sudden cardiac arrest. Priya had been sitting on a bench no more than a few feet away when she
heard a sound she could not initially identify — a kind of strangled gasp — and looked up to see a
middle-aged man stumble and fall hard onto the path. She watched people rush to him, watched someone
perform compressions, watched a woman she assumed was his wife drop to her knees and begin to sob
over his body. The paramedics arrived, but he was not revived. Priya stood frozen throughout, unable
to move or look away, and when she finally returned to work that afternoon she said nothing to
anyone about what she had seen.

In the weeks that followed, the memory did not settle. It came back in pieces that refused to
arrange themselves into a coherent order — she could not determine, when she tried to reconstruct
the sequence, whether the woman had started crying before or after the compressions began, or
exactly when she herself had stood up from the bench. What she was certain of was the sound of that
gasp, the weight of the woman's grief, and the way the afternoon light had looked on the path. When
the memory returned — unbidden, often at the register at work — it arrived with an emotional force
that made her have to grip the counter and wait for it to pass. She described it as a wave of grief
so complete that she felt she was drowning in something that was not even her own loss, and she had
come to believe that this was a sign that something in her was fundamentally broken.

At the store, certain moments became charged in ways that were difficult to manage. A customer
crying at the returns desk — over something minor, a gift that had not worked out — could send
Priya's vision swimming, a lightheadedness rising fast enough that she had to excuse herself to the
stockroom. She had begun to dread any conversation that edged toward what had happened that day,
deflecting questions from her husband with vague reassurances, and she had told no one at work
anything at all. She had also developed a persistent alertness that she could not switch off: she
scanned the shop floor continuously, tracking the movements of customers and colleagues, watching
for any sign that someone might fall or collapse. She startled badly when the stockroom door banged,
when a rack of clothing toppled, when someone called her name unexpectedly. She was certain, in a
way she found difficult to articulate, that the world had revealed itself to be a place where
catastrophe could arrive without warning, and that the only reasonable response was to remain
watchful at all times.

At home, she had quietly reorganised her life around avoiding friction. She went along with whatever
her husband or children wanted in the evenings, not because she did not have preferences, but
because she had found that conflict of any kind left her feeling exposed and unsafe in ways she
could not explain. She had stopped attending the community events she had once enjoyed. Her husband
had noticed the withdrawal and had grown worried; it was his gentle, repeated concern — and finally
his direct request that she speak to someone — that brought her to the clinic.

What Priya most feared, sitting in the intake room, was being asked to talk about what she had seen.
She was not sure she could do it without losing control entirely. What she most hoped for, though
she barely allowed herself to say it, was to find a way to remember that afternoon without feeling
that the grief of a stranger had permanently taken up residence inside her.
