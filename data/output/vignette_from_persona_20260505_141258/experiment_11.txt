Marguerite Hollander is a 43-year-old European American woman who has worked as a head chef at a
mid-sized restaurant in Portland, Oregon for the better part of a decade. She lives with her long-
term partner and presented for psychological assessment following what she described, with visible
reluctance, as "a year of not being able to get back to myself." Her PCL-5 score of 80 places her in
a range consistent with severe post-traumatic stress, and the picture that emerged across the intake
and early sessions was of a woman who had survived something she still could not quite believe had
happened to her.

Approximately fourteen months before seeking treatment, Marguerite was admitted to the ICU following
a rapid-onset septic infection that had gone unrecognised for several days. What began as what she
assumed was exhaustion from a brutal winter service season became, within seventy-two hours, a
cascade of organ stress that left her intubated and sedated for nine days. She has described waking
in the ICU with no continuous memory of how she arrived there, surrounded by the sounds of monitors,
the smell of antiseptic, and the sensation of the ventilator tube being removed from her throat — a
moment she returns to again and again, though she cannot always say why. Her partner had been told
at one point that there was a real possibility she would not survive. Marguerite was not told this
directly until weeks later, and she has said she is still not sure she has absorbed it.

In the months that followed discharge, the memories of those days did not settle into the past the
way she expected them to. Instead, they arrived without invitation — not as recollections she could
examine at a distance, but as something closer to re-entry. She would be mid-service, moving between
the pass and the line, when the smell of bleach used to sanitise surfaces would close around her and
she would be back in that room, the sounds and smells and the weight of the sheets pressing in from
every direction at once. These episodes were not brief. In those moments the hospital felt more real
to her than the kitchen she was standing in. Afterward, she would find herself shaking before she
had registered any conscious fear, her body already flooded with alarm while her mind was still
catching up. What frightened her most in these moments was not the memory itself but the certainty
that her body was no longer trustworthy — that it had failed her once without warning and was
continuing to betray her now, reacting to bleach and the hiss of steam as though she were still
dying.

She had come to believe, with a quiet but absolute conviction, that something fundamental had been
altered in her. She described it not as sadness but as a kind of interior absence — an inability to
feel pleasure or warmth that she was increasingly certain was permanent. She avoided her partner's
suggestions of weekend trips or celebratory dinners, not from hostility but because occasions
designed to produce happiness felt, to her, like tests she would fail. The grief that accompanied
any deliberate attempt to recall the hospitalisation was overwhelming and disorganising, and she had
learned to keep the memories at arm's length by staying busy, by feeling as little as possible, and
by delaying sleep until exhaustion made consciousness impossible. She rarely went to bed before two
in the morning. Even then, she could not relax — lying in a quiet room felt, paradoxically, more
threatening than the noise of a working kitchen.

At work, the consequences had become impossible to ignore. Her sous chef had taken on more of the
creative decisions because Marguerite found herself unable to sustain the kind of expansive thinking
that menu development required. Two members of her kitchen team had commented, carefully, that she
seemed different. Her partner had stopped suggesting the celebratory dinners.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment three weeks before her intake appointment, when
she stood in the walk-in cooler and realised she had been in there for nearly twenty minutes without
knowing why she had gone in. She had come to believe her body had been permanently changed by what
happened. What she most hoped, she said quietly, was to be told that she was wrong.
