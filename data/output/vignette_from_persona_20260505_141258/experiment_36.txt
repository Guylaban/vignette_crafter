Darlene is a 65-year-old widowed woman of North American descent who has spent the better part of
four decades working as a military medic. She is accustomed to crisis — to the particular discipline
of staying functional when the body in front of her is failing. What she was not prepared for was
the night, fourteen months ago, when crisis came for her.

She had been driving home from a late shift along a two-lane highway when a pickup truck ran a red
light at an intersection and struck her sedan broadside at full speed. The impact was on the
driver's side. The windshield shattered inward. Her door folded against her left hip and shoulder,
and for several minutes she was pinned in the wreckage, conscious, smelling gasoline and hot metal,
listening to the tick of the cooling engine and the distant approach of sirens. The truck that hit
her was a dark-coloured late-model Ford, and in the seconds before impact she had seen it clearly —
the grille filling her side window, the headlights blazing. She was extracted by first responders
she had never met, which was its own disorientation for a woman who had always been the one doing
the extracting. She sustained fractures to her left clavicle and two ribs, a mild traumatic brain
injury, and lacerations requiring surgery. She survived. She has not, in any meaningful sense, felt
safe since.

In the weeks following the accident, Darlene returned to modified duty — desk work, supply
coordination — while her fractures healed. She told herself this was temporary, that she would
settle back into herself once the pain subsided. Instead, something else took hold. Dark-coloured
pickup trucks began to feel like threats. She noticed them everywhere: in parking lots, in oncoming
traffic, in the peripheral vision she had always trusted. When she drove past the intersection where
the accident occurred — unavoidable on her usual route to base — her body would lock up, her hands
rigid on the wheel, her breath shallow. She had come to believe that the feeling of discomfort
itself was a signal, that unease in the body was the body detecting something real. And so
discomfort, in almost any form, became something to be managed rather than tolerated.

She began eating in the evenings — not from hunger but from a need to interrupt something she could
not name. A bag of chips, a second dinner, whatever was available. She also learned to keep the
television on constantly, the radio loud in the car, her phone in her hand — anything to keep her
attention from landing on what was underneath. When images of the crash surfaced, she pushed against
them deliberately, trying to hold the memory at arm's length. But the memory did not behave like
memory. When it returned, it returned as event rather than recollection — she was back in the
crushed car, smelling the gasoline, hearing the sirens that had not yet arrived. She has described
sitting at her kitchen table and suddenly gripping the edge of it because the floor felt like it was
tilting. She cannot always find words for what these moments are like; she knows what happened, can
state the facts plainly, but the experience itself sits beyond language, and when she tries to speak
about it in any depth she becomes so flooded that she has to stop entirely.

At work, the consequences have been concrete. Darlene's role now occasionally requires her to review
accident reports accompanied by crash scene photographs — vehicles crumpled, glass spread across
pavement. She has begun requesting that a colleague handle these files, offering logistical
explanations. Her supervisor has noticed. She is aware that her standing, built over decades of
steadiness under pressure, is beginning to feel precarious.

What finally brought her to the clinic was a conversation with her daughter, who pointed out that
Darlene had not driven after dark in six months and had turned down Thanksgiving because it required
highway travel. Darlene sat with that for several days before making the appointment. What she is
most afraid of is that the woman she was before the accident — competent, self-sufficient, someone
others relied upon — is simply gone. What she is most hoping for is some evidence that this is not
true.
