Adaeze Okonkwo is a 48-year-old Nigerian-American rabbi serving a mid-sized congregation in a mid-
Atlantic city. She came to the intake appointment dressed carefully, her posture composed in the way
of someone who has spent years learning to hold herself steady in front of others. She spoke quietly
and precisely, pausing at intervals as though measuring each word before releasing it.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Adaeze was present at a community gathering hosted at a municipal
building two blocks from her synagogue when a gas explosion tore through the adjacent structure. The
blast occurred without warning in the late afternoon, during a period when the street outside was
crowded with families leaving an after-school program. The concussive force shattered windows and
threw several people to the ground. Adaeze was standing near the entrance when the explosion hit. In
the seconds that followed, she found herself unhurt amid smoke and screaming, and she made a
decision — one she has been unable to stop revisiting — to move toward the exit rather than toward
the area of greatest injury, where she could hear voices calling out. She has told herself since
that she was disoriented, that she did not know the layout, that others were already moving toward
the injured. None of these explanations have quieted what she carries.

In the weeks after the explosion, Adaeze returned to her congregational duties. She led services,
offered pastoral care, delivered sermons. To her community she appeared present. Privately, she had
begun to reorganize her life around a set of convictions that felt less like choices than like
facts. She had come to believe that her failure to act decisively that afternoon was not a moment of
shock but a revelation of her character — evidence of something broken in her that no amount of
subsequent goodness could repair. She was certain that nothing genuinely good could come to her now,
not as punishment exactly, but as a kind of natural consequence, the world having shown her what she
truly was. She told herself she had to remain watchful at all times, because the explosion had
confirmed what she now understood to be true: safety is an assumption people make right up until the
moment it is taken from them.

Her vigilance became constant and exhausting. Seated at her desk preparing a d'var Torah, she found
herself pausing to scan the window, tracking movement on the street below. During Shabbat services,
when she stood before her congregation, she was aware of a persistent sense of being observed — not
spiritually, but in a way that felt threatening, as though someone in the room was watching her with
intent she could not identify. She had begun checking the exits of every room she entered. At night
this watchfulness intensified; she lay awake long after her husband fell asleep, unable to accept
that the house was secure despite his reassurances, despite the locked doors, despite every
reasonable evidence of safety.

Certain sounds undid her entirely. A car backfiring on the street outside the synagogue, a door
slamming in the building's hallway — any sharp percussive sound returned her, without transition, to
the moment of the blast. She had quietly stopped watching the evening news, telling her husband she
was too tired, because coverage of building fires or infrastructure disasters made the fourteen-
month-old afternoon feel immediate again. When congregants raised the event in conversation — as
they sometimes did, the community having its own relationship to that day — she redirected them,
changed the subject, moved on. Intimacy with her husband had also receded; she had withdrawn from
physical closeness without fully acknowledging to herself that she had done so.

What finally brought Adaeze to seek help was a bat mitzvah ceremony three weeks ago. Standing at the
bimah, she watched a thirteen-year-old girl read from the Torah and felt nothing — not joy, not
connection, not the particular fullness she had felt in that moment hundreds of times before. She
drove home and sat in her car for forty minutes. She was not sure what she was most afraid of in
coming to therapy. What she said, carefully, was that she hoped to find a way to be present again —
for her congregation, for her husband, and in some form she could not yet name, for herself.
