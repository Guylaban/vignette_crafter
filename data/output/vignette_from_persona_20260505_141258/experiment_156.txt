Rodrigo is a 62-year-old Cuban American man who has spent the better part of four decades as a
professional dancer and dance instructor in Miami. He came to the intake appointment moving
carefully, as though the body that had once been his instrument now required constant negotiation.
He is married, soft-spoken, and chose his words with the deliberateness of someone who has learned
that certain words open doors he would rather keep closed.

Fourteen months ago, Rodrigo was driving home from a late evening rehearsal when a pickup truck ran
a red light at highway speed and struck the driver's side of his sedan. The impact spun the car
across two lanes before it came to rest against a concrete median barrier. What he remembers — and
what he cannot entirely remember — is the shriek of metal compressing around him, a sound so
absolute it seemed to replace all other sound in the world, and then the horn, his own car's horn,
blaring without interruption in the silence that followed. He was conscious throughout. He was
alone. It took emergency responders nearly twenty minutes to reach him, and he spent those twenty
minutes unable to move, listening to the horn and to what he later described only as "other sounds"
he cannot name even now. When he tries to put the event into a coherent account, the narrative
fractures. He can describe before and after but the center of it resists language entirely,
surfacing instead as a sudden auditory intrusion — the grinding of metal, the horn — that arrives
without warning in the middle of a conversation, a lesson, a meal.

In the weeks after the accident, Rodrigo returned to teaching because the studio was what he knew
and because stillness felt dangerous. But the studio brought its own difficulties. Students would
approach him from behind to correct their posture or offer a steadying hand, and the unexpected
contact would send a jolt of alarm through him so sharp that he once stumbled backward into a
mirror. He began positioning himself at the edge of every room, facing the door, locating the exits
before he located anything else. He told himself this was practical. He had come to believe, with a
quiet but immovable certainty, that he had no real capacity to prevent harm from reaching him — that
the accident had confirmed something true about his life rather than interrupted it. The shame
attached to this belief was not abstract. When the memories came, they brought with them a wave of
humiliation he could not fully explain, a sense that what had happened to him was a verdict.

He began staying up past two or three in the morning, moving through the house in the dark, telling
himself he was not tired. Sleep felt like a threshold he did not trust himself to cross. On nights
when exhaustion overtook him, he would sometimes find himself deliberately trying to go elsewhere in
his mind — to empty himself out before the images could arrive. He worked hard, in waking hours, to
keep the event at the edge of his thoughts, and when it pressed forward he pushed back, though the
effort left him hollowed out by midday. His wife, Marisol, noticed that he had become unreachable in
a way she could not name. He was present at the dinner table but not present. She stopped asking
about the accident because asking seemed to cost him something visible. The distance between them
grew in the way that distances grow when neither person acknowledges them — gradually, and then all
at once.

What finally brought Rodrigo to seek help was a moment in class three weeks ago when a student
played a recording through a small Bluetooth speaker and the feedback squeal from the device —
sharp, sustained, electronic — sent him to the floor with his hands over his ears. His students
stood frozen. He sat on the studio floor and understood, perhaps for the first time, that he could
not manage this alone. He arrived at the intake hoping, he said, to feel like himself again — though
he confessed, almost in the same breath, that he was no longer certain such a self still existed.
