Harold is a 77-year-old white American man who has worked as a delivery driver for most of his adult
life, a job he has held well into his eighth decade partly out of financial necessity and partly
because the routine of it — the familiar routes, the small talk at doorsteps, the sense of being
useful — had always suited his temperament. He is married, and by his own account his wife has been
his steadiest anchor. He came to the clinic several months after being assaulted during an evening
delivery run, and the distance between that night and his first appointment was filled with a kind
of private struggle he had not told anyone about in full.

It happened in early autumn, on a residential street he had driven dozens of times. He had stepped
out of his van with a package when two men approached him from behind a parked car. What followed
was brief but brutal — he was shoved hard against the side of his vehicle, punched twice in the
face, and had his wallet and phone taken before the men ran. He was left on the ground, disoriented,
blood from a cut above his eye running into his collar. A neighbor eventually called for help. The
whole incident lasted perhaps ninety seconds, but in those ninety seconds something shifted in
Harold's understanding of the world that has not shifted back.

In the weeks that followed, Harold found that the world he thought he knew — one in which a man
doing honest work on a familiar street was essentially safe, in which the ordinary social contract
held — had simply stopped making sense to him. He had believed, without ever articulating it, that a
certain kind of life earned a certain kind of protection. That belief was gone, and in its place was
something closer to bewilderment. He had come to feel that he could not trust his own judgment in
situations that had once seemed straightforward. He replays small decisions from that evening — why
did he park where he did, why did he not see them sooner — and each replay leaves him less certain
of himself rather than more. What frightened him most was not the physical injury, which healed, but
the suspicion that he was no longer a reliable reader of situations, that his instincts had failed
him when they mattered.

Harold returned to work within two weeks, which surprised his wife, though she did not press him on
it. What she did not know was that staying busy was the closest thing to relief he had found. On his
route, he keeps the radio loud and moves quickly between stops. When the van slows near any parked
car on a residential street, particularly in the late afternoon when the light begins to drop, his
body tightens and his eyes sweep the pavement in both directions. He does not linger at doorsteps
the way he once did. He eats more than he used to — at rest stops, in the van, in the evenings in
front of the television — and he knows it, though he would struggle to explain exactly why the
eating helps except that it occupies him in some immediate, physical way. In the evenings he eats
more still, often well past the point of hunger.

He has worked hard not to think about the assault directly. He has told himself, and half-believes,
that turning the memory over would undo him — that he is managing precisely because he is not
looking at it. He does not know what would happen if he did, and he does not want to find out. This
has meant that conversations with his wife about that night have remained thin and deflecting, and
she has noticed the distance without understanding its source.

What finally brought Harold to the clinic was a moment at work three months after the assault: he
froze completely outside a customer's house, unable to make himself walk up the path, and sat in the
van for twenty minutes until he drove away without delivering the package. He filed it as a wrong
address. He has not told his employer. He came to therapy, he said, because he does not want to lose
the job, and because he is tired of feeling like a man he does not recognize. What he hopes for,
quietly, is to feel trustworthy to himself again.
