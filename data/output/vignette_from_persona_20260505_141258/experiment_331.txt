Dolores Whitfield is a 73-year-old widowed painter who lives alone in a small house on the outskirts
of Tucson, where she has spent the last decade building a modest but respected reputation for her
landscape oils. She came to the intake appointment dressed carefully, her silver hair pinned back,
carrying herself with a kind of deliberate composure that seemed, over the course of the session, to
require considerable effort to maintain.

Approximately fourteen months ago, Dolores was among a group of elderly residents displaced by a
catastrophic industrial chemical fire at a storage facility adjacent to a senior community center
she attended weekly. The fire spread rapidly in the late afternoon, and Dolores found herself caught
in the evacuation chaos, pressed against other frightened people as emergency personnel moved
through the crowd. A first responder grabbed her firmly by the shoulders to redirect her away from
the smoke, and the acrid chemical smell — thick and sweet and wrong — filled her lungs. Around her,
people were weeping openly. She remembered standing very still in that posture, shoulders gripped,
unable to move, watching the building burn. Several people she knew were hospitalized with smoke
inhalation. One acquaintance did not survive.

In the weeks that followed, Dolores noticed changes she could not easily name. She began sleeping
poorly, lying awake in her quiet house listening for sounds at the door or the windows, certain in
those dark hours that something was about to go wrong again. She became irritable in ways that
surprised and shamed her — snapping at her neighbor, once raising her voice at a checkout clerk over
something trivial. These outbursts felt foreign to her, as though something coarse had moved into
her body without permission.

When memories of the fire returned, they came not as a narrative but as a series of fixed images —
the orange light against the smoke, the face of a woman crying nearby, the grip on her shoulders —
still and precise, like photographs she had not chosen to keep. In those moments, she could feel her
own body stiffen, her shoulders rising and tensing into the exact position she had held that
afternoon, and with the images came a wave of guilt she struggled to articulate: that she had
frozen, that she had not helped, that she had simply stood there while others suffered. She had come
to believe that the guilt confirmed something true about her character.

Dolores stopped watching the news entirely. She told herself it was a reasonable choice, that there
was too much suffering in the world and she was simply protecting her peace, but she knew, in a more
honest part of herself, that any report of industrial accidents or mass evacuations could pull her
back without warning. In her studio — the one place that had always felt entirely hers — she found
that she could no longer work in silence. She kept the radio on at a low murmur, or played
audiobooks, anything to keep her mind occupied and moving. Painting, which had once been a practice
of deep attention, became a way of staying busy rather than staying present. A gallery owner she had
worked with for years noticed the change in her recent canvases and gently withdrew an upcoming show
offer, a loss Dolores absorbed with a flatness that troubled her more than the disappointment
itself.

Physical touch had become almost intolerable. When her daughter visited and reached out to embrace
her, Dolores stiffened and pulled away. She could not explain it without describing the fire, and
she had been trying very hard not to do that — not to think about it, not to speak about it, not to
let it take up any more of the life she had left. The smell of alcohol at a dinner gathering — a
guest's glass of wine passing near her — had once sent her to the bathroom, where she stood gripping
the sink until the sensation passed.

What finally brought her to seek help was a conversation with her daughter, who told her quietly
that she seemed to have disappeared. Dolores could not argue with that. She had come to believe that
she would never again feel the way she once had — that whatever had been open in her before the fire
had closed permanently. What she feared most, sitting in the intake room, was that this was simply
true. What she hoped, more tentatively, was that someone might tell her otherwise.
