Nadia Khalil is a 26-year-old non-binary veterinarian of Lebanese descent who presented for
psychological assessment approximately four months after being mugged at knifepoint on a Tuesday
evening in the parking structure adjacent to the animal hospital where they work. Nadia had stayed
late to monitor a post-operative patient and was walking to their car, hands full with a tote bag
and a travel mug, when a man stepped from between two parked vehicles and pressed a blade against
their ribs. He took their phone, their wallet, and the tote bag. The whole encounter lasted perhaps
ninety seconds. What Nadia remembers most viscerally is the smell — motor oil and something sweetly
chemical, like cheap cologne or solvent — and the sound of their own voice saying, without any prior
decision, "Okay, okay, take it, please, take it," accommodating every demand with a kind of
desperate, automatic softness. They were released unharmed. They drove home shaking. They did not
call the police that night.

In the weeks that followed, Nadia continued to report to work, because the animals needed them and
because stopping felt like admitting something they were not ready to admit. But the parking
structure had become a gauntlet. They began arriving before dawn when the structure was better lit
and leaving only with colleagues, scanning every concrete pillar and parked car for a silhouette
that did not belong. Inside the hospital itself, when a large or agitated client stepped too close,
Nadia felt their voice go soft and accommodating in the same automatic way it had in the structure,
agreeing to things they did not mean, apologising for things that were not their fault. They
recognised what was happening and felt ashamed of it, but the compliance arrived before the
recognition, like a reflex.

At home, the vigilance did not switch off. Nadia's partner, Reem, noticed them checking the locks
multiple times before bed, pausing at the sound of footsteps in the hallway outside their apartment,
turning to look behind them when they walked from the kitchen to the bedroom. Nadia had come to
believe, with a quiet but firm certainty, that safety was simply not guaranteed anywhere — not in a
well-lit apartment, not with someone who loved them nearby. They could not rely on that love as a
buffer. When Reem offered reassurance, Nadia heard it as something kind but structurally useless,
the way a weather forecast is useless once it is already raining.

The intrusions arrived without schedule. A client's cologne in the exam room could return Nadia to
the parking structure completely — not as a recollection but as a re-immersion, the smell of motor
oil suddenly present, the pressure at their ribs suddenly real. When they tried to describe the
mugging to Reem or to their supervisor — who had gently asked whether Nadia wanted to file an
incident report — they found the account dissolving mid-sentence, the thread of it dropping
somewhere they could not locate, leaving them standing in silence with their mouth open. After the
second attempt, they stopped trying to explain.

What accumulated over months was a progressive narrowing. Nadia began declining evening social
engagements, then weekend ones, then anything that required walking to a car alone or navigating
unfamiliar streets. Reem had been patient, but the relationship had grown careful and slightly
formal in the way relationships do when one person is managing something the other cannot fully
reach. Professionally, Nadia's supervisor had begun scheduling them exclusively for daytime shifts,
an accommodation Nadia had accepted with relief and then felt diminished by. They had trained for
years to be capable. They had come to believe that person — the capable, curious, genuinely happy
person who had chosen this work — was simply gone.

What finally brought Nadia to seek help was a morning when they sat in their car in the hospital
parking structure for twenty-two minutes, unable to open the door. They told themselves, as they had
many times before, that the mugging had not been that serious, that other people experienced far
worse. The thought did not move them. They called the hospital to say they would be late, and then
they called a therapist. What they most hope for, they said at intake, is to stop feeling like a
stranger in their own life. What they most fear is that the person they were before that Tuesday
evening is not recoverable.
