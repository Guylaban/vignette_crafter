Valentina Reyes is a 38-year-old Salvadoran American woman who works on the assembly line at a food-
processing factory in the outskirts of a mid-sized Midwestern city. She came to her first intake
appointment wearing dark clothing and speaking quietly, as though conserving energy. She described
herself, almost in passing, as someone who used to laugh a lot. That detail seemed to cost her
something to say.

Eleven months ago, Valentina's younger brother, Marco, was killed in a car accident two blocks from
their shared apartment. He had been coming home from a late shift when another driver ran a red
light. Valentina learned what had happened when she heard sirens from her window — a long, layered
wail that seemed to go on too long — and stepped outside to see the flashing lights stopped at the
corner she and Marco had walked together hundreds of times. She reached him before the paramedics
had covered him. She has not been able to fully account for the minutes that followed, but she
remembers the sound of the sirens continuing even after there was nothing left for them to do, and
she remembers understanding, in a way that felt physical rather than cognitive, that her life as she
had known it had ended on that corner.

In the weeks after Marco's death, Valentina found herself returning obsessively to the sequence of
that night — the timing of her shift, whether she had delayed him somehow, what would have happened
if she had asked him to wait. She has continued this in the months since, lying awake on her break
or staring at the conveyor belt at work, running through the details again and again as though the
right arrangement of facts might eventually produce an answer that makes the loss bearable. She has
come to believe that if she could only understand it fully, something would settle inside her. So
far, nothing has.

She began avoiding anything that carried the shape of grief. She stopped attending church after a
fellow parishioner mentioned losing a parent, and the words landed on her like a blow. She changed
the radio station at work the moment any conversation turned toward loss or death, sometimes
removing her earbuds entirely and working in silence rather than risk hearing something that would
bring Marco back to her in the wrong way. When a coworker's mother died last spring, Valentina
called in sick for three days rather than navigate the condolence cards and quiet talk of funerals
that moved through the factory floor. Her supervisor has noted her absences and spoken to her twice;
she has not explained. She has not attended Marco's grave since the burial, and she declined to go
to a cousin's quinceañera last month because the venue was adjacent to the cemetery where he is
buried.

Valentina has also found herself seeking reassurance from the few people she still allows close —
her mother, and one friend from her hometown. She calls them at odd hours, asking whether they think
she is doing all right, whether they can tell she is still herself. She is not sure what answer she
is hoping for. What frightens her most is that she no longer believes one exists. She has come to
feel that she does not belong anywhere she used to belong — not in her family's gatherings, where
everyone else seems to be moving forward; not at work, where the noise and routine feel like a
language she once spoke fluently and now only approximates; not even in her own apartment, where
Marco's absence is a constant and specific presence.

She described herself in the intake as someone whose happiness was a feature of who she was — not a
mood, but an identity. That person, she said plainly, is gone. She does not use the word destroyed
lightly, but she used it: her life has been destroyed, and she is not sure it can be rebuilt into
anything she would recognise.

What finally brought her in was her mother, who told her gently that she was disappearing. Valentina
did not argue. She came to therapy not with hope exactly, but with a willingness to be told whether
hope is still a reasonable thing to have.
