Tariq Nassar is a 38-year-old Lebanese-American hairdresser who presents to outpatient therapy
following a period of escalating distress that he describes, with some embarrassment, as feeling
like he is "losing his grip on reality." Single and living alone in a one-bedroom apartment, he has
been cutting hair at a mid-size salon for eleven years, a job he once found grounding and social. He
is soft-spoken in session, choosing his words carefully, and he laughs at the wrong moments — a
habit he seems aware of, touching his jaw afterward as though checking something.

Two years ago, Tariq was diagnosed with acute myocarditis following what he had dismissed as a
persistent flu. Within days of that dismissal, he collapsed in the back room of the salon, was taken
by ambulance, and spent nine days in the cardiac care unit. He has described those nine days in
fragments: the smell of the antiseptic wipes used before each IV insertion, a smell he cannot name
but would recognise anywhere; the sensation of the cardiac monitor leads pressing into his chest;
and above all a period of several hours during which he genuinely did not know whether his heart
would hold. The doctors had been careful but not reassuring. He had lain in that bed certain, at
least once, that he was dying.

He was discharged with a clean bill of health and told his recovery was complete. For several weeks
he functioned — returned to work, resumed his routines. Then, gradually, things began to surface. He
started waking from nightmares he could not always recall, but the waking itself carried the full
weight of whatever had happened inside the dream: his chest tight, his hands trembling, the
conviction that something was catastrophically wrong with him. He would lie in the dark for an hour
or more, unable to distinguish the residue of sleep from present reality. On those mornings he would
arrive at work already depleted, his body humming with a tension he could not discharge.

At the salon, the triggers found him. A client once showed him a photo on her phone — a hospital
room, a relative recovering from surgery, nothing dramatic — and Tariq had to excuse himself to the
bathroom, where he sat on the closed lid of the toilet until the wave passed. He could not explain
to himself what the wave was, exactly: not a clear memory, not a picture, but an emotional weight so
complete it felt physical. Sometimes, trimming hair close to a client's scalp, he catches a faint
chemical smell — something in a product, something industrial — and for a moment the salon recedes
and something else is present, not visible, just there, olfactory and insistent. He has learned to
breathe through these moments without dropping his scissors, but the effort costs him.

What frightens Tariq most is not the memories themselves but what he has come to believe they mean
about his body and his mind. He is certain that a body that has healed should not continue to behave
as though it is still in danger, and the fact that his does — the sudden surges of physical
reactivity, the chest tightness that arrives without warning, the way his muscles seem to brace for
impact during a routine workday — reads to him as evidence that something remains broken inside him,
something the cardiologists missed or something worse, something psychological. He has begun to
think, quietly and with considerable shame, that he may be going crazy. He does not use that word
lightly; he uses it the way a person uses a word they have been turning over for months.

His annual physical is overdue by fourteen months. He has rescheduled it three times. The prospect
of lying on an examination table, of a stethoscope on his chest, of a blood pressure cuff tightening
on his arm, produces a dread so specific and so physical — a recalled pain in his sternum, a nausea
that is not quite nausea — that he has simply stopped making the appointment.

What finally brought him in was a conversation with his younger sister, who told him plainly that he
had become someone she did not recognise. He sat with that for two weeks before calling. He arrives
hoping, above everything else, to be told that what is happening to him has a name, and that the
name is not the one he has been afraid it might be.
