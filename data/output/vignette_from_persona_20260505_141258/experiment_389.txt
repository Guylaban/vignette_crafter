Linh Tran is a 35-year-old Vietnamese American physical therapist who presents for her initial
intake following a prolonged period of domestic abuse perpetrated by her former partner, with whom
she remained in a relationship for nearly four years before separating eighteen months ago. She is
currently married to a different man, whom she describes as gentle and patient, and she acknowledges
that her difficulties are now placing considerable strain on a relationship she deeply values.

The abuse Linh endured was primarily physical, and it followed a pattern she did not recognize as
dangerous until it had become entrenched. Her former partner would frequently grab her wrists
without warning, pinning her arms to prevent her from leaving a room until an argument had concluded
on his terms. On one occasion that she returns to repeatedly in session, he held her forearm against
a heating element on the stove — not long enough to cause lasting scarring, but long enough that the
specific sensation of dry, radiating heat against her skin became inseparable, in her nervous
system, from the experience of being utterly without recourse. She remembers his face very close to
hers, telling her she had brought this on herself. She believed him then, and some part of her
believes him still.

When Linh attempts to describe that night, or others like it, she loses the thread almost
immediately. She will begin a sentence, pause, and then look at the clinician with a kind of
bewildered apology, as though the story has simply ceased to exist inside her. What does not lose
coherence is the sensory experience: the memory does not feel stored to her — it feels current, as
though the event is not something that ended but something she is perpetually inside. When it
returns in full, the emotional force is such that she has had to excuse herself from sessions mid-
sentence, pressing her hands flat on her thighs and breathing until the wave subsides.

Her work as a physical therapist has become a daily negotiation with her own body's alarm system.
Therapeutic touch is the medium of her profession, and she administers it dozens of times each day —
guiding a patient's shoulder through its range of motion, applying pressure to a lumbar spine,
placing a warm compress against an injured joint. The warmth of a hydrocollator pack against her own
skin, incidentally encountered when she reaches across a treatment table, can stop her mid-movement.
She has developed the habit of wearing long sleeves regardless of the clinic's temperature, and she
positions herself carefully during manual techniques so that patient contact is always initiated by
her rather than received unexpectedly. When a patient reaches out to steady themselves and touches
her arm without warning, she has learned to convert her flinch into the appearance of professional
repositioning. Her supervisor has noticed that she seems distracted, and Linh is acutely aware that
her clinical presence is not what it was.

She had come to believe, with a certainty that resists examination, that what happened to her
reflected something essentially wrong with her — a flaw in her character that had invited the abuse
and that would, if she were not vigilant, invite harm again. She scans waiting rooms and hallways
for men whose posture or affect reminds her of her former partner, and she has quietly rearranged
her schedule to avoid patients she has categorized, by some private and exhausting calculus, as
potentially dangerous. She dwells at length on the woman she was before: more confident in her body,
more present with her husband, less afraid of being touched by someone who loves her. She also
returns, circularly and without resolution, to the moments she might have left sooner, said
something differently, read the signs she now believes were always there.

What finally brought Linh to seek help was her husband reaching for her hand during a film they were
watching at home — an ordinary gesture — and her pulling away before she could stop herself. She saw
his face register the hurt before he composed it into patience. She is most afraid that she has
become someone permanently diminished, that the flaw her former partner named in her is real. What
she is hoping, tentatively, is that a clinician might tell her otherwise.
