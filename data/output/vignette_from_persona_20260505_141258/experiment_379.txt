Marcus Heller is a 49-year-old architect, married, who presented for treatment following a traumatic
incident he witnessed approximately fourteen months ago. He describes a period of gradual
deterioration that has finally, in his own words, "made it impossible to keep pretending things are
fine."

The event occurred on a Saturday afternoon when Marcus was visiting a construction site in an urban
neighborhood to review progress on a mixed-use development he had designed. Walking back toward his
car along a side street, he witnessed a brutal assault — a man being beaten by two attackers in an
alley between two buildings. The violence was prolonged and close. Marcus froze at the mouth of the
alley, unable to move or intervene, watching the attackers use their fists and then a length of
pipe. He remembers the sound of impact, the smell — a combination of garbage from a nearby dumpster
and something metallic and biological he could not name at the time — and the sight of the victim's
body going limp on the concrete. The attackers fled past him. He stood there for what he later
learned was nearly eight minutes before he called 911.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus found himself unable to account for the sequence of what he had
seen. When he tried to reconstruct it — whether for the police, or later for his wife — the pieces
came out of order, or large portions simply were not there. More distressing still was a quality he
struggled to articulate even to himself: he knew what had happened, he had been present, and yet
when he sat across from his wife at dinner and she asked him how he was doing, the words for what he
carried would not come. He would open his mouth and find nothing. She began to interpret his
silences as withdrawal. He had no way to correct her impression.

When the memories did surface, they did not feel like recollections. They felt immediate and present
— the smell especially, which could be triggered by something as ordinary as a garbage truck passing
his office building on collection days, or by the iron-tinged air near a construction site,
environments he had once moved through without a second thought. His stomach would lurch before his
mind caught up, nausea arriving as a kind of early warning system his body had installed without his
consent. Any sudden movement in his peripheral vision — a colleague gesturing too quickly, a door
swinging open — sent a spike of alarm through him that he found deeply embarrassing in professional
settings.

Marcus had come to believe, in a way he could not fully justify but also could not dislodge, that
something intrinsic to him had placed him at that alley at that moment — that he was, in some
essential way, the kind of person to whom terrible things happen and who fails to act when it
matters. He felt set apart from colleagues and friends who moved through the world with what seemed
to him an unearned ease. At work, this had concrete consequences. He had stopped conducting site
visits alone, rerouting that responsibility to junior staff with explanations that sounded
professional but were driven by the fact that he could not enter a building without first locating
every exit, could not sit in a meeting room without positioning his chair against the wall where he
could see the door. His firm's open-plan studio, which he had once championed, had become a daily
ordeal. A senior partner had noted his disengagement in a recent review.

At home, his wife had stopped asking about the incident. The silence between them had thickened into
something neither of them addressed directly.

What finally brought Marcus in was a presentation he was required to give in a glass-walled
conference room on the fourteenth floor of a downtown building — a room with no wall behind the
presenter's position. He stood at the front, felt the exposure at his back, and could not begin. He
excused himself and did not return. He called for an appointment the following morning.

What he most fears, he said quietly at intake, is that nothing about him will ever feel safe again.
What he hopes, with considerably less certainty, is that he is wrong.
