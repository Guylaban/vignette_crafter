where he has spent the better part of two decades building a respected career in documentary and
editorial work. He came to the intake session quietly, choosing the chair nearest the door without

as the version of Rangi that returned from Papua New Guinea fourteen months ago.

province, where Rangi had travelled alone to document a land-rights dispute for an international
magazine. On his third day in the region, he was detained by a faction of armed men connected to one
of the parties in the conflict. He was held for eleven days in a concrete outbuilding with no
natural light, his hands bound for long stretches, his camera equipment confiscated. His captors
were unpredictable in their movements — entering without warning, sometimes in groups, sometimes
alone — and the sound of footsteps approaching the door became, over those eleven days, the signal
around which every moment of terror was organised. He was eventually released without physical
injury following intervention by a local journalist, but the experience left a mark that no clean
bill of health could address.

In the months after his return to Portland, Rangi noticed that the world had reorganised itself
around threat in ways he could not fully articulate to his wife, Diane. He began scanning every
person who entered a room, reading their body language for signs of intent before he had consciously
decided to do so. A stranger shifting weight from foot to foot, a colleague reaching across a desk
too quickly — any sudden movement by another person placed him immediately on guard, his chest
tightening, his jaw clenching before his mind had caught up. He had come to believe, in a way that
felt less like a thought and more like a fact, that dangerous people were a permanent feature of any
environment, and that his survival depended on identifying them first.

This belief shaped his behaviour in ways that accumulated steadily. Before entering any space — a
coffee shop, a gallery opening, a client's studio — Rangi now maps the room for exits, identifying
which door he could reach fastest and what might obstruct him. He chooses his seat with care, always
angling for a position that offers a sightline to the entrance and a wall at his back. His body, he
reports, never fully settles. There is a baseline tension that runs through him from the moment he
wakes, a readiness that does not distinguish between a crowded restaurant and an empty street.

His work has been the most visible casualty of this reorganisation. Documentary photography requires
presence in unpredictable environments — markets, protests, community gatherings — and Rangi has
turned down three significant commissions in the past year because he could not trust himself to
function in those settings. He accepted a smaller corporate contract instead, shooting controlled
interiors with known personnel, but even there he found himself asking colleagues repeatedly whether
they thought the building's security was adequate, whether the neighbourhood was considered safe,
whether a particular person on staff seemed "off" to them. Diane has heard variations of these
questions at home for over a year. She answers them with patience, but both of them know that her
reassurance holds for only a short time before the uncertainty returns.

What finally brought Rangi to seek help was a moment at his nephew's birthday party last month, when
a child ran toward him suddenly from behind a doorway. He was across the room before he understood
what had happened, heart hammering, certain for one terrible second that he was back in that
outbuilding. He stood outside on the porch for twenty minutes, unable to go back in. His nephew was
nine years old. Rangi told the intake clinician that he does not recognise himself in moments like
that. What he is most afraid of is that this is now simply who he is. What he is most hoping for, he
said after a long pause, is to be able to walk into a room and just be in it.
