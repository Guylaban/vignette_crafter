Boon-Nam Thongchai is a 74-year-old Thai-American man who has been married for nearly fifty years
and has worked as a welder for most of his adult life. He was referred for psychological assessment
after his wife contacted their family physician, concerned that her husband had become increasingly
withdrawn and was sleeping no more than two or three hours a night. Boon-Nam himself came to the
first session reluctantly, sitting with his hands clasped tightly in his lap, and said very little
until the third meeting, when he began, haltingly, to describe what had happened to him more than
four decades ago in the region of Thailand that borders Cambodia, during a period of cross-border
violence that displaced entire villages and left civilians caught between armed factions whose
quarrels were not their own.

He had been in his late twenties, newly married, living in a rural community where he worked as a
laborer. One afternoon, following a rumor that government forces were moving through the area,
several men from the village — Boon-Nam among them — decided to move their families toward a larger
town along a road that ran through open farmland. The convoy of vehicles was moving slowly when the
shelling began. Boon-Nam was driving a small truck. The sound of the first explosion was preceded,
he recalls, by a particular quality of engine noise from a vehicle ahead of him — a low, grinding
acceleration — and then everything became smoke and screaming. He remembers fragments: a door that
would not open, the sensation of not being able to draw breath, a woman he did not recognize lying
in the road. He does not remember how he got out of the truck or what happened in the minutes that
followed. Several people from the convoy did not survive. Boon-Nam did, and he has never been
entirely sure why.

He arrived in the United States in the early 1980s and built a life that, by most external measures,
appeared stable. He raised children, kept steady employment in a fabrication shop, and was known
among his coworkers as quiet but dependable. For many years the memories stayed at a distance, or at
least that is how he describes it. But in recent years, particularly since his retirement was
interrupted by a return to part-time welding work at a smaller shop located near a busy arterial
road, the distance collapsed. The shop sits alongside a street where delivery trucks accelerate hard
from a traffic light, and the low, grinding sound of a heavy vehicle pulling away is, for Boon-Nam,
no longer simply noise. In those moments he finds himself short of breath, his chest tightening in a
way that he knows is not his heart but that frightens him nonetheless, and he is briefly somewhere
else — or rather, somewhere else is briefly here, pressing in on him with a vividness that he
struggles to articulate. He has told no one at the shop about any of this.

He avoids driving on open highways and takes only familiar, enclosed routes. When his grandchildren
put on news programs or documentary footage involving conflict in Southeast Asia, he leaves the room
without explanation. In conversation, if the subject edges toward the past, he redirects — mentions
the weather, asks about someone's children, thinks deliberately of his workbench, the specific
weight of a welding rod in his hand. He has come to feel that open, exposed spaces carry a
particular danger he cannot fully name but will not test.

What troubles Boon-Nam most deeply is not the memories themselves but what he makes of them. He is
certain that he made a wrong decision that day — that if he had acted differently, stayed, taken
another road, refused to join the convoy — the outcome would have been different. At the same time
he is equally certain that the men responsible for the violence have lived their lives without
consequence, and this sits in him like something that has never been digested. The story feels, when
he tries to hold it, like something that happened to a stranger — too extreme, too senseless to
belong to his own life — and yet the missing parts of it, the gaps where memory simply is not,
frighten him more than what he can recall.

He came to treatment, finally, because his wife told him she was losing him. What he hopes for, he
said quietly at the end of the third session, is to sleep through the night. What he fears is that
telling the story completely will make it more real, not less.
