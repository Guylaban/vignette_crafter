Tariq Mansour is a 29-year-old single man of Lebanese descent who works as a train conductor on a
commuter rail line in the northeastern United States. He presented for an initial psychological
evaluation approximately four months after witnessing a fatal accident on the tracks, describing
persistent distress that had, in his words, "stopped feeling like something that would pass on its
own."

The incident occurred on a cold November morning, the temperature hovering just below freezing, when
Tariq was positioned at the front of his cab and saw a man on the tracks ahead. Despite emergency
braking, the collision was unavoidable. Tariq was the first crew member to reach the scene, stepping
down onto the gravel embankment in the bitter cold, and what he encountered — the visible injuries,
the blood spreading across the frost-whitened ground — became the images that would not leave him.
He had knelt briefly, instinctively, before other responders arrived, and the cold air had bitten at
his bare hands and face throughout. He was placed on administrative leave for several weeks,
interviewed by investigators, and eventually cleared to return to work. On paper, the process was
orderly. Inside, something had not resolved.

When Tariq returned to the cab in January, the job that had once felt purposeful began to feel
unbearable in ways he struggled to articulate to his supervisors. The work environment itself had
become a landscape of intrusion. On mornings when the temperature dropped sharply and the cold
pressed against his face through the cab window, or when the chill of the metal door handle met his
palm, the sensation carried him back without warning — not as a memory he chose to revisit but as
something that simply arrived, vivid and total, with the gravel and the blood and the silence before
the other responders shouted. The cold was everywhere in winter rail work: in the drafts along the
platform, in the gear he wore, in the air itself. He began layering clothing beyond what was
practical, adjusting the cab heat to uncomfortable levels, doing whatever he could to keep the
temperature on his skin from dropping below a certain threshold he could not precisely name but
recognised immediately when it was crossed.

On one afternoon run in late February, a passenger suffered a medical emergency and was helped off
the train by paramedics. Tariq saw the blood on the platform from the cab window — a small amount,
objectively — and spent the remainder of his shift gripping the controls with both hands, certain
that something catastrophic was about to happen again. He had come to believe that his perception of
safety was simply unreliable, that the ordinary assurances of colleagues or dispatchers —
"everything's fine," "it's under control" — were well-meaning but ultimately meaningless. He was
certain the world did not provide the kind of guarantees those phrases implied, and no amount of
reassurance from people he respected seemed to reach him at the level where the certainty lived.

At home, the vigilance did not lift. Tariq described lying in bed each night scanning for sounds he
could not name, unable to release the alertness that felt necessary even in his own apartment. He
had not been sleeping well for months — not because of nightmares specifically, but because allowing
himself to fall asleep felt like a surrender of attention he could not afford. He had begun
cancelling plans with friends, partly from exhaustion and partly because explaining any of this felt
impossible. A close friend, someone he had known since university, had stopped reaching out after
several unanswered messages. Tariq noted this flatly, without apparent anger, as though it were
simply the natural consequence of who he had become.

What finally brought him to seek help was a conversation with a senior conductor who had noticed the
changes in him and said, quietly, that he had seen this before and that it did not have to stay this
way. Tariq was not fully convinced of that, but he came anyway. What he feared most, he said during
the intake, was that the vigilance and the cold and the images had become permanent features of him
rather than responses to something that had happened. What he hoped for, more tentatively, was some
way to do his job again without bracing for catastrophe every time the temperature dropped.
