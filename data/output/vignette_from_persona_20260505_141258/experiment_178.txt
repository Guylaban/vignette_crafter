Khải is a 27-year-old Vietnamese-American journalist who came to the clinic after what he described,
quietly and with some difficulty, as "a long time pretending everything was fine." He lives with his
current partner in a mid-sized city, works for a regional news outlet covering local politics, and
presents as composed, articulate, and visibly uncomfortable whenever the conversation moves toward
the events of the previous two years.

The abuse began gradually, as it often does. Khải had been in a relationship with a man he met
through a mutual colleague, and for the first several months it seemed unremarkable. The violence,
when it started, was concentrated and deliberate — punches to the torso, hands around his throat,
once a blow to the side of his head that left him disoriented for hours. What stayed with him as
much as the physical pain was the particular geography of those moments: the enclosed space of an
apartment, another man's body between him and the door, the ordinary sounds of the city continuing
outside as though nothing was happening. His former partner was physically larger than him, and Khải
had learned, over time, that stillness was safer than resistance. He had learned to go quiet.

He left the relationship approximately eighteen months ago, but the leaving did not bring the relief
he had anticipated. In the weeks that followed, he found that certain ordinary moments would
suddenly collapse the distance between then and now. Riding the bus to a press briefing one morning,
the vehicle stalled in traffic for several minutes and Khải felt his chest tighten into something
close to panic — not because of the delay itself, but because he had come to understand, somewhere
below conscious reasoning, that when things stopped moving without explanation, it meant something
bad was about to happen. He began taking rideshares when he could afford them, then cycling, then
rearranging his schedule to avoid the bus altogether. His editor noticed he was late to assignments
he had previously been punctual for, and the explanation Khải offered — that he was having some
transportation issues — felt both true and entirely inadequate.

His work required him to spend time with sources, attend meetings, conduct interviews, and many of
these interactions involved being alone with men in offices, corridors, or parked cars. Each time,
something in him remained on watch. He was certain, in a way he could not argue himself out of, that
you could never really know who would harm you, that trust extended to another person was simply a
form of exposure. Even when colleagues he had known for years told him he was safe, that the story
was off the record, that they could speak freely, he found it impossible to settle. He would wake at
two or three in the morning and lie still, listening, checking.

When the memories returned — and they returned without warning, in the middle of a sentence, in the
shower, once during an interview he was conducting — they did not feel historical. They felt
immediate. He would feel the pressure in his ribs again, the specific ache along his left side, and
he would have to stop whatever he was doing, sometimes excusing himself, sometimes simply going
silent in a way his current partner had begun to notice and worry about. The emotional force of
these returns was such that he could not stay inside them long enough to process anything; he would
push the memory away and then sit with the residue of it for hours.

What he did with the anger was bury it. He had decided, not quite consciously, that anger was
dangerous — that feeling it or showing it made him someone capable of something, though he could not
always articulate what. Instead, he turned inward, cataloguing what he had done wrong, the moments
he had not left sooner, the signs he had missed. He ate less. He told himself this was about
control, about discipline, though he acknowledged in session that it was also about punishment.

What finally brought him in was his current partner asking, gently but directly, whether Khải
thought he deserved to feel better. He had not been able to answer. He came to therapy hoping, he
said, to stop feeling like a person waiting for the next thing to go wrong — though he was not yet
sure he believed that was possible.
