Marguerite Hollenbeck is a 76-year-old divorced woman of European descent who serves as a city
council member in a mid-sized Midwestern municipality. She came to the clinic in the late autumn,
following what she described, with characteristic understatement, as "a difficult few months." Her
PCL-5 score of 48 places her well within the clinical range for PTSD, though she herself resisted
that framing at first, noting that what happened to her occurred more than six decades ago and that
she had, as she put it, "built a perfectly functional life on top of it."

What Marguerite witnessed as a child was not a single incident but a sustained domestic architecture
of violence. Her father, a man she describes as charming in public and ferocious at home, beat her
mother regularly throughout her childhood in their small house in rural Ohio. The most formative
episode, the one she returns to in fragments, occurred when she was approximately nine years old.
She had been woken by the sound of her mother being struck against the kitchen wall, and when she
crept to the doorway she saw her father's face—lit from below by the stove light—contorted in a
particular way she has never been able to fully name. Her mother's silence was, she recalls, worse
than any sound. Marguerite stood frozen in that doorway for what felt like a very long time before
retreating to her room. That image—or rather, the emotional residue of it—has never fully left her.

For most of her adult life, Marguerite managed. She married, divorced, raised children, and built a
distinguished civic career. But in the past year, several things converged. Her father, now 101 and
residing in a memory care facility two hours away, is still alive. She visits him occasionally out
of a sense of duty she cannot entirely explain, and each visit leaves her destabilised for days
afterward. Seeing him—diminished, confused, sometimes gentle—does not neutralise what she carries.
If anything, the dissonance between who he is now and what she knows him to have been produces a
kind of emotional flooding she struggles to contain.

The council chambers, where Marguerite has spent years navigating budget debates and zoning
disputes, became unexpectedly fraught when a colleague, during a heated session on housing policy,
made an offhand remark about "what goes on behind closed doors in families." The comment was not
directed at her, and no one else seemed to register it. But Marguerite felt something seize in her
chest—not a memory exactly, but the full weight of a feeling she associates with that kitchen
doorway, arriving without any image attached to it. She sat through the remainder of the meeting in
a state of effortful composure, then drove home and did not sleep.

This pattern—an emotion arriving without its image, vast and sourceless—has become more frequent.
She describes it as "being ambushed from the inside." At the same time, when she does sense a memory
beginning to surface, she finds herself deflecting it before it can complete itself, turning her
attention sharply elsewhere, reaching for her phone or her notes. She has become skilled at this,
though it costs her something she cannot quite articulate. What she can articulate is that her
memories of that period feel more present than memories of her wedding, her children's births, her
decades of public service—more textured, more insistent, as though they belong to a more real
version of time.

A routine cardiac examination last spring—electrodes placed, a technician's hands on her chest, the
particular helplessness of lying still while someone else has authority over your body—left her
shaken in a way she found humiliating and could not explain to her cardiologist.

What finally brought her to seek help was simpler than any of this: her youngest daughter asked,
gently, whether she was all right. Marguerite found she could not answer. She had come to believe,
over a long life, that endurance was its own form of resolution. What she most fears now is that she
was wrong about that. What she most hopes for is something she phrases carefully: "to be able to
remember without being taken back."
