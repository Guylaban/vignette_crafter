Maren Holst is a 28-year-old elementary school teacher of Norwegian descent who presented for
psychological assessment following a prolonged period of stalking that began approximately fourteen
months ago. She lives with her long-term partner in a mid-sized city and had, by all accounts, built
a life she found genuinely satisfying — a stable classroom, a close circle of friends, a
relationship she described as grounding. That sense of safety collapsed incrementally over the
course of nearly five months, during which a former acquaintance pursued her with an escalating
persistence that moved from unwanted messages to physical surveillance of her home, her school, and
her daily routes.

The stalking did not announce itself as dangerous all at once. It began with texts she dismissed as
awkward, then progressed to appearances she told herself were coincidences. The moment that seems to
have lodged most deeply occurred on a Tuesday evening when Maren was locking up her classroom after
an after-school meeting. The hallway was empty and unusually quiet — that particular, pressurised
silence of a building that has just exhaled its last occupants — when she became aware of breathing
behind her. Close, deliberate, unhurried. She turned to find the man standing less than two metres
away, watching her. He said nothing. The encounter lasted perhaps twenty seconds before he walked
away, but in those seconds Maren found herself entirely unable to speak, her body rigid, her mind
producing no coherent language for what was happening. She eventually called the police, and their
arrival — officers asking her to recount events she could not sequence, pressing for details she
could not locate — left her feeling exposed and disbelieved rather than protected.

The stalking was eventually addressed through a restraining order, but the legal resolution brought
Maren little relief. In the months that followed, her experience of the world reorganised itself
around a set of signals she had learned, in the most visceral way, to treat as warnings. The sound
of heavy breathing — a colleague with a cold, a child running in from recess — would stop her mid-
sentence, her attention seized and narrowed. Unusual quiet in the school corridors, the kind that
falls between bells, would send a cold alertness through her that she could not talk herself out of.
She had come to believe that silence of that quality was not neutral; it was the moment before
something happened. She began scanning hallways before entering them, positioning herself in her
classroom so that she could see the door at all times, and tracking the movements of anyone who
lingered near the school entrance.

Strangers approaching her in ordinary situations — a parent wanting to introduce themselves, a
colleague she hadn't met — registered in her body as threat before her mind could intervene. She had
come to understand people, broadly, as individuals whose intentions could not be assumed benign.
When police cars appeared in her vicinity, even parked and unattended, she felt a complicated wash
of fear and something closer to shame, a re-experiencing of the night she had stood in that hallway
unable to adequately explain what had happened to her. She had come to feel, with a certainty that
resisted contradiction, that her life as she had imagined it was no longer available to her.

Her memory of the stalking period is fragmented in a way she finds deeply disorienting. Certain
moments are rendered in her mind with almost unbearable clarity — the texture of the hallway floor,
the precise quality of that silence — while other stretches are simply absent. Fragments surface
without invitation and then vanish before she can examine them. When she attempts to describe her
experience to her partner, she finds herself stranded mid-sentence, aware of what she knows but
unable to produce words adequate to it. This has placed a quiet but accumulating strain on the
relationship she once described as her anchor.

What finally brought Maren to seek help was a parent-teacher evening three weeks ago, during which
she found herself unable to remain in the room when a father she did not recognise walked in late
and stood near the back. She left her own classroom, stood in the corridor, and wept. What she most
fears, she said at intake, is that this is simply who she is now. What she most hopes is that she is
wrong.
