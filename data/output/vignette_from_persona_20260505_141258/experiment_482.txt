Caleb Morrow is a 30-year-old carpenter and husband who presented for his first psychological
evaluation approximately eight months after witnessing a serious industrial accident at a
residential construction site where he had been working. He arrived visibly guarded, speaking in
short sentences and frequently stopping mid-thought, as though the words themselves were obstacles
he could not quite move around.

The event occurred on an otherwise unremarkable Tuesday morning. Caleb and a colleague, a man he had
worked alongside for nearly three years, were framing the second floor of a new build when the
scaffolding gave way. Caleb did not fall. His colleague did, landing on a partially assembled
subfloor strewn with exposed nails and debris. Caleb was the first to reach him. He remembers
pressing his hands against the man's torso to slow the bleeding — the warmth and wetness of it, the
way the pressure of his own palms felt against torn skin — and he remembers the metallic taste in
his mouth, which he later attributed to having bitten the inside of his cheek in the seconds after
the fall. His colleague survived but required multiple surgeries and has not returned to work. Caleb
has not spoken to him since.

In the weeks that followed, Caleb noticed that certain moments at work would stop him entirely.
Passing a coworker with a gash from a utility knife, even a minor one, could bring the full scene
back with a completeness that felt less like remembering and more like being returned there against
his will. The memory did not arrive as a story he could tell himself; it arrived as sensation — the
weight of his hands pressing down, the smell of sawdust and blood mixed together, the specific taste
at the back of his throat. He has told his wife only that something happened at work and that he is
dealing with it. When she has reached for him in ordinary moments of affection — a hand on his
shoulder, her fingers brushing his arm — he has flinched or pulled away without meaning to, and he
cannot explain to her why her touch feels, in those instants, like something he is not entitled to
receive.

Over the following months, Caleb began eating heavily in the evenings, working through bags of chips
or large second portions of dinner in a way he described as almost automatic, something his hands
did while his mind went somewhere else. He has also developed a habit he finds shameful to describe:
a deliberate effort, when distress becomes acute, to go somewhere inward and blank — to simply not
be present in his own body. He does not have a name for what he is doing. He knows only that it
offers a few minutes of relief and that he always comes back feeling worse.

He has almost entirely stopped thinking about the accident in any directed way, sidestepping the
subject in his own mind with a consistency that has made it impossible to process what he witnessed.
He told the intake clinician that he cannot seem to put what happened into a sentence that makes
sense — that when he tries, the words feel wrong or too small, and he stops.

Caleb has come to believe, with quiet certainty, that he is not the same person he was before that
morning. He was once someone who trusted his own hands, his own judgment, his own steadiness in a
crisis. Now he is certain he cannot rely on himself in the ways that matter. He described his life
as feeling broken in a way he does not expect to be fixed, and he used the word destroyed without
drama, as a plain statement of fact. He fears the damage is permanent — not a wound that heals but a
structural change, like a beam that has been stressed past its tolerance.

What finally brought him to seek help was his wife telling him, gently but directly, that she felt
she had lost him. He does not want to lose her. He arrived hoping, cautiously, that someone might
help him find language for something that has so far existed only as sensation and dread.
