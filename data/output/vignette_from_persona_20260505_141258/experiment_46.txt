Rangi Parata is a 63-year-old Māori man, married and working as a senior financial analyst at a mid-
sized investment firm in Auckland's financial district. He was referred for psychological assessment
following a significant deterioration in his functioning over the past eighteen months, tracing back
to the night his car was struck by a freight truck on a rural highway outside the city.

It had been a late evening in winter. Rangi had been driving home from a client dinner, the road
dark and largely empty. A truck, whose driver had fallen asleep at the wheel, crossed the centre
line and hit Rangi's vehicle almost head-on at speed. The impact was catastrophic. His car was spun
off the road and came to rest in a drainage ditch, partially submerged. For what felt to Rangi like
an eternity — later confirmed to have been over forty minutes — he was trapped in the wreckage in
near-total darkness, the engine ticking and groaning around him, the smell of petrol and hot metal
filling the cabin. He could hear other vehicles passing on the road above, their engines and tyres
audible but their headlights unable to find him in the ditch. He was certain, at several points
during those forty minutes, that he was going to die alone in the dark.

He survived with serious but ultimately treatable injuries. Physically, he recovered well.
Psychologically, the months that followed told a different story. He returned to work after three
months of medical leave, and it was there that the shape of his suffering began to reveal itself.
The firm's underground car park, dimly lit at the far end near the stairwell, became something Rangi
could not simply walk through. The moment he moved from the brightness of the street into that
enclosed, shadowed space, his body would respond before he had formed any conscious thought — heart
hammering, breath shortening, legs slowing. He had come to believe that his nervous system simply
could not be trusted to wait for his mind to catch up, and this frightened him as much as the dark
itself.

The smell of petrol, encountered at a service station or even near a colleague's car, could pull him
back into the wreck without warning — not as a recollection he chose to visit, but as something
immediate and present, the darkness and the smell and the sounds of the highway above him arriving
all at once as though the accident were unfolding again in real time. He would sometimes hear,
clearly and involuntarily, the grinding of metal and the distant passing of engines, as vivid as if
he were still in the ditch. These moments were not, to Rangi, memories in any ordinary sense. They
felt like the present tense.

He had stopped driving at night entirely. He had also, more gradually and more reluctantly, begun
avoiding any situation that required him to be in a dark or enclosed space — underground venues,
windowless meeting rooms, the cinema he and his wife had visited together regularly for years. His
wife, Hinerangi, had at first been patient and accommodating, but the cumulative weight of his
withdrawal had begun to strain their relationship. She had asked, carefully and with evident love,
whether he was perhaps avoiding things that might actually help him heal. He had not been able to
explain to her why thinking about the event felt, to him, like a risk he could not afford to take —
that if he allowed himself to go there, he was certain he would not be able to hold himself
together.

At work, Rangi had taken to sitting only in seats that faced the door and arriving early enough to
identify every exit before colleagues filled the room. He woke most nights in the small hours, lying
rigid in the dark beside his sleeping wife, listening and checking. He had come to believe that
vigilance was not a burden but a necessity — that the world had revealed itself as a place where
catastrophe could arrive without warning, and that the only rational response was to remain
perpetually alert.

What finally brought Rangi to seek help was a morning when he sat in his car in the firm's car park
for forty-five minutes, unable to make himself go in. He had sat there, a man of thirty years'
professional experience, and wept. What he most hoped for, he said at intake, was to be able to sit
in a dark room with his wife and watch a film again. What he most feared was that the person capable
of doing that no longer existed.
