Esperanza Villanueva is a 78-year-old Guatemalan-born woman who has worked for the past two decades
as a librarian at a small public branch in a predominantly Latino neighborhood of San Antonio. She
is married, soft-spoken, and known among her colleagues for her meticulous care of the collection
and her patience with elderly patrons who come in primarily for company. She presented for
psychological evaluation after her youngest daughter, concerned by her mother's increasing
withdrawal and sleeplessness, persuaded her to seek help. Esperanza agreed, though she arrived at
the first session holding her handbag in her lap with both hands, as if prepared to leave at any
moment.

The event Esperanza identifies as the origin of her current distress occurred more than fifty years
ago, when she was a young woman in her mid-twenties living in a rural village outside Huehuetenango.
During a period of intense military counterinsurgency operations, soldiers came to her village
before dawn. She remembers the smell — woodsmoke mixed with something chemical and acrid, a burning
she could not identify then and cannot name now — as the first signal that something was
catastrophically wrong. Her husband at the time, her two small children, and several neighbors fled
into the surrounding hillside. In the chaos of that flight, Esperanza became separated from a
younger sister who had been staying with her. She never saw her sister again. The family eventually
made their way through Mexico and crossed into the United States, arriving with almost nothing.
Esperanza rebuilt her life with characteristic determination, raised her children, remarried, became
a citizen, and found in the library a kind of sanctuary — ordered, quiet, and governed by rules she
could understand.

For decades she managed. But in the months following her retirement from full-time hours and a
reduction in her weekly schedule, the structure that had held things in place began to thin. She
found herself sitting in rooms with too much silence in them. The memories, which had always been
present at the edges, began arriving with a sensory immediacy she found deeply disorienting. When
she thinks about that night, she does not experience it as a narrative she can recount — she has
told her daughter almost nothing, and in session she reaches for words and finds them inadequate,
falling silent and gesturing vaguely with one hand. What arrives instead is the smell: woodsmoke and
that chemical sharpness, which she sometimes encounters near the library's boiler room or when a
patron comes in from a bonfire. On those occasions she has learned to find a reason to step outside,
though she cannot fully explain to herself or others why she must.

Esperanza spends considerable mental energy going over the events of that night, returning again and
again to the question of why this happened to her family, to her village, to her sister. She had
come to believe, in a way she experiences as factual rather than interpretive, that she had failed
her sister by not turning back, and the guilt that accompanies any detailed recollection of the
event is immediate and physical — a tightening in her chest she associates with moral weight rather
than medical symptom. She blames herself with a specificity and consistency that has not diminished
across decades.

At work, she remains watchful in a way her colleagues have noticed without understanding. She scans
the library entrance each time the door opens, and she has gradually narrowed the spaces in which
she feels genuinely safe to her office, her own home, and her daughter's kitchen. Staff meetings in
the larger conference room leave her tense and distracted, and her difficulty concentrating during
those gatherings has led her supervisor to gently inquire whether she is managing her workload. She
keeps herself occupied as much as possible — volunteering for additional cataloguing tasks, filling
her evenings with activity — and experiences stillness as something to be avoided rather than
sought.

What finally brought her to the clinic was a morning when she smelled the boiler exhaust and stood
frozen in the library corridor for what a colleague later told her was several minutes. She has no
clear memory of that interval. What she hopes for, she said quietly at the end of the intake, is
simply to stop feeling that she is still running — and what she fears most is that she deserves to
be.
