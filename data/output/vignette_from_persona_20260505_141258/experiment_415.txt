Rangi Tūhoe is a 54-year-old Māori man, married and currently serving as a combat pilot with decades
of operational experience behind him. He was referred for psychological assessment following a life-
threatening accident that occurred eight months ago during a routine training sortie, and he has
only recently agreed to engage with mental health support — a decision that, by his own account,
took considerable internal negotiation.

The incident occurred when a catastrophic mechanical failure caused Rangi's aircraft to enter an
uncontrolled descent at low altitude. For approximately forty seconds — a duration he can
reconstruct with meticulous, almost clinical precision — he worked through emergency procedures
while the aircraft continued to fall. He ejected at the last viable moment, sustaining significant
physical injuries on landing in rough terrain. What he describes most vividly, however, is not the
physical impact but the forty seconds themselves: the particular quality of the cockpit sounds as
warning systems cascaded, the sensation of the stick offering nothing, the moment he understood that
the aircraft would not recover. He survived. The aircraft did not.

In the weeks that followed, once his physical injuries had stabilised, Rangi noticed that something
in him had not returned to baseline alongside his body. He continued to report for duty in a limited
capacity, and it was here — in the very environment that had defined his professional identity for
thirty years — that the first persistent difficulties announced themselves. Warning tones during
simulator sessions, the particular vibration signature of certain aircraft on the flight line, and
the visual geometry of low-altitude approaches had each become charged with a quality he struggled
to articulate. He would feel his attention narrow and his body organise itself around the
possibility of emergency, even in conditions that were objectively controlled and safe.

What Rangi had not anticipated was how far this watchfulness would travel beyond the cockpit. At
home, in the house he shares with his wife Aroha, he found himself unable to sit with his back to a
doorway, unable to settle into a room without first noting the exits. He would stand briefly at the
threshold of their kitchen or living room, eyes moving to windows and doors before he allowed
himself to enter fully. Aroha noticed before he named it. He told her it was habit, a pilot's
situational awareness. He had come to believe, in a way that felt entirely rational to him, that
this monitoring was simply what a man in his position owed to himself and his family — that to stop
scanning would be to make the same mistake twice.

Alongside the hypervigilance, something quieter and more frightening had taken hold. Rangi found
himself sitting across from Aroha at dinner, watching her laugh at something on television, and
feeling a distance he could not bridge — not anger, not sadness exactly, but an absence where
feeling should have been. He had come to believe that whatever the accident had taken from him in
those forty seconds, it had taken something irreversible. He no longer recognised himself in his own
responses. The man who had once felt fierce pride at his daughter's graduation, who had wept
privately when his father died, seemed to have belonged to a version of himself that no longer
existed. He was certain the damage was permanent — not a wound in recovery, but a structural change,
the way metal fatigues and does not return to its original form.

This conviction had shaped the months since the accident in ways that had quietly accumulated. He
had declined invitations from colleagues, shortened conversations with Aroha, and stopped attending
the community gatherings that had anchored his sense of belonging within his whānau. Aroha had
eventually told him plainly that she was losing him, not to absence but to a version of him she did
not know. It was this — not the sleeplessness, not the vigilance, not the clinical recommendation —
that finally brought him to seek help.

What Rangi fears most is confirmation of what he already suspects: that the person he was before the
descent is gone, and that no amount of talking will retrieve him. What he hopes for, more quietly,
is to be wrong.
