Kai was seventy-one years old and had worked as a cashier at a large grocery store for nearly two
decades. He was a large, quiet man of Oceania heritage, married for forty-six years to a woman he
still called his best friend. He came to therapy at his wife’s insistence, though he could not say
what he hoped would change. What he could say, in fragments, was that something had broken in him
three years ago, on a Tuesday afternoon in late October.

He had been bagging groceries at register four when a young man in a hooded jacket walked through
the automatic doors. The doors opened with a soft hydraulic hiss. Kai remembered that sound. He
remembered the man stopping, reaching into his jacket, and pulling out a handgun. Kai did not
remember what happened next. He knew, intellectually, that he had hit the floor, that the security
guard had tackled the man, that no one was shot, that the police came. But when he tried to tell his
wife about it that night, his mouth went dry and the words stopped. He could only shake his head.
The story was not there. He had pieces—the hiss of the door, the grip of the gun, the tile floor
against his cheek—but they did not connect into anything he could hold. He had come to believe that
his mind had failed him in the moment, and that this failure meant something terrible about his own
weakness.

In the weeks that followed, the automatic doors at work became unbearable. Every time they slid
open, his chest tightened and his hands went cold. He started arriving an hour early so he could be
at his register before the first customers came in. He asked for a lane farthest from the entrance.
His manager, a young woman who had not been there that day, agreed at first, but over time the
accommodation frayed. Kai could not explain why he needed it. When she pressed him, he felt the same
blankness come over him—the story dissolving before he could speak it. He was certain that if he
tried to tell her what happened, he would sound crazy, or worse, that he would start crying and
never stop. So he said nothing, and she moved him back to register one.

That was when the watching began. He found himself scanning every customer’s hands, their jackets,
the way they walked through the door. He watched for sudden movements—a child reaching for a candy
bar, a teenager pulling out a phone—and each time his body reacted as if the gun were already out.
He startled so badly one afternoon when a coworker tapped his shoulder that he knocked a display of
canned soup to the floor. The other cashiers laughed. Kai did not laugh. He felt dead inside,
hollowed out, as if the man who used to joke with customers and remember their names had been
replaced by a trembling machine that only watched for threat.

At home, the door sounds followed him. His wife was a woman who liked to open windows, to let air
move through the house. Every time a door slammed—a bedroom, a closet, the trunk of the car—he felt
the same spike of fear. He began walking around the house with his hands half-raised, ready. He
checked the locks three times before bed. He stopped going to his grandson’s first birthday party
because he could not bear the sound of babies crying; the high, urgent pitch cut through him like
the alarm he had never heard, the one that should have gone off before the man walked in. His wife
went without him, and when she came home she was quiet. He knew she was hurt, but he could not make
himself explain.

What ate at him most, though, was not the fear. It was the injustice. The young man had been
arrested, but the charges were reduced. Kai had attended one court date, sat in the back, watched
the man’s lawyer argue that it was a misunderstanding, that the gun was unloaded, that no one was
hurt. No one was hurt. The words had followed him home. The people who were supposed to protect
him—the security guard, the police, the district attorney—had let the man walk away with probation.
There had been no trial, no reckoning. Kai had come to believe that the system was a lie, that
safety was a lie, that the only person who could protect him was himself, and he was too slow, too
old, too frozen to do it.

He came to therapy because his wife told him she could not live this way anymore. She said she
missed him. He did not know how to tell her that he missed himself too. What he was most afraid of
was that the story would never come together, that he would spend the rest of his life jumping at
doors and watching strangers’ hands, that the deadness inside him was permanent. But he showed up.
He sat in the chair, hands on his knees, and waited to see if someone could help him find the
missing pieces.
