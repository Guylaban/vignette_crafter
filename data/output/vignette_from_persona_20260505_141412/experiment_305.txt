Leila, a forty-three-year-old pharmacist, could not remember a time when the smell of rubbing
alcohol had been neutral. For most people it meant a clean wound, a routine shot. For Leila, it was
the scent that preceded the blow. Her earliest memory of it was the sharp, medicinal tang that clung
to her father’s breath after he had been drinking, a smell that saturated the hallway outside her
childhood bedroom. She had learned, long before she could articulate it, that when that smell
arrived, a command would follow—clean your room, come here, don’t look at me like that—and that no
matter what she did, the command was a trap. Compliance did not prevent the slap. Defiance
guaranteed it. The authority in his voice was absolute, and it was never, ever safe.

Now, Leila worked in a busy retail pharmacy, a job she had once found orderly and meaningful. The
glass counter, the white coats, the rows of bottles—it had felt like a place of healing. But over
the past year, the pharmacy had become a minefield. Every time her manager, a tall man with a
clipped tone, walked over and said, “Leila, I need you to check the order,” her stomach dropped. Her
throat closed. The words were reasonable, even polite, but to Leila they arrived as a direct echo of
a voice from thirty-five years ago. She would freeze, nod, and then stand in the back aisle, unable
to remember what she had been told to do. Her concentration had become so fragmented that she had
made two dispensing errors in the last quarter—errors she caught, but only just. She had started to
believe that she was failing at the one role that still mattered: being a competent professional who
could be trusted.

At home, the pattern was worse. Leila’s husband, Samir, had noticed her pulling away. She no longer
agreed to go out to dinner with friends, and she had started declining invitations to family
gatherings. She told herself it was exhaustion, but the truth was more specific. She had come to
believe that any situation that might make her feel happy or hopeful was a trap. Hope, she had
learned, was a setup for disappointment so crushing it could break you. So she avoided it. She
avoided the feeling of a good laugh, the anticipation of a weekend trip, the warmth of Samir’s hand
on her back. She kept her emotional world flat and grey, because flat and grey felt safe.

But flat and grey did not stop the memories. They came without warning, and when they did, they were
not like ordinary remembering. They were more real than the present. Leila would be standing at the
sink, washing a dish, and suddenly she was seven years old again, pressed against the wall, the
smell of alcohol thick in the air, her father’s hand raised. The emotion that hit her was so
overwhelming—a tidal wave of terror and shame—that she would have to drop the dish and sit down, her
hands shaking. At other times, the opposite happened. She would recall the same scene, but feel
nothing at all. She would watch it like a movie about a stranger, a little girl who was not her, and
the numbness was somehow more frightening than the terror, because it made her wonder if she had
ever been real.

The helplessness that had defined her childhood had become a permanent belief. She was certain that
she could not stop bad things from happening, that she was fundamentally powerless. Nothing in her
life felt meaningful anymore. She went through the motions—work, dinner, sleep—but the thread of
purpose had snapped. When the intrusive thoughts became unbearable, she had developed a private,
shameful strategy. She would dig her fingernails into her forearm until she broke the skin, or press
a paperclip into her palm. The sharp, physical pain cut through the emotional chaos, pulling her
back to the present. It was the only thing that worked, and she hated herself for needing it.

Sleep had become a battleground. She lay in bed each night, rigid, her ears tuned to every creak of
the house, every distant car. She was not sure what she was waiting for, only that she could not let
her guard down. The false alarms had become constant: a door slamming in the pharmacy, a man raising
his voice on the subway, Samir walking into the room too quickly. Her body reacted first—heart
pounding, breath shallow—and her mind caught up later, whispering, *You are safe. It is over.* But
her body did not believe it.

What finally brought Leila to a therapist’s office was a single moment. She had been standing in the
pharmacy aisle, holding a bottle of antiseptic, when the smell hit her. She had frozen, unable to
move, and her manager had found her there, staring at the bottle, tears streaming down her face. She
had told him she was fine, but she was not fine. She was terrified that she was losing her mind. She
was terrified that she would hurt herself badly one day, or that Samir would finally give up on her.
She came to therapy not because she believed it would help, but because she had run out of other
options. What she wanted, more than anything, was to feel something other than this constant,
watchful dread. She wanted to believe that a life without the smell of alcohol in it could be a life
worth living.
