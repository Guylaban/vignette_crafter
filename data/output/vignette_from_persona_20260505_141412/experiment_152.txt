Margaret was seventy-nine years old, a widow living alone in the small house she had shared with her
husband for forty years, and she still worked three days a week as a tailor in a dry-cleaning shop
downtown. She had been a happy person once—she could say that without hesitation, as though speaking
about someone else. She remembered herself as someone who laughed easily, who looked forward to the
holidays, who called her friends just to chat. That woman felt like a stranger now. What she felt
instead was a kind of deadness, a hollow place behind her ribs where feelings used to live. She had
come to believe that nothing good could happen to her anymore, and that belief felt like a fact, not
a mood.

The event that had changed everything happened fifty-seven years ago, when she was twenty-two and
serving as a nurse in a field hospital during the Vietnam War. She had been assigned to triage
during a mass casualty event after a mortar attack. What she remembered most vividly was not the
chaos or the noise but the blood—the way it pooled on the concrete floor, the way it soaked through
her uniform at the knees, the way it kept coming no matter how many tourniquets she tied. But she
could not put the memories in the right order. She knew what had happened, but the sequence eluded
her: she would see a young soldier’s face, then the ceiling, then the floor, then the face again,
and she could not say which came first. She had tried to tell her husband about it once, many years
ago, but the words would not come. She could see it, feel it, smell it, but she could not seem to
put the frightening event into words. She had never tried again.

What did come, unbidden and unwelcome, were the intrusive thoughts. They arrived without warning—a
flash of an image, a smell of antiseptic and mud, a sound she could not name. The worst triggers
were certain conversation topics. If someone at the shop mentioned war, or the military, or even a
medical show on television, Margaret would feel her chest tighten and her mind go white. She had
learned to steer conversations away, to excuse herself to the back room. But the most potent trigger
of all was the sight of blood. A customer with a cut finger, a nosebleed, even a small red stain on
a piece of fabric—these would send her reeling. She had begun to check every piece of clothing
before she touched it, scanning for red. She had started wearing reading glasses not for her eyes
but to blur the details of the world.

In the months and years that followed, Margaret had developed habits that made sense to her even as
they narrowed her life. She began avoiding her friends. It was not that she disliked them; it was
that being around them required her to feel something, and she had learned that feeling anything at
all was dangerous. She pushed away positive emotions because they felt unsafe—a moment of happiness,
a flicker of hope, these seemed to invite disaster, as though the universe would punish her for
letting her guard down. She avoided situations that might make her feel happy or hopeful: she
stopped going to birthday parties, she declined invitations to dinner, she stopped watching the news
because the good stories hurt worse than the bad ones. The consequence was a slow, quiet erosion of
her social world. Her friends stopped calling. Her daughter, who lived two states away, had begun to
sound frustrated on the phone. Margaret knew she was disappearing, but she did not know how to stop.

When she remembered the event, what she felt most was not fear or sadness but intense rage. It came
up from somewhere deep and wordless, a hot fury at the army, at the war, at the young men who had
died, at herself for surviving. The rage frightened her more than the sadness ever had, because it
felt like it belonged to someone else, someone violent and out of control. She had learned to
swallow it, to keep her face still, to say nothing.

What finally brought Margaret to seek help was not the rage or the memories or the avoidance, but
the numbness. She had looked at herself in the mirror one morning and realized she could not
remember the last time she had felt anything at all—not joy, not grief, not even anger. She was not
sure if she wanted to feel again, but she was certain she could not go on like this. She was afraid
that if she opened the door to her memories, she would drown in them. But she was also afraid that
if she did not, she would simply fade away, a ghost in her own life, until there was nothing left.
