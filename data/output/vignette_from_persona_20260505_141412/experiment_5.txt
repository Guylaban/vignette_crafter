Minh was twenty years old when he arrived in the region as a relief worker, driven by a sense of
purpose he had carried since his family’s own history of displacement. He was assigned to a small
field hospital near a contested town, where his role was to coordinate the distribution of medical
supplies and help triage incoming casualties. For the first three months, the work was brutal but
manageable. He learned to read the distant thud of artillery, to tell by the pitch which direction
the shells were heading. He learned to work alongside armed escorts from the local peacekeeping
force, men whose uniforms—heavy blue helmets, dark fatigues, the insignia of an international
mission—meant safety, meant order.

That changed on a Tuesday afternoon. A convoy arrived under fire. Minh was outside the hospital
entrance when a military jeep screeched to a halt, and the soldiers inside—wearing those same blue
helmets, those same dark fatigues—jumped out shouting. For a moment, Minh thought they were coming
to help unload the wounded. Then one of them turned and looked directly at him. The soldier’s eyes
were fixed, unblinking. Minh felt the gaze land on him like a weight. The soldier raised his rifle
and began walking toward him, still watching, still staring. Minh’s legs locked. He could not move.
The soldier’s mouth was moving, but Minh heard only the ringing in his ears. Later, he learned that
the soldier had been yelling at him to get down, that there was a sniper in the building behind him.
But in that moment, what Minh registered was not an order—it was a threat. The uniform, the weapon,
the eyes: all of it fused into a single image of imminent danger.

The convoy was secured. The sniper was neutralized. Minh went back to work. But something had broken
inside him. In the weeks that followed, he found himself scanning every uniformed figure he saw, his
chest tightening before his mind could catch up. His body began to react as if it had its own
memory, separate from his thoughts. His hands would sweat. His heart would hammer. He started
avoiding the soldiers he had once worked beside, taking longer routes through the compound to avoid
passing their posts. He told himself it was just stress, that he needed rest. But when he finally
returned home to the United States, the reaction did not stop.

Now, at twenty-two, Minh is a student again, living in a college town. He had hoped that distance
from the war zone would reset his nervous system. Instead, the world has become a minefield of
ordinary moments. A police car idling at an intersection makes his throat close. A campus security
officer in a dark uniform walking past the library forces him to cross the street. He cannot look at
their faces. He cannot meet their eyes. He has come to believe that if he makes eye contact with
someone in authority—a police officer, a guard, even a professor in a blazer who looks too
official—they will see something in him, something that marks him as a target. He knows this is
irrational. He tells himself that. But his body does not listen.

The worst part, for Minh, is what has happened to his social life. Before the deployment, he had a
close circle of friends from his relief work training program. They were the people who understood
the world he had entered. But now, when they invite him to coffee or to dinner, he cannot bring
himself to go. He imagines sitting across from them, trying to explain why he flinched at a car
horn, why he can’t stop looking over his shoulder. The thought exhausts him. He has stopped
responding to their messages. He tells himself he is sparing them the burden of his brokenness, but
the truth is more painful: he is ashamed. He had gone to help, and instead he had been broken. He
had failed in the most important role he had ever taken on. He was supposed to be the one who stayed
calm, who kept the supplies flowing, who held it together. Instead, he had frozen. And now, back in
a safe country, in a safe town, he cannot stop bad things from happening to him because he cannot
stop his own body from creating danger where there is none.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment three weeks ago. He was walking to a lecture when
a campus police SUV pulled up beside him at a crosswalk. The officer inside glanced at him—a neutral
glance, probably just checking for traffic—and Minh felt his vision narrow. He stumbled backward
onto the curb, nearly fell, and stood there shaking while the SUV drove on. A passing student asked
if he was okay. Minh could not answer. He walked home and did not leave his apartment for four days.
On the fifth day, he called the university counseling center. He is not sure what he hopes for,
exactly. He is afraid that talking about it will make it worse, that naming the fear will give it
more power. But he is also afraid of staying like this—afraid of uniforms, afraid of eye contact,
afraid of his own friends, and most of all, afraid of a body that keeps telling him he is still in
danger, even though he is safe.
