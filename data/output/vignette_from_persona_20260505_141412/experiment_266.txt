Javier had been a cashier at the same grocery store in downtown Baltimore for twenty-three years,
long enough that most of his regulars knew his name and his story—or at least the version he used to
tell. He was seventy-six now, a widower of eight years, and until recently he had taken quiet pride
in the precision of his work: the way he could bag six items in a single motion, the way he
remembered which customers liked their eggs double-bagged and which ones wanted their bread on top.
That was before the memory started coming back not as a story he could tell, but as something that
happened to him, again, in the middle of the checkout line.

The event itself had taken place in 1992, during the Siege of Sarajevo, where Javier had been
working as a relief coordinator for a small humanitarian organization. He had been born in the
United States to Spanish immigrant parents, but he had felt a pull to go, to help, to prove
something to himself about what kind of man he was. One afternoon, his convoy was stopped at a
checkpoint that was not supposed to be there. He remembered the crack of the rifle, the way the
glass in the driver’s side window turned into a white spiderweb before it fell inward, the warm
wetness on his neck that he later learned was the driver’s blood. He had lain on the floor of the
van for what felt like hours, listening to the shouting in a language he half-understood, certain
that at any moment the door would open and he would be next. He was not injured. He was not taken.
But something in him had never really left that floor.

For decades, he managed. He returned to the States, married, raised two daughters, worked the
register, went to Mass on Sundays. He told himself the war was behind him, that he had been lucky,
that other people had suffered far worse. But after his wife died, the old vigilance crept back.
Now, in the checkout aisle, Javier found himself scanning customers not for their groceries but for
their hands—watching for anything that could become a weapon, a sudden movement, a pocket that might
hold more than a wallet. He had become certain, without quite being able to say when it started,
that the world was a dangerous place, and that he was no longer the man who could navigate it. The
thought arrived fully formed, as if it had always been true: he was no longer capable of doing the
things that had once defined him.

The driving was the first thing to go. He stopped getting behind the wheel after a morning in June
when, pulling out of his driveway, he saw a delivery truck idling at the corner and felt his chest
clamp shut. The truck was just a truck, but in that moment it was the armored vehicle from the
checkpoint, and his hands were shaking so badly he had to pull over. He told his daughter he was
getting too old for the road, that his eyes weren’t what they used to be. But the truth was he could
not bear being inside a vehicle at all—not driving, not riding as a passenger. The bus was worse,
because he could not control who sat next to him, could not see what they were holding. So he
walked. He walked two miles to the grocery store every morning, rain or heat, because walking meant
he could see everything coming.

At work, he had developed a system. He kept his back to the wall. He scanned the aisles over the
heads of customers. He watched other people closely for signs that they might harm him—the way a man
adjusted his jacket, the way a woman held her purse too tightly. He knew, intellectually, that these
were ordinary people buying milk and bread. But his body did not believe it. His body believed that
danger was imminent, that he needed to stay alert, and so he did, even when it exhausted him. He had
difficulty falling asleep at night because he needed to stay alert, and when he finally did sleep,
he woke at the smallest sound—a car door, a creaking floorboard—his heart already pounding, his mind
already running through the exits.

He had lost his faith somewhere along the way, though he could not pinpoint the moment. It was not a
dramatic renunciation; it was more like a quiet evaporation. He still went to Mass because his wife
had loved it, and because the ritual was familiar, but he no longer believed that anyone was
listening. He thought often about why the event had happened to him, why he had been spared when the
driver had not, what it meant that he had gone on to live a full life while so many others had not.
He did not arrive at answers. He arrived only at a kind of hollow bewilderment.

Sometimes, when a customer asked him a question or when a child dropped a jar in the next aisle, a
memory would surface—not as a thought, but as a picture, a vivid image of the shattered windshield
or the pattern of blood on the driver’s shirt. He could see it perfectly, in color, and yet he felt
nothing. He felt completely numb, as if it had happened to a stranger, as if the man on that floor
was someone else entirely. And then, without warning, the numbness would disappear and he would be
back inside the moment—the smell of diesel and copper, the weight of the body pressed against his,
the certainty that he was about to die. It was happening right now, even though it was thirty years
ago.

He had started pushing away positive emotions because they felt unsafe. When his granddaughter
hugged him, he felt a flash of warmth and then a wave of something like dread, as if happiness
itself were a trap, as if letting himself feel good meant letting his guard down. He had come to
believe that vigilance was the only thing keeping him alive, and that to relax was to invite
disaster.

What finally brought him to the clinic was a panic attack in the middle of a Wednesday afternoon
shift. A customer had dropped a glass bottle of olive oil, and the sound of it shattering on the
linoleum had sent Javier to his knees behind the register, his hands over his ears, his breath
coming in short, ragged gasps. His manager had called his daughter, who had driven him home—he could
not get in the car, so she had walked with him, slowly, for two miles—and then she had made an
appointment. He sat in the waiting room now, his hands folded in his lap, staring at the floor. He
was terrified of what it would mean to talk about these things, to let them out into the light. But
he was also, somewhere beneath the fear, hoping that someone could tell him how to stop living
inside that van.
