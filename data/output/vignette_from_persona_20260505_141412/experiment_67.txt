Leilani was forty-six years old, a veterinarian who had spent most of her career treating companion
animals in a quiet suburban practice. She had grown up in a coastal town in the Pacific Islands
before moving to the United States in her twenties, and she still spoke of home with a kind of
warmth that seemed to belong to another lifetime. That lifetime ended on a Tuesday afternoon three
years ago, when she and her husband were visiting her cousin’s family in a region that had suddenly
become a war zone. They had been caught in a market during an aerial bombardment. Leilani remembered
the sound first—a whistle that grew into a roar, then the ground shaking so violently that she
thought the earth itself was splitting open. She had thrown herself over her cousin’s youngest
child, a boy of four, and she remembered the weight of debris raining down on her back, the smell of
dust and something metallic, and the way the boy’s small body trembled beneath hers. She did not
remember how they got out. That part of the memory came in fragments—a hand pulling her arm, a wall
of fire, a stranger’s face covered in blood—and none of the pieces seemed to fit together in a way
that made a coherent story. What she did remember, with a clarity that shamed her, was that
afterward, in the relative safety of a United Nations shelter, she had been unable to stop shaking.
She had been useless. She had not been able to help anyone. The feeling that she could not rely on
herself had taken root that night and had never let go.

In the months that followed, Leilani returned to her work, but the world felt different. She had
always been a calm presence in the examination room, steady with frightened animals and anxious
owners. Now she could not sit still. She found herself scanning the waiting room constantly, noting
exits, watching the hands of strangers, listening for the pitch of a voice that might precede
something sudden. She became unable to relax even in her own home, which she knew was safe, but
knowing did not seem to matter. Her body stayed alert. She had difficulty falling asleep because she
needed to stay vigilant, and when she did sleep, the memories came like a film that played over and
over, always the same fragments—the whistle, the roar, the boy’s trembling body—and she would wake
with her heart pounding and a feeling of intense shame that she could not name. The shame was the
worst part. She was certain that she had done something wrong, that she had failed in some
fundamental way, and that what had happened to her was deeply unfair and that nothing had been done
about it. No one had been held accountable. The war continued. The boy’s family had fled. Leilani
was left with the memory and the certainty that she could not stop bad things from happening to
anyone, least of all herself.

Her avoidance was subtle at first. She stopped driving with her husband, telling him she preferred
to take her own car. She began to dread the casual touch of a colleague’s hand on her shoulder, the
unexpected brush of a client’s arm as they leaned over an exam table. Loud noises became
unbearable—a dropped instrument tray in the clinic would send her into a cold sweat, and she started
wearing noise-canceling headphones during procedures, telling herself it was for focus. At home, she
arranged the furniture so that no one could approach her from behind. She slept on the side of the
bed closest to the door. She stopped attending family gatherings because the unpredictability of
children running and shouting felt like too much. Her husband grew quiet and hurt, and she knew he
did not understand why she flinched when he reached for her in the dark, but she could not explain
it. She was certain that if she let herself relax, even for a moment, something terrible would
happen again.

What finally brought Leilani to seek help was not the flashbacks or the shame or the sleeplessness,
though all of those had worsened. It was a moment in the clinic when a golden retriever, frightened
by a sudden noise, snapped at her hand. The bite was minor. But Leilani had frozen. She had stood
there, unable to move, her ears ringing, her vision tunneling, and she had thought: I can’t even do
this anymore. She had excused herself, locked the bathroom door, and wept. That evening, she told
her husband that she needed to talk to someone. She was afraid of what that might mean—afraid that
talking would make the memories more real, that she would be judged for her helplessness, that
nothing would ever change. But she was more afraid of the alternative: a life spent bracing for a
blow that had already fallen.
