Leila, a 45-year-old auditor of Middle Eastern descent, had been divorced for nearly a decade when
she finally walked into a therapist’s office. She had come because her supervisor, a patient woman
who had grown tired of Leila’s refusal to attend any team-building events held outdoors, had issued
an ultimatum: get help or lose the job. Leila sat rigidly in the chair, her eyes scanning the room’s
exits before she could focus on the therapist’s face.

The event that had dismantled her life happened twenty years ago, during her deployment as a
logistics officer in a combat zone. She had been responsible for a supply convoy. What she
remembered came in disconnected pieces—the heat, the dust, the smell of diesel fuel, and then a
single, perfect second of silence before the world turned inside out. An IED had struck the vehicle
ahead of hers. She could recall fragments: the sound of the blast, a ringing in her ears that
wouldn’t stop, and the sight of a soldier’s boot lying in the road, still laced. But there were
vast, gaping holes in the memory. She could not, no matter how hard she tried, remember pulling
anyone from the wreckage, though she knew she must have. She could not remember the face of the
driver of the vehicle ahead. The memory was an incomplete picture, a puzzle with most of its pieces
missing, and the gaps haunted her more than the fragments she did have.

In the years that followed, Leila had come to believe a terrible thing about herself. The event had
happened, she was certain, because of the sort of person she was. She should have protected those
soldiers. She had failed. This conviction had calcified into a deep, unshakeable character blame.
She did not deserve to feel safe. Her body, however, refused to accept this verdict. It kept
reacting as if the danger were still present, a traitor inside her own skin. A sudden bang from a
car backfiring in the parking lot would send her heart crashing against her ribs, her hands
trembling, her breath shallow. She had come to see her own body as an enemy that would not listen to
reason.

Her daily life as an auditor had become a careful, exhausting performance. She worked in a high-rise
office building, and she had chosen a cubicle in the deepest, most interior corner of the floor. It
was her safe zone. Any open space—the lobby atrium, the plaza outside, even the wide aisles between
cubicle banks—felt like a killing field. She had developed a precise ritual for arriving at work.
She would park in the same spot in the underground garage, closest to the stairwell. She would look
behind her three times before unlocking her car door. She would scan the garage for signs of
threat—a figure standing too still, a vehicle with its engine running. Before she could sit at her
desk, she had to identify every possible exit from the floor, planning her escape route from her
chair to the stairwell, to the elevator, to the fire escape. It was the only way she could feel even
remotely comfortable.

Her nightmares were vivid, but the worst part was the dream residue. She would wake in the dark, her
husband long gone, the sheets soaked with sweat, and the smell of a specific cologne would linger in
her nostrils—the cologne the soldier in the lead vehicle had worn. It was a cheap, spicy scent, and
she encountered it in the real world often enough to be undone: a man in the elevator, a stranger on
the street. The olfactory trigger would drop her, without warning, straight back into the fragments.
The consequences of her vigilance had been relational. Her husband had left because he could no
longer live in a house where the curtains were always drawn, where she insisted on sleeping with the
bedroom door open so she could hear the front door, where every trip to the grocery store was a
tactical operation. She had lost him, and she had lost her friends. She had become a person who
could only feel safe in a very limited number of places: her cubicle, her car, her apartment.

She spent hours engaged in counterfactual thinking, replaying the day of the convoy in her mind,
trying to rewrite it. If only she had chosen a different route. If only she had delayed the
departure by ten minutes. If only she had been a different person, the sort of person who could have
protected her people. These thoughts were a constant, grinding loop. What finally brought her to the
clinic was not insight, but exhaustion. She was tired of the constant checking, the planning, the
scanning. She was tired of being afraid of her own body. She sat across from the therapist and said,
in a voice so quiet it was almost a whisper, that she wanted to stop feeling like the worst thing
that had ever happened to her was her own fault. She did not believe it was possible. But she was
willing to try.
