Rajesh is a 50-year-old South Asian American animator who lives with his wife in a quiet suburban
home, but the silence there has become unbearable to him. He sought treatment at the urging of his
wife, who told him gently that she could no longer watch him “disappear into his own head” every
night. What finally pushed him to call a therapist was a moment three weeks ago when his adult
daughter, visiting for the weekend, mentioned she had a routine blood test scheduled. Rajesh felt
his chest clamp shut, his vision narrowing, and he had to excuse himself to the bathroom where he
sat on the edge of the tub, breathing in the faint scent of bleach from a cleaning wipe, and felt
his mind slide backward.

The event that shattered him happened seven years ago. His daughter, then eleven, was diagnosed with
a rare autoimmune condition that required repeated hospitalizations, surgeries, and a long course of
chemotherapy. Rajesh had been the one to stay overnight in the hospital room, sleeping on a recliner
that smelled of antiseptic and stale coffee, watching her tiny chest rise and fall under the thin
white sheets. He remembered the precise beep of the IV pump, the way the fluorescent lights never
went off, the moment a resident used the word “life-threatening” while looking at a chart instead of
at him. His daughter survived and is now healthy, but Rajesh never recovered.

In the months after she came home, Rajesh began to notice that he could not tolerate being alone at
night. If his wife traveled for work or if he had to stay in a hotel for an animation conference, he
would lie awake, his heart pounding, certain that something terrible was happening somewhere and he
would not know about it until it was too late. He started to insist that his wife text him every
hour when she was out, and if she did not respond within ten minutes, he would call her, his voice
tight and clipped. He knew this was unreasonable, but the alternative—the image of her lying
unconscious while he sat oblivious—felt like a fact, not a fear.

At work, Rajesh is a senior animator at a small studio. He is known for his meticulous attention to
detail, but his colleagues have noticed that he has become brittle. When the studio moved to a new
building that smelled faintly of disinfectant, Rajesh asked to work from home for two weeks. He told
his supervisor it was a “renovation allergy,” but the truth was that the smell of antiseptic sent
him straight back to the hospital corridor, to the moment a doctor pulled him aside and said the
words “we need to prepare for the worst.” He began to mentally rehearse worst-case scenarios before
every team meeting: what if the project fails? What if someone gets sick? What if the building has a
fire? He told himself this was being prepared, but it only left him exhausted and hollow.

His most consuming habit, the one he is most ashamed of, is the rumination. Sometimes, late at
night, he finds himself replaying the hospital scenes and imagining getting even—not with the
doctors, who did their best, but with the universe, with fate, with the randomness that had targeted
his child. He would not call it revenge, exactly, but he would picture himself shouting at the
oncologist, or suing the hospital for not catching it sooner, or writing angry letters to a God he
does not believe in. He knew these thoughts were pointless, but they gave him a fleeting sense of
control, a way to channel the helplessness into something that felt like action.

He also began to seek reassurance constantly. He asked his wife repeatedly, “Are you sure she’s
okay? Are you sure?” He asked his daughter’s pediatrician the same question at every follow-up, even
when the blood work was perfect. He asked his own doctor during a routine physical, “Is there
anything wrong with me? Could I have something I don’t know about?” He knew he was exhausting the
people around him, but the relief from their answers lasted only minutes before the doubt crept
back.

The final straw came during a routine dental cleaning. The smell of the office—the alcohol wipes,
the latex gloves—triggered a full flashback. He sat in the chair, frozen, while the hygienist asked
him if he was okay. He could not speak. He left without the cleaning and drove home shaking. That
night, he told his wife he needed help.

Now, in the therapist’s office, Rajesh is terrified of one thing above all: that he will never feel
safe again. He is hoping, quietly, that someone can teach him how to stop the spiral before it
starts—but he is also afraid that if he stops preparing for the worst, the worst will finally
happen.
