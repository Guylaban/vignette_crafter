over fourteen months. The event that had broken her life open happened on a Tuesday evening in late
October, after she had stayed late to close out a quarterly report. She had left the office just
after eight, walking the six blocks to the train station as she had done hundreds of times before.
She was wearing headphones, listening to a podcast, when she felt a hand close around her upper arm
from behind. The grip was hard and sudden, and before she could turn, a man yanked her backward into
the narrow alley between two buildings. She dropped her bag. He was wearing a dark hood, and she saw
his face only for a moment—a flash of jaw, an eye, the glint of a blade he held low near her ribs.
He told her to give him her phone and her wallet, and she did. He was gone in under a minute. She
stood in the alley, shaking, and then she walked to the station and took the train home. She told
her husband, and she filed a police report the next morning. The man was never caught.

In the weeks that followed, Leilani found that the memory of that minute did not behave like a
normal memory. When it came back—and it came back many times a day—she could taste the metal of the
train station railing she had leaned against afterward, or the stale coffee she had drunk at her
desk that afternoon. The taste was so sharp and specific that it pulled her back into the alley as
if she were still there. Worse, her body would lock into the same position it had been in when the
man grabbed her: her right arm slightly raised, her shoulders hunched forward, her breath caught
mid-inhale. She would find herself frozen like that on the subway, or at her desk, or in the grocery
store aisle, unable to move until the wave passed. And when the wave passed, she often thought,
*This cannot have happened to me.* The memory felt too extreme, too cinematic, to belong to her own
life.

Leilani began to change how she moved through the world. She stopped wearing headphones outside. She
walked home from the train station a different way each night, taking longer routes to avoid the
alley, but she could not avoid footsteps. Footsteps behind her, or even nearby, made her heart slam
against her ribs. She would stop walking and turn around, or she would duck into a store until the
person passed. She also began to scan for the offender’s face, even though she knew rationally that
he was not likely to appear in her neighborhood. Still, she found herself studying the jawlines of
men in hoodies on the platform, and each time she thought she saw him, her stomach dropped. The
worst trigger was unexpected touch. Her husband, reaching for her hand under the dinner table, would
make her flinch so hard she knocked over her water glass. He learned to announce himself before
touching her, and she was grateful, but she was also ashamed.

longer than expected to load, she became convinced that something terrible was happening—that the
delay itself was a sign of danger. She could not relax, even at home in her locked apartment, even

different street, if she had screamed. She deliberately tried to leave her body when the memories
came, blanking out in front of her computer screen, and she dwelled on what she had done wrong,
turning the event over and over in her mind as if she could find the mistake that would undo it.

began to believe that no one could keep her safe, not even the people who told her she was safe now.

What finally brought Leilani to therapy was a morning in late December. She was standing in her
