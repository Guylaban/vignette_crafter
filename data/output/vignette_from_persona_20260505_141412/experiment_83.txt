María is a 61-year-old married woman who works as a bus driver for a city transit authority. She
came to therapy at the insistence of her husband, who has grown increasingly worried about the
changes he has seen in her over the past eighteen months. She sat quietly in the chair, her hands
folded tightly in her lap, and spoke in a low, careful voice, as though she were afraid of what
might come out if she spoke too freely.

The event that changed everything happened on a rainy November afternoon. María was driving her
usual route through a busy intersection when she saw a car run a red light and collide with a
delivery truck. The impact was catastrophic. The car spun, flipped, and came to rest on its roof.
María pulled her bus over, called dispatch, and ran to the scene. She was the first person to reach
the driver, a young woman who was unconscious and bleeding heavily from a wound on her head. María
pressed her hands against the woman’s skull, trying to stop the blood, but it kept coming, warm and
thick, soaking through her gloves and running down her wrists. She stayed there, crouched in the
broken glass and rain, until paramedics arrived. The young woman died at the hospital two hours
later.

In the weeks that followed, María could not stop replaying that moment. The memory did not feel like
a memory. It felt like being pulled back into the intersection, the rain on her face, the smell of
gasoline and blood, the terrible wet weight of her hands against the woman’s head. Sometimes the
memory came without any image at all—she would be standing in her kitchen, and suddenly her chest
would tighten and her stomach would drop, and she would be flooded with the same helpless terror she
had felt that day, without knowing why. The memory felt present, not past. She had come to believe
that she had failed in the most fundamental way. She should have done more. She should have known
what to do. She should have saved her. She was certain that if she had acted faster, or called for
help sooner, or applied more pressure, the woman would have lived. She could not forgive herself for
that failure, and she had begun to wonder, in the dark hours of the night, whether her life was
worth continuing at all.

María started to change in ways that puzzled and frightened her family. She could no longer bear to
be touched. When her husband reached for her hand, she pulled away. When her grandchildren hugged
her, she stiffened and made an excuse to leave the room. Physical contact felt invasive, dangerous,
as though it might trigger something she could not control. She also developed rituals she kept
secret. Before she left the house each morning, she stood in the hallway and counted silently to
seven, then repeated the number seven seven times. If she did not do this, she was convinced that
something terrible would happen—a crash, an accident, someone else dying because she had not been
careful enough. The rituals spread. She began tapping surfaces in patterns, whispering numbers under
her breath, and avoiding anything that reminded her of loss. She stopped visiting her mother’s
grave. She deleted photos of her late father from her phone. She could not bear to see a funeral
procession on television.

At work, the triggers were everywhere. Blood appeared on the street after a minor fender bender and
she had to pull over, her hands shaking, her vision narrowing. A car cut her off and she slammed the
brakes, certain she was about to see another collision. She began to dread every intersection, every
left turn. She avoided driving past the site of the accident, taking longer routes that added twenty
minutes to her run. Her supervisor noticed she was arriving late and seemed distracted. María told
him she had been having trouble sleeping, which was true, but she did not tell him that she woke
most nights drenched in sweat, her heart pounding, the young woman’s face inches from her own.

What finally brought María to seek help was a moment at the dinner table. Her husband asked her a
simple question about her day, and she burst into tears. She could not explain why. She only knew
that she felt hollow, that she was tired of carrying the weight of what she had done and failed to
do. She was afraid that if she kept going this way, she would lose her job, her marriage, and
herself. She was not sure she wanted to live, but she was not sure she wanted to die either. She
came to therapy because she did not know what else to do, and because somewhere, beneath the guilt
and the rituals and the relentless reliving, she still hoped there might be a way to feel like
herself again.
