Raymond is a fifty-one-year-old welder from a small coastal town in the Pacific Islands, now living
with his wife in a modest house on the outskirts of a midwestern American city. He came to therapy
at his wife’s insistence, after months of her watching him grow quieter, jumpier, and more distant.
He had always been a steady, practical man, but something had changed in him eighteen months ago,
and neither of them knew how to get back to the person he used to be.

The event itself was not unusual for a man who spent his life around heavy machinery. Raymond was
driving home from a late shift at the fabrication shop, the road slick with rain, when a pickup
truck swerved into his lane. He remembered the headlights coming at him, the sound of metal grinding
against metal, and then the world tilting as his own truck rolled onto its side. What he remembered
most vividly, though, was the smell. The other driver had been drinking, and the alcohol from that
man’s breath had filled the cabin when he leaned in to see if Raymond was alive. That smell—sweet,
sharp, chemical—had soaked into the air, into Raymond’s clothes, into his memory. He had been pinned
for forty minutes before the fire crew cut him free, and during that time, he had lain with his left
arm twisted behind his back, his ribs pressed against the steering wheel, his leg bent at an angle
that did not feel right. He had been certain he was going to die there, alone, in the dark, with
that smell and the sound of his own breathing.

In the weeks after the accident, Raymond tried to return to normal. His physical injuries—three
cracked ribs, a fractured wrist, and a deep bruise across his thigh—healed slowly but steadily. What
did not heal was the way his body remembered. When a car in the shop’s parking lot backfired,
Raymond would find himself frozen, his left arm involuntarily twisting behind his back, his ribs
aching as if the steering wheel were still pressing into them. He would feel the exact position of
his body during the crash, as though his muscles had recorded the moment and could replay it without
his permission. Sometimes, when he was lying in bed at night, the memory would return with such
force that he could smell the alcohol again, could hear the screech of metal, could feel the cold
rain on his face—all at once, as if he were still in that overturned truck. He would sit up,
drenched in sweat, and his wife would ask what was wrong, and he would say nothing, because he did
not have words for the way his own body had become a trapdoor back to that night.

Raymond’s work as a welder became a minefield. The shop was full of sounds he could not control: the
clang of metal, the hiss of a torch, the rumble of a forklift. Every car sound from the street
outside—a horn, a revving engine, the screech of brakes—made his chest tighten. He started wearing
heavy ear protection even when he did not need it, and he began avoiding the parts of the shop
closest to the loading bay, where delivery trucks came and went. His foreman noticed that he had
become slower, more hesitant, and Raymond could not explain why. He was afraid that if he told
anyone, they would think he was weak, or worse, that they would confirm what he already suspected:
that he was not the same man anymore.

The worst trigger, though, was the alcohol smell. It came from places he could not anticipate: a
coworker’s breath after lunch, a spilled beer at a family barbecue, the cleaning solvent he used on
his tools. Each time, his body would lock up, and he would feel the pain in his ribs and his wrist
and his leg, even though those injuries had healed months ago. He stopped going to family
gatherings. He stopped meeting friends for dinner. He told his wife he was just tired, but the truth
was that he could not bear the risk of encountering that smell in a place where he was supposed to
be relaxed.

By the time he walked into my office, Raymond had been avoiding driving for six months. He took the
bus to work, or his wife drove him, and he felt a constant, low-grade dread that he could not shake.
He was afraid of his own memories, afraid of the way they could hijack his body without warning.
What he wanted, he said, was to stop feeling like a stranger in his own life. He wanted to go back
to being the man who could fix anything with his hands, who did not flinch at the sound of a car
engine, who could sit in a room without scanning for threats he could not name. He was not sure that
was possible, but he was here, and that was the first step he had taken in eighteen months.
