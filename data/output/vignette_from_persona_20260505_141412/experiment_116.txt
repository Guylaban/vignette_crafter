Marcus is a 34-year-old Navy officer who has spent nearly fifteen years in service, much of it at
sea. He came to therapy at the urging of his wife, who had grown increasingly worried about the
changes she saw in him over the past eighteen months. He agreed to come, he said, because he was
tired of feeling like a ghost in his own life.

The event that changed everything occurred during a routine humanitarian mission. His ship had been
deployed to provide disaster relief after a catastrophic hurricane struck a coastal region. Marcus
had been part of a team sent ashore to assist with evacuations. What he encountered was not the
orderly operation he had trained for. The streets were flooded, the power was out, and the air was
thick with the smell of saltwater, fuel, and alcohol from a nearby distillery that had been
breached. In the chaos, he had helped carry a young child through chest-deep water to a makeshift
shelter. He remembered the child’s grip on his neck, the way the water pushed against his chest, the
absolute darkness inside the building where they took shelter. He remembered thinking, *this is not
supposed to happen to me*. Even now, the memory feels so extreme that he sometimes struggles to
believe it actually happened. The images come back as vivid pictures—the black water, the child’s
face, the smell—but they feel disconnected from the person he was before.

In the months that followed, Marcus found himself changing in ways he did not fully understand. He
began avoiding seeing friends, even those he had served with for years. When they called, he let the
phone ring. When they invited him to barbecues or gatherings, he made excuses. He told himself he
did not deserve their company. A quiet, persistent thought had taken root: he deserved to suffer
because of what had happened. He could not articulate why—he had done his job, he had helped—but the
feeling was absolute. He began to avoid any circumstance that reminded him of the event. He stopped
going out after dark. He would not walk through a room with the lights off. He asked his wife to
keep the blinds open during the day, but at night he insisted on a lamp burning in every room. The
darkness, even in his own home, made his body tense, as if the water were rising again.

The smells were the hardest. He could not tolerate the smell of alcohol. It did not matter if it was
beer, wine, or hand sanitizer—the moment it reached his nose, his chest would tighten, and he would
feel his arms lock, his neck strain, as though he were still carrying that child through the flood.
He would find himself standing rigid, his hands clenched, his breathing shallow, his body reenacting
a position he had not chosen. He had come to believe that the memory lived in his muscles, not just
his mind.

His work as a Navy officer became a minefield. His duties required him to be on base, to move
through crowded hallways and briefing rooms, to stand in formation. But crowds now felt suffocating.
The press of bodies, the noise, the inability to see an exit—all of it triggered a surge of panic
that he had to fight to control. He learned to stand near walls, to keep his back protected, to scan
for escape routes. His commanding officer noticed he had become withdrawn, less willing to take on
leadership roles. Marcus told himself he no longer belonged anywhere—not on the ship, not at home,
not among his friends. He used to be a happy person, he remembered that version of himself, but that
man felt like a stranger. Now he was always miserable, always watching, always waiting.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment at home. His wife had asked him to pick up
takeout. He drove to the restaurant, but when he saw the crowd inside, he could not get out of the
car. He sat in the parking lot for forty-five minutes, gripping the steering wheel, his body locked
in that familiar position, the smell of cooking oil and beer drifting through the vents. He drove
home without the food. His wife found him in the garage, sitting in the dark car, and she did not
yell. She just sat down on the floor next to the driver’s door and waited. That night, she told him
she was afraid for him, and he realized he was afraid too. He came to therapy hoping to feel less
like a stranger in his own skin, though he was not sure that was possible. What frightened him most
was the thought that the happiness he had lost was gone forever, and that he would spend the rest of
his life feeling set apart from everyone else.
