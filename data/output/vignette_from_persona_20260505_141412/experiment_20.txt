Rashmi is a 44-year-old South Asian woman who works the evening shift at a big-box retail store in a
suburban strip mall. She came to therapy at the urging of her husband, who had watched her shrink
over the past fourteen months into someone he barely recognized. She sat on the edge of the
armchair, back straight, hands folded in her lap, and began to speak in a low, careful voice about
what she had seen.

It happened on a Tuesday in late October, just before the store closed. Rashmi was straightening the
clearance rack near the front registers when a man in a dark hoodie walked through the automatic
doors, pulled a handgun from his waistband, and shot the security guard twice in the chest. The
guard, a young man named Devin who always joked with her about her lunch leftovers, fell backward
into a display of Halloween candy. The shooter ran. Rashmi did not scream. She did not run. She
stood frozen behind a pillar, watching Devin’s blood spread across the linoleum in a slow, dark
pool, and she could not, for what felt like a very long time, make her legs move. Other customers
and coworkers were shouting, crying, running for the back exit, but Rashmi stood there, and the
image of Devin’s face—surprised, then empty—seemed to press into her like a physical weight.

In the weeks that followed, the store reopened, and Rashmi went back to work because she did not
know what else to do. But the quality of her days changed. She found she could no longer stand near
the front registers without her vision swimming and her ears ringing. The dizziness would come on
without warning—sometimes when a customer raised their voice, sometimes when she heard a shopping
cart clatter, sometimes when another employee laughed too loudly. She would grip the edge of a shelf
and wait for the feeling to pass, telling herself she was fine, she was fine, but her heart would be
slamming and her palms slick. She began to avoid the front of the store entirely, asking her manager
to let her stock the back aisles instead. He agreed, but it meant she worked alone now, and the
loneliness pressed on her in its own way.

At home, her husband noticed that she had stopped talking about her day. When he asked, she would
shrug or say she was tired. What she did not tell him was that she could not hold the events of that
October evening in her mind in any kind of order. She knew what had happened, but the sequence felt
scrambled—she would remember the sound of the gunshot before she remembered seeing the gun, or she
would see Devin’s face before she remembered the automatic doors opening. And every time the memory
surfaced, it came with a wave of grief so dense and sudden that she would have to stop whatever she
was doing—stirring tea, folding laundry—and sit down until it passed. It was not just sadness; it
was a kind of drowning. She had let herself imagine, once or twice, what Devin might have been
thinking in that last second, and the thought made her chest feel like it was caving in.

She began to scan her environment constantly. At work, she checked the entrance doors every few
minutes. She watched customers’ hands. She noted anyone wearing a hoodie or a jacket that could hide
something. She did this automatically now, without deciding to, and it exhausted her. At home, she
sat with her back to the wall. She startled at the sound of a car backfiring, at her husband
clearing his throat behind her, at the microwave beeping. She had become, she knew, jumpy in a way
that embarrassed her, but she could not stop it.

She also found herself thinking, obsessively, about why this had happened to her. Not why the
shooting had happened—that, she could accept as random violence—but why she had been the one
standing at that particular rack, at that particular moment. What did it mean? Was it a sign? A
punishment? She turned the question over and over, as if an answer might release her from the grip
of the memory, but no answer came.

To manage her fear, she had developed a quiet strategy: she agreed with everyone. At work, she said
yes to extra shifts she did not want. She nodded when a customer complained about a price she knew
was fair. She let her coworkers take credit for her ideas. She had come to believe, somewhere
beneath conscious thought, that if she was agreeable enough, if she caused no friction, if she made
herself small and pleasing, she would be safe. Conflict felt like danger now. Disagreement felt like
an invitation for something terrible to happen. Her husband had started to notice that she deferred
to him on every decision, even small ones like what to eat for dinner, and he found it unsettling.
She knew this, but the alternative—asserting herself, risking someone’s displeasure—filled her with
a dread she could not explain.

What finally brought her to therapy was a panic attack in the break room at work. A coworker had
been talking about the anniversary of the shooting, and Rashmi had felt the dizziness rise, then the
grief, then a kind of mental white-out in which she could not remember where she was or what year it
was. She had excused herself to the bathroom and sat on the floor, trembling, for twenty minutes.
That night, her husband told her gently that she could not go on like this. She agreed, not because
she believed therapy would help, but because she was too tired to argue. What she hoped for, more
than anything, was to stop feeling like she was waiting for something terrible to happen again. What
she feared was that she would never stop.
