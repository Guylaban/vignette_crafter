Ravi is a 39-year-old interior designer, married, and living in a bustling suburb of Chicago. Two
years ago, his younger brother, Anish, died unexpectedly from a pulmonary embolism at the age of 34.
Anish had been perfectly healthy, had just finished a half-marathon, and had called Ravi the night
before to talk about a vacation they were planning together. The next morning, Ravi’s sister-in-law
called screaming. Ravi drove to the hospital, but Anish was already gone. Ravi never got to say
goodbye, and he never got an explanation that felt satisfying—just a doctor saying, “Sometimes these
things just happen.”

In the months that followed, Ravi’s life began to narrow in ways he did not fully recognize at
first. He stopped answering calls from his sister-in-law, not because he was angry, but because
hearing her voice brought back the sound of that phone call, and his chest would tighten before his
mind could catch up. He found himself avoiding conversations where people mentioned losing someone,
even casually. When a colleague said, “I’m so sorry for your loss,” Ravi would nod and change the
subject, but inside he felt his body go cold and his heart start pounding as if something terrible
was about to happen again. He began to dread the calendar. The anniversary of Anish’s death loomed
for weeks beforehand, and when it arrived, Ravi would stay home from work, close the blinds, and
refuse to look at his phone. He told himself it was just a day, but his body seemed to know it was
coming before he did—his sleep would fragment, his jaw would clench, and he would snap at his wife
over small things like leaving the milk out.

At work, Ravi had always prided himself on his calm, collaborative style. But recently, he had
started to rehearse worst-case scenarios in his head before every client meeting. What if the client
hates the design? What if they fire me? What if I can’t pay the mortgage and we lose the house? He
knew these thoughts were irrational, but they felt necessary—like if he imagined the worst, he could
somehow control it, prevent it from surprising him the way Anish’s death had. His wife noticed he
had become irritable, quick to anger over minor frustrations. He had snapped at her for misplacing
his keys, then spent the rest of the evening in a fog of guilt and shame. He had also stopped
attending family gatherings where he knew someone might bring up Anish’s name, and he had not
visited the cemetery once since the funeral. He told himself it was because he was too busy, but the
truth was that the thought of standing at that grave made his stomach turn and his vision blur.

What frightened Ravi most was the flashbacks. They came without warning—while he was driving, while
he was selecting fabric samples, while he was sitting at dinner. Suddenly he would feel the cold of
the hospital waiting room, hear the fluorescent lights buzzing, and smell the antiseptic. His body
would react before his mind understood: his hands would tremble, his breathing would become shallow,
and he would feel as if he were back in that moment, watching the doctor’s mouth move without
hearing the words. He had learned to hide it, to excuse himself to the bathroom and wait for it to
pass, but the cost was mounting. His wife had started sleeping in the guest room after he woke her
one night screaming Anish’s name.

Ravi finally sought help because his wife told him she could not watch him disappear anymore. He
came to the first session with his jaw tight and his hands clasped in his lap. He said he wanted to
stop feeling like he was waiting for the next terrible thing to happen. He said he wanted to be able
to remember his brother without feeling like he was dying all over again.
