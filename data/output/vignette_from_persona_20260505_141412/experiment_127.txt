Mai sits in the chair across from me with her hands folded tightly in her lap, her knuckles pale
against the worn fabric of her work jeans. She is thirty-eight years old, a welder, and she has not
spoken to anyone about what happened in nearly four months. She tells me she is here because she
cannot sleep anymore, and she is afraid that if she does not talk to someone, she will lose her job.

The event happened on a Tuesday afternoon in late September. Mai was working a double shift at the
fabrication shop, finishing a rush order of steel beams for a construction site across town. She had
been welding for fourteen years and prided herself on precision, on the clean lines of her beads, on
never having caused an accident. That day, she was tired. She remembers that clearly—the way her
eyes burned and her shoulders ached. She was tacking a support bracket onto a beam when a coworker,
a man named Danny who had been at the shop for only three weeks, walked into her work zone without
warning. Mai did not see him. She swung the torch to adjust her angle, and the arc caught him across
the forearm. He screamed. The smell of burned fabric and skin filled her helmet. She remembers the
taste of it—acrid, metallic, like copper and burnt hair—and she can still taste it now, sometimes,
when she closes her eyes. Danny survived. He lost significant tissue and nerve function in his right
arm. He has not returned to work. Mai has not spoken to him since the hospital.

What Mai remembers of the event is fragmented, incomplete. She remembers the scream, the smell, the
taste. She remembers the foreman pulling her away. But she cannot remember dropping the torch, or
what she said to the paramedics, or how she got home that night. The gaps in her memory terrify her.
She has come to believe that the missing pieces mean she must have done something even worse than
she remembers, that her mind is protecting her from the full truth of her negligence. She dwells on
this constantly. She replays the moment over and over, searching for the split second where she
should have looked, should have checked, should have known. She tells me she has gone over it a
thousand times, and every time she arrives at the same conclusion: she should have been paying
attention. She should have seen him. She is a professional, and she failed.

In the weeks that followed, Mai began to avoid people. At first it was small—she stopped eating
lunch in the break room, ate alone in her truck. Then she started leaving the shop five minutes
early to avoid walking past Danny’s empty station. Soon she was avoiding the grocery store, the gas
station, any place where she might run into someone who knew. She stopped answering calls from her
sister. She told herself it was easier this way, that she did not deserve company, that other people
would only remind her of what she had done. The isolation felt like punishment, and punishment felt
right.

At work, her concentration has deteriorated. She has made three errors in the past month—small
mistakes, but dangerous ones for a welder. Her supervisor has noticed. She was pulled aside last
week and told to take some time off. Mai is certain that if she loses this job, she will have
nothing left. She is also certain that she deserves to lose it.

Sleep is a nightmare. She lies awake for hours, staring at the ceiling, her heart pounding, her mind
cycling through the same loop of guilt and self-blame. When she does drift off, she wakes gasping,
the taste of copper in her mouth, her body rigid with adrenaline. Strong physical reactions have
become routine: the hiss of a welding torch, the smell of burnt metal, even the sound of a
coworker’s footsteps approaching from behind—all of it sends her heart racing, her palms sweating,
her chest tight with a terror she cannot name.

She has come to me because she is exhausted. She is afraid of what she might do if she keeps going
this way. She is afraid of the dark thoughts that have started to surface, the ones where she
wonders if Danny would be better off if she had never been born. She is not looking for
forgiveness—she told me that flatly in our first session. She is looking for a way to stop the loop,
to quiet the taste in her mouth, to be able to walk into the shop without her body screaming that
she is a danger to everyone around her. She wants to work again. She wants to sleep. She wants to
believe that she is not irredeemably broken. But she is not sure she can.
