Miguel leaned forward in the chair, his hands resting on his knees, the same posture he took before
a difficult surgery. At forty, he was one of the most respected trauma surgeons in the hospital,
known for his steady hands and unshakeable calm under pressure. But that composure had become a
performance, a mask he wore through twelve-hour shifts, and it was cracking. He had come to this
first session because last week, during a routine debridement, a nurse had dropped a metal tray. The
clatter against the tile floor had sent him reeling backward, his heart hammering, the scalpel
trembling in his gloved hand. For a long moment, he had been somewhere else entirely, and when he
came back, the smell of burnt tissue from the cautery had filled his nostrils, and he had had to
excuse himself to the locker room, where he sat shaking for ten minutes.

The trauma itself had happened eighteen months ago, during a wildfire season that had swept through
the hills outside the city. Miguel had been driving home after a double shift, exhausted, when the
fire jumped the highway. He remembered the sky turning a strange orange-brown, the smoke so thick
that his headlights seemed to dissolve into it. He had pulled over, gotten out of the car, and then
the world had become a roar of wind and heat. He had run, not knowing which direction, the smoke
searing his throat, his eyes streaming, until he had stumbled into a ditch and lain there, face
down, as the fire passed overhead. He had survived with minor burns and smoke inhalation, but what
stayed with him was the sound—the deafening crackle and groan of the fire consuming everything—and
the smell, a thick, sweet-bitter odor of burning vegetation and melted plastic that he could still
summon in his memory as if it were in the room with them now.

In the months that followed, Miguel had tried to manage on his own. He found that the memories would
hit him without any warning, triggered by something as ordinary as a car backfiring or a patient’s
family lighting a candle in the chapel. He would be standing at the scrub sink, and suddenly he was
back in that ditch, the heat on his back, the terror a physical pressure in his chest. The memories
felt incomplete, fragmented—he could see the orange sky, feel the grit of dirt under his palms, but
the sequence of events was jumbled, with gaps he could not fill. And when the memory faded, what
remained was a grief so sharp it surprised him, a sorrow for something he could not name, as if he
had lost more than just a few minutes of safety.

He had developed a set of strategies to keep these intrusions at bay. At home, he would pour himself
a glass of whiskey, sometimes two, and then another, until the edges of his mind felt dulled and the
images lost their power. He had also turned more and more to his wife, initiating sex almost
compulsively, using the physical intensity to anchor himself in the present, to replace the terror
with something else. He knew, vaguely, that this was not a solution, that it was putting a strain on
their marriage—she had started to ask him, gently, if he was okay, and he had lied and said he was
just tired from work. At the hospital, he had become an expert at distraction, throwing himself into
the most complex cases, volunteering for extra shifts, staying late to review charts until his eyes
burned. The busyness worked, for a time. But it also meant he never processed anything, never let
himself feel the full weight of what had happened.

What finally brought him here was not the drinking, or the strain on his marriage, or even the near-
miss in the operating room. It was the grief. He had begun to notice that when he remembered the
fire, when the full memory crashed over him unbidden, he felt an overwhelming, crushing grief that
made no sense to him. He had not lost anyone in the fire. He had not lost his home. And yet, when
the memory came, he wept, and he could not stop. It was that helplessness, that loss of control over
his own emotions, that frightened him most. He was afraid that one day, in the middle of a surgery,
the grief would take him, and his hands would stop being steady, and someone would die because he
could not hold himself together. He hoped, in this room, to find a way to make the memories stop
coming without warning, or at least to learn how to survive them without falling apart.
