The first time Rafael saw his father hit his mother, he was five years old. It happened in the
kitchen of their small apartment in San Antonio, a place that had always smelled of cumin and warm
tortillas until that night. He remembers it not as a story that unfolded, but as a series of
snapshots: his mother’s hand frozen above the stove, the way the yellow linoleum looked under the
table where he had hidden, the sound of a belt buckle striking the floor. Those moments are still
clear and detailed in his mind, as vivid now as they were nearly sixty years later. What he feels
when he recalls them is not just the old fear, but an intense, crushing guilt—the guilt of a boy who
did nothing, who stayed hidden under that table while his mother took the blows meant to be
silenced.

Rafael is sixty-four now, a jeweler by trade, working alone at a small bench in the back of a shop
in downtown Los Angeles. He has been divorced for fifteen years, and his children live in other
states. For decades, he told himself that the past was the past. But the past has a way of
insisting. He has come to believe that he failed in the most important role of his life—that he was
supposed to protect his mother, and later, his wife, and later still, his own sense of self. This
belief sits inside him like a splinter. He is certain he is a weak person, and that certainty has
hollowed out his connection to the community and culture he once belonged to. He no longer attends
the family gatherings his cousins host. He stopped going to the local panadería where the men from
his old neighborhood would argue about soccer and politics. The noise of those gatherings, the
raised voices of men laughing or debating, became unbearable.

The triggers are everywhere. Shouting of any kind—a car horn, a customer raising his voice to ask
for a discount, a television argument in the next room—sends a jolt through him that he cannot
control. He has stopped taking the bus because of the men who talk loudly on their phones. He avoids
being alone with men whenever possible, and he will rearrange his entire schedule to sidestep
confrontations, even small ones. At work, when a supplier calls to dispute an invoice, Rafael lets
it go to voicemail and then deletes it. He has lost two wholesale accounts this year because he
refused to return those calls.

To manage the fear, Rafael has developed habits that have become as rigid as his posture. He cannot
relax. The idea of sitting still, of letting his guard down, feels dangerous to him, as though
relaxation itself is an invitation for something terrible to happen. So he keeps his hands
busy—polishing rings, setting stones, cleaning tools—long after the shop has closed. He constantly
monitors his own thoughts, watching for any sign that he might be losing his grip, that he might be
going crazy the way he feared his father was crazy. He tries hard not to think about the trauma, but
the effort of suppression is exhausting, and the memories slip through anyway, especially at night.

His threat monitoring has become automatic and relentless. In any room he enters, his eyes scan for
potential weapons or dangerous objects—a letter opener on a desk, a heavy ashtray, a chair that
could be swung. He watches other people closely, reading their faces for signs that they might harm
him. Even when his daughter tells him over the phone that he is safe, that the old world is gone, he
finds it impossible to believe her. The feeling of safety is a language he no longer speaks.

Rafael finally came to therapy because he could no longer sleep. He was falling asleep at his bench,

find if he stops watching, stops monitoring, stops holding himself so tightly. But he is also tired.

chair, his hands folded in his lap, his eyes moving slowly around the room.
