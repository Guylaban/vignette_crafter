Kofi is a 27-year-old man of Ghanaian descent who drives for a rideshare company in a mid-sized
American city. He came to therapy at the urging of his cousin, who had noticed that Kofi had stopped
coming to family gatherings and seemed increasingly isolated and on edge. Kofi himself was not sure
what he hoped for, only that he was exhausted.

The event that shaped his life happened when he was nine years old, living with his father in the
Bronx. His father, a man Kofi described as hardworking and strict, had a particular way of
disciplining him that involved making Kofi kneel on a cold linoleum floor for hours, sometimes with
his hands pressed flat against the icy tile. The punishment was always preceded by the sound of
footsteps—heavy, deliberate, approaching from behind—and then the cold would spread up through his
palms and knees until his whole body felt locked in place. He remembered the texture of the
linoleum: a slightly raised, bumpy pattern that pressed into his skin and left temporary
indentations. His father would stand behind him, silent, and the temperature of the floor would seep
into him, and he would feel his body contract as if trying to disappear.

Now, as an adult, Kofi lives alone in a small apartment. He works long shifts, often driving late
into the night, and he has come to believe that he is permanently changed for the worse. He says it
quietly, without self-pity: “I’m not the same person. Something broke in me back then, and it never
healed.” He is certain that his body has become his enemy. Even when he is safe—sitting in his
parked car, or lying in bed—his heart will suddenly pound, his skin will prickle, and he will feel
that old cold spreading across his palms. He knows intellectually that no one is coming, but his
body does not believe it. He has tried to explain this to people, but they tell him he is safe now,
and he finds it impossible to believe them. Their words feel like a foreign language.

The triggers are everywhere, woven into the fabric of his daily life. When he picks up a passenger
and the seatbelt buckle has a certain bumpy texture, his hand jerks back. When he hears footsteps
behind him on the sidewalk—especially if they are heavy and steady—his shoulders lock and he has to
fight the urge to sprint. And the temperature: he cannot stand cold floors. He wears slippers
constantly, even in summer. He has removed the bathmat from his bathroom because the texture of it
reminded him of something he cannot name, and he now steps onto a towel instead.

To manage this, Kofi has developed a set of strategies that have slowly hollowed out his life. He
avoids intimacy completely. He has not dated in years, and he has stopped responding to friends who
try to get close. He is afraid that if someone touches him, or if he lets himself feel attached, the
old cold will come back, or worse, he will snap at them or push them away. So he stays alone. When
the memories press in during his shifts, he distracts himself by turning the radio up loud, or by
focusing obsessively on the road, or by counting license plates. And sometimes, when the pressure is
unbearable, he smokes marijuana in his car between rides. He tells himself it helps him sleep, but
he knows it also helps him not feel.

The consequences have been concrete. He has lost his connection to his community. His church used to
be a central part of his life—the Ghanaian congregation, the shared meals, the elders who knew his
family—but he stopped going two years ago. He cannot bear the thought of being surrounded by people,
of being seen, of having to explain why he flinches when someone claps him on the back. He has also
become reckless. He drives too fast sometimes, especially at night, weaving through traffic, taking
risks that he knows could get him in trouble. He is not trying to hurt himself, exactly, but he is
not trying to stay safe either. He is hypervigilant in a way that exhausts him: scanning every
rearview mirror, checking every sound, always watching for footsteps that are not there.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was a moment in his car last month. He had picked up a
passenger who, after getting in, reached forward to adjust the air conditioning. The cold air hit
Kofi’s hand, and for a second he was nine years old again, kneeling on that floor. He pulled the car
over, told the passenger to get out, and sat shaking for twenty minutes. That night, he called his
cousin. He is not sure if therapy can help, but he knows he cannot keep living like this. What he is
most afraid of is that he is right—that he really is permanently damaged, and that no amount of
talking will change the cold that lives under his skin. But he is also afraid of staying the same.
