Leila is a 24-year-old single woman who works as a tailor in a small shop in Dearborn, Michigan. She
came to therapy at the urging of her aunt, who had grown worried about the changes in her niece over
the past eight months. Eight months ago, Leila was driving home from work on a rainy November
evening when a pickup truck ran a red light and T-boned her sedan at an intersection. The impact
spun her car into a lamppost. She remembers the sound of metal crumpling, the shattering of the
driver’s side window, and the sudden, suffocating pressure of the airbag against her chest. For what
felt like an eternity, she was trapped, her door jammed shut, the smell of gasoline thick in the
air. She was certain she was going to die, that the car would explode. A passerby pulled her out
through the passenger window before the paramedics arrived. She walked away with a fractured wrist
and deep bruising, but the physical injuries healed within weeks.

What did not heal was the feeling that the world had become a trap. Leila now lives in a state of
constant, low-grade dread. She has come to believe that disaster can strike at any moment, without
warning, and that the only way to survive is to be ready. She finds herself scanning every room she
enters, her eyes darting to exits, to the faces of strangers, to any object that could become a
weapon or a hazard. At the tailor shop, where she spends her days bent over sewing machines and
fitting clients, she cannot focus. Every time a car backfires outside or a door slams, her heart
lurches and she feels the same terror she felt in the crushed car. She has taken to positioning her
worktable so that her back is against the wall, facing the door. She feels unsafe any other way. Her
boss has noticed she jumps at the bell on the shop door, and her coworkers have stopped trying to
startle her as a joke—they learned quickly that she would react with a scream and trembling hands.

The worst part of the day is the drive home. Leila has not driven since the accident. She cannot
bring herself to get behind the wheel. The mere thought of sitting in a driver’s seat, of
approaching an intersection, sends her into a panic. She relies on her aunt for rides, or she takes
the bus, but being a passenger is almost as bad. Every time the vehicle slows down, she braces for
impact. She mentally rehearses the worst-case scenario: the truck coming from the left, the sound,
the crushing feeling. She does this to feel prepared, as if rehearsing the horror might somehow make
it less likely to happen, or at least make her ready for it. But it only keeps the event fresh. Her
memories of the crash are not static; they are vivid, intrusive, and replay in her mind like a film
she cannot turn off. When she remembers it, she is back inside the car, her chest tight, the smell
of gasoline in her nostrils, the absolute certainty that she is about to die. The terror is
identical to what she felt that night.

Her social life has shrunk. She used to meet friends for coffee, but now she avoids it. She cannot
trust that people are what they seem. She looks at strangers and wonders what they are capable of.
The world, she has concluded, is a fundamentally dangerous place, and she has to be especially
careful because you never know what can happen next. She has difficulty falling asleep because she
feels she needs to stay alert, as if letting her guard down for a moment would invite catastrophe.
She lies awake, listening to the sounds of her apartment building, her muscles tense.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment at work last week. A client’s child, playing near
the front window, suddenly slammed a toy car against the glass. Leila screamed, dropped the fabric
she was cutting, and began to cry uncontrollably. She could not stop shaking for an hour. Her aunt,
who works at the shop part-time, took her aside and told her she could not keep living like this.
Leila agreed to come to therapy, but she is not sure it will help. What she fears most is that this
feeling of being hunted will never go away, that she will spend the rest of her life bracing for the
next disaster. What she hopes for, though she can barely admit it, is to one day sit in a car
without her heart racing, and to feel, even for a moment, that she is safe.
