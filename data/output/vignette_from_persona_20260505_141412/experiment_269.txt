Kiet Tran was eighty years old, a retired data scientist who had spent the last decade of his career
building predictive models for humanitarian logistics. He lived alone in a quiet suburb of Portland,
in a house that had grown too large for one man since his wife, Linh, passed away six years ago. For
the first few years after her death, he had managed well enough—gardening, volunteering at the local
library, keeping up with his adult children on video calls. But that was before the nightmares
started, before the world had begun to feel like a place where danger lurked behind every ordinary
moment.

The event that had reshaped everything happened in 1995, when Kiet was fifty-one and working as a
relief coordinator in a refugee camp on the Thai-Cambodian border. He had been there for eighteen
months, overseeing food distribution and medical supply chains. One afternoon, a group of armed men
forced their way into the administrative compound. They were not soldiers from any official army;
they were local militia, drunk and angry about a perceived slight from the camp leadership. Kiet
watched as they beat a young translator nearly to death, smashed radios and computers, and then
turned their attention to him. They held a gun to his temple and made him kneel in the mud for three
hours while they argued about what to do with him. What stayed with him, what he could still feel in
his bones when the memories came, was not the fear of death but the sheer, grinding helplessness—the
knowledge that there was no escape, no leverage, no option but to wait and see if they would pull
the trigger. He had been released eventually, roughed up but alive, and had returned to the United
States and built a quiet life. For thirty years, he had told almost no one.

Now, the quiet life had collapsed. The memories had stopped being memories. When they came—and they
came often, several times a week—they did not feel like recollections of something past. They felt
like the event was happening right now, in his living room, in the grocery store, in the middle of
the night. He would wake from a nightmare with his heart pounding, the smell of mud and diesel in
his nostrils, and for a full minute he would not know where he was. The memory played like a film he
could not stop, the same scenes on a loop: the translator’s face, the gun against his temple, the
sound of the men arguing in a language he barely understood. He had come to believe that these
reactions meant he was going crazy. An eighty-year-old man, a scientist who had spent his life
analyzing data and probabilities, and he could not control his own mind.

The world, too, had changed. Kiet had always been a cautious man, but now he saw threats everywhere.
The news was unbearable—any report about conflict, about refugees, about people behaving badly
toward one another, sent him into a spiral of agitation. He had stopped watching the evening news
two years ago. He had stopped reading the newspaper. When his son mentioned something about a
protest in another country during a phone call, Kiet had hung up abruptly and then spent the rest of
the day pacing his kitchen, his jaw clenched so tight that his teeth ached. He was terrified of his
own anger. He had never been a violent man, but lately he felt a rage simmering just beneath the
surface, and he was certain that if he ever let it out, he would do something terrible—scream at a
stranger, break something, hurt someone. He rehearsed conversations before every interaction,
running through possible scenarios in his head, trying to anticipate what might trigger him, trying
to stay ahead of the fury. Even a simple trip to the grocery store required preparation: he would
stand in his hallway for five minutes, going over what he might say to the cashier, what he would do
if someone cut in front of him, how he would exit quickly if he felt the anger rising.

He was constantly scanning his environment. In public, his head swiveled, checking corners, watching
hands, noting exits. His body felt perpetually tense, coiled like a spring, ready to react to a
threat that never materialized but that he was sure was coming. The irritability had cost him. His
daughter had stopped visiting as often because he snapped at her over small things—a dish left in
the sink, a question asked twice. He had withdrawn from the few friends he had left. He could not
explain to them why he could not bear to hear about the news, why he flinched when someone raised
their voice in a restaurant, why he sometimes sat in his car for ten minutes before going into a
building, just scanning the parking lot for anything unusual.

What finally brought Kiet to a therapist’s office was not the nightmares or the anger or the
constant scanning. It was a moment of clarity after a particularly bad night. He had woken from a
dream—the same dream, the mud, the gun, the voices—and had sat on the edge of his bed, shaking, and
realized that he had not felt truly safe in two years. He was eighty years old. He did not want to
spend whatever time he had left living like this. He was afraid of what the therapy might stir up,
afraid that talking about it would make the memories worse, afraid that the therapist would confirm
that he was, in fact, going crazy. But he was more afraid of another decade of this half-life, of
dying alone in a house full of unspoken terror. So he had made the appointment, and now he sat in
the waiting room, his hands folded in his lap, scanning the room for exits, rehearsing what he would
say.
