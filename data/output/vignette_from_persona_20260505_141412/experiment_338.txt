At forty-one, Phan used to move through space the way other people breathe—effortlessly,
intuitively, as if his body were a language all its own. He had been a dancer since he was a boy in
Vietnam, and then professionally for two decades in the United States. But that was before the aid
convoy. Before the six months he spent as a relief worker in a contested region of the Middle East,
distributing medical supplies to villages that had been shelled and re-shelled until they were
hardly more than rubble. He had gone because he believed in something—in the idea that a person
could stand between violence and the vulnerable and make a difference. He had not anticipated the
morning when a mortar struck the warehouse where he and three others were loading trucks. He had not
anticipated the fire, the screams, the way the air turned thick and chemical. He had not anticipated
that he would be the one who ran.

Phan had been standing near the rear bay door when the first explosion tore through the front of the
building. The heat wave knocked him flat. For a moment, everything was dust and ringing, and then he
saw the flames spreading fast, crawling up pallets of bandages and bottled water. He heard a
colleague—a woman named Samira—calling for help from somewhere near the office. He remembered the
layout. He remembered that the rear exit was twenty feet behind him. He also remembered that he did
not go toward her voice. He turned and ran. By the time the fire crews arrived, Samira was dead. The
official report said she had been trapped by falling debris. Phan knew only that he had not tried to
find her. He had not even looked back.

In the months that followed, that moment replayed itself in his mind not as a memory but as a
recurring, uninvited present. He would be standing at the kitchen counter, reaching for a glass, and
suddenly he was back in the warehouse—the heat on his skin, the acrid smell of burning plastic and
insulation flooding his nostrils. The smell of anything burnt, even toast, could trigger it. So
could the smell of alcohol, because there had been a spilled bottle of medical sanitizer near the
exit, its sharp odor mixing with the smoke. And so could the time of day: late morning, around
10:45, when the mortar had hit. He learned to dread that hour. He learned to keep the windows
closed, to avoid cooking, to ask his wife not to pour wine in his presence. He never explained why.

What frightened Phan most was not the memory itself but what the memory told him about who he was.
He had believed himself to be a good person, someone who would act bravely in a crisis. His flight
from the warehouse had violated that belief so thoroughly that he could no longer trust his own
character. He was certain that his reaction proved he was a lousy coper—weak, selfish, fundamentally
unreliable. He began to notice this failure everywhere. When he felt his heart race at the smell of
smoke, he told himself that other people would have handled it calmly. When he could not bring
himself to rehearse with his dance company because the studio’s heating system sometimes emitted a
faint burnt odor, he concluded that he could not rely on himself to function in any demanding
situation. He stopped taking lead roles. He stopped taking any roles that required improvisation,
because improvisation demanded a trust in his own instincts that he no longer possessed. His agent
called it a creative block. Phan knew it was something worse.

The consequences accumulated quietly. His wife, Mei, began to notice that he flinched when she
hugged him unexpectedly. He stopped going to the grocery store because the cleaning aisle smelled of
bleach, which reminded him of the sanitizer. He stopped attending family dinners at his in-laws’
house because someone always lit candles. He told Mei he was tired, overworked, that the company was
demanding too much. She believed him at first. But after a year of this, she had grown frustrated
and hurt. She did not know about Samira. He had never told her. He could not bear to speak the words
aloud, because speaking them would mean admitting, in a permanent way, that he was the kind of man
who ran.

What finally brought Phan to seek help was a single moment in the dance studio. He was warming up
alone, trying to stretch his hamstrings, when the building’s furnace kicked on and a faint burnt
smell drifted through the vents. He was on the floor in seconds, his body rigid, his mind back in
the warehouse, his hands pressed against the wooden floorboards as if he were trying to push himself
upright and run again. When it passed, he lay there for a long time, staring at the ceiling. He was
afraid that if he did not change something, he would lose Mei, lose his career, lose whatever was
left of the life he had built. But what he hoped for, more than anything, was to one day believe
that he was not defined by the worst moment of his life.
