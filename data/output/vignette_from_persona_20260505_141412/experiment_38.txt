Leilani was sixty-six years old, a curriculum designer who had spent decades shaping lesson plans
from her home office in a quiet suburban neighborhood. She had been divorced for nearly fifteen
years and lived alone, which suited her. What she did not tell her colleagues during Zoom meetings
was that she kept her back to the wall in every room, and that she had not set foot in a grocery
store in over three years. The trauma that had reshaped her life occurred when she was a twenty-
three-year-old medic deployed to a forward operating base in Helmand Province. She had been on a
routine supply convoy when the lead vehicle hit an IED. The explosion was followed by small-arms
fire, and Leilani spent the next four hours pinned behind a disabled Humvee, treating a soldier
whose leg had been nearly severed while rounds cracked overhead and the rotor of a medevac
helicopter thudded in and out of earshot, never landing because the landing zone was too hot. She
remembered fragments: the wet sound of her own breathing inside her helmet, the soldier’s hand
gripping her wrist so hard it left bruises, the smell of diesel and blood. But the pieces did not
connect. She could not tell the story from beginning to end. When she tried, the words would not
come. She knew what had happened—she knew it with a certainty that sat like a stone in her chest—but
the memory felt unreal, as though it had happened to someone else, a woman she did not recognize.

In the decades since, Leilani had built a life around the certainty that she could not trust herself
to do the right thing. The feeling had started during the convoy, when she had frozen for what felt
like an eternity before crawling to the wounded soldier. She had acted—she had saved his life—but
the doubt remained. She had come to believe that she was always one mistake away from failing, and
that the only way to keep herself and others safe was to stay hypervigilant. She was on guard all
the time, scanning windows, scanning faces, tracking the sound of every engine that passed her
house. Helicopters were the worst. When she heard one, even from miles away, her throat would close
and she would find herself standing in the middle of the room, not remembering how she got there.

The avoidance had crept in slowly. At first, she simply stopped going to crowded places. Then she
stopped going out at all, except for essential errands, which she timed for hours when the streets
were empty. She never allowed herself to feel a strong emotion if she could help it. Anger, grief,
even joy—she pushed them down, because she was certain that if she let one feeling through, the rest
would follow in a flood she could not control. She had learned to sit in meetings with a pleasant,
neutral expression while her heart pounded and her eyes darted toward the exit. In every room she
entered, she calculated the distance to the door. She always chose a seat with a clear path. Her
colleagues thought she was just particular about where she sat. They did not know that she rehearsed
her escape route every time someone scheduled a mandatory in-person workshop.

The isolation became a fact of her life. She felt set apart from others, as though she were watching
the world through glass. Her divorce had been quiet and mutual—her husband had grown tired of her
refusal to travel, her insistence on sleeping with the bedroom door open, her startle response when
he came up behind her. She did not blame him. She had come to believe that she was simply not like
other people, that she carried something inside her that made connection impossible. She had stopped
trying.

What finally brought Leilani to seek help was not the hypervigilance or the avoidance, but the
intrusion. A memory of the event had begun coming to her mind suddenly, without warning, during work
hours. She would be reviewing a curriculum module on collaborative learning when the image of the
soldier’s hand on her wrist would flash across her vision, and she would lose her train of thought.
It happened three times in one week, and she realized she could no longer manage it alone. She
called a therapist who specialized in trauma. She was terrified that talking about it would make it
worse, that she would finally have to feel everything she had spent forty-three years avoiding. But
she was also tired. She wanted, for the first time in decades, to believe that something could
change.
