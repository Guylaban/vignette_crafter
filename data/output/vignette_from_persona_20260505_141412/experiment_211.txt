Sister Margaret, a 55-year-old nun, had spent the last thirty years living within the quiet rhythms
of her convent, a life she had always found profoundly safe and meaningful. That sense of safety was
shattered on a cool Tuesday afternoon in March. She had been driving Sister Anne, her closest friend
in the order, to a specialist appointment in the city. The rain was steady, the roads slick. At a
four-way intersection, a driver ran a red light at speed. Margaret saw the truck coming from her
left, and she remembers, with excruciating clarity, the sound—a deep, metallic groan that seemed to
go on for seconds—and the violent, twisting lurch of the car. She remembers the glass fracturing
into a white spiderweb on her window. She remembers turning her head and seeing Sister Anne’s face,
frozen in a moment of surprise, before the world went dark for a span she cannot account for. The
next thing she knows with any certainty is the smell of gasoline and the hiss of the airbags
deflating. The moments in between are a complete blank, a void that frightens her more than the
images she does hold. She was uninjured. Sister Anne died at the scene.

In the weeks that followed, Margaret found that the memory was not a story she could choose to tell
or not tell. It was a trap that sprang without warning. A flash of headlights at dusk, the squeal of
tires on pavement a block away, even the specific grey light of a rainy afternoon—any of these could
instantly drop her back into the passenger seat, the sound of the impact ringing in her ears as if
it were happening again. The images were mercilessly clear and detailed, except for that crucial,
terrifying gap. She became obsessed with that missing time. She would replay the moment over and
over, trying to force the memory to appear, certain that if she could just remember, she would
understand how she had failed.

The self-blame was absolute. She told her confessor, “The event happened because of the way I acted.
I should have seen the truck. I should have braked harder. I should have done *something*.” She had
come to believe that she was fundamentally unreliable, that she could not trust herself to keep
another person safe. The most damning thought, the one that circled back hour after hour, was that
she had failed to protect Sister Anne. She was a nun, a servant of God, and she had failed in the
most basic duty of a human being: to protect a friend. This belief became a kind of fixed truth in
her mind.

To manage this unbearable certainty, Margaret began to build a life of rigid control. She started
with the obvious: she would not drive. She could not risk being behind the wheel again. She avoided
the street where the accident happened entirely, taking a bus that added forty minutes to her weekly
trip to the grocery store. But the control soon spread. She began to avoid anything that reminded
her of Sister Anne—the garden they had tended together, the hymns they used to sing, even the smell
of lavender, which Sister Anne had loved. Her world began to shrink into the small, safe corners of
her room and the chapel. The most troubling behavior, the one she was most ashamed to admit, was the
mental ritual. Every night, before she could sleep, she had to perform a silent, repetitive prayer
in her mind, counting the Hail Marys in a specific pattern. If she was interrupted, or if she lost
count, she had to start over from the beginning. She was certain that if she did not complete the
ritual perfectly, something terrible would happen to someone else she loved. The ritual was
exhausting, but the thought of stopping it was terrifying.

The consequences were concrete. Her role in the convent had been as a pastoral visitor, calling on
the sick and elderly. She could no longer do this work; she could not bear the responsibility of
driving others, and she found that being in a car with anyone, even as a passenger, triggered a
suffocating panic. She had been reassigned to administrative work in the office, a job she found
hollow and isolating. Her relationships with the other sisters had grown strained. They tried to be
kind, but she could not explain the rituals or the fear without feeling like she was losing her
mind. She had come to believe that her faith, which had once been her bedrock, was now a source of
condemnation: God was a witness to her failure.

What finally brought her to seek help was not a sudden insight, but a slow, grinding exhaustion. She
had been caught in a loop of her own making for eight months, and she was tired. She was tired of
the rituals, tired of the fear, tired of the shrinking world. In the therapist’s office, she sat
very still, her hands clasped tightly in her lap. What she was most afraid of was that the therapy
would confirm what she already believed—that she was broken, that she could not be fixed, that her
failure was irredeemable. But what she was hoping for, with a fragile, almost silent hope, was that
she might one day be able to walk into the garden again without seeing Sister Anne’s face in the
flowers, and feel only the peace that was supposed to be there.
