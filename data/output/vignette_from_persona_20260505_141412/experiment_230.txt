For as long as he could remember, Amir had prided himself on precision. At fifty-four, working as a
software engineer in a downtown office tower, he spent his days refining algorithms, isolating
variables, and eliminating bugs. He believed that if you understood the system well enough, you
could control the outcome. That belief had been shattered one evening three years ago, when he had
stayed late to finish a deployment. A colleague—a man Amir had trusted, someone who held a senior
position in the company—had asked to speak with him privately in a storage room. Once the door
clicked shut, the colleague pinned him against a metal shelf and assaulted him. Amir had frozen. He
had not screamed. He had not fought back. And in the weeks that followed, that stillness became the
detail he could not forgive himself for.

In the immediate aftermath, Amir told no one. He finished the deployment, drove home, and showered
for forty minutes. He told himself it was a misunderstanding, that if he just kept working, the
feeling would pass. But it did not pass. Within a month, he found himself unable to walk past the
storage room on his floor. He started taking the stairs instead of the elevator, adding twelve
minutes to his commute each morning, because the stairwell door was on the opposite side of the
building. He began avoiding the company cafeteria when he saw anyone in a suit—anyone who looked
like an authority figure, anyone who might have the power to trap him in a room again. The colleague
had worn a dark blazer that night, and now Amir found himself tensing at the sight of any uniform:
police officers on the street, security guards in the lobby, even the maintenance staff in their
branded polo shirts. He knew it made no sense. He knew a janitor was not a threat. But his body did
not care.

The sirens started to matter about six weeks afterward. An ambulance passed his apartment window one
Saturday morning, and Amir found himself on the floor, heart pounding, certain that something
terrible was happening again. He began checking the street before leaving his building. He started
mapping routes in his head that avoided hospitals, fire stations, any place emergency vehicles might
congregate. His world began to shrink. He stopped going to the grocery store after dark, because
that was when the sirens seemed loudest. He stopped accepting invitations to dinner with male
friends, because being alone with a man—any man, even one he had known for twenty years—made his
throat close up. He told himself it was temporary. He told himself he was being careful.

But careful became a cage. Amir came to believe that he was never truly safe unless he was in his
apartment, with the door locked and the blinds drawn. He developed a habit of scanning every room he
entered: checking the exits, noting the positions of other people, cataloging potential weapons or
obstacles. He did it automatically now, even in his own office, even when he was alone. He had
become certain that danger was always lurking, that the next attack could come from anywhere, and
that if he let his guard down for a single second, he would be caught again. The worst part was that
he knew it was irrational. He could feel the mismatch—the empty hallway, the quiet coffee shop, the
harmless colleague—and still his heart would race, his palms would sweat, and he would have to
leave.

The injustice of it gnawed at him constantly. He had reported nothing. The colleague still worked on
the same floor. Amir had to coordinate code reviews with him, attend meetings with him, pretend
nothing had happened. He had come to believe that the system had failed before he even tried it—that
no one would believe a fifty-four-year-old man who had not fought back. And so the unfairness sat
inside him like a stone, growing heavier each time he had to shake the man’s hand at a stand-up. He
blamed himself, too. He replayed the moment again and again: the way he had stood there, the way he
had said nothing, the way he had walked out afterward and acted as if everything was fine. He was
certain that the assault had happened because of the way he had acted—or failed to act. He had lost
faith in his own judgment, and worse, he had lost the ability to feel anything normal. Joy felt
foreign. Grief felt like a locked room. He told his therapist in the first session that he could not
remember the last time he had laughed without checking over his shoulder first.

What finally brought Amir to seek help was a performance review. His manager had noticed his
declining productivity, his avoidance of collaborative work, his refusal to attend team-building
events. Amir was told that if things did not improve, his position might be at risk. That
threat—losing the one structure that still anchored his days—frightened him more than the memories.
He came to therapy wanting to find a way to function again. But underneath that, what he really
wanted was to stop living in a world where every siren meant danger, where every closed door meant a
trap, and where the man who hurt him still walked the same hallways while Amir stayed frozen in
place.
