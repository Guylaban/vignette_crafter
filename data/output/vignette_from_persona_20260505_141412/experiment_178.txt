Malai was twenty-seven years old, a journalist for a regional news outlet in the Pacific Northwest,
and he had spent the last eighteen months trying to disappear inside his own life. The relationship
that had nearly killed him had ended two years ago, but the man who had done it—a fellow journalist,
older, charismatic, someone Malai had trusted completely—still existed in the same professional
world. That was the problem. The newsroom was a small ecosystem, and Malai had learned that safety
was not something other people could guarantee for you.

The physical abuse had escalated slowly, the way these things often do. What began as sharp words
during arguments became a hand gripping his arm too tightly, then a shove against a wall, then a
punch to the ribs while Malai was pinned on the bed. He remembered the precise moment he had
understood he was in real danger: he had been trying to leave the apartment, and his partner had
blocked the door, then slammed him back against the kitchen counter. Malai’s lower back had struck
the edge of the granite, and a searing pain had shot through his kidneys. He had crumpled, and his
partner had stood over him, breathing hard, and said, “See what you make me do.” In that moment,
Malai had believed it. He had believed he was inadequate, that he had provoked this, that if he were
a better partner—more attentive, less argumentative, less himself—none of this would have happened.

That belief had not faded. It had calcified. In the months after he finally left, Malai had found
himself endlessly replaying the relationship, sifting through every interaction for the thing he had
done wrong. He would lie awake at three in the morning, his body rigid, running through the same
loop: the text he should not have sent, the tone he should not have used, the boundary he should not
have set. He never allowed himself to feel anger toward his ex-partner. That emotion felt dangerous,
like a door that should stay locked. Instead, he turned the anger inward, and when that became
unbearable, he stopped eating. Food felt like a need he did not deserve to meet. He would go entire
days on coffee and a single granola bar, telling himself he was just not hungry, though his hands
trembled and his concentration frayed.

His body had become a map of the event. When a memory returned—and it returned often, without
warning, with a vividness that made the present moment dissolve—he would feel the exact pain in his
lower back, the bruising pressure of a hand on his chest, the sensation of being pinned. The memory
did not feel like something that had happened. It felt like something that was happening, right now,
in the room with him. When it came, he had to stop whatever he was doing—mid-sentence, mid-edit,
mid-walk—and wait for it to pass, his heart pounding, his breath shallow, his whole body braced for
a blow that had already landed.

Public transport had become impossible. The bus was a confined space full of strangers, and Malai
could not sit with his back to anyone. He would stand near the exit, his eyes scanning the
passengers, cataloguing every man who boarded, his body tensing. He was certain that any one of them
could be a threat. He had learned that people you trusted could hurt you; strangers were just people
you had not yet given the chance. Being alone with any man—a colleague in an elevator, a neighbour
in the hallway, a source for an interview—sent his heart into a sprint. He had started requesting
that his interviews be conducted by phone or video call, and his editor had noticed. Malai had said
he was trying to be more efficient, but the lie felt thin even to him.

At night, he woke repeatedly, his eyes snapping open in the dark, listening. He would check the
locks on his apartment door, then check them again, then stand in the hallway and listen for
footsteps that were not there. His girlfriend, who had been patient and bewildered, would ask what
he was afraid of. He could not explain it in a way that made sense. Even when she held him and said,
“You are safe,” he did not believe it. He could not believe it. Safety was not a fact he could
absorb; it was a feeling that had been permanently removed from his body.

What had brought him to seek help, finally, was not the fear or the pain or the hunger. It was the
story he had been assigned to write—a profile of a local politician—and the politician had asked to
meet for coffee. Alone. In a café. Malai had sat in his car outside the café for twenty minutes, his
hands gripping the steering wheel, and then he had driven home and called in sick. He had spent the
rest of the day in his apartment, not eating, not moving, replaying the moment he had failed to walk
through a door. He was afraid that he was becoming someone who could no longer do his job. He was
afraid that the man who had hurt him had taken more than he had realised. And he was afraid that if
he let himself feel the anger he had never allowed himself to feel, it would consume everything.
