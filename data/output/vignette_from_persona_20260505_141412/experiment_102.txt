Priya had always thought of herself as someone who paid attention to detail. At twenty-three, she
was the youngest UX designer on her team, known for catching the edge cases that everyone else
missed. That precision was what had made her so good at her job, and it was also what had destroyed
her. The accident happened on a rainy Tuesday evening in November, on the stretch of highway between
her office in downtown Austin and the apartment she shared with her husband, Rohan. She had been
driving home after a late sprint review, her mind still half on a user flow that wasn’t resolving,
when her phone buzzed in the cupholder. She glanced down for a fraction of a second—just long enough
to miss the brake lights in front of her. The impact was a hard, sickening crunch of metal, and when
she got out of her car, she saw the woman in the other vehicle slumped forward, not moving. The
woman survived, but the doctors said she would never walk without assistance again. Priya had not
driven since that night. She told herself it was practical—Rohan could drive, and they lived close
enough to a bus line—but the truth was that the moment she sat behind the wheel, her hands went cold
and her chest tightened until she couldn’t breathe. She became a passenger in her own life,
literally and otherwise.

At work, the triggers were everywhere. Her desk faced a window that looked out onto the parking lot,
and every time she saw a car pull in, she felt a spike of dread that she had to swallow down. The
office itself had become a place of quiet vigilance. She avoided the break room during peak hours
because too many people meant too many faces, and some of those faces—a woman with dark curly hair,
a man in a grey sedan—reminded her of the person she had hurt. She had started eating lunch at her
desk with her back to the door, headphones on but no music playing, just listening for footsteps
that might bring a conversation she wasn’t ready to have. Her performance had slipped. She missed
details now, the very details she had once prided herself on catching, because her attention was
always split between the task on her screen and the constant scanning of her environment for
anything that might remind her of what she had done.

At home, the vigilance took a different form. Priya had become obsessed with replaying the accident
in her mind, trying to find the exact moment where she could have done something differently. If she
had left the office five minutes later. If she had waited until the next morning to finish the
wireframes. If she had just kept her eyes on the road. The thoughts came unbidden, usually at night
when she was trying to fall asleep, and she had learned to shove them down with fierce effort. She
told herself not to think about it, not to let the images surface, but the suppression only made the
thoughts feel more urgent, more insistent, pressing against the inside of her skull like something
trapped. She had stopped telling Rohan about these internal battles. He had been patient at first,
but she could see the worry in his face, the way he hesitated before asking how her day was, and she
didn’t want to be the reason he looked at her like that. So she smiled and said she was fine, and
then she spent the evening scrolling through her phone, avoiding the silence where the thoughts
lived.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was a meeting at work. Her manager had pulled her aside and
gently suggested that she take some leave, because the quality of her work had dropped and she
seemed distracted. The word “distracted” hit her like a physical blow. She had spent months trying
to prove that she was still the same person, still capable and careful, and here was the evidence
that she was not. That night, she sat on the bathroom floor with her back against the tub and cried
in a way she hadn’t allowed herself to do since the accident. She was terrified that she would never
feel safe again, that she would always be this version of herself—jumpy, hollow, haunted by a moment
that lasted less than a second. But underneath the fear, there was a thin thread of something else,
a hope that maybe if she talked to someone, she could find a way to stop replaying the crash long
enough to start living again. She didn’t want to be forgiven. She just wanted to stop being afraid
of her own mind.
