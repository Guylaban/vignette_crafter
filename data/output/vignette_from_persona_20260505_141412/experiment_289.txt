Mariana was twenty-three when the chemical spill happened, a first-generation college student
working nights at a small industrial laundry facility to cover her tuition. She had been
transferring a drum of industrial solvent when the seal failed. The liquid splashed across her
forearms and chest before she could drop the container; the vapor hit her face, and for several
seconds she could not breathe. She remembers the burning in her throat, the way her skin seemed to
tighten and prickle, and the smell—sharp, clinical, like the inside of a hospital sterilizing unit.
A supervisor rushed her to the emergency room, and she spent four hours in decontamination, being
flushed with water and monitored for respiratory failure. The doctors told her she would be fine.
Her body did not agree.

In the weeks that followed, Mariana quit the laundry job immediately and told herself she would move
on. But she could not shake the feeling that something fundamental had broken inside her. She had
always trusted her body—it had carried her through long shifts, through late nights studying,
through the stress of being the first in her family to pursue a degree. Now, her body seemed to have
its own agenda. Her skin would prickle and flush at nothing. Her chest would tighten when she walked
past a janitor’s closet at the university, catching a whiff of cleaning solution. She began to avoid
the science building entirely because the antiseptic smell from the labs made her throat close up.
She told herself it was reasonable, that she was just being careful, but she knew, deep down, that
she was running from something she could not name.

The memories themselves were slippery. Mariana knew she had been injured, knew she had been in the
ER, but when she tried to tell her roommate what happened, the words would not come. She could feel
the burn, could feel the panic, but the sequence of events was a jumble—she could not say whether
the decontamination had come before or after the doctor’s exam, whether she had been sitting or
lying down. What came back most vividly was the physical sensation: a stinging, tightening pain
across her chest and forearms, exactly where the solvent had touched her skin. Sometimes, lying in
bed at night, she would feel it again, as if the chemical were still eating into her flesh, and she
would gasp and sit up, convinced she was being poisoned all over again.

Mariana began to avoid darkness. She did not consciously decide to; it just became unbearable.
Waking up in the middle of the night, in the pitch-black of her studio apartment, she would feel the
same helpless disorientation she had felt in the ER, not knowing where the threat was coming from.
She started sleeping with the bathroom light on, then with the overhead light in the living room.
Her electricity bill doubled. She stopped going to evening classes and rearranged her schedule so
she was never walking home after sunset. Her grades slipped, but she told herself it was temporary.

She also could not stop scanning. Every time she entered a room, her eyes moved across the ceiling,
the corners, the floor, searching for something—a leaking pipe, a spilled container, a warning sign
she might have missed. She checked the seals on her water bottles before drinking. She avoided
anyone who smelled like rubbing alcohol or bleach. When a friend touched her shoulder unexpectedly
in the library, Mariana flinched so hard she knocked a stack of books to the floor. She apologized,
but inside she felt a surge of anger and shame. Her body was betraying her. It was reacting as if
every touch were an attack, every smell a poison. She had come to believe that something was
fundamentally wrong with her—that she was defective, that her body had turned against her, that she
could no longer trust herself to be safe in a world full of invisible threats.

By the time she sought help, Mariana was exhausted. She had stopped going to campus altogether for
three weeks, telling her professors she had a medical issue. She was afraid to leave her apartment
during the day, and at night she could not sleep. What finally pushed her to call a therapist was a
moment of clarity: she had been standing in her kitchen, staring at a bottle of dish soap, her heart
pounding, unable to bring herself to touch it. She realized she could not keep living this way. She
was terrified that therapy would not work, that she was too broken to fix. But she was also
terrified of staying the same.
