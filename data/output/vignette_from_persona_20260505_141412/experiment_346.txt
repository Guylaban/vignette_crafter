Ethan is a twenty-one-year-old married man who works as a social ministry worker in a mid-sized
American city. Two years ago, he deployed overseas as a civilian relief worker during a period of
active armed conflict. He was stationed near a forward operating base, and his role involved
distributing food and medical supplies to displaced families in a region where Arabic was the
dominant language. One afternoon, while his convoy was stopped at a checkpoint, a group of local men
began shouting in Arabic. Ethan had heard shouting like that many times before, but this time the
tone was different—urgent, angry, escalating. Before he could understand what was happening, gunfire
erupted from a nearby building. He dove to the floor of the armored vehicle, and for what felt like
an eternity he lay there, pressed against the metal, listening to the shouts and the shots and the
screams of a colleague who had been hit. The colleague survived, but Ethan has never forgotten the
sound of that voice, or the way his own body went cold with a terror so complete it felt like the
world had stopped.

In the months that followed, Ethan returned home and tried to resume his life. But the memory of
that afternoon does not behave like a normal memory. When he talks about the event—when a caseworker
at his ministry asks him about his deployment, or when a veteran in his congregation mentions a
combat experience—Ethan feels the same raw terror he felt on the floor of that vehicle. The memory
does not feel like something that happened to him; it feels like something that is happening to him
right now, in the present moment. Sometimes, when he tries to describe it to his wife, he stops mid-
sentence because the story sounds so extreme, so far outside the realm of ordinary life, that he
cannot believe it actually happened to him. He has come to wonder if he is losing his grip on
reality.

Ethan’s work as a social ministry worker now places him in constant contact with the very things
that terrify him. His office is in a neighborhood where many refugees and immigrants live, and he
frequently hears Arabic spoken on the street. Every time he hears that language shouted—even in
laughter, even in greeting—his body locks up and he scans for an exit. He has begun to avoid the
parts of his route that pass by the local mosque, not because he holds any prejudice, but because
the sound of Arabic voices in prayer or conversation sends him into a state of hypervigilance that
lasts for hours. He is certain that strangers approaching him, even in ordinary situations like a
grocery store or a gas station, pose a threat. He watches their hands, their faces, their sudden
movements. He has become so sensitive to motion that when someone turns quickly or raises an arm, he
flinches and feels his heart hammer.

To manage this constant sense of danger, Ethan has developed a rigid set of nightly routines. He
stays awake until two or three in the morning, delaying sleep because he knows that when he does
finally close his eyes, he will wake repeatedly to check that he is safe. He listens for footsteps
outside the bedroom door, for the creak of the stairs, for any sound that might signal an intrusion.
His wife has grown frustrated with his refusal to go to bed at a normal hour, and she has started
sleeping in the guest room. Ethan knows this is hurting their marriage, but he cannot bring himself
to change the habit—it feels like the only thing keeping them safe.

He has also noticed that he avoids anything that might make him feel happy or hopeful. He turns down
invitations to parties, to game nights with friends, to casual dinners. He has come to believe that
if he lets himself feel good, something terrible will happen to punish him for it. Instead, he
spends his evenings alone, replaying the event in his mind, trying to figure out how it could have
been prevented. He runs through every detail: if he had noticed the men earlier, if he had told the
driver to take a different route, if he had shouted back. The rumination gives him the illusion of
control, but it also keeps the memory fresh and raw.

What finally brought Ethan to seek help was a moment last week when his wife told him she could not
live like this anymore. She said she felt like she was married to a ghost. Ethan agreed to come to
therapy, but he is terrified of what he might find. He is afraid that if he talks about the event in
detail, he will be swallowed by the terror all over again. He is also afraid that he is broken in a
way that cannot be fixed. What he hopes for, more than anything, is to feel like the memory belongs
to the past—to be able to hear a voice raised in another language and not feel like he is back on
that floor.
