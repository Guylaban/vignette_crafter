Sanjay is a 44-year-old nurse who works the night shift at a community hospital in Queens, and he
came to therapy at the urging of his wife after months of deteriorating sleep, mounting
irritability, and a quiet but pervasive withdrawal from their shared life. The event that changed
everything happened ten months ago, just after 2 a.m., as he walked from the hospital parking garage
to the emergency department entrance. A man stepped out from between two parked vans, pressed
something hard into Sanjay’s ribs, and demanded his wallet and his phone. Sanjay handed them over
without a word, but the man shoved him against a concrete pillar anyway, hard enough that Sanjay’s
shoulder blade cracked against the edge and his right wrist bent back under his own weight. The man
ran. Sanjay sat on the cold asphalt for several minutes before he could stand, and by the time
security arrived he was already telling them he was fine, that he just needed to get to his shift.

That moment has not left him. When he closes his eyes, he does not remember the mugging in the way
he remembers other things—fuzzy, faded, reconstructed. Instead, the memory arrives as a series of
still images, perfectly sharp and utterly silent, like slides projected onto the inside of his
skull: the glint of the garage lights on the van’s rear window, the exact pattern of cracks in the
concrete pillar, the man’s jacket zipper at eye level. There is no narrative, no sequence of cause
and effect. Just photographs. And accompanying each image, a precise, localized pain—a hot spike in
his right shoulder, a dull ache in his left wrist—that replicates exactly what he felt during the
shove, even though neither area is injured anymore. He has learned to recognize the warning signs: a
flicker of brightness at the edge of a visual memory, a sudden tension in his shoulder, and then he
is back in the garage, even if he is standing in his own kitchen. To keep this from happening,
Sanjay has become an expert at keeping his mind occupied. He listens to podcasts on his commute,
plays word games on his phone during any idle moment, and fills every silence with internal chatter.
If his mind goes quiet, the pictures come.

He has come to believe, with a certainty that feels like fact, that there is something fundamentally
wrong with him—something that made the mugging happen. He was walking at 2 a.m., yes, but so did
other staff. He was alone, yes, but the garage was considered safe. The logic he lands on is that
the attacker saw something in him, some weakness or willingness, that marked him as a target. He
does not say this out loud, but privately he scans his own reflection for what that quality might
be, and finds only confirmation that he is somehow responsible. This belief has bled into every
corner of his life. When his wife suggests a weekend trip, he thinks: nothing good can happen to me
anymore. When a colleague mentions retirement plans, he feels a hollow certainty that he has no
future worth imagining. He cannot picture next year, let alone five years from now. The world feels
closed.

At work, Sanjay’s vigilance has become a full-time job in its own right. He constantly scans the
emergency department waiting room, the hallways, the break room—anywhere someone might be hiding or
approaching from an unexpected angle. He chooses his seat in the staff lounge based on which chair
gives him a clear view of both doors, and he will stand rather than sit in a spot that leaves his
back exposed. On his walk from the parking garage to the entrance, he now checks behind him every
few steps, sometimes turning his whole body to confirm no one is following. He has taken to checking
mirrors—rearview mirrors in the car, the reflective surfaces of vending machines, the glass of
darkened office windows—to see who or what might be behind him. What he cannot do is stop. The
checking feels necessary, like a ritual that keeps danger at bay, but it also exhausts him. He has
started arriving at work thirty minutes early just to account for the slow, hypervigilant walk from
the car.

Sleep has become a battleground. He lies in bed replaying the images, or anticipating them, or
trying not to anticipate them, and his mind refuses to settle. His wife has begun sleeping in the
guest room on her nights off, because she cannot tolerate his restlessness. Sanjay knows this is a
problem, but he also knows that when he does finally drift off, he often wakes with a start, his
shoulder aching, his heart pounding, unsure for a disorienting moment whether he is in the garage or
in bed.

What finally brought him to therapy was a quiet evening last month when his wife asked him to sit
with her on the couch and watch a movie. He agreed, but spent the entire ninety minutes with his
back to the wall, checking the window behind him every few minutes, unable to follow the plot. She
turned to him at the end and said, very gently, “You’re not here anymore, Sanjay. I miss you.” He
had no answer. He is not sure he knows how to come back. He is afraid that the checking will never
feel like enough, and equally afraid that if he stops, something terrible will happen again. He
hopes, quietly and without much conviction, that therapy might help him feel less trapped.
