For Marcus, the accident happened on a Thursday afternoon in late October, three years ago now. He
was driving home from the clinic where he worked as a physical therapist, a route he had taken
hundreds of times. A delivery truck ran a red light at an intersection Marcus had crossed without
thinking for a decade. He saw it coming, had time to think *this is it*, and then the world became
noise and glass and the smell of gasoline. He woke up in the ER with three cracked ribs, a fractured
clavicle, and a deep, persistent sense that the ground beneath him had turned to water. The physical
wounds healed over the next few months, but something else settled into him like sediment in still
water.

In the weeks that followed, Marcus began to notice that his own body no longer felt like an ally. As
a physical therapist, he had spent his career teaching people to trust their limbs, their balance,
their strength. Now he found himself flinching when he stood up too quickly, convinced that his
knees might buckle or his ribs might splinter again. He had come to believe that the accident had
permanently damaged something inside him that no x-ray could detect. When a patient asked him to
demonstrate a stretching routine, Marcus hesitated for the first time in his professional life. *I
can no longer trust my own body*, he thought, and the thought carried the weight of a verdict.

His environment began to feel like a landscape of hidden threats. At the clinic, he found himself
scanning rooms for objects that could be used as weapons—a pair of scissors on a desk, a heavy
trophy on a shelf, a fire extinguisher in its bracket. He noticed them all, catalogued them without
wanting to. When a colleague dropped a metal tray in the treatment room, Marcus hit the floor before
he knew what he was doing. The startle response embarrassed him deeply, but he could not control it.
His concentration frayed; he would lose the thread of a patient’s description of their symptoms, or
find himself staring at a treatment plan without reading a word.

Marcus developed routines to manage the anxiety. Every evening, he cleaned the kitchen counters and
the bathroom sink until they gleamed, arranging the bottles and jars in precise rows. He told
himself it helped him feel in control, though he was not sure what he was controlling. On his drive
to work, he mentally rehearsed every possible disaster: a car swerving into his lane, a patient
collapsing, a fire in the building. He ran through what he would do, step by step, as though
preparation could keep the world from cracking open again. But the rehearsals never ended, because
the list of worst cases was infinite.

The nightmares began about two months after the accident. He would wake up gasping, the sound of
metal crunching still ringing in his ears, his wife’s hand on his shoulder. The dream residue stayed
with him through the morning, a taste of ash in his mouth. He learned to sleep on his back, rigid,
so that he would not jostle his ribs in the night. Unwanted thoughts arrived at unpredictable
moments—while he was washing his hands, or tying his shoes, or watching a patient walk down the
hallway. The image of the truck, the sensation of the impact, the certainty of death. He would shake
his head, blink hard, and try to return to the present.

At work, Marcus found himself avoiding the treatment rooms near the back of the clinic, where the
windows faced the street. He told his supervisor he preferred the interior rooms for the lighting.
He did not say that he could not bear to watch cars pass by, that every flash of motion made his
chest tighten. His wife noticed that he had stopped going to the grocery store, that he left social
gatherings early, that he seemed to be shrinking into himself. She asked if he wanted to talk to
someone. He said he was fine.

What finally brought Marcus to seek help was a morning in early spring, three years after the
accident. He was standing in his kitchen, cleaning the same counter for the third time, when he
realised he could not remember the last time he had felt safe. Not in his home, not at his job, not
in his own skin. He had become a stranger to himself—a man who scanned rooms for weapons, who
rehearsed disasters, who trusted nothing and no one, least of all his own body. He was afraid of
what he might become if he did not find a way out. He was afraid of what he might already be.
