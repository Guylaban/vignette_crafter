Frank is a 66-year-old software engineer, married for over forty years, who came to therapy at his
wife’s gentle but persistent urging. He sat forward in the chair, hands resting on his knees, and
described a life lived on high alert. He had always known something was different about him, but it
was only in the last few months, after a series of small, inexplicable reactions, that the shape of
it had begun to clarify.

The event itself had happened more than fifty years ago, in the basement of his childhood home. His
father, a man whose moods could shift without warning, had cornered him there. Frank remembered the
cold concrete against his bare legs, the smell of damp and furnace oil, and the specific, searing
sensation of his father’s hand—hot, flat, and hard—landing on the back of his neck. It wasn’t the
worst beating, but it was the one that had lodged itself permanently. The temperature of that hand,
the way it felt like a brand on his skin, had become a fixed point in his memory. He could still
feel it, sometimes, without warning. A memory that came not as a picture, but as a sudden, full-body
flash: the cold floor, the hot palm, the heart pounding in his chest so hard he thought it would
break his ribs.

He had learned, over the decades, to manage. As a software engineer, he found comfort in the
predictable logic of code, in systems that followed rules. His work environment was controlled,
quiet, and solitary. But even there, the vigilance never stopped. He scanned every room he entered,
a habit so automatic he no longer noticed he was doing it. He noticed potential weapons everywhere:
a letter opener on a desk, a heavy stapler, the sharp edge of a paper cutter in the supply closet.
He didn’t think of them as threats in a conscious way; his body simply registered them, catalogued
them, before his mind had even caught up. He had come to believe that this was just how the world
worked—that danger was always waiting, and that only constant, exhausting watchfulness kept him
safe.

The consequences of this vigilance had crept into every corner of his life. He had stopped accepting
social invitations years ago. Seeing people he had once enjoyed—colleagues from old projects,
neighbors who waved from their driveways—now felt like a burden. He couldn’t relax around them. He
was certain they could see something wrong with him, that they could sense he was different, an
outsider playing a part. He had come to believe, with a quiet and unshakeable certainty, that people
could not be trusted. Not because they were malicious, but because they were unpredictable. And
unpredictability was the thing he feared most.

The most difficult consequence, and the one that had finally brought him to therapy, was the toll it
had taken on his marriage. He loved his wife, but he had built a wall of avoidance between them. He
could not bear to be touched unexpectedly. A hand on his shoulder from behind would make him flinch,
his body jerking away before his mind could register it was her. He had started to avoid physical
contact altogether, pulling back from hugs, sitting at the far end of the couch. He had also,
without really meaning to, begun to avoid looking at his own body. He dressed in the dark, avoided
mirrors in the bathroom, and felt a deep, wordless aversion to the sensation of his own skin. He did
not know why. He only knew that his body felt like a place where something bad had happened, and
that paying attention to it was dangerous.

What frightened him most was the way his body reacted before he could think. A sudden noise, a door
closing too hard, and his heart would be racing, his hands cold, before he even knew what had
triggered it. He had spent his whole life trying to be in control, to be rational, to keep the world
orderly. But his own body had become a source of chaos he could not manage. He came to therapy not
because he believed he could change, but because his wife had said, with tears in her eyes, that she
missed him. And he realized, with a hollow ache, that he missed himself too. He hoped, more than he
dared to admit, that there might be a way to feel safe without having to stay so hidden.
