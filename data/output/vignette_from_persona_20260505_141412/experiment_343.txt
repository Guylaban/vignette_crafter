her family’s boat. She came to therapy at the urging of her husband, who had grown increasingly
worried about her. The event that changed everything happened two years ago, on a clear morning in
late September. Aminata’s younger brother, Kofi, had joined her for a day of fishing—something they
had done together since childhood. As they were hauling in a net near the harbor, a car veered off
the coastal road and crashed through the guardrail, plunging onto the dock below. The car struck
Kofi directly. Aminata saw it happen. She was only a few feet away. She remembers the sound, the way
the car crumpled, and the terrible stillness afterward. She remembers dropping the net and running
to him, but by the time she reached him, he was already gone.

In the weeks and months that followed, Aminata could not stop replaying the moment. She asked
herself over and over what she could have done differently. If she had called out to him a second
earlier, if she had pulled him closer to her, if she had noticed the car’s trajectory sooner—surely
there was something. She became certain that she had failed him, that her actions, or lack of
action, had sealed his fate. She told herself that she should have protected him, that as his older
sister it was her job, and she had not done it. The guilt settled into her bones. She began to
believe she would never forgive herself.

The anniversary of the accident arrives each September like a storm she cannot outrun. In the days
leading up to it, she feels a familiar dread, her chest tightening, her hands trembling. She cannot
bring herself to talk about what happened, and when others mention car accidents or crash scenes on
the news, she feels her stomach drop and her heart race. She has learned to avoid certain
conversations entirely. If someone at the docks starts discussing a fender bender or a highway
collision, she walks away, her face blank, her pulse pounding in her ears. She has also stopped
driving along the coastal road, taking long detours through town instead, even though it adds thirty
minutes to her commute.

At home, Aminata has changed in ways that worry her husband. She has become irritable, snapping at

too afraid to close her eyes. When she finally sleeps, it is fitful, and she wakes feeling as though
she has not rested at all.
