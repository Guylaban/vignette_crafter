Mariana is a fifty-four-year-old combat engineer who spent twenty-two years in the United States
Army, much of that time deployed to active combat zones. She is married to a retired mechanic, and
they live in a quiet suburban neighborhood outside of San Antonio. She came to therapy at her
husband’s urging, though she was not sure what talking could possibly change. Her PCL-5 score of 62
reflected the severity of what she had been carrying for nearly a decade.

The event that fractured her life happened during her third deployment, in a forward operating base
in eastern Afghanistan. A routine route-clearing patrol had been called back early, and Mariana was
in the motor pool inspecting a damaged MRAP when an improvised explosive device detonated near the
perimeter. The blast wave threw her against a fuel truck. She remembers the heat—a specific, searing
temperature on her skin, as though the air itself had turned solid and burning. She remembers the
fire that erupted from the vehicle next to her, flames climbing the side of a supply tent, and the
shouting—men screaming orders, names, warnings, all blending into a roaring chaos. She cannot tell a
clear story of what happened after that, not even to herself. The minutes are a smear of noise and
heat and the smell of diesel. She knows she helped pull two soldiers to cover. She knows she was
later told she had a concussion and second-degree burns on her forearms. But the sequence, the logic
of cause and effect, the narrative she could tell another person—that never formed.

In the years since, Mariana has learned to live inside a body that feels constantly tense, braced
for something. She scans every environment she enters, her eyes moving from corners to doorways to
windows before she can stop them. At the grocery store, she notices the exits. At a restaurant, she
positions herself with her back to the wall. She has come to believe that the world is a dangerous
place, and that this vigilance is the only thing keeping her safe. Unusual silence terrifies her—a
quiet house, a pause in conversation, the absence of traffic noise on a Sunday morning—because
silence, in her experience, is what comes before the blast. She has difficulty concentrating on
anything that requires sustained attention. She will be reading a manual or watching a television
show and suddenly she is back in the heat, hearing the shouting in her head, the exact pitch and
urgency of those voices, and she has no idea how much time has passed.

She has developed a pervasive strategy of avoiding anything that could be stressful. This has
narrowed her life considerably. She stopped attending family gatherings because someone might ask
about her service. She stopped driving past the VFW hall. She stopped watching news coverage of
military operations, then stopped watching news altogether. She told herself she was protecting her
peace. But the cost has been concrete. Her husband has taken over all household responsibilities
that involve leaving the house, because Mariana cannot tolerate the unpredictability of errands. He
handles the bills, the shopping, the appointments. She has not visited her adult daughter in three
years. She has not had a conversation with a friend that lasted more than ten minutes. She numbs her
feelings because when she lets herself feel anything, what rises first is a cold, grinding sense of
injustice—that what happened to her was deeply unfair, and that nothing has been done about it. She
does not know who she would hold accountable, or what reparation would look like. She only knows
that something is wrong with her as a person, that she is broken in a way that cannot be fixed, and
that the rest of the world goes on living while she stays frozen in that moment of heat and noise.

What finally brought her to seek help was not her own distress but a single moment of terror. Her
husband was grilling in the backyard, and a gust of wind made the flames flare. Mariana saw the
fire, felt the remembered temperature on her skin, and before she knew what she was doing, she had
grabbed a garden hose and sprayed the grill until it was dead. Her husband stood there, stunned, and
said nothing. Later that night, he told her quietly that he could not watch her live like this
anymore. She is afraid of what will happen if she lets the memories come fully, afraid that she will
fall apart and never put herself back together. But she is also tired. She is tired of the scanning,
the tension, the silence that feels like a threat. She came to therapy because she does not know
what else to do, and because she cannot bear the thought of losing the one person who has stayed.
