Before the accident, Arjun had thought of himself as someone who noticed things. He was a writer,
after all—he paid attention to the details other people missed: the way light fell across a room,
the cadence of a friend’s laugh, the small, unspoken rhythms of daily life. That was what made his
stories feel alive. But now, noticing things had become a curse. Every creak of the floorboards in
his apartment, every sudden clatter from the kitchen, every unexpected sound sent a jolt through his
chest that left him breathless and shaking. He had started sleeping with the television on, not
because it helped him relax—he could not relax anywhere, not even in his own bed—but because the
constant drone of noise felt safer than the silence, which was punctuated by sounds that made him
feel as though something terrible was about to happen again.

The terrible thing had happened eight months ago, on a Tuesday afternoon in late October. Arjun’s
older brother, Ravi, had been riding his bicycle home from work along a road they had both taken a
hundred times. A driver ran a red light. By the time Arjun got to the hospital, Ravi was already
gone. There was no warning, no slow decline, no chance to say goodbye. Just a phone call, a taxi
ride, and a room where a doctor used words that did not seem to belong to Arjun’s life. In the weeks
that followed, Arjun had tried to hold onto the belief that the world made sense—that bad things
happened for reasons, that there was some order beneath the chaos—but that belief had shattered
completely. He no longer trusted that the world was safe. He no longer trusted that anything was
safe. He had come to believe that disaster was simply waiting, hidden behind ordinary moments, and
that he was powerless to see it coming.

He stopped making plans. Not just big plans, like travel or career goals, but any plans at all. When
a friend asked if he wanted to grab coffee next week, Arjun said he would let them know, and then he
never did. When his editor asked about his next project, he said he was still thinking about it. The
truth was that thinking about the future felt like tempting fate. Every time he imagined a happy
event—a holiday, a dinner out, a quiet weekend—he was certain that something would happen to destroy
it. It was safer to live in the present, or better yet, in the past, where the worst thing had
already happened and could not happen again.

But the past was not safe either. Grief reminders were everywhere. The smell of Ravi’s favorite
curry from the Indian restaurant down the street made Arjun’s stomach turn. The sight of a bicycle
locked to a rack outside a shop made him cross to the other side of the road. He had stopped going
to the park where they used to play chess on Sunday afternoons. He had stopped answering calls from
his mother, because her voice sounded too much like Ravi’s. He knew she was worried about him, but
every time she asked if he was okay, he felt a desperate urge to ask her the same question back,
over and over, as if her reassurance might somehow stick. He kept asking his roommate, his remaining
friends, even the barista at the coffee shop where he used to write—“Do I look okay? Do I seem
normal?”—but the answers never lasted. The fear always came back.

He had once been a happy person. He remembered that version of himself the way one remembers a
character from a book read long ago. That Arjun had laughed easily, had looked forward to things,
had believed that the world was basically good. That Arjun was gone. In his place was someone who
jumped at the sound of a car horn, who lay awake at three in the morning staring at the ceiling, who
could not imagine a single future event without feeling a cold certainty that it would never come to
pass. He came to therapy because his roommate had finally sat him down and said, “You cannot keep
living like this.” Arjun did not know if he could live any other way. But he was tired. He was so
tired. And for the first time in months, he let himself hope that maybe, just maybe, there was a way
back to the world he had lost.
