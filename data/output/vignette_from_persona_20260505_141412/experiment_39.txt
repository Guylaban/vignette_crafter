For twenty-nine years, Leo Tran had stood in courtrooms and argued for the accused. He had been a
public defender long enough to believe that even the worst moments in a person’s life could be
shaped into a coherent story, presented to a jury, and resolved. That belief ended on a Tuesday
evening in November, two years ago, when his wife, Mai, collapsed in their kitchen while washing
rice. Leo heard the thud from the living room and found her on the tile floor, her face turned away
from him. He called 911, he remembered that, but what he remembered next came only in fragments—the
sound of the paramedic’s radio crackling a series of numbers he could not make sense of, the sight
of Mai’s slipper lying on its side near the stove, the way his own voice sounded far away when he
tried to answer their questions. He could not put those pieces together in order no matter how many
times he tried. When he closed his eyes, he heard the radio again, that staticky burst of syllables
that meant nothing to him but that he was certain, in the moment, meant something terrible was about
to happen.

In the months that followed, Leo did not tell anyone that his memories came in broken pieces. He
told his colleagues that he was fine, that he was taking some time, that he would be back soon. He
did go back, eventually, but the first day he walked into the courthouse, he found himself scanning
every face in the hallway with a speed that felt automatic and exhausting. He was looking for
someone dangerous, though he could not have said who. What he noticed instead was how many people
there were, how close they stood, how easily a person could be hidden in a crowd. He began arriving
an hour early so he could enter the building before the rush, and he left as soon as his last case
was called, walking to his car with his keys already between his fingers. At home, the checking
started quietly—he would touch the front door lock before bed, then check it again before he turned
off the light, then get up once more to be absolutely certain. He knew it was locked. He had checked
it three times. He checked it again anyway.

Leo had always been a man who ate methodically, but after Mai died, he found himself standing in
front of the refrigerator at ten o’clock at night, eating leftover noodles straight from the
container without tasting them. It was not hunger. It was a way to stop his mind from replaying the
sound of the paramedic’s radio, a way to fill the silence in the apartment with something other than
the fragmented images that rose up without warning. He gained weight, but he did not care. What he
cared about was the television. He had always watched the evening news, but now he could not bear
it. Any story about a sudden death, a heart attack, a person collapsing in their home—he would feel
his chest tighten and his throat close, and he would turn it off mid-sentence. He stopped watching
hospital dramas entirely. He cancelled his streaming subscriptions for crime documentaries. His
daughter called him one evening and asked if he had seen a particular film that was nominated for
awards, and he said no, he had not, and he did not explain why.

What frightened Leo most was the way his body reacted to nothing at all. He would be sitting in his
office, reviewing a file, and suddenly his heart would pound as if someone had shouted in his ear.
His hands would shake. He would look around the room and find nothing wrong—no threat, no danger, no
reason for the terror that flooded through him. But the feeling was real. He had come to believe
that his own body was lying to him, that it had become a faulty alarm that rang for no reason, and
that there was no way to trust himself anymore. And underneath all of that, what he felt when he
remembered Mai was not the fear. It was the grief. It came over him like a wave that knocked him
sideways, and when he remembered her hands in the sink, the rice grains scattered on the counter, he
would hear her voice in his head saying something ordinary—his name, maybe, or asking him what he
wanted for dinner—and he would have to sit down because the loss was as fresh as the day it
happened.

He came to therapy because his daughter told him she was worried. She said he was not himself. Leo
did not know what that meant anymore. He was afraid that if he let himself feel the grief fully, he
would not be able to function at all. But he was also tired of checking the locks.
