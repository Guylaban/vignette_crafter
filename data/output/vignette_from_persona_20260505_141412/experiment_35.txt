For the first forty-seven years of her working life, Eleanor had walked into rooms full of strangers
and felt only the familiar hum of professional readiness. As a social worker in a county agency, she
had spent decades entering the homes of volatile families, sitting across from men who were angry or
grieving or both, and she had never once thought about where the exits were. Now, at eighty, she
could not enter a waiting room without her eyes moving instantly to every door, every window, every
possible way out. She had retired three years ago, but the scanning had not retired with her.

The event that changed everything had happened fifty-two years ago, but Eleanor could not have
described it as a story with a beginning, a middle, and an end. When she tried to tell her doctor
what had occurred, the memories came to her as snapshots: the sound of the apartment door slamming
shut, the sight of his hand against the wall beside her head, the feeling of the floor against her
cheek. She knew these images belonged to the same night, but she could not arrange them in order.
The sequence was gone. What remained were moments, each one frozen and vivid, and when she recalled
them she felt a grief so total that it seemed to have no object—not for the man she had loved, not
for the woman she had been, but for something larger that had been taken from her and could not be
named.

She had ended the relationship within a month of that night and had never spoken of it again. For
decades, she had believed she had simply moved on. She had built a life—work she cared about, a
small apartment she kept orderly, a few trusted friends. She had told herself that what happened
belonged to the past. But the past, she had come to understand, was not something that stayed in its
place. It was something that leaked.

The first sign had been the doors. She began to notice that she could not tolerate the sound of a
door opening unexpectedly. The click of a latch, the groan of hinges, the sharp slam of a
cabinet—these sounds made her heart lurch as if she had been touched without warning. Then came the
men. She had always been comfortable with male colleagues, but now she felt a low, persistent unease
whenever she was alone with a man, even men she had known for years. She began to arrange her
schedule so that home visits were always with female clients. She stopped attending agency events
where she might be left in a room with a male coworker. She told herself she was being sensible, but
she knew, in the part of her mind that did not lie, that she was afraid.

The nights were the worst. She had always been a light sleeper, but now sleep felt impossible. She
would lie in bed, her body rigid, listening to the building settle, and she would try not to think
about what had happened. She had developed a strategy for this: she would try to erase the memory
entirely, as if she could scrub it from her mind like a stain. But the erasing itself became a kind
of thinking, and the thinking kept her awake. She had also learned that if she stayed up late
enough—past midnight, past one—she could sometimes avoid the dreams altogether. She had come to
dread the moment of falling asleep, because she never knew what snapshot would be waiting for her.

She had tried to make sense of it. She spent hours turning the event over in her mind, asking
herself why it had happened, what she could have done differently, what it meant about her. But the
questions led nowhere. Her beliefs about the world—that people were essentially decent, that hard
work and kindness would protect her, that she could trust her own judgment—had been destroyed. She
felt, in a way she could not articulate, that she was contaminated. The dirt was not on her skin but
inside her, and no amount of washing could reach it.

What finally brought her to the clinic was not the fear or the grief or the sleeplessness. It was
the isolation. She had stopped seeing her friends because she could not explain why she flinched
when a door opened, why she sat with her back to the wall in restaurants, why she could no longer be
alone with her own nephew. She had come to believe that people could not be trusted, but she had
also come to believe that she could not be understood. She was eighty years old, and she was tired
of carrying the snapshots alone. She did not know if talking would help. But she had run out of
other ideas, and she was afraid of what would happen if she kept trying to erase what would not stay
erased.
