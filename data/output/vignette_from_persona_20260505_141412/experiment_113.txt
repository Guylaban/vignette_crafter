At forty-six, Marcus had spent twenty-three years as an auditor for a regional insurance firm in
Honolulu, a career built on predictability, on columns of numbers that always added up, on risk that
could be calculated and contained. He had been married to Leilani for nineteen years, and they had
two daughters, now grown and living on the mainland. On the first Tuesday of May, three years ago, a
flash flood swept through the valley where their home sat. Marcus had been at work. Leilani had been
at home. By the time he made it through the rising water to their street, the house was gone, and so
was she. He never found her body. The official report listed her as a drowning victim, presumed
swept out to sea. What Marcus remembered most vividly, what returned to him in the most unbearable
way, was not the water itself but the smell—the thick, wet smell of mud and gasoline and rotting
vegetation that had filled the air for weeks afterward. Even now, when he least expected it, that
smell would surface. He would be sitting at his desk, reviewing a client’s expense reports, and
suddenly the air in the room would change, and he could taste it too, a gritty, metallic tang on his
tongue, as though he had just swallowed floodwater. The memory was so extreme, so vivid, that it
sometimes felt unreal, like a story that had happened to someone else. But it had happened to him,
and he could not stop it from happening again.

The consequences of that morning reshaped every part of his life. He had moved to a small apartment
in a high-rise downtown, as far from the valley as he could get while still keeping his job. He told
himself it was practical, easier to maintain, closer to the office. But the truth was that he could
not bear to be near any physical location that reminded him of the flood—the low-lying roads, the
bridges, the stretches of coast where debris had washed up. He stopped driving entirely. He took the
bus, always the same route, always the same seat, because unfamiliar places felt impossible. He
could not assess the danger in a place he did not know. And so his world had shrunk to a narrow
corridor: his apartment, the bus stop, the office, the grocery store two blocks away. He had become
an expert at scanning for threat. Silence, for instance, had become unbearable. When the bus was too
quiet, or when the hallway outside his apartment was still for too long, his chest would tighten. He
was certain that something dangerous was about to happen. He had also noticed that any delay—a bus
that was late, a line at the grocery store that moved more slowly than expected—sent a spike of
alarm through him. Something bad must be happening. He could not stop bad things from happening to
him. That was the belief that had settled into his bones like a low-grade fever.

He had come to believe that relaxing was dangerous. On the rare evenings when he caught himself
feeling calm, or when he sat down to watch a movie and felt his shoulders drop, a sharp dread would
cut through the ease. He would stand up. He would find something to do. He had started smoking
again, a habit he had quit fifteen years ago, because the act of smoking gave his hands and his
mouth something to do, and because the ritual felt like a form of vigilance. He did not talk about
the flood. When colleagues asked how he was doing, he said he was fine. When his daughters called
and tried to get him to talk about their mother, he changed the subject or said he had to go.
Talking about it felt like opening a door he had spent three years trying to keep shut. He had no
interest in therapy, no interest in support groups. What would be the point? The world was a
dangerous place. He knew that now. And he felt dead inside, hollowed out, as though the flood had
taken not just his wife but his capacity to feel anything at all.

What finally brought him to seek help was not insight or a crisis. It was a performance review at
work. His manager had noted that Marcus seemed distracted, that his usually meticulous reports had
begun to contain errors, that he had missed two deadlines in the past quarter. The manager
suggested, gently, that he take some leave. Marcus heard the unspoken threat: get help, or your job
is at risk. He made an appointment with a psychologist because he was afraid of losing the last
predictable thing in his life. He sat in the waiting room with his hands on his knees, breathing
through his mouth, trying not to smell anything. He did not know if talking would help. He was
almost certain it would not. But he was also tired of living in a world that felt, every moment,
like the water was about to rise again.
