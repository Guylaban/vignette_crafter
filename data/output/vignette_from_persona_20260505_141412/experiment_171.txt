Somsak is a 66-year-old roofer who has been married for over forty years. He came to therapy at his
wife’s urging, though he is not sure what talking can do now. The event that changed everything
happened three years ago, when he was called to repair a leak on the roof of a small industrial
cleaning facility. The job seemed straightforward, but as he worked near a vent, a cloud of chemical
fumes rose and engulfed him. He remembers the smell vividly—sharp, sweet, and metallic, like
antiseptic mixed with something burning. His throat closed, his eyes streamed, and he could not
breathe. He crawled to the edge of the roof, gasping, certain he was going to die right there. A
coworker called an ambulance, and Somsak spent three days in the hospital on oxygen. The doctors
said his lungs were irritated but not permanently damaged. He was sent home with instructions to
rest.

He did not rest. Instead, he found that he could not stop replaying the moment the fumes hit him.
The memory is like a film that plays over and over in his mind, always the same sequence: the vent,
the smell, the panic, the feeling of his lungs seizing. Sometimes, without any clear image, the full
emotional intensity of the event washes over him—the terror, the suffocation—and he is left shaking,
unable to understand why. The memory itself is very clear and detailed, more vivid than anything
else in his life. He can still taste the chemical on his tongue.

The smell became a trap. Any antiseptic scent, any hospital or clinic waiting room, triggers the
same physical response. Worse, he discovered that related smells—cleaning products, paint thinner,
even strong air fresheners—can do it too. He now avoids going to the doctor altogether, which
worries his wife because of his age. But what frightens Somsak most is the dark. Nighttime, or
waking up in the dark, brings the memory rushing back. He cannot say why, but the darkness feels
like the blindness of that moment when the fumes clouded his vision. He has come to believe that he
cannot rely on himself anymore. Before the event, he was a competent man who could fix anything. Now
he is certain the world is a dangerous place, and that he is not equipped to handle it. He feels he
has no future.

His avoidance has become a way of life. He no longer goes anywhere unfamiliar, because he cannot
assess the danger. He sticks to the same routes, the same stores, the same few rooms in his house.
At work, he used to be the one who spoke up about safety concerns. Now he never disagrees or asserts
himself, to avoid any conflict that might escalate into something unpredictable. He tries hard not
to think about the trauma at all, pushing the memories down, but they surface anyway, especially at
night. He dwells constantly on how he used to be before the event—active, confident, unafraid. That
man feels lost to him.

The consequences have been concrete. He was given light duty after the incident, but his
concentration has suffered. On the roof, he finds himself unable to focus, his mind drifting to the
smell or the darkness, and he has made small errors that could have been dangerous. His supervisor
has noticed. Somsak is terrified of being let go, but he cannot explain why he is struggling. At
home, he is unable to relax even when he knows he is safe. He sits rigidly in his chair, scanning
the room, waiting for something bad to happen. His wife has stopped asking him to go out for dinner
or to visit their grandchildren, because he always says no. She is patient, but Somsak sees the
worry in her eyes, and it makes him feel worse.

What finally brought him here was a night last month when he woke up in the dark, gasping, certain
he was back on that roof. He could not catch his breath for several minutes. His wife held him and
said, quietly, that she could not watch him suffer like this anymore. Somsak agreed to come, though
he is not sure what he is hoping for. He is most afraid that nothing will work, and that he will
spend the rest of his years trapped in this small, fearful life. But somewhere, beneath the
hopelessness, there is a faint wish to feel like himself again, even if he cannot imagine what that
would look like.
