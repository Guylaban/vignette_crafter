Kevin is a 28-year-old social media manager who lives with his girlfriend in a mid-sized city, and
he came to therapy at her urging after months of escalating tension between them. He had served a
single deployment as a combat medic in a forward operating base in Afghanistan three years ago, and
the event that now governed his inner life had unfolded over roughly forty-five minutes during a
routine resupply convoy. An IED had struck the lead vehicle, and Kevin had been the first responder.
He had pulled two men from the wreckage, one of whom had lost most of his right leg below the knee.
Kevin had applied tourniquets, packed wounds, and held pressure on the femoral artery of a soldier
whose face he could still see clearly when he closed his eyes—except that when he tried to hold that
face in his mind, it broke apart into disconnected fragments: the glint of a watch, the wet sound of
breathing, the color of the blood against desert sand, none of it arranging itself into a coherent
story. Sometimes the emotion came without any image at all. He would be standing in his kitchen
making coffee and suddenly his heart would pound, his hands would shake, and he would feel the full,
raw horror of that morning without any picture in his head. The memory felt so extreme that part of
him still could not believe it had actually happened to him. He would think, *This cannot be real.
This did not happen to me.*

Kevin had come to believe that the experience had permanently damaged him, that he had changed for
the worse and could never change back. He was certain that someone else—someone braver, someone
better trained—would not have gotten into that situation in the first place, as though he had
somehow failed by being present. At work, he managed three brand accounts and spent most of his day
writing copy and scheduling posts, but he had developed a rigid habit of rehearsing every
conversation before it happened. Before a quick call with a client or a check-in with his
supervisor, he would run through possible questions and answers in his head, sometimes writing them
out, because he was terrified of being caught off guard. He avoided any situation that put his body
under stress: he had stopped going to the gym, stopped running, stopped even walking quickly if he
felt his heart rate rise, because the feeling of physical strain reminded him of the adrenaline of
that day and he could not tolerate it.

Driving had become nearly impossible unless he was alone. His girlfriend had asked him repeatedly to
drive them to dinner or to visit her family, and each time he made an excuse. When he did drive
alone, he flinched at every shadow on the road. Helicopters flew over the city occasionally—medical
transport, news choppers—and the sound would freeze him mid-sentence, his palms slick, his throat
tight. In public, he found himself scanning for visible injuries on others. A man with a cane, a
woman with a bandage on her arm, a child with a scrape: each one pulled his attention and held it,
and he could not look away. He avoided military uniforms and police officers, crossing streets to
avoid walking near a recruiting center or a patrol car. At night, he struggled to fall asleep
because he felt he needed to stay alert. He lay in bed listening, his body tense, and if his
girlfriend touched him unexpectedly in the dark he would jerk away with a sharp intake of breath,
which had begun to hurt her feelings.

The consequence that finally brought him to therapy was relational. His girlfriend had told him,
gently but directly, that she felt like she was living with a stranger who was always on guard. She
said she missed the person who used to laugh and plan weekends and reach for her hand. Kevin had no
counterargument. He was afraid of what therapy might unearth—afraid that talking about the memory
would make it more real, more permanent—but he was also afraid of losing her. He sat in the intake
chair with his hands on his knees, his jaw tight, and said he wanted to feel like himself again,
though he was not sure that version of himself still existed.
