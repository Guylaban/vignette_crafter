Arthur is a sixty-two-year-old bank teller who has been married to his wife, Linda, for thirty-eight
years. He came to therapy at her urging, though he was not entirely sure what talking could do. The
event that changed everything happened eighteen months ago, on a rainy Tuesday afternoon in
November. Arthur had been working the drive-through lane at the bank where he had spent the last
twenty-two years. A customer in a red sedan had pulled up too far forward, and Arthur, distracted by
a conversation with a coworker, pressed the button to raise the security barrier without checking
the side mirror first. The barrier struck a young mother who had been walking her child in a
stroller along the sidewalk that ran beside the lane. The metal arm caught her across the temple.
She collapsed. The child was not hurt, but the woman suffered a traumatic brain injury and spent
three weeks in the ICU. Arthur learned later that she would never fully recover her short-term
memory or her ability to speak without effort.

What Arthur remembers of that moment comes only in fragments. He does not recall pressing the
button. He does not recall the sound. What comes back to him, without warning and without his
permission, is the image of the stroller tipping sideways, the child’s face turning toward him, the
woman’s hand opening and closing on the wet pavement. These pieces arrive in the middle of a
conversation, or as he is unlocking the front door, or when he is half-asleep in bed. And when they
come, the terror is as fresh as it was that afternoon—his chest tightens, his mouth goes dry, and
for a few seconds he is certain he is about to die, though he cannot say why. The guilt that follows
is worse. It sits in his chest like a stone. He had been careless. He had been distracted. He had
not checked. And now a woman who was someone’s daughter, someone’s partner, someone’s mother, would
never be the same.

Arthur has come to believe that he is fundamentally inadequate. He tells himself that a competent
person, a careful person, would have looked. He tells himself that his reactions since the
accident—the sleeplessness, the jumpiness, the way he cannot seem to pull himself together—prove
that he is a lousy coper, that he does not have what it takes to handle something hard. He is
certain that he has permanently changed for the worse. The man he was before, the one who joked with
customers and remembered their names, who could sit through a Sunday dinner without his mind racing,
that man is gone. In his place is someone who feels broken and untrustworthy.

The changes showed up slowly at first, then all at once. Arthur stopped going to the grocery store
because the sight of a red car in the parking lot made his stomach drop. He began taking the back
roads to work, adding twenty minutes to his commute, because the main road passed the bank and he
could not bear to see the drive-through lane. At work, he asked to be moved to the teller window
farthest from the drive-through, and his manager, who knew what had happened, agreed. But even
there, Arthur found himself scanning every customer who walked through the door. He watched their
hands, their faces, their children. He could not relax. He was always on guard, always waiting for
something to go wrong again. His coworkers noticed that he had stopped attending the monthly team
lunches, that he no longer stayed late to help close up. They stopped inviting him.

At home, the strain was worse. Linda would reach out to touch his arm while he was reading the
newspaper, and Arthur would flinch so hard he knocked over his coffee. He knew it was irrational. He
knew she was not a threat. But the unexpected contact sent a jolt through him that he could not
control, and then he would feel ashamed, and then he would withdraw further. He had difficulty
falling asleep because he felt he needed to stay alert, as though something dangerous might happen
if he let his guard down. He lay awake beside Linda, listening to the house settle, his body rigid.
Even when he was curled on the couch with the door locked and the dog at his feet—a place he knew,
objectively, was safe—he could not let go of the tension in his shoulders. He could not stop
watching the window.

What finally brought Arthur to seek help was not his own distress, but Linda’s. She had found him
standing in the kitchen at three in the morning, staring at the toaster, and she had cried. She said
she did not recognize him anymore. Arthur looked at her and realized he did not recognize himself
either. He came to the first session hoping, though he would not say it out loud, that someone might
convince him he was not beyond repair. He was afraid that he was.
