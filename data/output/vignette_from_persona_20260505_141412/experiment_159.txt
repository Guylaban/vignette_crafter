their own hardships, could not remember a time when she had felt safe in her own body. The event
that had fractured her sense of safety had happened when she was a child, in the home of a relative

began—episodes of being grabbed by the arm, shaken, and struck across the face and back. What she
recalled most vividly now, decades later, was not the sequence of blows but the way her body would
freeze mid-memory: her shoulders would hunch, her arms would draw in tight against her ribs, and she
would feel as though she were once again bracing against a blow, unable to move or speak. She could
not seem to put the event into words. When she tried to describe it to a doctor once, the sentences
would not come; she could only gesture vaguely at her own chest and arms, as if the story were
written in her muscles rather than in language. And when the memory surfaced—often without warning,
triggered by a sudden movement or a raised voice—what rose in her was not sadness but a hot,
wordless rage that frightened her as much as the original helplessness had.

Her work in social ministry meant she spent her days in crowded community centers, church basements,
and the homes of clients. She had chosen the profession because she wanted to be useful, but now
every interaction felt like a test of survival. She had come to believe that she could not trust
anyone to be near her, especially not alone. She had developed a strict rule: she would never allow
herself to be in a closed room with only one other person. This meant she scheduled all client
meetings in open-plan offices or hallways, and if a colleague asked to speak privately, she would
suggest they walk outside or wait until someone else was nearby. She was certain that if she were
alone with someone, something bad would happen—she could not say what, only that her body would
know, and by then it would be too late. At work, she always chose a seat near the door, and during
meetings she would angle her chair so that she could see both the exit and everyone in the room. She
had begun to look behind her frequently when walking down the street or through the office corridor,
checking if someone was following. She knew, logically, that no one was there, but the compulsion
was stronger than reason.

Her irritability had become a problem. Small things—a coworker tapping a pen, a client speaking too
slowly—would trigger an angry outburst that left her ashamed and isolated. She had snapped at her
supervisor twice in the past month, and her team had begun to walk carefully around her. At home,
she was often alone, which was a relief and also a kind of punishment. She had been divorced for
nearly a decade, and she had no desire to date; the thought of being physically close to someone
made her skin crawl. She had stopped going to the gym because the locker room felt too enclosed, and
she had turned down a promotion that would have required her to attend evening meetings in a
building with only one stairwell.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment at work that she could no longer dismiss. A
client, a gentle older man, had reached out to touch her arm in gratitude, and she had yanked her
arm away so violently that she knocked over a stack of files. Her heart had pounded, her vision had
narrowed, and she had felt—for a terrifying instant—as though she were back in that childhood room,
bracing for the next blow. Afterward, she sat in her car for twenty minutes, shaking, and realized
that she could not go on like this. She was afraid of what she might do if the rage ever broke
through in front of the wrong person. She was afraid of ending up completely alone. And more than
anything, she was afraid that she would never feel safe in her own skin—that the little girl who had
learned to freeze and brace and survive would never get to stop. She came to therapy not knowing if
she could be helped, but knowing she could not keep living this way.
