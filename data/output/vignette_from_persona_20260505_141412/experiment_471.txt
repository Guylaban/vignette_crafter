The accident happened on a soundstage in Burbank, during the final week of principal photography.
Marcus had been standing at the monitor, a few feet from a rigged lighting truss that, according to
the safety report later, had been improperly secured by a junior grip who had been working eighteen
hours straight. The truss fell. It missed him by inches, crashing onto the dolly track where his
lead actor had been standing moments before. The sound was enormous, a percussive shriek of twisting
metal and shattering bulbs, and then the entire stage went black as the electrical system tripped.
In that darkness, Marcus had felt something he had never felt in twenty-five years of filmmaking:
pure, animal certainty that he was about to die. He could not see, he could not move, he could only
hear the groaning of the rig above him and the screaming of the crew. It took seven minutes for the
emergency lights to come on. Seven minutes in total blackness, waiting for the rest of the rig to
come down on his head.

That was eleven months ago. Marcus had finished the film—he had to, the studio had three hundred
people on payroll—but he had not been on a soundstage since. He had edited the movie entirely from
his home, in a room with the blinds always open and a lamp on at all times. Darkness had become
unbearable. He could not be in a dark room, could not walk his girlfriend to the car at night, could
not even close his eyes in the shower without his chest seizing. The memory of that blackout was not
a story he could tell; it was an image that had no words attached to it. When he tried to describe
the accident to his therapist—he had finally made an appointment two weeks ago—he found himself
gesturing helplessly. The memory was all pictures: the angle of the truss, the spray of sparks, the
shape of the darkness. He could not seem to turn it into language.

What frightened Marcus more than the memory itself was what the memory said about him. He had always
thought of himself as someone who handled pressure. He directed action sequences. He managed egos.
He made decisions in chaos. But the man who had stood frozen in that blackout, who had felt his
knees buckle and his mind go blank, was not that person. That person was inadequate. That person was
a lousy coper. And the person he had become since—the man who jumped at the sound of a dropped
clipboard, who flinched when a PA closed a door too hard, who spent entire days avoiding the film’s
rough cut because he knew it would take him back to that stage—that person was unrecognisable. He
did not know who this was. He did not like him.

The hypervigilance had become a full-time job. Every unexpected sound was a potential threat: the
hiss of a soda can opening, the thud of a delivery truck’s tailgate, the crackle of a walkie-talkie
in the next room. His girlfriend had started to warn him before she closed a cabinet. She had
learned to approach him from the front, to speak softly, to never, ever startle him. He hated that
she had to do this. He hated that he had become someone who needed managing. He had started working
from home not just because of the darkness, but because the soundstage was an assault of loud,
unpredictable noises—calls of “speed,” the slam of a clapperboard, the rumble of a dolly. He had
tried to return once, three months after the accident. He had made it to the parking lot, heard a
grip drop a C-stand, and had driven home with his hands shaking on the wheel.

Lately, Marcus found himself thinking about why this had happened to him. Not in a spiritual way—he
was not a spiritual man—but in a relentless, circular way that felt like picking at a scab. Why had
he been standing exactly there? Why had he not checked the rig himself? Why had he not seen it
coming? The questions looped through his mind during the long hours when he was supposed to be
working but could not focus. He would open the editing software, then close it. He would pour a
coffee, then forget to drink it. He would scroll through his phone, looking for anything to pull his
attention away from the images that played behind his eyes. The distraction worked for a few
minutes, sometimes a few hours. But the images always came back.

What finally brought Marcus to the clinic was a simple, practical problem: he was three weeks from
the premiere, and he could not bring himself to walk into a theater. The thought of sitting in the
dark, surrounded by loud, sudden sounds—applause, laughter, the rumble of the projector—made his
chest tighten until he could not breathe. He had told his producer he was having “anxiety issues.”

person still existed. But he was willing to sit in a stranger’s office and try to find out.
