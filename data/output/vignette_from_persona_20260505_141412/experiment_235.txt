Kwame is a fifty-year-old plumber, a husband, and a father of three grown children. He came to
therapy at the insistence of his wife, who had grown exhausted by his sleeplessness and his refusal
to leave their home except for work. He is a tall, broad-shouldered man whose hands, calloused and
still stained with pipe dope from the morning’s job, rest motionless on his knees as he speaks. He
describes himself as a man who used to fix things—leaks, boilers, broken relationships between his
kids. Now, he says, he cannot fix anything, least of all himself.

The assault happened fourteen months ago, on a Tuesday evening. Kwame had just finished a job in a
basement apartment, and as he walked to his van in the alley, a man came up behind him and struck
him in the back of the head with what Kwame believes was a length of iron pipe. He remembers the
blow, the way his knees buckled, the sensation of his own blood running warm down his neck. He
remembers the man going through his pockets, taking his wallet and his phone. What he cannot put
into words is the rest of it—the minutes that followed, the feeling of lying face-down on the
gravel, the sound of footsteps fading. When he tries to tell the story now, his mouth goes dry and
the images flood back: the pattern of the gravel inches from his eye, the rusted undercarriage of a
dumpster, the shadow of the man’s legs as he walked away. He feels a wave of guilt so sharp it makes
him wince. He should have looked behind him. He should have parked closer to the door. He should
have fought back. The guilt is not a thought but a physical weight, a certainty that he failed
himself and his family by being so easily taken.

In the months since, Kwame has come to believe that he is no longer capable of the things that once
defined him. He used to be the one people called in a crisis—a burst pipe at midnight, a flooded
basement. Now, when his phone rings, his chest tightens. He is certain he will make the wrong
decision, that he will miss a critical step, that he will let someone down. This self-doubt has
crept into every corner of his life. He no longer volunteers to drive his wife to church because he
fears he will take a wrong turn or freeze at an intersection. He no longer offers advice to his
youngest son, who is learning the trade, because he cannot trust that he will say the right thing.
What happened to him was deeply unfair, he knows this, and nothing has been done about it. The
police never found the man. The case is closed. The injustice sits inside him like a stone, and
every time he remembers the assault, he feels it again—a raw, wordless sense that the world owes him
something it will never give.

Kwame’s sense of safety has collapsed to a single point: his own home, and even then, only certain
rooms. He feels exposed in the kitchen, where the back door has a window he cannot stop glancing at.
He can only sleep in the recliner in the living room, facing the front door, because the bedroom
feels too far from an exit. Sleep comes in fragments—an hour here, two hours there—and when he
wakes, it is often because he has heard a noise or felt a shift in the air. His wife has stopped
asking him to come to bed. She sleeps alone now, and he knows this is a kind of failure too.

At work, Kwame operates on high alert. He refuses to work in basements or alleyways. He tells his
dispatcher he only takes jobs with street-level access and good lighting. The other plumbers have
noticed. They joke that he has become picky. He does not explain. When a customer steps suddenly
into his peripheral vision—a hand reaching for a tool, a child running past—Kwame flinches, his
whole body bracing for a blow. He has dropped a wrench this way, nearly fallen off a ladder. He
knows it looks strange, but he cannot stop it. The startle is not a choice; it is the body
remembering before the mind can catch up.

What finally brought Kwame to therapy was a moment last week. His wife told him she was thinking of
visiting her sister for a few days, just to get some rest. She said it gently, but he heard the
exhaustion underneath. He sat in his recliner that night, watching the door, and realized he could
not remember the last time he had felt anything close to peace. He is afraid that if he keeps going
this way, he will lose his marriage, his job, his sense of himself entirely. He is hoping, though he
does not say it directly, that someone can teach him how to feel safe again—or at least how to stop
feeling so afraid.
