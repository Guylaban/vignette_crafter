Maya was forty-three years old, married, and worked the second shift in IT support for a regional
school district. She had been born in Ghana, raised in a strict household where discipline was
delivered with an open hand and a closed fist, and where the bush behind her family’s compound was
where she was sent to collect firewood—and where, between the ages of seven and twelve, her uncle
found her. She had never told anyone the full story, not even her husband, Derek. What she carried
instead was a certainty that had calcified over decades: that something in her had made it happen.
That she was the sort of person such things happened to. That there was something wrong with her,
intrinsically, at the level of character.

The abuse had occurred in the dry season, when the harmattan winds stripped the landscape of color
and the tall grass turned brittle and brown. Her uncle would wait for her near the termite mounds at
the edge of the clearing, where the acacia trees cast long, thin shadows. She learned to read the
quality of the light, the angle of the heat, as warning signs. Her body had learned before her mind
did. Now, thirty years later and six thousand miles away, her body still knew. She sat in her
cubicle at the district office, headphones on, troubleshooting a teacher’s login issue, and her
shoulders were up around her ears. Her jaw was clenched. She could not remember the last time her
muscles had felt soft. She called it being vigilant; Derek called it being jumpy. When he walked
into the kitchen without warning, she sometimes yelped. When a car backfired in the parking lot, she
dropped whatever she was holding. She knew, intellectually, that these were false alarms. But
knowing did not stop her heart from hammering or her palms from sweating. Her body acted as if the
danger were still present, as if the uncle were still standing behind the next door.

That was another thing: doors. She could not be in a room with the door locked. The first time Derek
locked the bathroom door out of habit, she stood in the hallway and pounded until he opened it, and
then she could not explain why. She had come to believe that being enclosed, being unable to get
out, was a trap. At work, she kept her office door propped open with a rubber wedge. When a
colleague closed it for a private conversation, Maya felt the air go thick and her vision narrow.
She made excuses to leave. She had stopped attending team meetings in the conference room because
the door had a latch that clicked shut automatically. Her supervisor had noticed. She had been
written up for missing two mandatory trainings, both held in a locked lab. Maya did not explain. She
could not say, *I need the door open or I will relive being seven years old in a shed with the bolt
drawn.*

Talking about the event was impossible. When a well-meaning human resources representative asked
during a wellness check if there was anything in her past that made her anxious in enclosed spaces,
Maya shook her head and said she was fine. She was not fine. Her concentration had frayed to the
point where she read the same ticket three times before she understood what the user needed. She
made small errors—closing a ticket without resolving it, routing a request to the wrong
department—and then spent hours worrying that she would be fired. She had come to believe that the
sort of person who attracts bad things also attracts bad outcomes at work. She was waiting for the
other shoe to drop.

Nature itself had become a trigger. She could not walk in the park near her apartment. The smell of
dry grass, the particular quality of late-afternoon sunlight slanting through leaves, brought her
back to the clearing behind the compound. She had stopped gardening. She kept the blinds drawn.
Derek wanted to hike on weekends; she said she was tired. She was always tired, but not from work.
She was tired from the constant scanning, the bracing, the readiness to react. She was tired of
being afraid of nothing.

What finally brought her to a therapist was a panic attack in the grocery store. She was in the
produce aisle, reaching for a bag of oranges, when the overhead fluorescent light flickered and cast
a brief, familiar shadow across the floor. She dropped the bag. She could not breathe. A store
employee asked if she needed an ambulance, and Maya said no, she just needed to leave. She left her
cart in the aisle and walked home. That night, she told Derek she needed help. She did not tell him
why. She was afraid that if she said it out loud, he would see what she saw: that there was
something fundamentally wrong with her. But she was also afraid of staying this way—of shrinking her
life down to the size of a room with the door open, of losing her job, of never standing in the sun
again without flinching.
