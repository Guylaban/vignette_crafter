The lions had been quiet that morning. Amara remembered the stillness of the bush, the way the light
fell through the acacia trees, the dust hanging in the air. She was leading a small group of
visitors along the southern ridge of the park, pointing out a herd of impala, when her satellite
phone buzzed. It was her husband’s mother. The words did not make sense at first. A heart attack.
Her husband, Daniel, had collapsed at his desk. By the time the ambulance reached him, it was over.
She stood there, on the ridge, the phone pressed to her ear, the tourists watching her, the sun hot
on her neck. She told them the tour was cancelled. She walked back to the ranger station in a daze,
the landscape around her suddenly foreign, as though she were looking at photographs of a place she
had once known.

That was fourteen months ago. Now, Amara still works in the same park, but she no longer leads the
dawn patrols. She has given up that responsibility, quietly and without explanation, shifting
herself to administrative work in the station office. She tells herself it is practical—she needs to
be indoors, near a phone, near people. But the truth is that the open space feels dangerous now.
When she stands on the ridge, the memory comes in fragments: the vibration of the phone, the dust in
the air, the impossible words. These pieces appear without warning, like a camera shutter opening
and closing, and then they vanish, leaving her breathless and disoriented. Other times, the memory
arrives as a single, frozen image—Daniel’s face the morning he left for work, the way he had smiled
and said he would see her that evening. She sees it like a photograph, clear and still, and the
grief that follows is so overwhelming that she has to sit down, her chest heaving, her hands
trembling.

She has come to believe that she is no longer capable of doing the things that defined her. The
woman who could track a leopard for hours, who could stay calm when a buffalo charged, who knew the
park better than anyone—that woman feels like a stranger. Amara feels dead inside, hollowed out, as
though the grief has scooped out everything that mattered. She is certain she has permanently
changed for the worse, that the damage is irreversible. She does not say this to anyone, not even to
her mother-in-law, who calls every week to check on her. What would be the point? She cannot explain
the way her body stays tense all the time, coiled like an animal waiting for the next blow. She
cannot explain that she avoids relaxing because relaxation feels dangerous, as though letting her
guard down will invite another catastrophe. She keeps her mind occupied constantly—listening to
podcasts during her shift, scrolling through her phone at night, replaying old conversations in her
head—anything to avoid the silence where the fragments might find her.

Sleep is a battleground. She lies beside the empty space where Daniel used to sleep, her jaw
clenched, her legs restless. When she finally drifts off, she dreams of the phone call, of the dust,
of his face in that frozen photograph. She wakes with her heart pounding, the grief already settling
in her chest like a second skin. Her husband’s absence is a physical weight she carries through
every hour of every day, and the strong negative feelings—the fear, the anger, the guilt—swirl
inside her without release. She is angry at him for dying, and then she is ashamed of that anger,
and then she is terrified that she is losing her mind.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment on the office computer, when she found herself
staring at an email about the next season’s patrol schedule. She realised she had been avoiding it
for weeks, that she had been delegating every field assignment to her colleagues, that her
supervisor had started to notice. The thought of going back out there, of standing on the ridge
where she had received the news, filled her with a dread so absolute that she could not breathe. She
called a clinic that afternoon. She is afraid of what she will find in therapy—afraid that she will
have to look at the photograph again, afraid that the grief will swallow her whole. But she is also
afraid of staying like this, of becoming a woman who no longer knows the shape of her own life. She
does not know if she can be fixed. She only knows that she cannot go on as she is.
