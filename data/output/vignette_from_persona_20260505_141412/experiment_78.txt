else. She ignored his repeated verbal refusals and physical attempts to stop her, and he found
himself frozen, unable to push her off or even call out. He remembered the specific smell of her
perfume—a sweet, cloying floral scent that had seemed pleasant before—and the way it mixed with the
smell of her sweat as she moved above him. He remembered the humiliating stillness of his own body,
how he had just lain there, telling himself to move, to fight, to do something, and how his limbs
had refused to obey. When it was over, she had rolled off him and said, casually, "See? That wasn't
so bad," as if she had done him a favor. He had dressed in silence and walked home in a daze, and he
had never spoken to her again.

In the months that followed, Amir did not tell anyone. He told himself it was his fault, that he
should have been stronger, that another man would have pushed her off or walked out the moment
things felt wrong. This self-comparison became a constant, grinding refrain in his mind: somebody
else would not have gotten into this situation. He had always been a happy person, he believed,
someone who laughed easily and enjoyed socializing, but now he felt permanently miserable, as if the
happy version of himself had been erased and replaced with this hollow, watchful stranger. The most
corrosive feeling, however, was the sense of humiliation. It was not just shame about what had
happened; it was a deep, unshakeable conviction that he was now fundamentally marked by it, that
anyone who looked at him could somehow see what had been done to him.

Amir began to change his life in small, then increasingly large, ways. He started wearing only
loose, dark, concealing clothing—hoodies and baggy pants—so that he would not be noticed. He avoided
eye contact and kept his head down. He stopped going to parties or even to the campus gym, because
he could not bear the thought of being evaluated, of having his body or his appearance judged by
anyone. At work, he took to arriving early and leaving late, timing his entry and exit to avoid the
crowded elevators. He always sat with his back to a wall and his eyes on the door. If a colleague
made a sudden movement—reaching for a stapler, turning quickly to ask a question—Amir would flinch,
his entire body going rigid. His muscles ached from the constant, unrelenting tension of being ready
to react, though he could never have said what he was reacting to.

The worst triggers were the ones he could not predict. A woman in the grocery store wore a perfume
that was similar to his ex’s, and he had to abandon his cart and walk out, his heart pounding. A
coworker made a joke about a bad date, using a phrase his ex had used, and Amir had to excuse
himself to the bathroom, where he stood trembling over the sink. He began to avoid unfamiliar places
entirely, because he could not assess the danger in them—he did not know where the exits were, who
might be there, or what smells might be waiting for him. His world shrank to his apartment, his
office, and the direct route between them.

had noticed that Amir refused to attend client meetings, always claiming he had too much work, and

out." Amir was terrified of losing his job, but he was even more terrified of the prospect of having
to explain why he could not function. He came to therapy hoping, he said, to find a way to stop
feeling so miserable, and perhaps to find a way to forgive himself for what he believed was his own
unforgivable weakness.
