Margaret, a 74-year-old physical therapist who had worked in the same clinic for over thirty years,
still arrived at work each morning before anyone else. She had done this for decades, but the reason
had changed. What had once been professional diligence had become something else—a quiet, preemptive
search. She would walk through each treatment room, check the supply closets, look behind the
curtains. Only when she had confirmed that every space was empty, that no one was waiting for her
who should not be there, could she begin her day.

The event that had reshaped her life occurred forty-two years ago, when she and her husband fled
their home country during a period of ethnic cleansing. They had been given twenty minutes to leave.
In that time, Margaret had packed a single bag, grabbed her toddler son, and walked out of the house
she would never see again. On the road, surrounded by other families, she had smelled the smoke from
burning villages long before she saw the flames. The smell of burnt wood and fabric had followed
them for days, carried on the wind, settling into her clothes and her hair. She had also smelled
alcohol on the breath of the armed men who stopped their convoy at a checkpoint. One of them had
leaned into the car window, his face inches from hers, and she had heard his voice—low, casual,
almost bored—as he asked for their papers. She could still hear that voice in her head, not as a
memory she chose to revisit, but as something that arrived without warning, unbidden and vivid. The
sound was so clear, so present, that for a moment she would lose track of where she actually was.
The images came too: the dust on the road, the line of cars, the faces of strangers she had never
seen before and never saw again. When these memories surfaced, they did not feel like something that
had happened long ago. They felt like they were happening right now.

Margaret had learned to manage these intrusions in the only way that seemed to work. She drank. Not
heavily, not every night, but regularly enough that she had come to rely on the dulling effect of
alcohol to quiet the images and sounds that otherwise would keep her from sleeping. She had also
developed a quiet habit of scanning. At work, she monitored the doorway of each treatment room. At
home, she listened. She had become exquisitely attuned to the sounds of her own house—the creak of a
floorboard, the click of a lock, the rustle of the wind against a window. Any unexpected noise made
her freeze, her heart accelerating before she had even consciously registered the sound. She often
reacted as if she were in danger when nothing was actually wrong. A car backfiring outside the
clinic had once sent her dropping to the floor behind her desk, and she had stayed there for several
minutes, trembling, unable to explain to herself or to the colleague who found her why she had done
it.

Strangers had become a problem. In the grocery store, in the waiting room of her own clinic, on the
sidewalk—anytime someone she did not know approached her, even with a simple question or a polite
nod, she felt a spike of alarm. She had learned to cross the street to avoid pedestrians. She had
stopped going to the pharmacy at busy times. She had also stopped talking about the past. When
friends or family asked about her life before coming to America, she changed the subject. When her
husband brought up their escape, she left the room. She could not bear to hear the story spoken
aloud, because speaking it made the images come, and the images made the sounds come, and then she
was back on that road again, the smell of smoke and alcohol filling her lungs.

Her husband had noticed the scanning, the drinking, the way she flinched at the doorbell. He had not
said much, but he had begun to look at her with a kind of worried patience that Margaret found
harder to bear than anger. What finally brought her to seek help was not her own distress, but a
mistake at work. She had misread a patient’s chart because she had been distracted by a sound in the
hallway—a man’s voice, low and casual—and she had nearly given the wrong instructions. The patient
had not been harmed, but Margaret had been shaken. She had come to believe that she was losing
control of something she had spent forty-two years trying to manage. She was afraid of what might
happen next. She was also afraid of what she might have to remember in order to get better.
