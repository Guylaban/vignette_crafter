The event that changed everything for Marcus happened on a Tuesday evening in late autumn, two years
ago. He had been driving home from his dental practice, tired after a long day of crown preps and
root canals, when he stopped at a gas station off the interstate to fill up. He was parked, pumping
gas, when a patrol car pulled in behind him. The officer stepped out and approached his car window
without explanation. Marcus had barely rolled it down before the man’s hand shot through the gap,
grabbing his collar, yanking him sideways. He was on the ground in seconds, knees in his back, the
officer shouting something about a stolen vehicle—a mistake that would take three hours to sort out
at the station, where they finally admitted the plates didn’t match. But by then the damage was
done. Marcus’s ribs had been cracked, his jaw wrenched so badly he couldn’t open his mouth fully for
weeks. And there was something else, something that stayed with him long after the bruises faded:
the sound of the officer’s radio squawking static just before the first blow, and the sharp,
grinding pain in his lower back as his body was twisted against the asphalt.

Now, two years later, Marcus cannot hear static without his chest tightening. He avoids watching
television shows that feature police scanners, and he has stopped listening to talk radio altogether
because the crackle of a bad signal sends him into a cold sweat. The memory of that night feels like
it belongs to someone else—a stranger’s life, not his own. When he tries to recall it, he often
finds himself thinking, *that didn’t happen to me*, even as the auditory flashbacks arrive unbidden:
the squawk of the radio, the officer’s breathless commands, the metallic click of handcuffs. And
then, without warning, a phantom ache will settle into his lower back, just above his left hip, the
same spot where the pavement had ground into his spine. He has come to believe there is something
fundamentally wrong with him, something that made this happen. He was just a dentist, a man in his
late fifties, driving a sensible sedan—why would an officer target him if not because of something
about his face, his skin, his presence? The injustice of it gnaws at him daily. Nothing was done.
The officer was not disciplined. Marcus filed a complaint; it was dismissed. His life, he is
certain, has been destroyed.

At work, Marcus has become a different man. He used to chat easily with patients, but now he finds
himself scanning the waiting room before every appointment, checking who is sitting where, whether
anyone has their hands in their pockets. He has started choosing his own seat in the break room with
his back to the wall, facing the door. He avoids any conversation that might become stressful—a
patient’s complaint about insurance, a hygienist’s question about scheduling—and he has begun to
cancel on patients he does not know well, claiming illness. His partner, Linda, has noticed that he
no longer wants to go to restaurants or grocery stores. He says it is because he is tired, but the
truth is that every stranger who walks toward him on the sidewalk feels like a potential threat. He
has stopped seeing his friends entirely; their invitations to dinner or a ballgame feel like demands
he cannot meet without first scanning the parking lot for police cars.

Sleep has become a battleground. Marcus lies awake for hours, his mind replaying the sound of the
radio, the pressure of the knee, the grinding pain. When he finally drifts off, he wakes in the
small hours, heart pounding, convinced he heard a car door slam outside. He has begun checking the
locks on the doors and windows three times before bed, and he insists on sleeping on the side of the
bed farthest from the door. Linda has grown frustrated; she does not understand why he cannot just
let it go.

What finally brought Marcus to seek help was a moment in his own office, three weeks ago. A new
patient—a young man in his twenties, wearing a hooded sweatshirt—walked into the operatory, and
Marcus felt his throat close. He could not speak. He stood frozen, staring at the man’s hands, until
his assistant touched his arm and asked if he was alright. He excused himself, went to the bathroom,
and vomited. That night, he told Linda he could not go on like this. He is afraid of what will
happen if he does not change—afraid he will lose his practice, lose her, lose himself entirely. And
yet, what he hopes for, more than anything, is to feel safe in his own skin again, to walk into a
room without first calculating the exits.
