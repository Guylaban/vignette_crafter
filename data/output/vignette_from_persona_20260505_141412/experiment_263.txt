The event that shattered Maya’s world happened three years ago, when she was twenty-seven and living
in a different city. She had been seeing a man named Derek for six months, a relationship that began
with intensity and slowly curdled into something she could not name at the time. The night it ended,
she had tried to leave his apartment after an argument about her wanting to see her friends. Derek’s
face had changed, the quiet in the room thickening into something that made her skin prickle. He
locked the door and for the next four hours, he questioned her, accused her, and when she tried to
answer, he struck her. He used his hands, a belt, and the lit end of a cigarette on her inner arm.
He told her she was worthless, that no one would believe her, that she belonged to him. She stopped
fighting after the first hour, learning to go still and silent, to make her breathing shallow, to
watch his hands instead of his eyes. She escaped when he fell asleep, and she never went back. She
did not report it. She moved two states away, changed her phone number, and told herself she was
fine.

Maya now works as a bank teller in a mid-sized suburban branch, a job she took because it felt
safe—enclosed, predictable, with a thick glass barrier between her and the world. But the glass does
not help. A customer raising his voice over a fee, even politely, makes her hands go cold on the
keyboard. She has learned to drop her gaze when anyone leans toward the window, her eyes fixed on
the monitor or the stack of deposit slips. She avoids eye contact with male customers entirely, and
with female customers who look at her too long. Her manager, a kind woman in her fifties, once
touched her shoulder to get her attention, and Maya flinched so hard she knocked a cup of pens onto
the floor. She had to excuse herself to the restroom, where she stood with her hands on the sink,
waiting for the shaking to stop.

At home, the trouble is worse. Her husband, Tom, is gentle and patient, but Maya cannot tolerate the
silence that falls between them in the evenings. When he reads quietly on the couch, the absence of
sound fills her chest with a crawling dread. She has come to believe that silence is a warning, that
something terrible is about to break through it, and she will interrupt him constantly—asking about
his day, turning on the television, filling the air with noise. Intimacy has become nearly
impossible. Sexual situations, even the suggestion of them, send her memory roaring back: the weight
of Derek’s body, the smell of his sweat, the sound of his voice telling her she was his. The memory
does not creep up on her. It arrives fully, in a single instant, as if she has been transported. She
can feel the carpet under her cheek, taste the blood in her mouth, hear the lock click. She has to
stop whatever she is doing, sometimes mid-sentence, and breathe until the world reassembles.

She spends money to escape. Online shopping, mostly—clothes she does not need, gadgets she never
uses. The packages arrive, she opens them, and for a few minutes the bright new thing holds the
darkness at bay. Then the guilt sets in, and she hides the purchases in the back of her closet. She
has accumulated over four thousand dollars in credit card debt this way, a secret she has not told
Tom.

At night, she wakes repeatedly, her eyes snapping open in the dark, her body rigid, listening. She
checks the locks on the bedroom door, the windows, the closet. She cannot stop herself. She is
certain that if she does not check, something will have changed, someone will be there. The
slightest sound makes her jump—a car door outside, the furnace clicking on. She startles so easily
that Tom has started announcing his movements before he enters a room: “Coming in, it’s me.”

What brought Maya to therapy was a morning last month when a man in a dark coat stood too close to
her teller window, not speaking, just watching her. He was waiting for a deposit slip. But for a few
seconds, Maya was not in the bank. She was back in Derek’s apartment, pinned under his stare. She
froze. Her supervisor had to call her name three times. That night, Maya told Tom everything. She is
here now because she is terrified of losing her job, her marriage, and her mind. She wants the
memories to stop hijacking her body. She wants to look her husband in the eye. She wants to sleep
through the night without checking the locks. More than anything, she wants to stop being afraid of
silence.
