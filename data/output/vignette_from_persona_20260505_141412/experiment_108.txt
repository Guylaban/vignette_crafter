For seventy-eight years, Thomas had considered himself a man who understood systems. As a database
administrator, he spent his days imposing order on chaos, structuring information so that it made
sense, so that it could be queried and predicted. Nothing in his long career had prepared him for
the way his own body would betray him. The event itself had been unremarkable in its onset—a routine
physical, a slightly elevated white blood cell count, a referral he had not taken seriously until
the word "leukemia" appeared in the specialist's notes. What Thomas remembered most vividly now, two
years after successful treatment, was not the chemotherapy or the hospital stays but a single
afternoon in the infusion center. The room had been too cold, and someone in the next chair had been
eating a tuna sandwich. That smell—tuna fish, metallic and insistent—had woven itself into the
moments when the nurse had checked his port, when he had watched the clear fluid begin its slow drip
into his arm, when he had understood with absolute certainty that he might not walk out of this
room. He could smell it now, writing this, as if the woman were sitting beside him. That was what
frightened him most: the memories did not behave like memories. They felt more real than the chair
he was sitting in. When they came, he was back in that cold room, the fear as fresh as the day it
had first arrived.

Thomas had divorced twenty years ago and lived alone in a modest suburban house. He had always taken
pride in his independence, in the quiet order of his routines. But after the illness, something
shifted. He began to notice that he could not relax inside his own home. The front door was locked
three times before bed. He found himself standing at the living room window at odd hours, scanning
the street for nothing in particular. He was certain, though he could not explain why, that danger
could find him anywhere. The world had become a dangerous place, and his body kept reacting as if
the threat were still present—muscles tight, heart accelerating at the sound of a car door, at the
creak of a floorboard. He had lost his faith in the basic decency of things. People, life, a higher
power—it all felt like a structure that had collapsed. He had nothing to query anymore.

The consequences crept in slowly. At work, he had always been reliable, but now he found himself
unable to concentrate. The smell of someone's lunch in the breakroom would send him spiraling, and
he would retreat to his cubicle, heart pounding, the memory of the infusion center so vivid that he
could feel the cold vinyl of the chair against his arms. He started taking his lunch in his car,
then eating at his desk with the door closed. His supervisor noticed, commented that he seemed
"distracted." Thomas could not explain that he was not distracted—he was too alert, too watchful,
too busy scanning for a threat that no one else could see. He began to dread the office potlucks,
the communal meals, the way a whiff of canned fish could undo an entire afternoon. He stopped going
to the grocery store during lunch hour. He stopped eating out altogether. His social life, already
thin, evaporated completely.

What finally brought Thomas to seek help was not the fear itself but the exhaustion. He was seventy-
eight years old, and he was tired of being on guard. He had survived the illness, but he had not
survived the aftermath. He sat in the therapist's office with his hands folded in his lap, and he
said, quietly, "I don't know how to feel safe anymore. I don't know if I ever will." What he hoped
for, more than anything, was not to be free of the memory—he knew that was impossible—but to stop
living inside it. He wanted the tuna sandwich to just be a tuna sandwich.
