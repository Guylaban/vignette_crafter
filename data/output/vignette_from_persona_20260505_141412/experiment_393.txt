Margaret was fifty-two years old when she finally walked into a therapist’s office, though she had
told herself for nearly three years that she did not need to. She was a judge, appointed to the
bench in her mid-forties, and she had spent her career believing that composure was a form of
authority. But composure had become something she had to grip with both hands, and she was
exhausted.

The trauma had happened when she was forty-nine, during a six-month deployment as a relief worker in
a conflict zone. She had been assigned to coordinate civilian evacuations from a town that was under
siege. One afternoon, a shell struck the building where she was sheltering with a group of families.
The ceiling collapsed. She remembered the sound—a deep, percussive crack that seemed to come from
inside her own skull—and then the smell of plaster dust and cordite and something metallic that she
later learned was blood. She had pulled a child from the rubble, a boy of about seven, and carried
him to a makeshift medical station. He had died in her arms. The taste of dust and copper stayed in
her mouth for days afterward. She could still taste it, sometimes, without warning. A sip of tap
water, a bite of bread, and there it was again: the metallic grit of that afternoon.

When the memory came back, it did not come back as a story. It came back as a full sensory reliving.
She would be sitting in her chambers, reviewing a brief, and suddenly she was back in that building.
She could hear the crack of the shell, feel the dust in her throat, see the boy’s face, smell the
smoke. The terror that flooded her was not a memory of fear; it was the same fear she had felt in
that moment, undiminished. Her heart would pound, her hands would shake, and she would have to
excuse herself to the restroom and stand with her palms pressed against the cool tile until she
could breathe again.

She had come to believe that other people could see what had happened to her. When she walked into a
courtroom, she was certain that the attorneys, the clerks, the bailiffs could somehow read the
memory on her face. She felt exposed, as though the trauma were written across her skin. This belief
made her withdraw. She stopped eating lunch with her colleagues. She stopped attending bar
association events. She told herself she preferred solitude, but what she really felt was a profound
isolation, a sense that she no longer belonged among people who had not seen what she had seen. The
world, she had concluded, was a dangerous place. Every courtroom felt like a potential collapse.
Every loud noise in the hallway was another shell.

She developed elaborate strategies to manage her terror. She avoided being in the dark; she slept
with a lamp on, and she kept lights on in every room of her house at night. She tried hard to
control her emotions, keeping her face neutral even when her insides were churning. And she tried,
with enormous effort, not to think about the trauma at all. She would push the memories away,
redirect her attention, focus on the details of a case. But the suppression only seemed to make the
memories more insistent when they broke through.

The anniversaries were the worst. The date of the shelling would approach like a storm, and she
would become irritable, sleepless, hypervigilant for days beforehand. She had learned to schedule
herself for routine hearings on that day, anything to keep her mind occupied, but the date still
carried a weight she could not lift.

Uniforms became a trigger. Police officers, military personnel, even security guards in uniform—she
would see one and feel her throat tighten. She had to remind herself that she was in a safe country,
in a safe city, but her body did not believe it. She had to look away, take a breath, steady herself
before she could continue.

What finally brought her to seek help was not a crisis, but a quiet recognition that she was living
a half-life. She had stopped dating years ago. Her relationships with her adult children had become
distant; they did not know why she had changed, and she could not explain it. She had begun to dread
going to work, not because of the cases, but because of the constant effort it took to appear
normal. She was afraid she would break down in open court. She was afraid the memories would take
her over completely. And she was afraid, most of all, that she would never feel safe again.
