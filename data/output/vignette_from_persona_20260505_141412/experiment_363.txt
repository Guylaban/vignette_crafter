Maya was nineteen years old, married, and unemployed, living in a quiet suburban apartment she had
once thought of as a safe haven. Eight months ago, she had been walking home from a late shift at a
diner when a man grabbed her from behind and dragged her into an alley. She remembered the smell of

of a motorcycle roaring past on the main street, drowning out her own screams, and she remembered
him shouting in Arabic—words she did not understand but that became etched into her memory as a
sound of pure threat. The assault itself lasted only minutes, but the memory of it had not faded or
softened. When it came back to her now, it came back whole, without warning. A car backfiring on her
street could drop her back into that alley, her heart hammering, her body frozen, as if she were
actually there again. Sometimes, when she thought about what happened, she could hear his voice in
her head, the exact pitch and rhythm of the shouting, and she could smell the alcohol again, sharp
and nauseating, even if no one nearby had been drinking.

In the weeks that followed, Maya stopped going out after dark. She told herself it was just common
sense, that anyone would do the same, but the rule became absolute: once the sun went down, she
stayed inside with the curtains drawn and all the locks checked twice. She stopped looking at her
own body. In the shower, she kept her eyes on the ceiling; when she changed clothes, she did it
quickly, without glancing down, because seeing her own skin reminded her of what had been done to
it. She avoided the date of the assault with a rigid, unspoken dread, and when the calendar
approached that day, she felt a physical sickness that made her stay in bed. Her husband, a quiet
man who worked construction, tried to be patient, but he did not understand why she flinched when a
motorcycle passed their building, or why she had stopped meeting him at the front door when he came
home late. Maya had come to believe that she was no longer capable of the things that had once
defined her—her independence, her quick laugh, her willingness to walk anywhere alone. She was
certain that somebody else, someone stronger or smarter or braver, would have stopped the assault
from happening, would have fought back or run or screamed loud enough to be heard. The fact that she
had not done any of those things felt like a permanent stain on who she was.

She began to listen. Not just to sounds outside, but to the quality of silence. She would sit on the
couch and strain to hear footsteps in the hallway, the creak of a door, the whisper of a stranger’s
presence. She frequently looked behind her when she walked to the mailbox during daylight hours,
scanning the street for anyone who might be following. The world, which she had once taken for
granted as mostly benign, now felt like a place where danger was hiding behind every corner, waiting
to spring. Her beliefs about how things worked had been completely destroyed. There was no longer
any sense of safety, only degrees of vigilance. She had stopped applying for jobs because the
thought of a commute after dusk, or even a shift that ended at five in winter when the sun was
already low, felt impossible. Her marriage was strained. Her husband did not know how to reach her,
and she did not know how to explain that she was living in a state of constant, low-grade terror,
punctuated by moments when the past crashed into the present and left her shaking.

What finally brought Maya to seek help was not a single crisis, but a slow recognition that she

listening for danger all the time.
