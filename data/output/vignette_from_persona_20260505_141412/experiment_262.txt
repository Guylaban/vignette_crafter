Arjun arrived at the clinic on a Tuesday afternoon, a full twenty minutes early, having spent those
twenty minutes pacing the corridor outside rather than sitting in the waiting room. He was twenty-
one years old, a public defender in a busy urban courthouse, and he had not told anyone why he was
here. Not his girlfriend, not his colleagues, not the senior attorney who supervised his caseload.
He was certain, in a way that felt less like a thought and more like a physical fact, that if he
told them he was losing his mind, they would confirm it.

The event that had fractured his life happened three years ago, when he was still a teenager. He and
his family had fled their home country during a period of ethnic violence. The journey had taken
weeks, but the moment that lived inside him now was a single night at a border checkpoint. Soldiers
had pulled their family from a crowded bus, separated the men from the women, and made him and his
father and his uncle lie face-down on the gravel. He remembered the cold sharp stones pressing into
his cheek, the weight of a boot on his lower back, the sound of his mother screaming from somewhere
he could not see. He had been certain he was about to die. He had been certain his mother was
already dead. The memory did not come as a story he could tell; it came as pictures, vivid and
silent, that would slide into his mind without warning. Sometimes, when the images hit, his body
would go rigid and he would feel the gravel against his face again, the pressure on his spine, the
absolute stillness he had been forced to hold. He would be standing in the courthouse hallway, or
sitting at his desk reviewing case files, and suddenly his body would lock into that exact position,
as if the ground had reached up through time to claim him again. Other times, the terror would flood
him without any image at all—a wave of pure, undiluted fear that left him shaking and confused,
unable to connect the feeling to anything in the present moment.

He had learned, over the months that followed, to watch his own mind constantly. He monitored his
thoughts the way a bomb disposal technician might watch a wire, alert for the first sign that he was
about to lose control. A stray image, a flicker of memory, a sudden drop in his chest—each was a
warning that he might finally tip over into something irreversible. He had come to believe that the
world was fundamentally dangerous, that safety was an illusion people told themselves because they
could not bear the truth. He kept himself on guard all the time. At work, he could not sit with his
back to the door. In the courthouse elevators, he stood in the corner, facing the doors, watching
everyone who entered. He had to be especially careful, he believed, because you never knew what
could happen next. A silence in the office hallway, the kind that fell when everyone was focused on
their work, would make his chest tighten. He had come to associate silence with the moments before
violence—the hush that had fallen over the bus before the soldiers boarded, the quiet that had
preceded the shouting and the guns. When things went quiet, he felt certain something dangerous was
about to happen.

Ordinary sounds made him jump. A colleague dropping a binder, the slam of a car door outside the
office, the sudden ring of a phone—each would send him lurching out of his chair, heart hammering,
ready to fight or flee. He would react as if he were in danger even when nothing was actually wrong,
and then he would have to explain himself, or pretend he had not reacted at all. He had stopped
talking about the event, or anything related to it, early on. When his girlfriend asked about his
family’s past, he changed the subject. When a coworker mentioned a news story about refugees, he
left the room. When the senior attorney assigned him a case involving an asylum seeker, he asked to
be reassigned without giving a reason, and the request had been noted as unusual. The consequences
had accumulated slowly. His girlfriend had started to pull away, confused by his refusal to share
anything meaningful about his life before America. His colleagues had begun to see him as odd, high-
strung, unreliable in ways that were hard to name. He had been passed over for a promising case
because the lead attorney wanted someone who could "handle the pressure."

What finally brought him to the clinic was a morning in court. He had been standing at the defense
table, waiting for the judge to enter, and the room had fallen into that particular silence that
precedes a proceeding. His body had seized. He had felt the gravel, the boot, the cold. He had
started shaking, and he had had to excuse himself to the restroom, where he had stood gripping the
sink for ten minutes, unable to stop the tears. He had realized, standing there, that he could not
keep this hidden much longer. He was terrified that he was going crazy. He was terrified that the
therapist would confirm it. But he was also, somewhere beneath the fear, hoping that someone might
finally tell him what was happening to him, and that it had a name.
