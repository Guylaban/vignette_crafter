Shanti was twenty-six years old when she was taken, though she had stopped counting the days by the
time she was released. She had been a tailor in a small shop in her hometown, her hands steady with
needle and thread, her days measured by the rhythm of fabrics and orders. The men who came for her
were not strangers—they were known to the neighborhood, part of a local political faction that had
been consolidating power through fear. They accused her of passing information to a rival group, a
charge she had never fully understood. What she remembered most clearly was the room: windowless,
concrete, with a single bulb that never went out. For three weeks, they questioned her, beat her,
and kept her in a state of constant sensory deprivation broken only by pain. She learned to stop
expecting night, stop expecting rescue, stop expecting anything at all. When she was finally let
go—dumped on a roadside outside the city—she walked until she found a bus station, her body bruised
but her mind already beginning to fracture in ways she would not recognize for months.

Her husband had arranged for her to come to the United States on a spousal visa soon after. He was a
quiet man, a mechanic, and he had done his best to give her a new life. But Shanti could not feel
safe. In their small apartment in a midwestern city, she found herself scanning every room before
she could sit down. The first time she walked into a grocery store, her eyes darted to the emergency
exits, counting them, measuring the distance, noting whether the doors pushed outward or inward. She
had never done this before. Now she did it without thinking, every single time. She could not sit
with her back to a door. She could not tolerate silence—the absence of sound felt like the concrete
room—but she also could not tolerate noise that she could not control. Her husband’s television in
the next room, a casual comedy, would make her chest tighten until she had to leave.

She stopped going to the doctor. She had developed a persistent pain in her lower back, likely from
the beatings, but the thought of a clinic—of being asked to undress, of a stranger’s hands near her
body—sent her into a cold sweat. She told her husband she was fine. She told herself the pain was
nothing. She had come to believe that any situation where she was not in control of her own body was
a trap, and she would rather live with the pain than walk into one. She also stopped leaving the
house for anything other than necessities. Her husband brought home groceries. She sewed from their
living room, taking in alterations for neighbors, working on a machine she had bought secondhand. It
was the only thing that felt familiar, the only time her hands remembered what to do without her
mind racing.

But even sewing became difficult when she felt a strong emotion. If a customer complained, or if her
husband raised his voice in frustration about a broken car part, Shanti would feel her throat close.
She would put the fabric down, walk to the bathroom, and sit on the floor until the feeling passed.
She had learned that strong emotions—any strong emotion—were dangerous. They blurred her vision,
made her heart pound, and brought back the smell of the concrete room. She could not afford to feel.
So she avoided anything that might make her feel: arguments, surprises, even moments of tenderness
that were too intense. Her husband began to pull away, confused and hurt. He did not understand why
she flinched when he touched her shoulder, why she would not let him hold her for more than a few
seconds.

Relaxation itself had become terrifying. The few times she had tried to rest—to lie down on the
couch with her eyes closed, to let her mind drift—she had been yanked back into the room by a
flashback so vivid she could feel the rope around her wrists. She learned to stay busy, to keep her
hands moving, to never let her guard down. Being alert felt like survival. Letting go felt like
death.

What finally brought her to a clinic was not her own decision. Her husband had found her one
afternoon on the kitchen floor, shaking, her eyes open but unseeing, muttering in a language he
barely understood. He had called a crisis line, and a caseworker had helped him find a therapist who
worked with survivors of torture. Shanti came to the first session because she was too exhausted to
refuse. She sat with her back to the wall, her eyes fixed on the door, and told the therapist that
she was not sure she could be helped. What she did not say—what she could not yet say—was that she
was terrified of what would happen if she stopped being afraid. She had built her survival around
vigilance, and she did not know who she would be without it.
