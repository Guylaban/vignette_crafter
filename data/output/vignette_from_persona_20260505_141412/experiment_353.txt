decades diagnosing and managing the most critical of cases, only to find himself utterly undone by a
threat he could not treat. He lives alone in a quiet apartment, single for many years, and the

healthy woman in her thirties, who told him gently, “Dad, you’re not okay.” What brought him through
the door was not a sudden crisis, but a slow, grinding exhaustion—a sense that he had been fighting
a battle that ended years ago, yet which he could never leave.

The traumatic event is not a single moment but a siege. When his daughter was eleven, she was
diagnosed with a rare, aggressive form of leukemia. For eighteen months, Ricardo lived in the
hospital. He was not just a father; he was a doctor, which meant he understood every lab value,
every complication, every statistical probability of survival in a way that other parents could not.
He watched her endure chemotherapy that left her vomiting and hairless, saw her spike fevers that
signaled sepsis, held her hand through a bone marrow transplant that nearly killed her. He remembers
the smell of antiseptic, the beeping of monitors, the way the fluorescent lights hummed at 3 a.m.
But what he cannot do is tell a clear story of any of it. When he tries, the sequence dissolves. He
knows what happened—he was there, he lived it—but he cannot find the words to describe it, even to
himself. The memory feels like a stack of photographs with no narrative thread, each image sharp but
disconnected. He recalls the moment a doctor told him her platelets had dropped to zero, but he
cannot say what happened the hour before or the hour after. This incomplete picture haunts him. He
has come to believe that if he could just assemble the story properly, he might understand why he
still feels so afraid.

In the years since his daughter’s recovery, Ricardo has developed a rigid set of internal rules. He
tries hard not to think about the trauma, and when the thoughts intrude, he pushes them away with
deliberate force. He keeps his mind occupied all the time. Retirement, which should have been a
release, became a liability—without the constant demands of patients and rounds, his mind has more
room to wander into dangerous territory. He now fills his days with crossword puzzles, streaming
documentaries, and long, aimless walks through the city. He avoids thinking about the event with the
same discipline he once used to scrub for surgery. But the thoughts do not obey. At unpredictable
moments, a flashback seizes him: suddenly he is back in the pediatric ICU, the smell of alcohol
wipes flooding his senses, his daughter’s small hand limp in his. He feels the terror as if it is
happening again, his heart pounding, his palms slick. These episodes leave him shaken for hours.

The world has become a landscape of hidden threats. Hypervigilance is his constant companion. In the

differential diagnoses. He scans waiting rooms for signs of illness. He cannot watch medical dramas
on television—the sight of a hospital bed, even a fictional one, triggers a spike of fear. He
experiences strong negative emotions that feel disproportionate: a wave of guilt that he did not do
enough, a flash of anger at the universe, a deep, nameless shame. He has come to believe that he
failed his daughter, even though she is alive and well. What frightens him most is the sense that
his own mind is unreliable—that the gaps in his memory mean he missed something, that if he could
only remember correctly, he would find the mistake he made.

The consequences have been concrete and relational. Ricardo has withdrawn from his medical
colleagues. He stopped attending the annual hospital galas, stopped meeting former residents for
coffee. He cannot bear the casual conversations about interesting cases, because they lead him back
to the worst case of his life. His daughter calls every Sunday, and he answers, but he keeps the
conversations short, steering them toward her job, her garden, anything but the past. He is
terrified that if he lets himself talk about it, he will fall apart completely. What finally brought
him to seek help was not his own suffering, but a moment when his daughter said, “Dad, I feel like I
lost you even though I survived.” He is afraid that his avoidance has cost him the very relationship
he fought to preserve. He is hoping, with a fragile and unfamiliar optimism, that therapy might help
him find the words he has lost, and perhaps, in finding them, find his way back to her.
