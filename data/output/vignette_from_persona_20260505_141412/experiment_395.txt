Zara had spent thirty-seven years working inside the state prison system, first as a corrections
officer and eventually as a guard on the maximum-security wing. She was sixty-eight now, a small,
wiry woman with steel-grey hair and a voice that could cut through concrete, and she had always
taken pride in her ability to read a room, to sense trouble before it started. That was what made
her good at the job—she could tell when something was off, when the silence in the corridor meant
something more than just quiet. It was that same instinct, she would later tell herself, that had
saved her life. But it had not saved him.

The assault happened on a Tuesday afternoon during a routine cell extraction. A younger inmate had
been refusing to comply, and Zara was part of the response team. She had done this hundreds of
times. But this time, the inmate had a shank he had hidden in his mattress, and when the team
entered, he lunged not at the officers in front but at the one who had been kind to him, the young
guard who had just transferred in, a boy of twenty-three named Marcus. Zara saw it happening, saw
the blade arc toward Marcus’s throat, and she moved. She got between them, took the blade in her
left forearm, and then she was on the ground, bleeding, and Marcus was dragging her out of the cell,
shouting for help. He survived. She survived. But in the weeks that followed, what stayed with her
was not the pain of the stitches or the physical therapy. What stayed was the certainty that she
should have done more. She should have seen it sooner, should have positioned herself differently,
should have protected Marcus from ever being in that room at all. She had failed to protect him, and
no amount of telling herself that she had taken the blade for him could undo that feeling.

In the months after the assault, Zara began to change in ways she did not fully recognise at first.
She had always been a person who valued her quiet evenings at home with her husband, but now silence
made her skin crawl. If the house was too still, if the refrigerator stopped humming for a moment,
she felt a surge of dread so physical that her heart would pound before her mind had even registered
why. She came to believe that unusual silence meant something dangerous was about to happen, that it
was the same kind of quiet that had fallen over the cell block just before the inmate moved. So she
started filling the silence with people. She began inviting neighbours over constantly, calling her
grown children every night, volunteering for every shift that kept her in the communal areas of the
prison. She kept herself constantly surrounded by people so that she was never alone with her
thoughts, because when she was alone, the thoughts came—images of the blade, of Marcus’s face, of
the moment she realised she could not stop it. She dropped important responsibilities, too. She
stopped attending the weekly safety briefings she had once led, stopped checking in on the younger
officers the way she used to, because being responsible for others felt like a weight she could no
longer carry. She had failed once; she could not risk failing again.

Her body had become a warning system that she could not turn off. Her husband would touch her
shoulder from behind to get her attention, and she would spin around with her fists up before she
had even seen who it was. Her body reacted before her mind, and she hated it, but she could not stop
it. She started scanning every environment she entered—the grocery store, the parking lot, the
hallway at work—checking for exits, for angles, for anyone who might be holding something or moving
too quickly. She had come to believe that people could not be trusted, not really, not even the ones
she had known for years. She saw potential threats in everyone now, and she felt like a permanent
outsider, as though the assault had cut her off from the ordinary world where people did not have to
think about blades and silence and the moment everything could change.

Sleep became impossible. She would lie awake next to her husband, her body rigid, listening to the
house, waiting for the silence to break. When she did sleep, she dreamed of the cell block, of the
blade, of Marcus’s face. She would wake up gasping, her forearm aching where the scar had healed.

What finally brought Zara to seek help was a morning at work when she found herself unable to enter
the cell block. She stood in the corridor, her body frozen, her heart hammering, and she knew that
if she went in, she would not be able to do her job. She would be too busy scanning, too busy
waiting for the silence to break. She was sixty-eight years old, and she had spent her whole life
being the person who walked into dangerous rooms. Now she could not even walk into a hallway. She
was afraid that if she did not find a way to stop this, she would lose the only thing she had
left—the work that had given her a purpose, the identity she had built over thirty-seven years. She
was afraid of what she might become if she could no longer be that person.
