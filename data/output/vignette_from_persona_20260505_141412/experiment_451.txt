Andrés, a 35-year-old musician from a Latin American family in Los Angeles, had spent his adult life
on stage, in studios, and at parties, always surrounded by the warmth of an audience or the
camaraderie of bandmates. That world ended on a rain-slicked highway two years ago, when his tour
van hydroplaned and flipped twice before landing in a ditch. The crash itself existed for him only
in fragments: the screech of metal, a single moment of weightlessness, a stranger’s face peering
through shattered glass. When he tried to deliberately recall the sequence of events—how the skid
started, whether he had screamed, who pulled him out—his mind went blank, as though the memory had
been erased. What remained were disconnected pieces that refused to form a whole: the smell of
gasoline, a shard of glass embedded in his palm, the sound of his own breathing, too fast. He was
certain he was only remembering parts of what had happened, and that the rest—the worst part,
perhaps—was missing entirely, locked away where he could not reach it.

In the weeks after the accident, Andrés found himself unable to sit still. When he was alone, the
fragmented pieces surfaced without warning, and his thoughts spiraled into a single, burning
conviction: what had happened to him was deeply unfair. He had been sober, he had been driving
carefully, he had done nothing to deserve the van twisting through the air. And nothing had been
done about it—no lawsuit, no apology from the road crews, no acknowledgment that his life had been
derailed by someone else’s negligence. That sense of injustice festered. More frightening, though,
was what he felt building beneath it: a rage so vast that he became terrified he would not be able
to control it. He imagined himself screaming at a stranger in traffic, or worse, hurting someone he
loved. The fear of his own anger became a constant companion, and he was certain that if he ever let
it out, he would do something terrible.

His solution was to never be alone. He began accepting every gig, every jam session, every late-
night hangout. He filled his calendar so completely that there was no hour in the day when he was
not surrounded by people. On the rare nights he came home early, he would call friends until someone
agreed to meet him for a drink. His wife, Mariana, grew worried; she wanted to talk about the crash,
to understand what he was feeling, but Andrés refused. Talking about the event or its aftermath felt
like opening a door he had nailed shut. He avoided thinking about it with the same determination,
steering his mind away whenever the fragments threatened to surface. The constant socialising
worked, in a way—as long as he was laughing, playing, or listening to someone else’s story, he did
not have to face his own.

But the cost was steep. His marriage became a series of missed conversations and unshared silences.
Mariana began sleeping in the guest room, and Andrés told himself it was better that way—she was
safer if he kept his distance. His music, once his deepest joy, had become mechanical; he played the
same covers night after night, his fingers moving automatically while his mind stayed carefully
blank. He had come to believe that his life had been destroyed by the trauma, that the person he
used to be—the one who wrote songs, who felt hopeful, who trusted his own hands—was gone.

What finally brought Andrés to a therapist’s office was a moment of quiet. A gig had been cancelled,
and he found himself alone in his apartment for the first time in months. The fragments came back:
the smell of gasoline, the weightlessness, the stranger’s face. He stood in the kitchen, his heart
pounding, and he was certain that if he did not do something, the rage would finally break through.
He called Mariana, and she drove him to the clinic. Now, sitting in the waiting room, he is most
afraid of what he might discover if he lets himself remember—and most hoping that someone can help
him find a way to live without being afraid of himself.
