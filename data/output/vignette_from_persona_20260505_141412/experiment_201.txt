The event that changed everything for Elena happened three years ago, on a warm October afternoon in
her studio in downtown Los Angeles. She was sixty-seven then, still working full-time as a fashion
designer, still running the small atelier she had built over four decades. A young intern named
Marisol had been with her for only six weeks. Elena had taken her under her wing—a bright, nervous
girl from El Salvador who reminded Elena of herself at that age. They were alone in the studio,
reviewing fabric swatches for the spring collection, when Elena heard a commotion from the street
below. She walked to the window and saw two men arguing outside the bodega across the way. She did
not think much of it until she heard the shot. She saw the younger man fall. She saw the blood
spread across the concrete. She saw the other man run. And then she saw Marisol, who had come up
beside her, staring with the same frozen horror. Elena pulled her away from the window, but it was
too late. Marisol had seen everything. For weeks afterward, Elena could not stop thinking that she
should have closed the blinds that morning. She should have kept Marisol at the cutting table. She
should have done something differently. She had been the one in charge, and she had failed to
protect a young woman who trusted her. The men who did the shooting were never caught. The police
came, took a report, and nothing happened. Elena read the news articles for months, searching for
updates that never came. She began to feel that the world was a place where terrible things happened
and no one was held accountable, where she herself had been part of the failure.

The memory of that afternoon does not come to Elena in fragments. It comes whole. She will be
standing at her ironing board, pressing a seam, and suddenly she is back at that window. She feels
the warmth of the afternoon sun on her forearms—that exact temperature, that precise sensation on
her skin—and she is trapped in the moment again. She hears the crack of the shot, sees Marisol’s
face reflected in the glass, smells the dust and fabric softener of the studio. It is not a memory;
it is a return. There is no warning. A shift in the air conditioning, a particular warmth in the
car, and she is gone. She has learned to avoid the news entirely. She deleted the apps from her
phone. She asked her husband to change the channel when the evening broadcast comes on. But the
temperature, the temperature she cannot control. On certain days, when the Santa Ana winds blow and
the air becomes dry and warm, she cannot leave the house. She stays in the bedroom with the air
conditioning on high, shivering under a blanket, because the cold feels safe. She has also come to
believe that positive emotions are dangerous. When she feels a moment of happiness—a compliment on a
dress, a laugh with her husband—she pushes it away. She is certain that feeling good makes her
vulnerable, that the moment she lets her guard down, something terrible will happen again. She
avoids grief, too. She has not visited her mother’s grave in two years, not because she does not
love her, but because she cannot bear to open that door. She is afraid that if she starts crying for
her mother, she will never stop, and she will drown.

The consequences have been concrete. Her atelier, once the center of her life, has become a place of
dread. She stopped taking on new clients. She cancelled the spring collection. She works from home
now, designing in a small room where she controls the thermostat and the blinds stay drawn. Her
husband, Miguel, has tried to help, but she has pulled away from him too. When he touches her arm in
the morning, she flinches, because she cannot separate the warmth of his hand from the warmth of
that afternoon sun. She knows this does not make sense, but knowing does not stop the feeling. What
finally brought her to seek help was a simple thing: she could not attend her granddaughter’s
quinceañera. The venue was outdoors, the day was warm, and Elena knew that if she stepped into that
sunlight, the memory would take her. She told her daughter she was sick. She heard the
disappointment in her daughter’s voice. That night, she sat in her cold bedroom and realized she was
losing the people she loved. She is afraid of what the therapy will ask of her. She is afraid that
talking about it will make the memory stronger, more real, more permanent. But she is also afraid of
spending the rest of her life in a room with the air conditioning on high, waiting for a warmth she
can no longer bear.
