a rhythm that felt borrowed. He did not sleep. He did not eat. He held her hand and prayed to
ancestors whose names he had not spoken aloud in years. When Maly finally opened her eyes and asked
for a banana, the relief that washed over him was so complete it felt like a physical collapse. But
something else had already taken root.

In the months that followed, Sophea found he could not hold the sequence of those seventeen days in
his mind as a story with a beginning, middle, and end. If someone asked him what happened, he would
open his mouth and find only fragments—the sound of a monitor alarm, the fluorescent glare of the
hallway at 3 a.m., the weight of his son’s hand on his shoulder. He could not say how the doctors
had explained the diagnosis, or what he had said to his daughter-in-law when she arrived. The pieces
refused to assemble. And yet, at the same time, the memory did not feel distant or faded. It felt
more real than anything around him. When he was pushing Maly on the swings at the park, the memory
of the hospital room would suddenly press in on him with a vividness that made the playground
dissolve. The beeping of the machines would fill his ears, and he would smell the antiseptic and the
cold, recycled air. For a moment, he was there again, and the present—the grass, the sunshine, his
granddaughter’s laughter—felt like a thin, unconvincing dream.

hospital bed. He refused to visit his own doctor for a routine blood pressure check, telling his son
he felt fine. The thought of sitting in a waiting room, of seeing a stethoscope or an IV stand,
filled him with a dread so immediate he could not explain it. He told himself the best thing was
just to keep moving. He filled every hour of every day: cleaning the house, cooking elaborate meals,
walking to the grocery store even when he did not need anything. He tried not to think about the
hospital, and when the images came, he would force his mind onto something else—the price of eggs,
the way the floorboards creaked, the list of chores for tomorrow. He kept his mind occupied all the
time, because stillness was dangerous.

The person he had become was unrecognizable to him. Before Maly’s illness, Sophea had been a man who

predictable. Now he looked in the mirror and saw a stranger—a man who flinched at sudden noises, who
watched people’s faces for signs of hidden danger. He had come to believe that people were not what
they seemed. A friendly smile from a cashier felt like a mask; a kind word from his son felt like
prelude to bad news. He was certain he had permanently changed for the worse. There was no going

His work as a nanny had once been a source of joy. He had cared for children his whole adult life,
first his own, then others’. But now, every cough from a child sent a spike of terror through his
chest. He found himself checking the children’s temperatures multiple times a day, listening to
their breathing while they slept. He no longer took them to the park or to playdates, inventing
excuses about traffic or weather. The parents noticed. They asked if he was feeling all right. He

needed someone with more energy. He did not argue. He told himself it was for the best.

What finally brought him to a clinic was his son’s quiet insistence. Sophea had stopped leaving the

laughed. He sat in the intake room with his hands folded, unable to look the therapist in the eye.
