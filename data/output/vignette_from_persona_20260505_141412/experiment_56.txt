The trauma that shaped Margaret’s life occurred before she could fully name it. She was six years
old, living with her mother and stepfather in a small house at the edge of a forest in northern
Michigan. Her stepfather, a quiet man who worked long shifts at a lumber mill, would come home and
find reasons to be alone with her. He would send her mother to the store for something trivial—a
missing ingredient for dinner, a forgotten errand—and then the house would fall silent. That silence
was the worst part. It meant he was coming. He would close the door to her bedroom, and the world
outside—the sound of birds, the hum of the refrigerator, the creak of floorboards—would vanish,
replaced by the sound of his breathing and the rustle of his clothes. She learned to dread the
absence of noise more than any loud sound. Afterward, he would tell her it was their secret, and she
believed him. She believed she was not a person anymore, just something he used and then put away.

Margaret is now seventy-three, divorced, and works as a forester in the same region where she grew
up. She has spent decades managing the very woods that once bordered her childhood home, a career
she chose because it kept her outdoors, away from enclosed spaces and the unpredictable silences of
indoor life. But the forest has not been a refuge. The sounds of the woods—the crack of a branch,
the sudden cessation of birdsong, the hush that falls before a storm—trigger the same visceral dread
she felt as a child. When a chainsaw cuts off abruptly and the forest goes quiet, her heart pounds
and she feels a cold wave of dissociation, as though she is no longer in her body but watching
herself from a distance. She has come to believe that her life was destroyed long ago, that the
trauma hollowed her out and left only a shell that goes through the motions of living. She often
feels like an object, not a person—something that exists to be used or ignored, never truly seen.

Margaret avoids exercise, though she knows it would help her aging joints and her mood. The feeling
of exertion, the heavy breathing, the pounding of her own heart—it reminds her too much of the
helpless, trapped feeling of being pinned down. Instead, she spends money impulsively. She buys
expensive tools she does not need, new boots when her old ones are still good, and online orders of
camping gear that arrive and sit unopened. The act of purchasing gives her a brief, fleeting sense
of control and distraction, but the packages pile up, and the credit card bills mount, and she feels
even more like a fraud—a woman who cannot manage her own life. She also avoids being alone in a room
or a building. She has arranged her small cabin so that every room has a window she can see out of,
and she leaves the radio on constantly, even when she sleeps. The silence that falls when the
station goes off-air at night terrifies her. She has not had a full night’s sleep in years.

Her work as a forester has become increasingly difficult. She now refuses to do solo fieldwork,
insisting on a partner even for routine inspections, which her colleagues find odd and burdensome.
She has been passed over for a promotion because her supervisor noted she was “not a team player in
the right way”—meaning she cannot be relied upon to work alone. Margaret knows this, but she cannot
explain it. She cannot tell them that being alone with a woman is worse than being alone with a man,
because women were the ones who left her alone with him. Her mother, her aunt, the neighbor who
heard nothing—they are all part of the same silent conspiracy. When a female colleague offers to
drive her to a remote site, Margaret makes an excuse. She feels a knot of fear and shame, and she
hates herself for it.

What finally brought Margaret to seek help was a moment of crisis. Last month, she was alone in her
cabin after a rare snowstorm knocked out the power. The silence was total, absolute. She stood in
the dark, unable to move, convinced that she was six years old again and that someone was about to
enter the room. She did not sleep for three days. She called a helpline from her cell phone, barely
able to speak. Now she sits in a therapist’s office, her hands clasped tightly in her lap, and says
she does not know if she can be helped. She is afraid of what she might find if she looks too
closely at the past. But she is also tired—tired of the spending, the isolation, the fear of every
quiet room. She hopes, faintly, that there might be a way to feel like a person again.
