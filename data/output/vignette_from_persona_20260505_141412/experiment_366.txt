Linh was sixty years old and had spent the last decade working the night shift at a call center in a
suburb of Los Angeles, taking customer complaints about billing errors and service outages from
people who never knew her name. She was married to a retired electrician named Bao, and they had
raised two children who now lived on the East Coast. Before the event that changed everything, Linh
had been a woman who kept a tidy house, attended the local Buddhist temple every Sunday, and called
her younger sister, Mai, every evening on her drive home from work.

Mai died suddenly eighteen months ago. She was fifty-seven, healthy, and had collapsed in her
kitchen while making tea. By the time Linh arrived at the hospital, Mai was already gone. The doctor
used words like massive coronary and no warning, but what Linh remembered most vividly was the
fluorescent light buzzing in the waiting room and the way her own hands trembled as she signed a
form she could not read properly. The moments that followed were fragmented in her mind—she could
see the silver edge of the hospital bed rail, the pattern of the linoleum floor tiles, the nurse’s
blue scrubs—but she could not recall who called Bao to pick her up, or how she got home that night.
Some parts of that hour were razor-sharp, others completely blank, and whenever she tried to hold
the whole sequence in her mind, she found herself pushing it away before she could reach the end. A
sudden piece would surface later, unbidden: the sound of the doctor’s voice saying “I’m so sorry,”
the weight of the plastic chair beneath her, and then nothing, as if the memory had been torn out of
a book.

In the weeks and months that followed, Linh began to notice that certain things could bring her to a
halt without warning. The sound of a baby crying in a grocery store aisle would make her chest
tighten so suddenly she had to put down her basket and leave. She had come to associate that
particular pitch with the waiting room—there had been a young woman holding an infant near the
vending machines, and the baby had been fussing while Linh waited for the doctor. She had never told
anyone this connection; she was not sure she fully understood it herself. More obviously, she
avoided funerals and cemeteries entirely. When a neighbor’s husband died, Linh sent money with Bao
and stayed home with the curtains drawn. She stopped driving past the hospital where Mai had been
taken. She stopped talking about Mai altogether. If Bao brought up her sister’s name at dinner, Linh
would stand up and begin washing dishes, her back to him, and he had learned to stop asking.

The most difficult change was with her social life. Before Mai died, Linh had belonged to a small
group of women from the temple who met for lunch every Saturday. They had known each other for
decades. Now, the thought of sitting across from them—women who had known Mai, who might mention
her, who might look at Linh with sympathy she could not bear—filled her with a dread that felt
physical. She began making excuses. First it was a headache, then a scheduling conflict, then
nothing at all. She simply stopped answering their calls. Bao went to temple alone. Linh stayed in
the house, which had become the only place where she could control what she encountered.

hospital memory without her permission: the buzzing light, the baby’s cry, the doctor’s blue scrubs,
then nothing. Sometimes she would feel as if she were back there, the fluorescent glare washing over
her, the fear so present that she would sit up in bed with her heart pounding, certain for a
disorienting moment that she was still in that waiting room. Bao had started sleeping in the guest
room because her restlessness kept him awake.

At work, the call center was a safe environment in some ways—she spoke to strangers, no one knew her
history, and the scripts were predictable. But there were moments when a customer’s voice on the
line would sound tired or distressed, and Linh would feel a surge of anger that frightened her, a
sharp, inexplicable rage that she had to swallow before she could speak. She had no idea where it
came from. She only knew that she was exhausted, that her life had shrunk to the space between her
front door and her cubicle, and that she had not visited Mai’s grave even once.

What finally brought her to seek help was a simple thing: Bao told her, quietly one morning, that he
missed his wife. Not the woman who sat in the dark living room watching the clock, but the one who

grief had become a locked room, and she was the only one inside. She was afraid of what she would
find if she opened the door, but she was also afraid of what would happen if she never did.
