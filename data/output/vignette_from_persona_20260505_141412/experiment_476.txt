Kwame is a 34-year-old parole officer who came to therapy at the urging of his partner, after months
of growing withdrawal and a recent incident at work that left him shaken. He is a tall, broad-
shouldered man who sits with his arms crossed, his gaze fixed on the floor. He speaks in a low,
careful voice, as if every word might cost him something.

The event that changed everything happened two years ago, in the apartment he shared with a man he
had been dating for nearly a year. Kwame had come home late from work, exhausted after a day of home
visits. They argued about something small—he cannot remember what now. What he remembers is the
smell of the antiseptic cleaner his partner had just used on the kitchen counters. He remembers the
sound of his own voice rising, then the sound of a hand connecting with his face, then the taste of
blood—copper and salt, filling his mouth. He remembers the feeling of the floor against his back,
the weight of someone he had trusted pressing down, the hospital smell that came later, in the
emergency room, when a nurse dabbed at his split lip with a gauze pad. He remembers thinking, *this
is not happening*, even as it was happening, because it felt like it was still happening, like it
would never stop happening. Even now, when the memory returns, it does not feel like a memory. It
feels like he is back on that floor, tasting the blood, hearing the thud of his own head against the
linoleum.

In the weeks and months that followed, Kwame did not tell anyone at work. He took a few sick days,
said he had the flu. He ended the relationship quietly, without explanation. He told himself he
would move on, that he would be fine. But he was not fine. He found himself avoiding eye contact
with colleagues, especially the men in his office. He started to notice how often men looked at
him—on the street, in the grocery store, during meetings—and he began to interpret those looks as
threats. A lingering glance from a stranger in a parking lot made his stomach clench. A client who
stood too close during a check-in made his breath go shallow. He became certain that other men could
see something in him, some weakness, some vulnerability that invited harm. He started scanning every
room he entered for exits, for obstacles, for the faces of men who might be watching him too
closely.

His body stayed on alert. He could not relax, even at home. His partner would reach for him in bed
and he would flinch, then feel ashamed. He began to push away any moment of warmth or pleasure,
because those feelings felt dangerous—like letting his guard down, like inviting another blow. He
avoided any conversation that might turn into a disagreement. At work, he stopped challenging his
parolees when they gave evasive answers. He stopped asking hard questions. He let violations slide,
because confronting someone meant conflict, and conflict meant danger. His supervisor noticed. Kwame
was put on a performance improvement plan. He told himself he did not care.

What he cared about, what he could not stop thinking about, was the injustice of it all. The man who
hurt him was never held accountable. Kwame never filed a police report. He had been too ashamed, too
afraid, too convinced that no one would believe him—a man, a parole officer, someone who was
supposed to be in control. He had come to believe that his life had been destroyed by what happened,
that there was no way back to the person he had been. He started to wonder, in the quiet hours of
the night, whether his life was worth living anymore. He did not tell his partner this. He did not
tell anyone.

What finally brought him to therapy was a moment at work, three weeks ago. He was in the office
break room when a colleague opened a bottle of antiseptic hand sanitizer. The smell hit Kwame like a
physical force. He heard the thud again. He tasted the blood. He stood frozen, gripping the counter,
the memory so vivid and so present that he lost track of where he was. His supervisor found him
there, pale and shaking, and insisted he take leave. His partner made the appointment. Kwame came
because he did not know what else to do. He is not sure he wants to be here. He is not sure he wants
to feel any of this. But he is tired of being afraid, tired of watching every man who walks past
him, tired of a life that feels like it ended two years ago and never started again.
