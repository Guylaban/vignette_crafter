Trung was seventy-seven years old, a retired firefighter who had spent thirty years in the
department, and he had not spoken of the war since he left Southeast Asia as a young man. He came to
the clinic at his wife’s insistence, though he could not say what he hoped for. What he knew was
that he had not slept properly in decades, and that the sound of an ambulance siren on the street
below their apartment could make him drop whatever he was holding. He knew that when he heard the
name of a certain person—a name that belonged to a boy he had known, a boy who had died—his chest
would tighten and his vision would narrow, and he would have to sit down before his legs gave out.
He did not understand why this still happened. He was old now. It should be over.

The event itself had taken place in a small village during the war, when Trung was a civilian caught
between forces. He had been walking with a neighbor, a boy of perhaps sixteen, when a mortar round
landed close enough to knock them both to the ground. Trung had been stunned, disoriented, his ears
ringing. When he turned to look for the boy, he saw that the boy’s body was no longer whole. The
memory was not something Trung recalled so much as something that arrived without warning, fully
formed and unbearably clear: the color of the boy’s shirt, the position of the limbs, the smell of
dust and metal. He could describe it now in exact detail, as though it had been filmed and stored
somewhere behind his eyes. But when he tried to tell himself that this was his memory, his own past,
it felt wrong. The memory felt like it belonged to someone else. He was not the person who had been
there. That person had been young, had been foolish, had walked into a situation that someone
smarter would have avoided. Trung had come to believe that another man would not have let that boy
die. Another man would have pulled him to safety, would have seen the danger coming. Trung had not.
He had frozen. And that was what he was, underneath the uniform and the years of service: a frozen
thing, an object that had simply been there while a child was killed.

He had spent his career as a firefighter running into burning buildings, and his colleagues had
called him fearless. They did not know that he was never afraid because he had already stopped
feeling like a real person. When he went into a fire, he moved like a machine, scanning walls and
ceilings for signs of collapse, checking every corner for a trapped civilian, his eyes moving
constantly. This vigilance had made him good at his job. It had also made him exhausted. At home, he
could not stop. He checked the locks three times before bed. He listened for footsteps in the
hallway. He lay in the dark with his eyes open, because lying in the dark was what he feared
most—not the dark itself, but what came with it: the memory that would slide into his mind the
moment he let his guard down. He had learned not to think about the event. He had learned that if he
started, he would not be able to stop. The thought would spiral, and he would end up sitting on the
edge of the bed, his hands shaking, imagining what he would do if he could go back. He imagined
revenge on the people who had fired that mortar. He imagined finding them, hurting them, making them
feel what the boy had felt. He knew this was useless. He was an old man. He could not change
anything. But the fantasy came anyway, and it was the only thing that made him feel less hollow.

His wife had noticed the changes over the years—the irritability, the way he startled at loud
noises, the nights he spent in the living room with the television on but the sound off, watching
the door. She had stopped asking him about the war a long time ago. But recently, when she heard him
muttering in his sleep, she had said, “You need to talk to someone.” He had come because he did not
want to lose her. He did not know if talking would help. He was certain that if he let himself think
about the event deliberately, he would not be able to handle it. The memory would swallow him. But
he was already being swallowed. He sat in the chair across from the therapist, his hands on his
knees, his eyes moving slowly around the room, checking the windows, the door, the corners. He was
waiting for something to happen. He did not know what. He only knew that he could not keep living
like this, and that he was afraid of what would happen if he stopped.
