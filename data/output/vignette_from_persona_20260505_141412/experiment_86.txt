Elena Reyes, a seventy-nine-year-old woman who had spent decades as a combat pilot, sat in the
consultation room with her hands folded tightly in her lap. She had come to therapy at the
insistence of her daughter, who had grown increasingly worried about the changes she had seen in her
mother over the past two years. Elena could not argue with her daughter's concern. She knew
something was wrong, even if she could not name it.

The event that had undone her happened on a Tuesday afternoon in the parking lot of a grocery store
near her home in San Antonio. She had been reaching for her shopping cart when she heard a siren
approaching, then another, then the screech of tires. A police cruiser had pulled into the lot,
lights flashing, and an officer had jumped out and sprinted past her toward a young man who was
running between the cars. Elena had frozen. She remembered the moment as a single, vivid snapshot:
the officer's hand on his holster, the young man's face, the angle of the sun catching the metal of
the badge. Then another snapshot: the officer's arm extended, the crack of a shot, the young man
falling. And a third snapshot: the officer standing over the body, his radio crackling. That was all
she had. Three frozen images, each one a picture in her mind that she could not connect into a
story. She had not seen the beginning or the end, only these isolated frames that played over and
over without warning, intruding into her thoughts at any moment. Sometimes, when they came, she
could smell the hot asphalt and the faint metallic tang of blood, even though she knew she was
sitting safely in her own living room.

Elena had always been a woman who could relax. As a pilot, she had learned to compartmentalize, to
set aside fear and focus on the task at hand. But now, she could not relax even when she knew she
was in a safe place. Her body remained on high alert, her shoulders tight, her stomach knotted, her
heart racing at the slightest unexpected noise. She had come to believe that danger was everywhere,
that she was never truly safe. When she walked into a room, her eyes went first to the corners, to
the shadows, to anything that could be a weapon. She noticed knives on kitchen counters, the heavy
base of a lamp, the sharp edge of a broken bottle on the sidewalk. She scanned every environment
constantly, checking for signs of threat, looking for exits, watching the hands of people around
her. She was certain that if she let her guard down for even a moment, something terrible would
happen.

She had stopped going to the grocery store. She had stopped going anywhere that might be crowded.
The thought of being in a place with too many people, where she could not see the exits or track
everyone's movements, made her feel like she could not breathe. She had also stopped driving past
the police station, and she crossed the street whenever she saw a patrol car. The sight of a
uniformed officer, the sound of a siren in the distance, sent a jolt of adrenaline through her that
left her shaking for hours.

Elena had developed a set of routines to manage her fear. She stayed up until two or three in the
morning, delaying sleep because the moment she closed her eyes, the snapshots would come. She wore
only loose, dark clothing now, because she had been wearing a light blue blouse on the day of the
shooting, and the sight of that color on anyone made her stomach turn. She checked her surroundings
constantly, even in her own home, peering around doorframes before entering a room, looking out the
window before opening the front door.

The consequences of these habits had accumulated quietly. She had stopped visiting her friend Maria,
who lived in a neighborhood with a high police presence. She had stopped attending her weekly bridge
game at the community center because the room was always too crowded. Her daughter had noticed that
Elena no longer answered the phone after dark, and that she had become short-tempered, defensive,
quick to snap. Elena knew she was pushing people away, but she could not stop herself. What
frightened her most was the feeling that she was losing control of her own mind, that the snapshots
could come at any moment, that her body would react before she could stop it, and that she would
never feel safe again.

She had come to therapy because her daughter had begged her, and because, for the first time in her
life, Elena did not know what else to do. She hoped that someone could help her stop the pictures,
stop the smell, stop the constant scanning. But she was afraid that nothing would work, that she was
too old to change, that this was simply how the rest of her life would be.
