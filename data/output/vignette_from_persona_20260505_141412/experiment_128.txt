Nadia, an eighty-year-old widow and retired social worker of Middle Eastern descent, sat in the
consultation room with her hands clasped tightly in her lap. She had come to therapy at the urging
of her daughter, but what had finally pushed her through the door was a moment she could no longer
outrun. A week earlier, while waiting for a routine blood test, the sharp, clean scent of antiseptic
had hit her nostrils in the waiting room, and before she could brace herself, she was drowning.
There was no image, no clear picture in her mind, but the feeling was absolute: the same crushing
weight in her chest, the same cold dread, the same overwhelming grief that had seized her thirty
years ago when her son, Amir, was diagnosed with leukemia. She could not see the hospital room,
could not recall the doctor’s face, but she felt the terror as if it were happening in that moment,
her body trembling, her breath short, her eyes filling with tears for no reason that the other
patients could see.

The trauma itself had unfolded over eighteen months. Amir was twelve when the fevers started, then
the bruising, then the diagnosis. Nadia had spent every day at his bedside, coordinating with
doctors, translating medical jargon for her husband, who spoke little English, and trying to hold
her family together. She had been the strong one, the one who made decisions, who signed consent
forms, who told Amir he would be fine. But in the quiet hours of the night, when the IV drips beeped
and the fluorescent lights hummed, she had felt a helplessness so profound it seemed to hollow her
out from the inside. She had prayed, bargained, begged. And when Amir finally went into remission,
Nadia had told herself the nightmare was over.

But it was not over. The nightmare had simply taken a different shape. Over the years, she had come
to believe that she had acted in ways that went against everything she stood for. She had been so
focused on survival that she had snapped at her husband, had missed her daughter’s school play, had
once, in a moment of exhaustion, wished it would all just end. She carried these memories like
stones in her chest, each one a proof that she had permanently changed for the worse. She was no
longer the warm, patient woman she had once been. She was someone who could not stop bad things from
happening, someone who had failed to protect her own child from the most basic threat of all.

The consequences of this belief had reshaped her life. She stopped going out, except for absolute
necessities. The world outside her apartment felt unpredictable, dangerous. When friends called to
invite her to dinner, she made excuses. When her daughter suggested a walk in the park, Nadia said
she was tired. But the real reason was simpler and more terrifying: she could not bear to hear
anyone talk about loss. A casual comment about a neighbor’s deceased husband, a news story about a
child with cancer, even a friend reminiscing about a parent who had passed—these words hit her like
physical blows, and she would feel the grief surge up, unbidden, as fresh as the day Amir was
diagnosed. She had learned to scan conversations for danger, to steer topics away from anything
resembling illness or death, and when she could not, she would push the thoughts out of her mind,
shoving them down with a force that left her exhausted.

The avoidance had narrowed her world to a small, safe radius. She had not visited a hospital in
years. When her own doctor recommended a physical examination, she cancelled the appointment and
never rescheduled. She would cross the street to avoid passing a pharmacy, and the sound of an
ambulance siren made her freeze in place, her heart hammering, until the noise faded. Her daughter
had noticed, had grown worried, had finally insisted that something was wrong. Nadia had agreed to
come to therapy not because she believed it would help, but because she was afraid of losing her
daughter’s patience, afraid of being left alone entirely.

Now, sitting in the quiet office, she could smell nothing more threatening than coffee and paper.
But she was certain that at any moment, the memory would force its way in again, without warning,
and she would be back in that hospital room, helpless and grieving. What she hoped for, more than
anything, was to find a way to stop feeling so much—or perhaps, to find a way to forgive herself for
having felt it at all.
