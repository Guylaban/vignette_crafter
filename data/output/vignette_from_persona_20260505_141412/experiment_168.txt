Marguerite is a fifty-four-year-old married woman who works as a chef in a busy restaurant kitchen.
She came to therapy at the urging of her husband, who had grown increasingly worried about the
changes he had seen in her over the past eight months. She is thin and tense, her hands folded
tightly in her lap as she speaks, her eyes scanning the room even in the relative safety of the
office.

The event that changed everything happened on a Tuesday evening in late autumn. Marguerite had
stayed late after service to do inventory, and the kitchen was quiet except for the hum of the walk-
in cooler. A new prep cook, a man in his late twenties who had been hired three weeks earlier, had
lingered behind. She had thought nothing of it at first—he had seemed reliable, even friendly. But
when she turned from the dry storage shelves, he was standing much closer than she had expected. He
backed her against the steel counter, his hand on her shoulder, his voice low and insistent. She
froze. He did not strike her, but he did not stop when she asked him to. He spoke to her in a way
that made her feel small and trapped, his body blocking the door. It lasted only a few minutes
before a dishwasher returning for a forgotten jacket startled them both, and the man stepped back
and walked out. Marguerite told no one. She finished the inventory, locked up, and drove home.

In the weeks that followed, Marguerite found that she could not describe what had happened. When her
husband asked why she seemed distant, she said she was tired. When a friend asked if something was
wrong, she said it was nothing. The truth was that the event would not come to her in words.
Instead, it came in flashes—the cold metal of the counter against her lower back, the smell of
garlic and sweat, the weight of a hand on her shoulder. These pieces arrived without context,
disconnected from one another, so that sometimes she would be standing at the stove and suddenly
feel herself pinned again, her body rigid, her breath shallow, as if she were still pressed against
that counter. She could not tell the story because there was no story—only fragments that did not
fit together.

At work, Marguerite began to notice changes in how she moved through the kitchen. She had always
been a commanding presence, calling out orders and tasting sauces with her fingers. Now she flinched
whenever someone came up behind her. A busboy reaching past her for a plate would make her jerk
sideways. She began to avoid the walk-in cooler altogether, sending junior staff to fetch what she
needed. She found herself scanning the dining room during service, watching the door, checking the
faces of men she did not know. When a stranger approached the pass to ask a question, her heart
would pound before she even heard what he was saying.

She stopped staying late. She stopped training new hires if they were male. She began to feel that
the world was full of people who could hurt her and that she could never know which ones would. The
man had seemed kind. He had smiled. He had asked about her grandchildren. And then he had cornered
her. She had trusted him because he worked for her, because she had trained him, because he had
seemed ordinary. Now she believed that trust itself was a trap. She began to watch her husband
differently. When he came up behind her in the kitchen at home, she would startle. When he tried to
hold her at night, she would lie still, her body tense, waiting for something she could not name.
She did not tell him why. She could not find the words.

The consequences accumulated slowly. She stopped attending the monthly dinner parties she and her
husband had hosted for decades. She stopped walking their dog in the evening. She began to drink a
glass of wine before bed, then two, to quiet the watchfulness that kept her awake. Her husband grew
hurt and then frustrated. He accused her of pulling away. She said nothing. At work, her sous chef
noticed that she had stopped going into the walk-in and that she flinched whenever the new line cook
walked past. Marguerite told him she was fine. She was not fine.

What finally brought her to seek help was a morning in the kitchen at home, when her husband walked
up behind her to kiss her neck, a gesture he had made a thousand times, and she spun around with a
chef’s knife in her hand. She saw the shock on his face. She saw the fear. She put the knife down
and began to cry. She came to therapy because she could not keep living like this, and because she
was terrified that she would never feel safe again, and because she could not bear the thought that
the man who had cornered her had taken not just that evening, but her marriage, her kitchen, her
life.
