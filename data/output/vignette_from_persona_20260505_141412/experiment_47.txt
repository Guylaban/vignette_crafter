Kofi is a 19-year-old UX designer who came to therapy at the urging of his supervisor, after several
months of declining performance and increasing isolation at work. He is single, of West African
descent, and lives alone in a studio apartment in a midsized city. He completed the PCL-5 with a
score of 65, and during the initial sessions, he described a single traumatic event that had
reshaped his entire sense of safety.

The event occurred about ten months ago, during his final semester of a design bootcamp. Kofi had
been working late nights on a capstone project, often alone in the co-working space where his cohort
met. A fellow student—a woman he had exchanged only a few polite words with—began to wait for him
outside the building. At first, it seemed coincidental. Then she started leaving notes on his desk,
commenting on things he had said in casual conversation, referencing details about his schedule that
he had never told her. Over three weeks, it escalated. She found his personal email, sent him long
messages about how they were meant to be together, and began appearing at the coffee shop he visited
every morning. Kofi reported feeling a creeping dread he could not name at the time. He told himself
he was overreacting. He did not want to cause trouble. He kept being polite, hoping she would lose
interest. One night, she followed him to his bus stop, grabbed his arm, and told him she knew where
he lived. He pulled away and ran. The next day, he reported her to the program director. She was
expelled, and a restraining order was filed. But by then, Kofi had already stopped sleeping.

In the months that followed, the world had become, in his words, "a dangerous place." He described
this not as an abstract belief but as a felt certainty that lived in his body. Every time he left
his apartment, he scanned the street for anyone who looked like they might be watching him. He
checked the faces of women in his vicinity with a hypervigilance that exhausted him. He had come to
believe that the event happened because of the way he acted—because he had been too nice, too
accommodating, too afraid of being rude. He was certain that if he had been firmer earlier, or if he
had not smiled at her that first time she said hello, none of it would have happened. This self-
blame sat inside him like a verdict: he had brought this on himself, and therefore he could not
trust his own judgment about who was safe.

As a UX designer, Kofi now works in an open-plan office with low cubicle walls and a constant flow
of collaborators walking past his desk. He had always enjoyed the energy of the space, but after the
stalking, he found himself unable to focus. He had to be especially careful—he was certain that you
never know what can happen next. He began to sit with his back to the wall, his laptop angled so
that no one could see his screen. He stopped wearing headphones because he needed to hear footsteps
approaching. He started coming in early and leaving late, timing his movements to avoid the crowd at
the elevator. His supervisor noticed that he had stopped attending team stand-ups and was
communicating only by Slack. When asked about it, Kofi said he was "just busy." In truth, he was
terrified of the small talk, of the possibility that a colleague might ask him a personal question,
of the vulnerability of being known.

Over the weeks and months, Kofi's avoidance had taken on a pattern that he could no longer ignore.
He had stopped going to the grocery store after dark. He had deleted his social media accounts. He
had stopped dating entirely—the idea of someone having access to his life, his routines, his home
address felt unbearable. His only social contact was with his mother, who he called once a week, and
even then he kept the conversations short and vague. The consequence that finally brought him to
therapy was occupational: he had missed three project deadlines, and his supervisor had placed him
on a performance improvement plan. Kofi was terrified of losing his job, not just because of the
income but because his apartment, his routine, his carefully controlled environment was the only
place he felt even remotely safe. He came to the first session with his hands clasped tightly in his
lap, and he asked, almost immediately, whether the therapist would need to record anything. He said
he was hoping to feel less afraid, but what he did not say—what he could not yet articulate—was that
he was also terrified of what would happen if he let his guard down. Because if the world really was
as dangerous as he now believed, then his vigilance was not a symptom. It was the only thing keeping
him alive.
