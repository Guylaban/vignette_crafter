The crash happened on a Tuesday evening in late October, on the interstate just outside of town.
Amir had been driving home from a routine audit at a manufacturing plant, the kind of day he had
done a hundred times before. He was stopped in a line of traffic when a pickup truck, its driver
later found to be three times over the legal limit, slammed into the rear of his sedan at nearly
sixty miles per hour. The impact drove Amir’s car into the vehicle ahead, and then the world became
a compressed, deafening silence of twisted metal and shattered glass. He remembers the smell most
vividly—the sharp, sweet stench of spilled alcohol that seeped through the crumpled doorframe from
the other driver’s cab, mixing with the acrid odor of deployed airbags and hot engine fluid. He was
trapped for twenty minutes before the fire department cut him free, and in that time, he was certain
he was going to die. He could not move his legs, could not hear anything but a high-pitched ringing,
and the only thing he could smell was that alcohol.

In the months that followed, the physical injuries healed—a fractured clavicle, some bruised
ribs—but something else had broken in a way that did not show on an X-ray. Amir, who had once prided
himself on his meticulous attention to detail and his ability to handle any file that crossed his
desk at the state tax office, now found that he could no longer trust himself to do the simplest
things. He had come to believe, with a certainty that felt absolute, that he could not rely on
himself. The thought would surface without warning: *If I couldn’t even see that truck coming, what
else am I blind to?* He became convinced that he was no longer capable of doing the things that had
once defined him—the careful analysis, the steady focus, the quiet competence that had made him a
reliable colleague and a dependable man. Worse still, he had begun to feel as though he would never
be able to feel normal emotions again. The world had gone flat, and he moved through it like a ghost
haunting his own life. The divorce, which had been finalized six months before the crash, now felt
like a confirmation of something he had always suspected: that he was fundamentally unable to hold
things together.

The triggers were everywhere, and they were relentless. Any car that had been in an accident, even a
minor fender-bender, would stop him cold. He would see the crumpled bumper of a sedan in a parking
lot and his chest would tighten, his vision narrowing as the memory of the impact reverberated
through his body. The smell of alcohol—a whiff of beer from a passerby, the scent of wine on a
colleague’s breath at an office party—would send him into a state of raw, animal alertness, his
heart hammering as if the danger were immediate. And then there were the cars themselves: a silver
sedan of the same make and model as his own would appear in traffic, and for a split second he would
be back in that moment, the world collapsing around him.

He stopped driving altogether. It was not a decision he made consciously; it was a gradual,
inevitable retreat. He took the bus to work, but even being a passenger was a torment. Every
unexpected jolt, every sudden brake, every loud exhaust backfire from a passing truck would make him
jump so violently that other passengers would stare. He had become hypervigilant in a way that
exhausted him. He was on high alert for any unexpected or loud sound—a door slamming, a car horn, a
dropped pan in the kitchen—and his body would respond before his mind could catch up, flooding him
with adrenaline for a threat that was never there. At the office, he found himself flinching when a
colleague dropped a binder, or when the mail cart rattled past his cubicle. The startle response was
humiliating, and he began to eat his lunch alone in his car—until he stopped driving, and then he
simply stopped eating lunch.

The undereating started as a way to cope, a small thing he could control. He told himself he was
just not hungry, but the truth was that food felt like an intrusion, a demand he could not meet. He
would skip breakfast, forget to eat during the day, and then find himself dizzy and weak by evening.
And when he got home to his empty apartment, he would collapse onto the couch and disappear into his
phone or a video game for hours, scrolling aimlessly, letting the glow of the screen fill his vision
until his eyes burned and his mind went numb. It was the only way he knew to avoid thinking. The
screen escape became a nightly ritual, a wall between himself and the images that waited in the
dark.

What finally brought him to seek help was not the fear or the startle or the hunger. It was a moment

staring at a spreadsheet for forty minutes without entering a single number, his mind blank, his

he did not find a way back. He was afraid that the man he had been was already gone, and that
nothing could bring him back. But he was also, somewhere beneath the numbness, hoping that he might
be wrong.
