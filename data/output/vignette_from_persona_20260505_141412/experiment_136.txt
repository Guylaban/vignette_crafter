The assault happened in the forensic lab where she had worked for thirty-two years. It was late,
past midnight, during a rush on a cold-case review. Eleanor was alone, cross-referencing bone-
fracture patterns against old autopsy photographs, when a colleague she had trusted—a man twenty
years her junior—cornered her by the evidence lockers. He did not strike her. He did not need to.
What he did was place his hand flat against her throat, pin her against the steel doors, and whisper
exactly what he would do if she ever told anyone about the discrepancy she had found in his case
files. The pressure of his palm against her larynx, the cold metal through her thin blouse, his
wedding ring pressing into her windpipe—those sensations had burned themselves into her body. She
did not report it. She told herself the threat was empty, that she could manage him, that she was a
professional. She finished the shift. She went home. She did not tell her husband.

In the weeks that followed, Eleanor began to notice that her body felt like a trap. When her
husband, Martin, mentioned their upcoming fortieth anniversary cruise—a trip they had saved for
years to take—she felt something detonate in her chest. Talking about the future had become
unbearable. Every plan, every calendar date, every mention of “next year” landed on her like a
physical weight. She would go quiet, stare at her hands, and change the subject. Martin assumed she
was tired. She let him believe that.

The memory of the assault would not stay still. Sometimes it arrived in fragments—the gleam of the
lockers, the pressure of the ring—but before she could reach the end, something in her mind would
fling the door shut. She would be left with a slammed-door feeling, her heart racing, the story
unfinished. At other times, the memory came complete but hollow: she could see the scene, hear his
voice, feel the cold metal, yet she felt nothing at all. It was as if she were watching a woman who
looked like her being attacked in a documentary. That numbness frightened her more than the rage
did, and the rage was considerable. When the full memory broke through—when she could not block it
fast enough—she felt a fury so pure and so hot that she had to grip the kitchen counter to keep from
breaking something. She had never been an angry person. She did not know what to do with this
stranger inside her.

She began to believe that she was inadequate. The thought arrived fully formed one morning as she
stood in front of the bathroom mirror, her hand touching her own throat. A woman who could not
defend herself, who could not report what happened, who could not even hold a coherent memory of the
event—that woman was not the person she had been. She had been happy once. She remembered that the
way one remembers a foreign country visited long ago. Now she woke miserable, and the misery had
become the background hum of her life.

Her strategies for survival were precise and, in their way, logical. She stopped exercising
entirely. The local gym had a row of treadmills facing a mirrored wall, and the sight of a man’s
forearm—the same width, the same watch—had sent her fleeing mid-stride three months ago. She never
went back. At home, she began to sit only in chairs that allowed her to place her back against a
wall, her eyes scanning the room. In restaurants, she chose the corner booth. In the lab, she
rearranged her workstation so that her chair faced the door, her spine pressed against the filing
cabinets. She told herself she was being practical. She was not. She was terrified.

Eating became a negotiation. She could manage a few bites of toast in the morning if she ate alone.
Dinner with Martin was harder—she would push food around her plate, claim she had eaten a late
lunch, and retreat to the bedroom. She had lost twelve pounds. Her clothes hung loose. Martin had
started to ask, gently, if she was ill. She said she was fine.

What finally brought her to seek help was not the weight loss or the sleeplessness or the rage. It
was a single moment in the lab, three weeks ago, when a junior analyst asked her opinion on a case
file. The file involved a cold-case assault. Eleanor opened the folder, saw the crime-scene
photographs, and felt her mind go blank. She could not speak. She sat there, her back against the
wall, her hand pressed to her throat, while the young woman waited for an answer. Eleanor excused
herself, walked to the bathroom, and locked the door. She stood there for twenty minutes, staring at
her own reflection, and for the first time she thought: I cannot do this alone.

She is afraid that therapy will make it worse. She is afraid that if she lets herself fully
remember, she will not come back. But she is more afraid of the life she is living now—the small
rooms, the empty plates, the future she cannot bear to name. She came here because she wants to
believe that the woman who was happy still exists somewhere, and that maybe, if someone helps her
open the door, she will find her still alive.
