Ravi is a 52-year-old South Asian man who works as a maid for a residential cleaning company in a
midsized Midwestern city. He came to therapy reluctantly, referred by his primary care doctor after
a routine checkup revealed elevated blood pressure and a tremor in his hands that had been getting
worse over the past year. When the doctor asked about stress, Ravi sat silently for a long moment,
then said, "I don't sleep. I don't feel like myself. I don't feel like anything." He agreed to come
only after the doctor told him his heart was showing signs of strain.

The event that changed everything happened eighteen months ago. Ravi had been cleaning a fifth-floor
apartment alone on a Tuesday afternoon. He was vacuuming the living room when he noticed the
client's elderly cat sleeping under the sofa. He finished the room, moved to the bedroom, and when
he returned to the living room to gather his supplies, he saw that the cat had crawled into the
vacuum cleaner bag he had left open on the floor. The cat was dead. Ravi remembers the moment as a
series of still images: the cat's body, the open bag, his own hands frozen on the vacuum handle. He
remembers the sound that came out of him, a kind of choked cry. He remembers calling his supervisor,
who told him to leave the apartment and come back to the office. He remembers the client calling him
later that evening, weeping, asking how this could have happened. He had no answer.

What haunts Ravi most is not the image of the cat itself, though that comes back to him without
warning. He can be pushing a cart down a hotel hallway, or standing at a red light, or reaching for
a cup of water in his own kitchen, and suddenly he is back in that apartment, the vacuum in his
hands, the weight of what he has done pressing down on him. The rage that floods him in those
moments is enormous, and it is directed entirely at himself. He has come to believe that he acted
carelessly, that he was rushing, that he failed to check the room properly because he was thinking
about his next job instead of paying attention to what mattered. He tells himself that a good person
would have looked. A careful person would have seen the cat. The fact that he did not means
something about who he is.

Since that Tuesday, Ravi has stopped feeling that his life has any direction. He goes to work
because he has to pay rent, but he no longer takes pride in his work. He used to enjoy making a
space clean and orderly; now he simply moves through the motions, his mind elsewhere. He has stopped
calling his adult daughter, who lives in another state, because he does not know what to say to her.
He is certain that if she knew what he had done, she would see him differently. He has stopped going
to the temple, where he used to attend weekly services. He tells himself he has no right to be
there, that he acted in a way that goes against everything he was taught about kindness and care
toward living things.

The worst part, Ravi says, is that the memory comes back without any warning. He can be fine, and
then something in his surroundings—the sound of a vacuum in another unit, the sight of a cat on a
windowsill, the smell of a particular cleaning solution—and he is back in that apartment, the rage
and shame and disbelief washing over him all at once. His hands start to shake. His chest tightens.
He has learned to excuse himself to the supply closet until it passes. His supervisor has noticed
that he takes longer on his routes than he used to. Ravi has told her he is just being more
thorough. In truth, he is avoiding certain apartments. He has asked not to be scheduled in buildings
that allow pets. He has started taking the stairs instead of the elevator because the enclosed space
makes him feel trapped when a memory hits. His social world has shrunk to almost nothing. He eats
alone, watches television without really seeing it, and waits for the next wave to come.

What finally brought Ravi to seek help was not the intrusive memories or the rage or the loss of
meaning, though all of those had been present for months. It was the moment last week when he was
cleaning a bathroom and caught a glimpse of himself in the mirror. He did not recognize the person
looking back. He thought, "I am not a person anymore. I am just what happened." That thought
frightened him in a way the others had not. He is not sure therapy can help. He is not sure he
deserves to feel better. But he came, and he sat down, and he told the story. He is afraid that if
he talks about it, he will only confirm what he already believes: that he is someone who did
something unforgivable. He is also afraid, somewhere underneath that, of staying like this forever.
