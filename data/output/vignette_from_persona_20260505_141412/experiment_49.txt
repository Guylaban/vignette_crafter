Thomas is a sixty-three-year-old publisher who, for the past several years, has been living inside a
waking nightmare he cannot shake. He is a large man, broad-shouldered, with the kind of steady,
deliberate presence that served him well in boardrooms and at editorial meetings. But now, that
steadiness is gone. He comes to the clinic at his wife’s urging, though he would never call it
that—he says he is here because he cannot keep going like this, and because his marriage is
beginning to fray under the weight of what he cannot control.

The event that changed everything happened three years ago, when he was attending an industry
conference in a high-rise hotel. A gas leak in the building’s kitchen caused an explosion that tore
through the ballroom during a cocktail reception. Thomas was standing near the bar when the blast
threw him against a wall, shattering his glasses and filling his lungs with smoke and the acrid,
sweet smell of alcohol and burnt wiring. He crawled through debris, past overturned tables and
people who were not moving, until he found an exit. He was not physically injured beyond cuts and
bruises, but something inside him broke that night. He has not been the same since.

The memory of that moment is not a story he tells; it is a series of pictures that appear in his
mind without warning, as vivid as the moment they were taken. He sees the chandelier swinging, the
carpet peeling up in waves, the look on a woman’s face as she pressed a napkin to her bleeding arm.
These images are not fuzzy or faded with time—they are sharp, detailed, and they arrive when he does
not want them. The worst times are when he wakes from a nightmare, still half-caught in the dream,
and the images linger like a film over his actual bedroom. He lies there, heart pounding, unable to
tell for a moment whether the smell of smoke is real or remembered.

The smell of alcohol is a particular torment. He cannot walk past a bar or stand near someone
drinking a beer without the olfactory memory yanking him back into that ballroom. At dinner parties,
he has started to excuse himself before the wine is poured. At home, his wife has stopped having a
glass of wine in the evenings, because she cannot bear the way he flinches when she brings it near
him. He knows this is unfair, but he cannot help it. The smell is a trigger he cannot avoid, and so
he avoids the people who might bring it into his space.

He has stopped seeing friends. Not all at once, but gradually, over months. Each invitation became a
calculation: will there be alcohol? Will someone ask about the conference? He started declining,
then stopped returning calls. His wife goes to social events alone now, and the silence between them
when she comes home is heavy with things neither of them says. He knows she is lonely. He knows he
is the reason. But the thought of sitting in a room full of people, of being asked a casual question
about his work, of having to talk about the event or even hear someone else mention a similar
story—it feels impossible. The memory forces its way into his mind when he is least prepared, and he
has learned that the only way to keep it at bay is to keep his world small.

At work, he has become a different man. Once known for his calm, collaborative style, he now insists
on sitting with his back to a wall in every meeting. He scans the room constantly—checking exits,
watching who enters, noting where people are standing. His colleagues have noticed; they think he
has become paranoid or controlling. He cannot explain to them that he is not looking for a better
angle on the quarterly report—he is looking for the nearest way out. Even in his own home, he feels
on guard. He jumps at ordinary sounds: the furnace kicking on, a car door slamming outside, the
creak of the staircase. His wife has learned to announce herself before entering a room, because she
once found him standing in the kitchen, white-knuckled, after she dropped a spoon.

He is exhausted. The hypervigilance does not switch off. He startles at sounds that would not bother
most people—the hiss of a coffee machine, the clatter of a delivery truck—and each time, his body
floods with adrenaline as if the explosion is happening again. He has come to believe that he is
broken, that something fundamental in him was shattered in that ballroom and cannot be repaired.
What frightens him most, he says, is not the memories themselves, but the feeling that he is losing
himself. He does not recognize the man who cannot sit in a restaurant, who avoids old friends, who
lies awake at night trying not to dream. He came to therapy because his wife told him she could not
watch him disappear anymore. And he came because, somewhere underneath the fear, he is still the man
who used to believe he could fix things. He hopes that might still be true.
