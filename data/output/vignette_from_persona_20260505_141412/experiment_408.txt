keep supply trucks running. She had been good at it—good with her hands, good under pressure, good
at being the person others could count on when a vehicle broke down in an exposed position. That was
what she had loved: the clarity of a problem you could fix, the satisfaction of something that
worked again because of what you did. She had been proud of that.

The attack came on a routine convoy escort. They were stopped at a checkpoint when the first mortar
round hit fifty meters to the left. Katherine was outside the vehicle, checking a tire pressure, and
the blast threw her into the side of the truck. She remembered the ringing in her ears, the dust so
thick she could taste it, and the way her commanding officer’s voice sounded like it was coming from
underwater. She remembered the second round landing closer, and the third hitting the vehicle behind
hers. She remembered running—no, crawling—toward cover, and she remembered the dizziness that came
afterward, the world tilting sideways every time she stood up. She was evacuated with a concussion
and a diagnosis of mild traumatic brain injury. The physical wounds healed. The dizziness did not.

Back home, Katherine tried to pick up where she had left off. She got a job driving a city bus,
thinking it would be straightforward—her route was fixed, her schedule predictable, her world
contained inside a vehicle she understood. But the dizziness returned without warning, sometimes
when she was turning her head to check a blind spot, sometimes when a passenger lurched into her
peripheral vision. Each time it happened, her heart would slam against her ribs, her palms would
slick the steering wheel, and she would be back in that dust and noise with no idea where the next
round would land. She learned to keep her back against the driver’s compartment wall, because that
was the only position where she could see the entire cabin at once. She learned to scan faces in her
rearview mirror, watching for anyone who moved unpredictably, anyone who might suddenly stand or
shout or reach for something. She told herself she was being careful. She knew, somewhere
underneath, that she was terrified.

things, who stayed calm, who knew what to do—was gone. She was certain that her life had been
destroyed in that dust and noise, that what remained was just a shell driving a bus through the same
streets every day. Nothing felt meaningful anymore. She went to work, she came home, she sat in her
apartment with her back to the wall and her eyes on the door, and she could not remember why any of
it mattered. She had stopped answering calls from her old army friends. She had stopped going to the
gym. She had stopped believing she could do anything that required her to be steady, to be capable,
to be someone others could count on.

What finally brought Katherine to seek help was a morning in late October when a passenger began
arguing loudly with another rider. The shouting was sudden, the movement unpredictable, and
Katherine felt the familiar dizziness wash over her—but this time, she also felt a violent surge of
physical reactivity, a jolt that made her slam on the brakes in the middle of an intersection. No
one was hurt, but her supervisor put her on administrative leave pending a fitness-for-duty
evaluation. She sat in her apartment that afternoon, back against the wall, and realized she could
not keep living this way. She was afraid of what would come next—afraid of losing her job, afraid of

combat zone. But she was also, somewhere beneath the fear, hoping that maybe the person she used to
be was not entirely gone. Hoping that maybe, if she could find a way to stop the world from tilting,
she could find her way back to herself.
