Frank is a 63-year-old construction worker, a widower of nearly five years, who came to see me after
his daughter insisted he talk to someone. He sat heavily in the chair, his large, calloused hands
resting on his knees, and for a long moment he said nothing. When he finally spoke, it was about the
woman who had been his partner for three years before she died. He did not call her his abuser. He
said her name, then stopped. The story came out in fragments, as if he were still trying to assemble
it himself.

The worst of it happened in the small house they shared. She was a volatile woman, quick to anger,
and her violence was not the kind that left marks a man could explain away at work. She would strike
him with whatever was near—a shoe, a lamp, her fists—but what he remembered most vividly was the way
her hands looked when she was about to hit him. The tendons standing out, the knuckles white. He
would see those hands coming and freeze. He told me once, his voice flat, that he had never hit her
back. He had been raised not to hit a woman, and so he had stood there and taken it, night after
night, until one night he simply stopped feeling anything at all.

Now, years later, those hands are everywhere. He cannot look at his own hands without a sick lurch
in his stomach. He avoids looking at the hands of women he passes on the street. In the hardware
store, he will cross an aisle to avoid a female cashier with large hands. At work, on the
construction site, he is fine until a female project manager holds up a blueprint and points—and he
sees her knuckles, and the world goes quiet, and he has to walk away. He tells himself he is being
ridiculous. He knows she is not going to hit him. But knowing does not stop the cold wash of fear
that comes over him.

He has become a man who lives by exits. When he walks onto a job site, the first thing he does is
locate every door, every window, every possible way out. He sits with his back to the wall in the
lunch trailer. He has left restaurants mid-meal because the only open seat was too far from the
door. His daughter has stopped inviting him to family gatherings because he always leaves early, and
she has begun to take it personally. He does not know how to explain that he is not rejecting her—he
is simply certain, in a way that feels like fact, that he cannot trust himself to stay safe. He
cannot rely on himself. That is what he says: "I can't rely on myself." He means it literally. He
believes that if something dangerous happened, he would freeze again, just as he did with her. He
sees his own inaction as proof of a fundamental weakness, a flaw in his character that makes him
less than a man.

To manage the constant, low-grade dread, he has started spending money he does not have. He buys
tools he does not need, electronics he never opens. The packages arrive, and for a few hours he
feels something other than the hollow numbness that has settled inside him. He calls it "dead
inside," and he means it. He does not feel joy. He does not feel anger, except in flashes that
frighten him. He does not feel grief for his wife, who died of cancer two years after the
relationship ended. He feels nothing, and then he feels guilty for feeling nothing, and then he
spends more money.

Sleep is a problem. He wakes several times a night, heart pounding, convinced he needs to check that
the doors are locked, that no one is in the house. He has no idea what he would do if someone were
there—he cannot rely on himself, after all—but the compulsion is overwhelming. During the day,
ordinary sounds make him jump: a car backfiring, a dropped hammer, a door slamming. His coworkers
have started to notice. They joke that he is jumpy. He laughs along, but he is ashamed.

What finally brought him here was an incident at a diner. A woman at the next table raised her voice
at a child, not even loudly, and Frank was out of his seat and out the door before he knew what he
was doing. He stood in the parking lot, shaking, and realized he could not keep living this way. His
daughter had been right. He needs help. He is afraid of what he might become if he does not get
it—more isolated, more numb, more broke. But what he hopes, though he cannot say it aloud, is that
someone will tell him he is not weak. That freezing was not a choice. That he can learn to trust
himself again.
