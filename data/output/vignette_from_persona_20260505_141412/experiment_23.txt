Therese was fifty-eight years old, a psychiatrist who had spent thirty years helping people untangle
the most disordered parts of their minds, and she could not tell a clear story of what had happened
to her own. The accident itself existed in fragments that refused to align. She remembered the sound
of metal, a sensation of rotation, the sudden pressure of the seatbelt against her collarbone, and
then a long, impossible silence. She knew, factually, that her car had been struck by a driver who
had run a red light at an intersection she had passed a thousand times. But when she tried to
assemble the sequence in her mind—the approach, the impact, the moment of flipping—the pieces would
not lock into place. She would start with one image and find herself abruptly standing in the
hospital corridor, or sitting on the curb with glass in her hair, with no memory of how she had
arrived there. What frightened her most was not the crash itself but this brokenness in her own
mind. She had always trusted her ability to construct a coherent narrative; it was the foundation of
her work. Now she could not even construct one for herself.

In the weeks and months that followed, the world around her began to feel like a place where danger
was simply waiting to reveal itself. She had always been a careful driver, but now she became
certain that every approaching vehicle was a potential collision. The sight of a car drifting
slightly toward the center line would send her heart into her throat, and she would find herself
gripping the steering wheel until her knuckles went white. She began to take longer, more circuitous
routes to avoid busy intersections, and eventually she stopped driving altogether. This was the
first concrete loss. Her practice was a fifteen-minute drive from her apartment; she had to cancel
all her in-person sessions and shift to telehealth, which meant seeing patients from the small home
office she had never intended to use. Her colleagues expressed concern, and she told them she was
fine, that it was merely a temporary precaution. But she knew, in a way she could not articulate,
that she had permanently changed for the worse. The woman who had once calmly de-escalated a
psychotic patient in an emergency room now could not trust herself to merge onto a highway.

She began to avoid sleep. Lying in the dark, with nothing to distract her, left her alone with the
fragments she could not order. She would keep the television on, or read until her eyes burned, and
when she finally did drift off she would wake repeatedly during the night, her body tense, listening
for something—a sound, a presence, a threat she could not name. She had come to believe that safety
required constant vigilance. At home, she arranged her furniture so that her back was always to a
wall, and she would sit in the same armchair in the corner of her living room, scanning the room for
exits before she could relax. She was jumpy in a way that embarrassed her. A colleague touching her
shoulder from behind would make her flinch so violently that she had to excuse herself to the
bathroom and breathe slowly until her hands stopped shaking.

Sex had become impossible. She had been divorced for years and had no partner, but she had
occasionally dated, and the thought of being physically close to someone now filled her with a quiet
dread. She could not explain why. She only knew that the idea of letting her guard down, of being
vulnerable in that way, felt like inviting disaster. She tried very hard to control her emotions, to
keep her face neutral and her voice steady, because she was terrified that if she let the fear
surface, it would never stop. She had been the one people came to for stability. Now she doubted
herself constantly. In sessions with her patients, she would second-guess her interpretations,
wondering if she was missing something, if she was still capable of doing the right thing. She had
always trusted her clinical instincts. Now she could not trust that she would know what to do in a
crisis.

What finally brought her to seek help was a morning in late autumn. She was sitting in her armchair,
her back to the wall, watching the headlights of cars pass across her ceiling, and she realized she
had not left her apartment in six days. She had been ordering groceries, canceling appointments,
telling herself she was being prudent. But as she sat there, she understood that she had built a
small, careful prison for herself, and that the woman who had once treated patients in the midst of
their worst moments was now afraid of the dark and the cars outside her window. She wanted to
believe that she could find her way back to herself. But a part of her, the part that had learned
that the world was dangerous and that she had been permanently broken, was not sure she deserved to.
