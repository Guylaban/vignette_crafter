Mei Lin is a 48-year-old tutor who came to therapy at the urging of her husband, though she was not
sure what talking could do. She had been married for fifteen years, but the trauma that reshaped her
life had come from a partner before him—a man she had lived with for two years in her early
thirties, whose violence had escalated slowly, then shattered her entirely one winter night. She had
never told her husband the full story. She had tried to bury it.

The event itself began as an argument over a phone call. He had accused her of lying, of hiding
something, and within moments his hand was around her throat. She remembered the pressure, the way
the room tilted, the sound of her own breathing turning into a thin whistle. What stayed with her
most, though, was the moment after—when he let go and she lay on the kitchen floor, staring up at
the fluorescent light, and saw his silhouette moving toward the knife block. He had not picked one
up. He had only stood there, breathing hard, then walked away. But that image—his shape against the
light, the knives just inches from his hand—had burned itself into her mind like a photograph. She
could still see it, clear and still and terrible, whenever she closed her eyes.

But the memory did not come as a whole story. It came in pieces: the cold linoleum against her
cheek, the whistle in her throat, the knife block, the light. She could not hold the sequence
together. And each piece, when it surfaced, brought with it the same raw terror she had felt in that
moment—as if she were back on the floor, not knowing if he would stop. Her body reacted before her
mind could catch up. She would be standing at the kitchen counter, reaching for a spoon, and
suddenly her heart would pound, her hands would shake, and only afterward would she realize she had
caught a glimpse of a steak knife in the drawer.

Over the years, Mei Lin had developed a quiet, constant vigilance that she barely noticed anymore.
She scanned every room she entered—classrooms, libraries, waiting rooms—looking for exits, for
angles, for anything that could be used as a weapon. She noticed dangerous objects everywhere: a
letter opener on a desk, a pair of scissors in a cup, a heavy glass paperweight. She did not think
about noticing; it simply happened. Her eyes moved automatically. She checked mirrors
constantly—rearview mirrors, store windows, the reflection in a darkened television screen—to see
who was behind her, who was approaching. She was watchful of people in a way that had become
exhausting. She studied faces for signs of anger, for the particular tightening around the jaw that
she remembered. She could not stop.

She had come to believe that she was permanently damaged, that the woman she had been
before—trusting, open, easy in her own skin—was gone forever. She felt like an outsider now, even
among people she had known for years. She would sit at a dinner table with her husband’s colleagues
and think: *You can never know who will harm you.* She looked at the kindest face in the room and
felt certain that kindness could be a mask. She did not say this aloud. She smiled, she nodded, she
made small talk. But inside, she was always watching.

Her work as a tutor had become a landscape of triggers. She met students in coffee shops and
libraries, and every new location required a scan. A young man who laughed too loudly made her pulse
spike. A father waiting for his child near the door—arms crossed, jaw set—sent her into a silent,
trembling alert. She had learned to position herself with her back to a wall, always facing the
entrance. She told herself it was just a habit. But the habit had narrowed her world. She no longer
agreed to meet students in unfamiliar neighborhoods. She had stopped going to the grocery store
after dark. She had not visited her sister in three years because the drive required a rest stop
where she once saw a man with a knife on his belt, and the memory of that sighting had lodged in her
like a splinter.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment in her own kitchen. Her husband had reached past
her for a chef’s knife to chop vegetables. She had frozen, breath caught, heart hammering—and he had
seen her face. He had put the knife down and asked her, quietly, what was wrong. She could not
explain. She could only shake her head. That night, he told her he was afraid for her, that she
seemed to be living in a war zone no one else could see. She had agreed to come to therapy because
she did not want him to worry. But what she really hoped—what she barely dared to admit—was that
somewhere inside her, the woman who had not been afraid might still exist, buried under the scanning
and the watching and the terror that came in fragments. She wanted to find her again.
