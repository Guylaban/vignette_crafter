At seventy-three, Meera still had the hands of an artist. For fifty years, those hands had
translated her clients’ vague wishes into rooms that felt like sanctuaries—selecting fabrics,
testing textures, running her palm across a bolt of silk or a swatch of linen to feel whether it was
right. She had built her reputation on that tactile intuition. Now, she could barely bring herself
to touch a piece of suede without her stomach turning.

The event that had unmade her happened on a Tuesday afternoon, two years after her husband Raj died.
She had been driving home from a client meeting in a neighborhood she did not know well, her GPS
rerouting her through a side street. A police cruiser pulled her over for what the officer later
said was a broken taillight. But from the moment the first officer approached her window—his uniform
dark and pressed, his hand resting on his belt—something in Meera’s chest had seized. He asked for
her license and registration. She fumbled. He asked again, louder. She remembered the way his voice
sharpened, the way another officer appeared on the passenger side, the way the afternoon light fell
across the fabric of their sleeves. What happened next she could not fully retrieve. There were
hands on her arms. There was the sidewalk against her knees. There was a texture—rough, synthetic,
the carpet of the patrol car’s back seat—pressing into her cheek. She remembered the pressure, the
smell of stale coffee and cleaning solvent. But the sequence was gone. She could not say whether she
had been handcuffed before or after she was placed in the car. She could not say who had spoken the
words “you’re being detained.” The memory came to her in fragments, like pieces of a shattered vase
that refused to fit back together. When she tried to describe it to her daughter Priya on the phone
that night, the words would not come. She knew what had happened—she had been pulled over, she had
been frightened, she had been released without charges after an hour—but the story itself felt
locked behind a door she could not open.

In the months that followed, Meera’s world contracted. She stopped driving on streets where she
might see a police car. Then she stopped driving at all. She told herself it was practical—her
eyesight was not what it used to be—but the truth was that every squad car she spotted from behind
the wheel sent a pulse of heat through her chest, and she could not afford to be that afraid while
operating a vehicle. The avoidance spread like a stain. She began taking the long way home from the
grocery store to avoid the main intersection where a police station sat. She stopped going to the
park where officers sometimes walked a beat. At first, her clients were understanding. But when she
cancelled three fabric consultations in a row because the showroom was located two blocks from a
courthouse, one of her long-term clients quietly moved their business elsewhere. Meera told herself
she did not care. Nothing good could happen to her anymore anyway. That was the thought that had
taken root in her mind, as stubborn and persistent as a weed. She had lost Raj. She had lost her
sense of safety. And now, she was losing her work, the one thing that had given her days shape.

Her body had become a traitor. She would be standing in her kitchen, stirring tea, and suddenly her
heart would hammer against her ribs as though she were being chased. There was no trigger she could
name—no siren, no uniform, no rough texture. Just her own skin, her own breath, her own pulse
insisting that danger was near. She had come to believe that her body was no longer on her side,
that it had been rewired by that afternoon to sound alarms at random. And because she could not
trust her own reactions, she had begun to fear even small upsets. A misplaced invoice. A phone call
from an unknown number. Each one felt like the beginning of something catastrophic, and Meera, who
had once managed entire renovation projects with calm authority, now found herself unable to cope
with the smallest disruption.

What finally brought her to seek help was not the avoidance or the fear, but a moment of silence.
She had been sitting in her living room, surrounded by fabric samples she had not touched in weeks,
and she realized she could not remember the last time she had felt anything like hope. She was not
living. She was hiding. And at seventy-three, she was terrified that this was all that was left—a
life reduced to staying still, staying quiet, staying safe. She did not know if talking could undo
what had been done to her. But she knew she could not go on like this.
