María Elena had been a project manager for twenty-three years, a job she once loved for its puzzles
and deadlines, its clean lines of responsibility. She was fifty-nine now, divorced for over a
decade, living alone in a two-bedroom apartment she had chosen for its light and its lock on the
front door. That lock had become a problem. She checked it three times before bed, and again at 2
a.m., and again at 4 a.m., and still she would lie awake, her neck a knot of hot wire, certain that
someone was on the other side of it. What frightened her most was that she could not stop this. She
knew, logically, that the door was bolted. She knew that her building had a doorman. She knew she
was safe. But her body did not know. Her body had its own knowledge now, and it had learned it six
years ago, in a windowless storage room behind a restaurant supply store in a city she no longer
visited.

The event itself had lasted four hours, though María Elena often felt it had not happened at all.
She would tell herself, sometimes aloud in the kitchen while washing dishes, that it had not really
been that bad. She had not been beaten. She had not been raped. She had been held at knifepoint by a
man who had followed her from the parking lot, who had forced her into the back of a delivery van,
who had driven her to that room and left her there while he argued on the phone about money. There
had been no chair. She had sat on the concrete floor, her back against a wall, her hands bound with
zip ties that bit into her wrists. The door had no handle on the inside. The only light came from a
crack beneath it. She had thought, for a long stretch of that four hours, that she would die there.
That no one would find her. That she had made a terrible mistake by parking in that lot. That she
should have known better. That she had done something wrong, something she could not name, that had
brought this upon herself.

In the months that followed, she had not sought help. She had gone back to work within a week. She
had told no one the full story, not even her ex-husband, who called to check on her and whom she had
dismissed with a clipped sentence about a mugging. She had come to believe that the best way to
handle it was to not handle it at all. She told herself the event had not really happened, or that
it had not been that bad, and for a while this magical thinking had worked. But the thinking itself
became a trap. She began to dwell on what she had done wrong. She went over and over the details of
that afternoon, trying to make sense of why she had parked there, why she had not screamed, why she
had not fought. She replayed the moment she had turned the key in the ignition, the moment she had
seen the man’s face in her rearview mirror, and she searched for the exact point where she could
have changed the outcome. She never found it. She only found more reasons to blame herself.

Her neck had never stopped hurting. She had seen two orthopedists, a chiropractor, a physical
therapist. The X-rays showed nothing. The pain was real, though, and it had become a trigger. Every
time she felt that familiar tightness, that hot wire pulling from her shoulder to her skull, she was
back on that concrete floor. She began to avoid locked spaces. She took the stairs at work, even
though she was on the eighth floor, because the elevator was a box with a closing door. She asked
her team to hold their meetings in open-plan areas, and when her boss assigned her a small
conference room for a quarterly review, she cancelled the meeting rather than enter it. Her
productivity slipped. Her colleagues noticed. She told them she had a back problem.

At home, the alertness never stopped. She could not relax. She would sit on her couch, the
television on, her hands gripping the remote, and she would feel a surge of panic at a creak from
the apartment above. She would react as if she were in danger, her heart hammering, her breath
shallow, even when she knew—she knew—it was only the neighbor walking to the bathroom. She had come
to believe that you could never know who would harm you. That anyone could be a threat. That safety
was an illusion. She used to be a happy person. She remembered that version of herself the way one
remembers a photograph of a stranger. Now she felt dead inside, hollowed out, going through the
motions of a life that no longer felt like hers.

What finally brought her to seek help was not the fear, but the numbness. She had looked at her
calendar one morning and realized she could not remember the last time she had laughed. She had not
called her sister in three months. She had stopped going to dinner with friends because she could
not bear the performance of being fine. She was afraid, still, of so many things—of locked doors, of
parking lots, of the night—but what she was most afraid of was that she would stay this way forever,
a woman who had survived a room with no handle and then spent the rest of her life living inside it.
