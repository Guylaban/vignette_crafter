The man who came to therapy, who asked to be called Marcus, was twenty-five years old and worked as
a housekeeper for a small hotel chain. He was single and lived alone in a studio apartment he kept
meticulously clean. He had been referred by his primary care doctor after a series of panic-like
episodes at work, but when he sat down in the chair, he did not immediately speak about the hotel.
He spoke about the dark.

Marcus’s trauma was not a single event but a texture of years. Between the ages of six and twelve,
he had been physically abused by his mother’s boyfriend, a man named Dale. Dale worked nights and
slept during the day, and the abuse happened in the hours after Marcus was supposed to be asleep.
Dale would come into his room, pull him out of bed by the arm, and strike him with whatever was at
hand—a belt, a wooden spoon, the flat of his hand. The worst times were when Dale forced him to
kneel on the bare floor in the corner of the living room, facing the wall, for hours at a time.
Marcus learned to stay perfectly still, to make himself as small and silent as possible, because any
sound—a sniffle, a shift of weight—would bring another blow. What he remembered most vividly, what
came back to him now with full sensory force, was the feel of the cheap linoleum under his knees: a
slightly sticky, cool, diamond-patterned surface that his skin would imprint with sweat. The memory
would hit him without warning—the cold floor, the smell of Dale’s cigarette smoke, the sound of the
television muttering in the next room, the dim light from a streetlamp through the blinds—and he
would be there again, completely, his heart slamming, his breath gone, his body frozen in a position
of surrender.

As an adult, Marcus had built a life around never being caught off guard. He had learned that you
could never know who would harm you, and that the people who seemed safest were often the most
dangerous. He had also come to believe that others could see what had happened to him just by
looking at him. He felt exposed, as though his history were written on his skin, and he dreaded the
moment someone would look at him and know. This feeling was strongest when he was alone with another
person. He had developed a quiet rule: never be alone with anyone, not even for a moment. At work,
he timed his cleaning of guest rooms to avoid intersecting with guests in the hallways. He would
wait in the supply closet until he heard a door close and footsteps fade, and only then emerge. His
coworkers thought he was shy or odd, but they left him alone. He had also begun to drop
responsibilities that required him to be around others. He had stopped visiting his sister because
she lived with a roommate, and he could not bear the idea of being in an apartment with two people
he did not fully trust. He had stopped going to the grocery store at normal hours, instead shopping
at three in the morning when the aisles were empty.

The most powerful escape Marcus had was in his own mind. When the world became too threatening—when
a child cried in the hotel lobby, when the cleaning cart’s wheel made a sound like a footstep, when
his hand brushed against a stiff polyester sheet that felt like linoleum—he would drift off into a
world of his own. He would imagine himself somewhere else entirely: a quiet beach, a forest, a room
with no doors. In these fantasies, he was safe. But the problem was that the fantasies were becoming
harder to reach. The real world kept breaking through. The memories were not just thoughts; they
were full sensory relivings that overwhelmed him so completely he had to stop whatever he was doing,
sometimes for minutes at a time. His body reacted before his mind could catch up—sweating, shaking,
his stomach clenching as if he were about to be hit. At night, he dreaded waking up in the dark,
because the dark was when Dale had come. He would lie rigid in bed, listening, his whole body primed
for a blow that no longer came.

What finally brought Marcus to seek help was a specific incident at the hotel. A guest had left a
child unattended in a room, and the child had begun to cry—a high, thin, desperate wail that cut
through the hallway. Marcus was standing outside with his cart, and the sound hit him like a
physical force. He dropped the cleaning spray and walked away. He walked out of the hotel, down the
street, and kept walking until he found himself in a park, sitting on a bench, shaking. He did not
go back to work that day. He did not call his supervisor. He simply disappeared. When he finally
went home, he realized he could not keep doing this. He was afraid of what he might lose next—his
job, his apartment, his tenuous grip on a life that already felt borrowed. He came to therapy
because he was exhausted, and because he was beginning to wonder if there was a world in which he
did not have to be on guard every second. He was not sure such a world existed, but he was willing
to ask.
