Meera never spoke about the night of the fire. Not to her colleagues, not to her mother who called
every Sunday from Chicago, and certainly not to herself if she could help it. She was thirty-two
years old, a nurse on the medical-surgical floor at a large public hospital in Queens, and for the
past fourteen months she had been trying to outrun something that felt less like a memory and more
like a trap door inside her own mind.

The event itself had lasted less than three hours. A propane tank explosion at a community festival
in late August. Meera had been off-duty, walking through the crowd with a friend, when the ground
shook and the air turned into a wall of heat. She had run toward the screams because that was what
she did—she was a nurse, she was trained, she was supposed to be the one who helped. What she
remembered now did not come as a story. It came as a series of disconnected shards: the weight of a
child’s body in her arms, the smell of diesel and burnt fabric, a woman’s hand gripping her wrist so
hard the nails broke skin. She could not arrange these pieces into a sequence. They did not fit
together. And sometimes, when she tried to hold them in her mind long enough to make sense of them,
she felt a kind of mental recoil—a slamming shut—as though her own brain refused to let her finish
the picture. She would find herself thinking about what she needed to buy at the grocery store, or
whether she had locked her car, and the memory would be gone, blocked before it could fully arrive.

The most disturbing part, the part that kept her awake in the hours before her night shifts, was how
unreal the whole thing felt. She knew she had been there. She had the scar on her forearm where a
piece of debris had caught her. She had the name of the child she had carried to the triage tent—a
boy named Daniel, seven years old, who had survived. But when she tried to hold the event in her
mind, it seemed so extreme, so outside the bounds of ordinary life, that she found herself thinking,
*This cannot have happened to me.* The thought did not feel like denial. It felt like a fact. And
that scared her more than the memories themselves, because if she could not trust that her own past
was real, what could she trust?

At work, Meera had become meticulous to the point of rigidity. She double-checked IV lines three
times. She arrived forty minutes early for every shift. She volunteered for the most chaotic
assignments—trauma bays, rapid responses—because in those moments she did not have to think. She
just acted. But the environment itself was becoming unbearable. The smell of antiseptic triggered
nothing. The sound of a cardiac monitor alarmed her into a state of hypervigilance that she could
not explain. She had begun to avoid the hospital cafeteria because the fluorescent lights buzzed at
a frequency that reminded her of the generator noise from that night, and she had stopped riding the
elevator alone because the enclosed space made her feel that the walls were about to buckle inward.

Her social life had narrowed to a thread. She had stopped seeing her friend Priya, the one who had
been with her at the festival, because every conversation circled back to that night and Meera could
not bear to hear it described aloud. She had stopped dating altogether. The thought of explaining
herself to a stranger—of having to say *something happened to me*—made her chest tighten until she
could not breathe. Her mother had noticed the distance. Meera told her she was tired. She was always
tired.

What finally brought her to the clinic was a panic attack in the supply closet at work, three weeks
ago. She had been reaching for a box of gauze when the fluorescent light above her flickered, and
suddenly she was not in the closet anymore—she was under the collapsed tent, the air thick with
smoke, and she could not find the child, she could not find the child—and then she was on the floor,
her hands over her ears, gasping. A colleague found her. The colleague was kind. Meera took a leave
of absence the next day.

She sat in the intake chair with her hands folded in her lap, her voice steady, her eyes fixed on a
point just past the therapist’s shoulder. She said she wanted to understand why she could not
remember the event properly. She said she wanted to stop feeling like a stranger to her own life.
But what she did not say—what she could not yet say—was that she was terrified of what she might
find if she let herself look directly at the memory. She was afraid it would swallow her whole. And
she was also afraid, in a quieter way, that if she did not look soon, she would spend the rest of
her life living in the shadow of something she could not quite believe had ever been real.
