the one who stayed calm, the one who could hold a scalpel steady while the world around him
descended into chaos. He had chosen combat medicine because he believed in the logic of it—that the
world, for all its brutality, was a place where a skilled hand and a clear mind could make a
difference. That belief was shattered in a single, fragmented moment he can no longer fully piece
together.

The event happened during a field hospital rotation near a forward operating base. A mass casualty
event had come in—a firefight that had turned into an ambush. Samuel remembers the roar of the rotor
blades, the shouting, the smell of blood and diesel. He remembers working on a young soldier, a
tourniquet, a frantic call for more pressure. Then, a deafening explosion that collapsed the tent
wall. He remembers nothing after that for what must have been minutes, only waking on his back, ears
ringing, the silence around him suddenly the loudest thing he had ever experienced. He had a broken
rib, a collapsed lung, and a memory that would no longer obey him.

The months that followed were a fog of disconnected images. He would be walking down a hospital
corridor and suddenly see a flash of a face, a gloved hand, a puddle of crimson on a white floor.

had happened in the seconds between the blast and the waking. The harder he tried to push through
the gaps, the more his mind seemed to slam a door shut, leaving him dizzy and gasping for air. He
learned to stop trying. He began to push the memory away before it could fully form, a reflex that
felt like survival but left him feeling hollow, as if the story of his own life had been ripped out
of a book.

breathlessness. A lingering effect of his lung injury meant that even climbing a flight of stairs
could leave him winded. And that feeling—the tightness, the inability to get air in—would instantly
transport him back to the moment he woke under the collapsed tent, convinced he was suffocating. He

inhale and exhale with a dread he could not explain.

called, the quiet of a break room between shifts—these moments felt more dangerous than any chaos.

fill every quiet moment with noise, wearing earbuds on his commute, leaving the television on all
night. Sleep, when it came, was shallow and interrupted.

What frightened Samuel most was not the memories themselves, but what they had done to him. He no
longer recognized the man in the mirror. The confident doctor who had once thrived under pressure
was gone, replaced by someone who jumped at a dropped bedpan and scanned every room for exits. His
belief that hard work and skill could impose order on the world had been replaced by a creeping

had to be on guard, he told himself, because no one else would be. But the guard was exhausting. It
made him irritable with colleagues, distant from friends. He stopped returning calls from his family

skin.

did not, but also because, deep down, he hoped that somewhere in the gaps of his memory, there might
still be a version of himself he could find again.
