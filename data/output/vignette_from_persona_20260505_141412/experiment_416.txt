the next moment would shatter was a constant, suffocating presence. She had come to believe, deep in
her bones, that she could not stop bad things from happening to her, that she was fundamentally
helpless in the face of a world that could turn violent at any second.

Decades later, at seventy-three, Clara worked as a QA engineer, a job that demanded precision,
predictability, and control. She had built a life around routines that felt safe. But the past had a
way of arriving unannounced. A colleague would walk up behind her desk too quickly, and her entire
body would lock before her mind had even registered the sound of footsteps. She would find herself
frozen, heart pounding, her hands gripping the edge of her chair as if bracing for a blow. The

entered, checking for exits, for the positions of men, for any sign of threat. She was superalert,
watchful, always on guard, and she had come to accept this as simply the cost of staying safe.

The memories themselves were strange, fragmented things. She could recall specific moments with a
photographic clarity—the pattern of the wallpaper, the angle of the afternoon light, the exact
position of her father’s hand as it rose. But when she tried to describe what had happened, the
words would catch in her throat. She knew what she had lived through, but she could not find
language for it, as if the part of her that could speak had been sealed off. And sometimes, in the

trusted, that the person who was supposed to love her had instead become the source of her terror.
