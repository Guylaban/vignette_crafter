Farid is a 78-year-old environmental scientist, a man who has spent his career reading the quiet
signatures of the natural world—soil composition, water tables, the slow creep of contaminants. He
is married, a father of three grown children, and has lived his entire adult life in the same sun-
scorched California valley where he was born to a Lebanese-American family. He came to therapy
reluctantly, at his wife’s urging, after a year of what she called “disappearing.” He is here now,
sitting in a chair with his hands pressed flat on his knees, and he struggles to tell his story. He
knows what happened—he knows it with a clarity that terrifies him—but when he tries to speak, the
words vanish. The memory is there, vivid and complete, but the language to hold it slips away like
water through a sieve. He has come to believe that if he cannot describe it, he cannot control it,
and if he cannot control it, it might still be happening.

The event occurred fourteen months ago. Farid was driving home from a field site, a routine survey
of groundwater contamination near an old agricultural well. It was late afternoon, the light long
and golden, and he was tired but content. He had solved a problem that day, traced a plume of
nitrates to its source, and he allowed himself a small, unfamiliar feeling: satisfaction. He felt
happy. He turned on the radio, hummed along to an old Fairuz song, and rolled down the window to let
the warm air hit his face. A few minutes later, he saw a man on the side of the road, a cyclist,
stopped and bent over his bike. Farid was not speeding. He was not distracted. But as he passed, the
man suddenly stepped backward into the lane to adjust something on his saddle, and there was no
time. The impact was a thud, a soft, terrible sound that Farid can still feel in his chest. He
stopped the car, ran back, and found the man on the asphalt, his leg bent at an angle that was not
an angle, his breath coming in short, wet, heavy gasps. Farid knelt beside him, and for what felt
like hours he listened to that sound—the ragged, desperate breathing of a man whose lung had been
punctured. He held the man’s hand until the ambulance came. He never learned his name. The man
survived, but with permanent injuries. Farid was not charged; it was ruled an accident. But he knows
that he was the one behind the wheel. He knows that he felt happy just before it happened.

In the months that followed, Farid’s world contracted. The first thing to go was his work. He had
always loved the solitude of field research, the long drives to remote sites, but now the car itself
felt like a trap. Every time he got behind the wheel, he would hear the thud again, feel the jolt
through the steering column. He stopped taking assignments that required driving. He stopped going
into the office. He told his supervisor he was retiring early, and he did it by email, because the
thought of saying the words aloud—of hearing himself describe why—made his throat close. His wife
noticed. She tried to hug him, to put a hand on his shoulder, and he would stiffen. Physical
affection, which had once been the bedrock of their marriage, now felt unbearable. When she came
close, he felt a surge of something like panic, a conviction that if he let himself feel safe, if he
let himself be held, the happiness would come back, and with it the certainty that he was about to
destroy someone else. He had come to believe that feeling good was dangerous. That relaxation was a
trap. That if he let his guard down, even for a moment, he would cause harm again.

The memory itself has a strange, flickering quality. When it comes—and it comes often, unbidden, in
the middle of a quiet evening or during a sleepless night—it feels as though he is back on that
road. The sun is the same sun. The air smells the same. He can feel the steering wheel in his hands,
and he knows that in a few seconds he will hear the thud, and he cannot stop it. The memory is so
extreme, so viscerally real, that at times he finds it hard to believe it actually happened to him.
It feels like a story he was told about someone else, a nightmare he once had, except the
consequences are real and permanent and his. He avoids thinking about it, but the triggers are
everywhere. The sound of heavy breathing—his wife’s after climbing stairs, a character in a movie
catching their breath—sends him right back to that roadside, kneeling in the gravel, listening to a
stranger die by inches. He has stopped watching television. He has stopped going to the gym. He has
stopped leaving the house except for essentials, and even then he goes at odd hours, when the
streets are empty.

What finally brought him to therapy was a moment of ordinary domestic life. His youngest daughter,
visiting from out of state, came up behind him and wrapped her arms around him in a hug. He froze.
Then he pushed her away, not hard, but firmly, and he saw the confusion and hurt in her face. She
asked him what was wrong, and he could not find the words. He knew what had happened to him. He knew
why he had reacted that way. But the language would not come. That night, his wife told him that he
was losing his family, that they did not understand, that they thought he was angry at them. He is
not angry. He is terrified. He is afraid that if he lets himself feel loved, he will feel happy, and
if he feels happy, he will hurt someone again. He is hoping, though he cannot quite say it, that
therapy might give him back the words. That it might help him believe that he is not still on that
road. That the accident is over, and that he is allowed to be held.
