Martha Amankwah is an 80-year-old married woman who has worked as a midwife for over five decades.
She came to therapy at the urging of her husband, who had grown increasingly worried about the way
she had changed since the accident. In her first session, she sat upright in the chair, her hands
folded tightly in her lap, and she spoke in a low, careful voice, as though she were still bracing
for something to go wrong. She had been in a car accident nine months earlier, on a rainy evening in
October, when a driver ran a red light and struck her car broadside at an intersection. The impact
crushed the driver’s side door, and Martha was trapped inside the vehicle for nearly twenty minutes
before emergency responders could cut her free. She remembers the sound of the sirens approaching,
then the terrible silence when they stopped, and the feeling of the metal pressing in around her,
the roof bowed low above her head, the door jammed so tightly against her hip that she could not
move even an inch. She remembers thinking, *this is it, this is how I die, locked inside this car.*

In the months that followed, Martha’s world began to narrow. She had always been a woman who moved
through life with a kind of steady competence, someone who could walk into a delivery room and calm
a panicking mother with a single glance. But after the accident, she found herself unable to step
into an elevator. The first time she tried, a few weeks after she was discharged from the hospital,
she stood in the lobby of her apartment building, watched the doors slide open, and felt her chest
seize. The space looked too small. The doors would close behind her, and she would be trapped. She
turned around and took the stairs, even though her hip still ached from the crash. She has not taken
an elevator since. At home, she began leaving doors open—the bathroom door, the bedroom door, even
the closet door. Her husband asked her why, and she said she needed to see that there was a way out.
She had come to believe, with a certainty that felt like logic, that if she were ever locked into a
room, something terrible would happen again.

The sirens were harder to avoid. Martha lives in a city, and ambulances pass her street several
times a day. Every time she hears one, her body reacts before her mind can catch up: her heart
pounds, her hands go cold, and she feels the old pressure of the crushed door against her ribs. She
has started keeping the television on at all times, turned loud enough to drown out the sounds from
outside. Her husband has complained that he cannot hear himself think, but Martha cannot bear the
quiet. In the quiet, she hears the sirens from that night, and then she sees the cars—the ones that
were hit, the ones that were crumpled and still. She sees them everywhere now. When she walks to the
grocery store, she flinches at every car that passes too close to the curb. She crosses the street
at intersections even when the light is green, because she no longer trusts that other drivers will
stop.

Sleep has become a battle. Martha lies awake night after night, her mind cycling through the same
images: the headlights, the impact, the metal pressing in. She gets up and walks through the house,
checking the locks on the doors, though she knows they are secure. She is always on guard. Ordinary
sounds make her jump—the clatter of a pot in the kitchen, the thud of a book dropped on the floor.
Her husband has stopped sneaking up on her playfully, the way he used to, because she reacts with
such fright that it upsets them both.

Martha has also begun to compare herself to others in a way she never did before. She finds herself
thinking that somebody else—somebody younger, somebody stronger, somebody less worn by decades of
work—would not have gotten into this situation in the first place. They would have seen the car
coming. They would have reacted faster. She tells herself that her body has been permanently damaged
by what happened, and she is not wrong about the hip, which still aches and sometimes gives way, but
the damage she means is something larger: a sense that the machinery of her own life has broken
down. She used to deliver babies, to hold new life in her hands. Now she cannot bear to be in a room
with the door closed. She says, flatly, that her life has been destroyed by the trauma. She does not
mean it as a metaphor. She means that the person she was before the accident—the capable,
unflappable midwife, the woman who could handle any emergency—no longer exists.

What finally brought Martha to seek help was a single moment. Her granddaughter, who is six, asked
her why she never comes to visit anymore. Martha had stopped driving, stopped taking the bus,
stopped going anywhere that required her to be in a vehicle or a confined space. She told her
granddaughter she was tired, but the lie sat heavily in her chest. That night, she told her husband
she was ready to talk to someone. She does not know if talking will change anything. She is afraid
that the person she has become is permanent. But she is also afraid of losing what little she has
left—her marriage, her family, the chance to hold her granddaughter on her lap without flinching at
every sound from the street.
