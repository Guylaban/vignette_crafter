Kwame is a sixty-five-year-old man who came to therapy at the urging of his wife, after months of
him sleeping in a chair by the front door of their apartment and refusing to go anywhere she could
not drive him. The trouble began three years ago, when a flash flood tore through his small coastal
town in the middle of the night. He had been working a late shift at the discount department store
where he stocks shelves and runs the register. The rain had been heavy all evening, but no one
expected the creek behind the strip mall to surge. Around two in the morning, water began seeping
under the loading dock door. Within minutes, it was ankle-deep, then knee-deep. Kwame and two
coworkers scrambled onto the steel shelving units in the back storeroom as the current grew strong
enough to topple pallets. He remembers the fluorescent lights flickering out, the sound of glass
breaking somewhere in the dark, and the cold water rising against his calves as he clung to a metal
beam, certain he would be swept away. They were rescued by emergency services just before the roof
partially collapsed, but for Kwame, that night never ended.

In the months that followed, the memory of the flood did not fade or soften. It played over and over
in his mind, a film he could not turn off. When he tried to recall the event, it did not feel like a
memory at all—it felt as though he was still standing on that shelving unit, the water still rising,
the panic still fresh in his chest. He would be at the grocery store, reaching for a can of soup,
and suddenly he was back there, his heart hammering, his hands gripping the cart as if it were a
metal beam. The feeling that it was happening right now, not something that had already passed,
terrified him more than anything. He had come to believe that the world was a profoundly dangerous
place, that disaster could strike at any moment without warning. He was certain he had been
permanently changed for the worse, that the man who had once been easygoing and unafraid was gone
forever.

To survive, Kwame developed a set of habits that made sense to him. He began to constantly scan his
environment for signs of threat, his eyes moving from ceiling to floor, checking for cracks in the
walls, listening for the sound of rushing water. As soon as he entered any room or building—the
break room at work, his own living room, his doctor’s waiting area—he scanned for exits. He made
sure to stay close to the exit at all times. At the department store, he positioned himself near the
front doors during his shifts, and if a customer asked for help in the back of the store, he felt a
spike of dread and often asked a coworker to go instead. He distracted himself with whatever was at
hand—counting inventory, focusing on the texture of a price tag, reciting the day’s schedule in his
head—anything to pull his mind away from the flood memory before it could fully take hold.

His sleep became a battleground. He had difficulty falling asleep because he needed to stay alert,
convinced that if he let his guard down, he would not hear the first signs of another flood. He
began sleeping in the armchair by the front door, where he could see the street through the window
and feel the doorknob against his hip. His wife, a retired nurse, tried to coax him back to bed, but
he could not explain why it felt impossible. He was on guard all the time, and the vigilance was
exhausting, but he was certain that if he stopped, something terrible would happen.

The consequences accumulated quietly. He stopped visiting his daughter, who lived across town,
because her apartment was on the ground floor. He no longer went to the community center where he
used to play chess. At work, his manager noticed he avoided the back storeroom entirely, and he had
to be reassigned to a register near the front entrance. His wife did most of the grocery shopping
now, because the supermarket’s layout—long aisles with no clear view of the doors—made him feel
trapped. What finally brought him to seek help was the morning he found himself standing in the
kitchen at three a.m., fully dressed, ready to leave the apartment because he had heard a faucet
drip and his body had responded as if the flood had returned. He knew then that something was
broken, and he was afraid it could not be fixed. He hoped, more than anything, that he might one day
feel safe enough to close his eyes and sleep beside his wife again.
