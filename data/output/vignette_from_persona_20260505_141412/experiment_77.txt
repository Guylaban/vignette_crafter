property, priding himself on anticipating problems before they arose. Nothing, however, had prepared
him for the morning a maintenance worker improperly mixed cleaning solvents in a basement storage
closet, releasing a cloud of acrid, chemical fumes that traveled up through the ventilation system.

guests while his own vision swam. He had not lost consciousness, but he had felt, for several
minutes, that he might die right there in the lobby, gasping for air that only made things worse.

In the weeks that followed, Richard noticed that his memory of that morning did not come as a
single, clear story. Instead, it arrived in fragments: the fluorescent lights flickering overhead,
the sound of a woman screaming somewhere behind him, the weight of a guest’s suitcase he had grabbed
without thinking. These pieces did not fit together in any logical order, and sometimes he would be
standing in his kitchen at home and suddenly feel the full, crushing terror of that moment—his heart
hammering, his chest tight—without any image or thought to explain why. What disturbed him most was
the smell. At odd times, with no warning, he would catch the faint odor of bleach and ammonia, as if
it were seeping through the walls of his own apartment, and he would freeze, certain that he was
about to be poisoned again.

Richard had come to believe that his body had betrayed him. He could no longer trust it to keep him
safe. His hands would tremble at the grocery store when a child dropped a jar on the floor, and his
lungs would seize up at the sound of a truck backfiring outside his window. He was certain, with a
conviction that felt unshakable, that his body kept reacting as if he were still in that basement
hallway, even when he was perfectly safe. He had begun to think of his own nervous system as an
enemy, something that would not listen to reason. And this terrified him more than the event itself,
because it meant that he could not stop bad things from happening to him. He felt helpless in a way
he had never felt before, not even during his divorce.

To manage this constant sense of threat, Richard had developed a set of habits that now governed his
daily life. He had not set foot in the hotel since the incident, and he had called in sick so many

agreed, but the absence of work left him with too much time to sit and monitor his own thoughts,
scanning them for signs that he was losing his mind. He would catch himself thinking, "That's a
crazy thought," and then spiral into worry that he was on the verge of a breakdown. At home, he kept
the television or a loud radio playing constantly, not for entertainment but to drown out the small,
unexpected sounds that made him jump—the creak of a floorboard, the hum of the refrigerator, the
distant siren that could have been an ambulance or could have been anything.

He had come to feel safe in only a very limited number of places: his own bedroom, with the door
closed and the windows locked, and a single chair in his living room that faced the front door.

footsteps in the hallway, for the sound of the building's ventilation system kicking on. He had
stopped cooking with any strong-smelling ingredients, because the scent of onions or garlic
sometimes triggered a wave of olfactory recall that left him shaking. He had stopped visiting his
daughter's house, because her apartment building had a faint, clean smell he could not identify, and

What finally brought Richard to seek help was a morning when he found himself standing in his own
bathroom, staring at the shower curtain, unable to remember how he had gotten there. He had been

else entirely, his heart racing, his hands cold, and no image in his mind at all. He realized then
that he was not getting better on his own. He was afraid of what might happen if he did not find a
way to stop this—afraid that he would lose his job, his apartment, his connection to his daughter.

and let it fully in. He hoped, more than anything, that someone could help him feel safe in his own
body again, though he did not know if that was possible.
