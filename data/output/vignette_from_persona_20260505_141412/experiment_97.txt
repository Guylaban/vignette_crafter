The day the world ended for Amir was a Tuesday in late autumn. He was on set, mid-scene, when his
phone buzzed in the dressing room. His wife, Leila, had taken their seven-year-old daughter, Samira,
to the park after school—a routine so ordinary it had never warranted a second thought. The call was
from a stranger, a paramedic. There had been an accident at the intersection near the playground. A
driver, later found to be under the influence, had run the light. Leila was gone before the
ambulance arrived. Samira had survived the initial impact but died two hours later in the operating
room. Amir had not been there. He had been on a soundstage, pretending to be someone else, while his
family was being erased from the world.

In the months that followed, Amir’s memory of that day did not play back like a story with a
beginning, middle, and end. Instead, it came to him in fragments—disconnected pieces that refused to
fit together. He would see Leila’s face in the morning, laughing over coffee, and then, without
warning, he would be looking at a photograph in his mind: the hospital corridor, the fluorescent
lights, the doctor’s mouth moving but no sound coming out. Another photograph: Samira’s small hand,
still and pale on the white sheet. He could not connect the woman who kissed him goodbye to the
woman who never came home. The images were vivid, static, and unbearable. And every time they
surfaced, he was flooded with a guilt so absolute it felt like a physical weight in his chest. He
would never forgive himself for not being there. He should have driven them himself. He should have
been the one in the car. He should have done more—anything more—to stop what happened.

Amir had always trusted the systems around him. He believed in traffic laws, in police, in
hospitals, in the basic competence of other people. That belief had been shattered. The driver had
been arrested, but within weeks the charges were reduced. There was a plea deal. No trial. No
accountability. Amir had sat in the courtroom and watched a man walk away with a sentence that felt
like a slap. The institutions that should have protected his family had failed. The people
responsible had faced no consequences. And Amir was left with the certainty that safety was a lie.
Even when friends or his therapist told him he was safe now, he found it impossible to believe. The
world was a place where things could fall apart without warning, and no one would be held to
account.

His work as an actor became impossible. He could not concentrate on scripts. He would read the same
line seven times and still not know what it said. His mind was always elsewhere, scanning for
danger, waiting for the next terrible thing. He began to avoid being alone. Being in a room by
himself felt unbearable—the silence pressed in, and the photographs in his mind came faster. So he
started going along with whatever anyone else wanted. If a colleague suggested dinner, he agreed. If
a friend wanted to meet at a crowded shopping centre, he went, even though the noise and the press
of bodies made his skin crawl. He hated crowds, but he hated being alone more. He would smile and
nod and say yes to everything, because saying no meant being left with himself, and being left with
himself meant the guilt and the images and the certainty that he had failed the people he loved
most.

The triggers were everywhere. A child crying in a supermarket would stop him cold, his chest
tightening, his vision narrowing. He would have to leave, but he could not explain why. Related
sounds—a car horn, a siren, the screech of brakes—could send him into a spiral for hours. He stopped
watching the news. He avoided movies with car crashes or hospital scenes. But the world was full of
sounds, and he could not control them.

What finally brought Amir to seek help was a simple thing: he could not be alone in his own home. He
had been staying with his brother for weeks, but his brother’s wife was growing tired of the
arrangement, and Amir could see it. He knew he was a burden. He knew he was pleasing everyone else
to avoid facing his own life. He wanted to go home. He wanted to sleep in his own bed. But the
thought of walking into that silent house, with its empty rooms and its photographs of Leila and
Samira, filled him with a terror he could not name. He was afraid that if he went back, he would
never leave again—not because he would hurt himself, but because the guilt would finally swallow him
whole. He came to therapy hoping to find a way to live in the same world that had taken everything
from him, though he was not sure such a thing was possible.
