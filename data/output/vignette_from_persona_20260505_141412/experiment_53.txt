Mai is a 71-year-old divorced woman of South Eastern Asian descent who works as a financial analyst.
She came to therapy at the urging of her adult daughter, who had grown worried by the changes she
had seen in her mother over the past three years. Mai did not argue with the referral, but she was
not sure what talking could do. What frightened her most was not the memory itself but the feeling
that she could no longer trust the world to operate the way it was supposed to.

The event that had shattered that trust occurred during a business trip to a regional office in
another state. Mai had been sent to audit a subsidiary, and on her second night there, she was
detained by two men who claimed to be security contractors for the company. They held her in a
windowless conference room for nearly eighteen hours, accusing her of corporate espionage,
threatening her with legal action, and refusing to let her leave or contact anyone. She was not
physically harmed, but she was not allowed to use the bathroom without an escort, and she was made
to sit in a hard chair facing a blank wall while the men argued on the phone with someone she never
saw. She was finally released when the corporate legal team intervened, but no one ever apologized.
The company offered a settlement in exchange for her silence, and when she refused to sign, her
position was restructured into a less meaningful role. She had come to believe that the institutions
that should have protected her—her employer, the local police who declined to investigate, the
corporate ethics office that took six months to close the case with no findings—had all let her down
in a coordinated, deliberate way.

In the months that followed, Mai found herself unable to stop thinking about how her life would have
been different if that trip had never happened. She would lie awake at night, long past midnight,
replaying the sequence of decisions that had led her to that conference room. If she had declined
the assignment. If she had insisted on a different hotel. If she had called her daughter before the
men arrived. She stayed up until two or three in the morning, sometimes later, because the moment
she tried to sleep, the images would press in: the beige wall, the fluorescent hum, the way the
men’s shoes squeaked on the linoleum. She tried hard not to think about it during the day, keeping
herself busy with spreadsheets and quarterly reports, but her concentration had frayed. She made
errors she had never made before, and her manager had begun to comment on her late submissions.

She had also developed a sharp, visceral sense that she was contaminated by what had happened. She
showered twice a day, sometimes three times, and she had stopped wearing the blazer she had worn on
that trip, even though it had been dry-cleaned. She felt dirty in a way that soap could not reach,
and she was certain that other people could sense it. At work, she kept her desk turned so that her
back was to the wall, and she had stopped eating lunch in the break room because the open space made
her feel exposed. She only felt safe in her own apartment, and even there, she checked the locks on
the windows three times before she could sit down. Night was worse. She found it harder to feel safe
after dark, and she kept a small flashlight on her nightstand even though she knew rationally that
no one was coming.

The consequences had accumulated quietly. She had stopped attending her weekly mahjong group because
it met in a community center with large windows and too many entrances. She had declined invitations
to her granddaughter’s school events because the crowds made her feel as though she might be
trapped. Her daughter had noticed the withdrawal and had asked, gently, whether Mai was depressed.
Mai did not think she was depressed. She thought she was broken in a different way. She could not
deal with even the slightest upset anymore—a missed bus, a delayed email, a loud noise in the
hallway—without feeling her heart race and her throat close. She had become a person who startled at
nothing, and she hated that.

What finally brought her to the clinic was a panic attack in the office elevator. The doors had
stuck for thirty seconds, and she had found herself unable to breathe, pounding on the metal,
convinced that she would be trapped again. Her supervisor had called her daughter. Now Mai sat in
the therapist’s office with her hands folded tightly in her lap, hoping that someone could help her
sleep through the night again, but afraid that the only real solution would be to stop needing to
trust anyone at all.
