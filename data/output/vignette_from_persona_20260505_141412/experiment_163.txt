Adisa is a 47-year-old architect, married for nearly twenty years, who came to therapy at her
husband’s urging after months of what she called “just not being myself.” She sat very still during
the first session, her hands folded in her lap, and spoke in a low, careful voice. She is a Black
woman of West African heritage, born in the United States to immigrant parents, and she has built a
career designing commercial spaces. She is precise, methodical, and used to being in control. That
control, she explained, had slipped away from her on a Tuesday afternoon about fourteen months
earlier.

She had been on a routine site visit to a partially completed office tower. The project was behind
schedule, and she had been distracted, running through a subcontractor’s change order in her head as
she walked toward an unguarded elevator shaft. She did not see the young electrician crouched near
the edge, working on a conduit. She did not hear him over the noise of a nearby saw. She stepped
forward, and her hard hat brushed a loose coil of cable. The coil snagged his ankle. He lost his
balance and fell. He fell two floors, landing on a concrete landing. He survived, but the fall
shattered his pelvis and crushed two vertebrae. He would never walk without assistance again. Adisa
remembers the sound of the impact—a wet, percussive crack—and the taste of dust and copper that
flooded her mouth as she screamed. She remembers the foreman’s radio crackling, and the high, thin
wail of the man’s voice before he lost consciousness. She remembers thinking, as clearly as if it
were spoken aloud, *I did this. I did this.*

In the weeks that followed, Adisa found she could not deliberately call up the sequence of events.
When she tried to reconstruct it for the safety investigation, her mind went blank, as if a curtain
had been drawn across the memory. But the memory came to her unbidden, and when it did, it arrived
through her senses. She would be sitting at her drafting table, and suddenly she could taste that
same metallic, dusty flavor, as if she had bitten her own cheek. She would be driving home, and the
sound of the radio—any radio—would dissolve into the foreman’s radio crackle, and she would hear the
man’s scream, clear and close, inside her head. These intrusions left her drenched in a cold sweat,
her heart hammering, convinced for a few terrible seconds that she was back on that site, that it
was happening again. Ordinary sounds—a car door slamming, a dropped tool in the kitchen—made her
jolt upright, her breath caught in her chest. She began to feel as though danger were everywhere,
even when nothing was wrong.

She could not concentrate. She would stare at blueprints for twenty minutes and realize she had read
the same dimension line seven times. She started making small errors in her calculations, errors she
had never made before. Her isolation grew. She became certain that other people—her colleagues, her
husband, even her own mother—could not possibly understand. *Somebody else would not have gotten
into this situation,* she told herself. A better architect, a more careful person, would have
paused, would have looked, would have seen the man. She began to feel set apart from everyone she
knew, as though she were living behind a pane of soundproof glass. The person she had become—jumpy,
forgetful, haunted—was unrecognizable to her. She did not know this woman.

To manage the unbearable weight of what she had done, Adisa developed private rituals. Every
morning, before she left the house, she performed a sequence of mental actions: she counted backward
from ten in three languages, then pictured a white wall, then repeated the phrase “no one will fall”
three times. She knew, on some level, that this was irrational, but the thought of skipping it
filled her with a dread she could not name. She also found herself, during quiet moments, mentally
replaying the accident and berating herself for every choice she had made that day. She would dwell
on what she had done wrong, turning it over and over like a stone. Occasionally, she tried to
comfort herself with the thought that the man had not died—*it could have been worse*—but the
comfort never lasted.

At work, she began to avoid the site. She delegated all field inspections to junior staff, citing a
full schedule. She stopped walking near any construction zone, even on the street. She took long
detours to avoid the sound of saws, the smell of concrete dust. Her career, once the center of her
identity, became a landscape of triggers. She came to believe that her own mind was untrustworthy,
that her attention was a dangerous thing.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment at home. Her husband asked her a simple question
about their weekend plans, and she began to cry, unable to answer. She told him she felt like a
stranger in her own life. She is afraid that she is broken beyond repair, that the person she has
become is permanent. But underneath that fear, barely audible, is a hope that someone might help her
find a way back—not to who she was before, but to someone she can stand to be.
