municipal work because she believed she could make a tangible difference. She was fifty-three then,

not the explosion itself, but the heavy, ragged breathing of a colleague who lay beside her in the
dark afterward, bleeding from shrapnel wounds. That breathing sound, wet and uneven, has never left
her. Neither has the image of the man’s face, lit briefly by a flare, his eyes wide and unseeing.
She sometimes thinks that memory cannot be hers. It feels like a photograph she was shown once,
belonging to someone else entirely, someone who was actually there. But then the images force their
way into her mind without warning — during a council meeting, while she is reviewing a budget line,
as she pours her morning coffee — and she knows they are hers, even as she wishes they were not.

breathing of a man on the bus, asthmatic and labored, makes her want to flee. She has learned to
avoid crowded places, though she cannot say why. She tells herself it is efficiency — she works
better from her office, with the door closed. But what she really fears is the moment a sound will
seize her, and she will be back in that compound, unable to breathe, unable to think. She has

watches them with a flat, hollow attention. This frightens her more than the intrusive images do.

of continuing as she is: a woman who once believed she could help others, now unable to help
