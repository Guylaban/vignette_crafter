Amara is a 25-year-old nurse who came to the United States from West Africa four years ago, and she
now lives with her partner in a quiet apartment near the hospital where she works. She arrived at
the clinic at the urging of her boyfriend, who had grown worried about the way she seemed to
disappear into herself for hours at a time, sitting blankly on the couch or staring at the
television without seeing it. Amara herself was not sure what she hoped for, only that she was
exhausted and frightened by the feeling that something inside her had broken and could not be fixed.

The event that changed everything happened two years ago, during a visit home to see her mother and
younger siblings in the city where she had grown up. A civil conflict had been simmering for months,
but Amara had believed the neighborhood where her family lived was safe. One afternoon, while she
was walking to the market with her older sister, gunfire erupted from two directions. They dropped
to the ground behind a parked car, and Amara watched a young man—someone she had known since
childhood, a neighbor’s son—fall a few meters away, his body jerking as bullets struck him. She
heard his mother screaming from a doorway. The shooting lasted perhaps ten minutes, but in Amara’s
memory it never ends. She and her sister crawled into a shop and hid until soldiers cleared the
street, but by then the neighbor’s son was dead, and Amara had seen his face, his open eyes, the
blood pooling on the asphalt.

When she returned to the United States, Amara told herself she was fine. She did not talk about what
she had seen. Her boyfriend asked once, gently, and she said she did not want to think about it, and
he did not press again. She found that if she kept moving—if she filled every moment with work, with
errands, with the small routines of her shift at the hospital—the memory stayed at a distance. But
it did not stay away. At unpredictable moments, the image of the neighbor’s son would flash before
her, and suddenly she was back on that street, the heat of the sun on her neck, the smell of exhaust
and dust, the sound of the shots so vivid that her body would jolt as though she had been struck.
These episodes left her trembling and disoriented, and she learned to recognize the signs—a racing
heart, a sense of dread—just before they came. When she felt one building, she would excuse herself
to the supply closet or the bathroom and sit with her eyes closed, willing herself to be somewhere
else. She had become skilled at drifting off into a world of her own, a kind of daydream where she
was safe and far away, but the relief never lasted long.

At work, Amara was competent and quiet, but her colleagues noticed that she avoided the emergency
department whenever possible. She had requested a transfer to a medical-surgical floor, telling her
supervisor she preferred the routine. The truth was that the ED reminded her too much of the chaos
and the blood and the sounds of people in distress. Even on her own floor, a patient crying out in
pain could send her heart racing, and she would have to steady herself against a wall. She had
stopped watching the news entirely, and she changed the channel whenever a war story or a report of
violence came on. She did not speak about the event with anyone, not even her boyfriend, because the
words felt impossible to form, and because she feared that if she started talking, she would fall
apart completely.

What haunted Amara most, though, was not the memory itself but what she believed it said about her.
She could not shake the feeling that she had been permanently humiliated by what happened—humiliated
by her own fear, by the way she had frozen on the ground, by the fact that she had done nothing to
help the neighbor’s son. She had been a nurse, trained to save lives, and she had lain there useless
while someone died. This thought circled back on itself until she concluded that she was simply
inadequate, that there was something fundamentally wrong with her as a person. Other people survived
terrible things and moved on; she could not even think about it without feeling her chest tighten.
She began to believe that the defect was inside her, that she was broken in a way that could not be
repaired.

The consequences of her avoidance accumulated quietly. She stopped visiting her family, making
excuses about work and money. She pulled away from her boyfriend, not because she was angry at him,
but because being close to anyone felt unsafe, as though her shame might be visible. She had come to
believe that if he really knew what she had done—or failed to do—he would see her the way she saw
herself.

What finally brought Amara to the clinic was a panic attack that occurred in the middle of a routine
shift. A patient’s family member began shouting in the hallway, and for a moment Amara was certain
she heard gunfire. She dropped to the floor, covering her head, and could not be calmed for nearly
twenty minutes. Her supervisor told her she needed to take leave and get help. Amara agreed, but
what she is most afraid of is not the panic itself. It is the possibility that talking about what
happened will confirm what she already suspects: that she is too damaged to be fixed, and that the
person she was before that afternoon on the market street is gone forever.
