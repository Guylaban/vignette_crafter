samples in a bright, quiet laboratory. She had moved from West Africa to the United States for
graduate school two years earlier, and she had built a life around precision and order. That was

first time. He was nineteen, and she had been so proud to show him her world. One evening, after a
long day of fieldwork, she had asked him to pick up dinner from a place a few blocks away. He never
came back. He was struck by a driver who ran a red light. Adama was the one who had to identify his
body. She had stood in the morgue, unable to speak, her mouth open but no sound coming out. That was
the moment that kept replaying, except she could never get to the end of it. Every time she tried to
bring the memory into focus, she would push it away before it fully arrived, as if her mind slammed
a door shut. She knew what had happened, but when her therapist asked her to describe it, she sat in
silence, the words dissolving on her tongue. The memory did not feel like the past. It felt like it
was happening right now, in the room, in her chest, and she would flinch as though the fluorescent
lights of the lab were the harsh lights of the morgue.

In the months that followed, Adama began to live in a state of constant, low-grade alarm. She could
not sleep. She would lie awake for hours, her mind circling the same question: why had she sent him
out that night? She told herself that somebody else—a better sister, a more careful person—would
have stopped it. She had come to believe that the event happened because of the sort of person she
was: someone who did not protect the people she loved. The injustice of it burned in her. Nothing
had been done. The driver had been charged, but the case had stalled, and the world kept moving as
if Kofi had never existed. That was when she started picking fights. She would snap at a coworker
for leaving a beaker unwashed. She would argue with her roommate about dishes until they both were
shouting. She knew, even as she did it, that she was creating drama to distract herself from the
weight she carried, but the weight was worse. The silence was worse.

At work, she began to notice small delays that had never bothered her before. When a sample result
took an hour longer than expected, her heart would race and she would assume something catastrophic
had happened—a spill, a contamination, a disaster. Her body reacted before her mind had time to
catch up. She would find herself gripping the edge of her desk, breathing fast, before she even knew
why. She stopped watching television or reading the news. Any story about a sudden death, any film
with a character who lost someone, sent her into a spiral of intrusive images. She could not bear
it. She avoided those programs the way she avoided the street where Kofi had died, taking a longer
route home even though it added twenty minutes to her commute.

The consequences had accumulated quietly. Her relationships frayed. Her roommate asked to move out.

Adama to seek help was not the fights or the sleeplessness, but a moment in the lab when a colleague
asked her a simple question about a data set and she could not find the words to answer. She stood
there, mouth open, the same paralysis she had felt in the morgue, and she realized she was losing

able to control. She hoped, more than anything, that someone could help her find a way to tell the
