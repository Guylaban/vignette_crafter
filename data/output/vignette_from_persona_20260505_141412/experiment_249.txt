the taste of metal in her mouth, the sight of a single white sneaker floating in brown water, the
feeling of someone’s elbow pressing into her ribs. These pieces do not connect, and when they
surface—always without warning, always when she is least prepared—they arrive with such force that
Mai has to stop whatever she is doing. She will be standing at the fry station, the oil hissing, and
suddenly she is back in that cold dark, and the emotion hits her chest like a physical blow. She has
to brace herself against the counter, breathing hard, until the manager touches her shoulder and she
snaps back.

It has been two years, and Mai has come to believe that the person she was before the storm is gone
forever. She says she will never feel normal emotions again, and by this she means the quiet
ones—the contentment of a slow afternoon, the mild irritation of a long line at the grocery store,
the simple pleasure of watching her husband tend the garden. What she feels now is a constant, low-
level dread, punctuated by spikes of terror. She has also become certain that she no longer belongs
anywhere. Not in her own home, not at work, not among the people who knew her before. She looks at

glass. She cannot explain it, but she knows, with a certainty that feels like fact, that she has
been set apart.

cannot. Night has become a problem. She lies awake for hours, listening to the creak of the house,

a child shouting in the street—makes her flinch and go rigid. At night, these sounds feel worse. The
darkness itself feels dangerous. She has started sleeping on the couch in the living room, with
every light on, the television playing a cooking show at low volume, because silence is worse. Her
husband has stopped asking when she will come back to bed. He sleeps alone now, and Mai knows he is
hurt, but she cannot explain to him why the bed feels like a trap, why the dark feels like a lid
closing.

the freezer door, the sudden clatter of a dropped tray. Each of these sounds makes Mai’s heart race.

about because she cannot hear the drive-thru orders. She has been written up twice. What frightens
her most is the feeling that she cannot stop bad things from happening. She used to believe she had
some control—that if she was careful, if she planned, she could keep herself and her family safe.
That belief is gone. The storm proved that safety is an illusion, and now every loud noise, every
dark room, every unexpected shift in the air feels like the beginning of the next disaster.

Mai came to therapy at her husband’s insistence, but also because she is exhausted. She is tired of
being afraid, tired of sleeping on the couch, tired of the way her body braces for impact even when
nothing is happening. She is afraid that this is all she will ever feel. What she hopes, though she
cannot quite say it out loud, is that someone will tell her how to find her way back to the person
she used to be—or, if that is impossible, how to live with the person she has become.
