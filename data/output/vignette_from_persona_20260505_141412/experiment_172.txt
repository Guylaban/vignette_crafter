Moshe is a 63-year-old rabbi who has led a small congregation in a Midwestern suburb for nearly two
decades, but the man who stands at the bimah each Shabbat is not the same man who arrived in America
thirty years ago. The event that reshaped his life happened in the winter of 1993, when he was still
living in a city that had once felt like home. Armed men had come to his neighborhood in the middle
of the night, shouting orders in a language that turned every familiar street into a trap. Moshe had
been hiding in a crawlspace beneath a neighbor’s floorboards, his wife’s hand clamped over her own
mouth, when a booted foot landed inches from his face and a rifle butt slammed through the planks
above him. The shouting had been what broke something inside him—not the gunfire, not the
splintering wood, but the sound of men’s voices raised in violent command, the certainty that they
could find him if they wanted to. He had stayed pressed to the cold earth for eleven hours, telling
himself that if he made a sound, they would kill everyone. What he remembers most now is the feeling
of his own body betraying him—his heart hammering so hard he was certain the men could hear it, his
hands trembling against the dirt, his stomach clenching as if his own guts were trying to escape. He
had not stopped the event from happening. Somebody else would have. Somebody braver, somebody
faster, somebody who had not frozen like a child while armed men tore through his home.

In the years since, Moshe has built a life that looks stable from the outside. He has a partner who
loves him, a congregation that respects him, a study lined with books that have always been his
refuge. But the body remembers what the mind tries to bury. When a congregant touches his shoulder
unexpectedly after a service—a gesture of warmth, of gratitude—Moshe’s entire frame goes rigid. His
breath catches. His skin crawls as if the hand is a blade. He has learned to smile and nod and step
away, but the feeling lingers for hours, a low thrum of alarm that tells him he is not safe. He has
also learned to scan every room before he enters it. He chooses his seat in the sanctuary based on
how protected he feels—back to the wall, exit visible, no one sitting behind him. His partner has
noticed that he will not sit in the middle of a row at restaurants or in movie theaters. He will not
sit with his back to a door. There are only a handful of places where the vigilance quiets: his
study, his bedroom, the small garden behind the synagogue. Everywhere else, the threat monitor runs
in the background, draining his ability to concentrate on sermons, on conversations, on the simple
pleasure of a meal.

The strategies Moshe has developed to manage his distress have become their own kind of prison. He
distracts himself constantly—podcasts during his commute, audiobooks while he gardens, the
television on low in the study even when he is trying to read. Silence is unbearable because silence
is what he heard in that crawlspace, and silence is where the shouting comes back. But distraction
only works for so long. When he finds himself alone with his thoughts, the self-blame rushes in. He
tells himself he deserves to suffer because of what happened. He should have done something. He
should have fought. He should have protected the others. A better man would have. A rabbi, of all
people, should have been a source of strength, and instead he was a trembling body in the dark.
These thoughts have hollowed out his capacity for intimacy. He has pulled away from his partner over
the years, not through anger but through a quiet, persistent distance. Emotional closeness feels
dangerous—it requires vulnerability, and vulnerability is what nearly got him killed. He does not
say this aloud. He does not know how. He simply stops sharing what he feels, stops asking for
comfort, stops letting anyone see the parts of himself he has come to despise.

What finally brought Moshe to seek help was a moment of failure that he could not explain away.
During a memorial service for a survivor of another conflict, a member of the congregation raised
his voice in grief—not shouting, not angrily, but loud enough, sharp enough—and Moshe felt his mind
go blank. He lost the thread of his sermon. He stood at the lectern with his hands shaking, unable
to form a sentence, while fifty faces looked up at him with trust he no longer believed he deserved.
That night, he told his partner he needed to talk to someone. He is terrified that therapy will
force him to go back into that crawlspace. He is equally terrified that if he does not, the space
inside him will keep shrinking until there is nothing left but the memory of a boot and a shout and
a man who could not save anyone.
