Mariama was seventy-two years old when she finally walked into a therapist’s office, though she had
carried the weight of that decision for nearly a decade. She was a forester by trade, a profession
she had taken up late in life after retiring from a university teaching post, and she loved the
quiet of the woods—or she had loved it, once. Now, even the rustle of leaves could make her stop
dead, her breath caught in her throat, her eyes fixed on the space between the trees where she was
certain someone might be watching.

The event that had reshaped her world happened thirty years ago, in her home country in West Africa,
during a period of civil conflict. She had been a civilian, a mother and a professor, living in a
small city that fell under siege. One night, an explosion tore through the neighborhood—a mortar
round that landed two houses down from her own. She remembered the sound not as a bang but as a
physical force that threw her against a wall, that left her ears ringing for days. In the aftermath,
she had crawled through the dark, over rubble and dust, searching for her neighbor’s children. She
never found them. What she found instead was a group of armed men moving through the street, their
flashlights sweeping the darkness, their voices low and urgent. She had pressed herself into a
doorway, certain that if they saw her, they would kill her. They passed. She survived. But something
in her had never stopped scanning that darkness.

In America, where she had moved to join her late husband’s family, the war was over and far away.
But the night was not. Every evening, as the sun went down, Mariama felt a familiar tightening in
her chest. She had come to believe that darkness itself was dangerous—that it concealed people who
meant her harm. She could not sleep without the television on, volume turned high, not for company
but for cover. The noise was a kind of armor; it drowned out the creaks of the house, the distant
car doors, the wind against the window. Without it, she was certain she would hear footsteps.
Without it, she would hear the bang.

Her work as a forester was supposed to be peaceful—a second career she had chosen precisely because
it offered solitude and order. But the forest had become a place of constant vigilance. She scanned
the tree line, the underbrush, the paths ahead, not for deer or for fallen branches but for the
shape of a person who did not belong. She watched the way other hikers moved: too quickly, too
slowly, too close to the trail. She had developed a habit of checking behind her every few seconds,
a small turn of the head that she knew made her look nervous, but she could not stop. She was
vigilant for dangerous people, and she had come to believe that dangerous people were
everywhere—that they looked ordinary, that they could be anyone. A man adjusting his backpack, a
woman walking a dog, a teenager on a phone: any of them could be a threat. She had no evidence for
this, but the feeling was as real as the pine needles beneath her boots.

Her colleagues had noticed. They thought she was anxious, high-strung, perhaps grieving her husband,
who had died three years earlier. They did not know that she could not sit with her back to the door
in the break room, that she chose a seat against the wall every time. They did not know that she
drove an extra twenty minutes home to avoid a street where a construction crew had been setting off
small blasts, because the sound—even muffled, even distant—sent her heart into her throat and her
hands shaking on the wheel. The avoidance had become a web of small decisions, each one reasonable
on its own, each one narrowing her life. She no longer went to the grocery store after dark. She no
longer visited friends who lived in neighborhoods where she did not know the streets. She had
stopped attending the community choir she had loved, because the rehearsal ran until eight in the
evening, and walking to her car in the dark had become impossible.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment at work. She had been marking trees for a
controlled burn, alone in a section of the forest, when a branch snapped behind her. She spun
around, heart pounding, and saw nothing. But she had been so certain—so absolutely certain—that
someone was there, that she had run. She ran through the woods like a woman being chased, her tools
clattering in her pack, until she reached her truck and locked the doors and sat there shaking. She
could not explain it. She knew, logically, that no one was there. But the fear had been absolute.
And she had realized, sitting in that truck, that she could not keep living this way—that she was
seventy-two years old, and she was still running from men who had passed her by thirty years ago.

She came to therapy hoping, she said, to stop being afraid of the dark. But what she really wanted,
what she could barely bring herself to say, was to stop feeling like she was still hiding in that
doorway, waiting to be found.
