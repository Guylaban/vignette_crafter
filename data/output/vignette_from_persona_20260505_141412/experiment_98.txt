Vincent is a fifty-eight-year-old hairdresser who has been married for thirty-two years. He came to
therapy at his wife’s urging, though he is not entirely sure what talking can do now. The event that
changed everything happened eighteen months ago, outside the barbershop where he had worked for two
decades. He was closing up late on a Thursday evening, a cool September night, when a man stepped
out from beside the dumpster and pressed something hard into Vincent’s ribs. The man smelled sharply
of alcohol, and that smell—cheap whiskey layered over sweat—is now a scent Vincent cannot separate
from the cold pressure of what he believed was a gun against his body. The man demanded his wallet
and his phone. Vincent handed them over without a word, his hands shaking so badly he dropped the
wallet twice. The man ran off into the alley, and Vincent stood there for what felt like a long
time, unable to move, unable to speak, his heart pounding so hard he thought he might be having a
heart attack.

In the months that followed, Vincent found that the event would not stay in the past. It came back
to him in vivid pictures—the glint of the streetlight on the man’s eyes, the brick wall beside the
dumpster, the angle of the man’s hand. These images arrived without warning, like a film that played
over and over, always the same sequence, always ending with that moment of frozen silence. He knew
exactly what had happened, could see it clearly, yet when his wife asked him to describe the
robbery, he found himself unable to form the words. The images were there, sharp and relentless, but
the sentences to explain them would not come.

Vincent began to avoid people. At first it was subtle—he stopped walking to the corner store for
lunch, started eating alone in the back of the shop. Then he began turning down invitations from his
colleagues, stopped attending the monthly staff meetings that had once been a source of camaraderie.
He told himself it was because people acted suspiciously now. He had come to believe that anyone who
moved too quickly, anyone who looked at him twice, anyone who stood too close—they were all
potential threats. He found himself scanning every customer who came into the salon, watching their
hands, their eyes, the way they stood. What frightened him most was that he could not tell the
difference between a genuine threat and an ordinary person anymore. Everyone seemed dangerous.

The avoidance brought consequences. His regular clients began to notice that he was distracted, that
he flinched when they touched his shoulder, that he seemed to be somewhere else. One longtime
client, Mrs. Delgado, corrected him gently when he started shampooing her hair before cutting it,
and Vincent felt a surge of something he could not name—shame, perhaps, or anger. He began to dwell
on what he had done wrong. He replayed the night of the robbery and asked himself why he had not
fought back, why he had not run, why he had not noticed the man sooner. He applied the same scrutiny
to every interaction: when a customer asked him to redo a trim, he spent hours afterward turning
over what he had done to deserve the criticism. He was certain that he was missing something, that
people could see his weakness, that he was failing in ways he could not fully grasp.

He tried hard not to think about the robbery. He told himself it was over, that he was fine, that he
needed to move on. But the harder he pushed the memories away, the more forcefully they
returned—those same images, that same film, playing at the worst possible moments. When a young man
came into the shop smelling of beer, Vincent’s hands went cold and he had to excuse himself to the
back room. He stood there, gripping the counter, trying to breathe, knowing that the smell was just
a smell, that the customer was just a customer, but unable to make his body believe it.

What finally brought Vincent to therapy was a moment in the salon last week. A new client, a woman
in her thirties, asked him a simple question about the price of a haircut, and Vincent found himself
unable to answer. His mouth opened but no words came out. He stood there, frozen, the image of the
robber’s hand pressing into his ribs flashing behind his eyes. The woman looked at him with concern,
and Vincent had to walk away. He went home early that day and told his wife that he could not keep
doing this. He is afraid that he is losing his ability to work, to speak, to be himself. What he
hopes for, though he cannot quite say it, is that someone might help him find the words that have
been lost to him.
