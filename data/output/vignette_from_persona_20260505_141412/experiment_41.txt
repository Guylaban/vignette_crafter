Kwame is a 37-year-old psychologist who came to therapy at the urging of a colleague who noticed he
had stopped taking new clients who reported sexual trauma, and had begun locking his office door
only from the inside, checking it repeatedly during sessions. He is single, lives alone, and works
in a community mental health clinic. The event that changed everything happened three years ago,
when he was still in graduate school. A man he had been dating for several months invited him to his
apartment. Once inside, the man locked the door, put the key in his pocket, and raped him. Kwame had
trusted him. He had no way out. He remembers the sound of the lock clicking, the weight of the key
in the other man’s pocket, the feeling of the walls closing in. He did not report it. He told no one
for over a year.

In the months that followed, Kwame found himself unable to sleep in his own bedroom unless he had
propped the door open with a shoe. He would lie awake, listening, certain that he needed to stay
alert. Even when he was exhausted, his body would not let go. He began to sleep on the couch in his
living room, where he could see the front door and the windows at the same time. He started
positioning himself in restaurants and coffee shops with his back to the wall, always facing the
entrance. At work, he found himself scanning the hallway before walking to the bathroom, and he
would only sit in the staff break room if he could see both doors. He became unable to relax, even
in settings he knew were safe — his own apartment, a friend’s house, his office after hours. His
body stayed tight, waiting.

He began to avoid certain things without fully deciding to. He stopped going to the gym because the
locker room had a heavy door that closed automatically. He turned down invitations to parties held
in basement apartments or windowless rooms. He started leaving social gatherings early, always
before he felt trapped. At the clinic where he worked, he began to feel uneasy when male colleagues
walked into his office and closed the door behind them. He started leaving the door ajar during
supervision, and eventually asked to be moved to a shared office space where the door was always
open. His supervisor noticed and asked if something was wrong. He said he was fine.

What frightened Kwame most was not the memories themselves, but what he had come to believe about
himself because of them. He was certain that others could see what had happened to him just by
looking at him. When a client mentioned a news story about sexual violence, he felt the comment land
on his skin like a spotlight. He began to dread sessions where trauma came up, not because he lacked
clinical skill, but because he was terrified that his own face would betray him, that the client
would see the victim in him and he would lose all authority. He started to feel that he had no
future. He could not imagine a relationship, a promotion, a life beyond this room. He became
convinced that he could not stop bad things from happening to him, that his own judgment was broken,
that he had failed to protect himself and would fail again.

Over time, his avoidance narrowed his world. He stopped dating entirely. He stopped visiting friends
who lived in apartments with lockable doors. He began to work only with clients he considered “safe”
— those with anxiety or depression, never trauma. His caseload shrank. His colleagues began to
worry. One of them, a woman he had trained with, finally sat him down and said she had seen this
before. She asked him directly if something had happened to him. He said no. But that night, he
could not sleep at all. He sat with his back to the wall in his living room, the front door in
sight, and he thought about the key in the other man’s pocket. The next morning, he called the
clinic’s employee assistance program and asked for a therapist.

Now, sitting in the intake session, Kwame is terrified. He is afraid that if he tells the full
story, he will fall apart and never be able to work again. He is afraid that his colleagues will see
him differently. But he is also, for the first time, hoping that someone will understand why he
cannot sleep with the door closed, why he cannot be in a room with a man he does not know, why he
feels that everyone who looks at him already knows. He is hoping that there is a way to stop living
with his back to the wall.
