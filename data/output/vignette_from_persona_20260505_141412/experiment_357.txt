medicine. She had always believed in the fundamental order of things: that hard work earned safety,

destroyed on a Tuesday evening, six months ago, as she walked from the hospital parking garage to
her car after a double shift.

intimacy of his body pressed against hers. He had not spoken. That was what she remembered most
vividly: the absence of any human sound. The silence itself had become the most terrifying part,
because in that silence she had understood that he could do anything, and no one would hear. She had
fought, and she had escaped with bruises and a fractured wrist, but something else had been broken
that she could not name.

Now, months later, the world had become a place of hidden dangers. Dr. Tran found herself scanning
every street corner, every parked car, with a vigilance that exhausted her. The silence that had
once been neutral was now a threat. When her husband turned off the television and the house went
quiet, her heart would begin to race before she could even identify why. Unexpected sounds—a car
backfiring, a dropped tray in the hospital cafeteria—sent a jolt through her chest that left her
trembling for minutes afterward. Strangers approaching her in the grocery store aisle made her step
backward, her hand instinctively reaching for her keys. She knew this was irrational; she was a
doctor, she understood the statistics, she knew the probability of being attacked again was low. But
knowing did not stop the terror.

The memory of the event itself had taken on a strange, fragmented quality. When she tried to recall
it, she did not see a coherent story but a series of snapshots: the concrete floor of the garage,
the fabric of his sleeve, and always, always the smell. The smell would come to her unbidden—in the
hospital hallway, in her own kitchen—and suddenly she would be back in that moment, her heart
slamming, her throat tight. The terror was as fresh as it had been that evening, and yet the memory
felt unreal, as if it had happened to someone else. She would think, *This cannot have happened to
me*, even as her body re-lived it.

them that. She had stopped wearing the fitted blouses and tailored jackets she had once favored,

night, when her husband was asleep. It was not about the money. It was about the focus. When she was
in a hand, calculating odds, watching the virtual cards fall, she did not think about the garage.

drown out the hypervigilance.

approximate age. She would ask a colleague to take over a case, offering a vague excuse about

said she was fine.

she could not continue as she was.
