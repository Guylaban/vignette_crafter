At 44, Evelyn had spent nearly two decades walking into hospital rooms where families were falling
apart, sitting beside the dying, holding space for the unspeakable. She had never doubted her
calling. Then, one Tuesday afternoon in late October, she was making her way across a crowded city
plaza to visit a parishioner when the world turned inside out. A bomb hidden in a municipal trash
can detonated as she passed within thirty feet of it. The blast threw her against a concrete
planter. For a long, ragged moment there was only pressure and heat and a ringing so total it seemed
to swallow every other sense. When she tried to stand, her legs would not obey. She could not find
the sky. People were screaming, but the sounds came to her in fragments, out of order, as if someone
had scrambled the tape. She remembers the smell of burnt metal and the sight of a woman’s shoe lying
on its side, still laced, with no foot inside it. She does not remember how she got to the hospital.
She does not remember who pulled her out. The memories that remain do not play in sequence; they
arrive as complete, vivid scenes that feel more real than anything that has happened since. She can
taste the dust in her mouth. She can feel the heat on her face. And she cannot, no matter how hard
she tries, put the pieces in the right order.

In the weeks that followed, Evelyn returned to work because she believed that was what chaplains
did. But something fundamental had shifted. She had always trusted that the institutions she
served—the hospital, the city, the systems of public safety—would hold. Now she was certain they had
not held her. They had not held anyone. The people who should have protected her had let her down,
and she could not unsee that truth. The world, she had come to believe, was a dangerous place, and
she was a lousy coper. She could not manage her own fear. She could not eat properly; meals became
an afterthought, then a burden. She would go entire shifts on nothing but coffee, telling herself
she just was not hungry, though in truth the act of eating felt like a luxury she had not earned.
She began to notice that being around people in authority—security guards, police officers, even
senior hospital administrators—made her skin prickle. She found ways to avoid them. She took the
long hallway to the chapel. She scheduled her visits when she knew the hospital’s safety director
would be in meetings.

In the chapel itself, a space that had once been her sanctuary, she could not sit still unless she
had chosen the seat herself. She always picked the chair with its back to the wall, near the fire
exit, where she could see both doors. If someone came in too quietly, she would watch them until
they sat down, cataloguing their hands, their coat pockets, the set of their jaw. Unexpected
sounds—a door slamming, a metal cart clattering, a loud laugh in the corridor—sent her heart into
her throat. She began to perform small mental rituals before every task: counting the syllables in a
prayer three times, tracing the shape of a cross in her mind before she could enter a patient’s
room. She knew, on some level, that these rituals did not prevent anything. But she was terrified
that if she stopped, something terrible would happen again.

The most confusing part, the part she could not explain to anyone, was what happened to her when she
felt something good. A moment of genuine warmth with a patient, a kind word from a colleague, the
sight of a child laughing in the waiting room—these things should have felt like relief. Instead,
they brought a wave of disorientation so intense that she had to excuse herself. Positive emotions
felt dangerous, as if they had been contaminated. She did not trust them. She did not trust herself
to feel them.

What finally brought Evelyn to seek help was a morning in the chapel when a hospital security guard
walked past the open door. He was a man she had known for years, someone who had always been kind.
But when she saw his uniform, the shape of his radio on his belt, her mind went blank with panic.
She could not breathe. She could not pray. She sat in her safe chair, gripping the armrests, and
understood that she could no longer do her job. She was afraid of what would happen if she kept
going. She was also afraid of what would happen if she stopped. She was hoping, she told the
therapist in her first session, that someone could help her find a way to live in a world that no
longer felt safe—and to forgive herself for not being strong enough to do it alone.
