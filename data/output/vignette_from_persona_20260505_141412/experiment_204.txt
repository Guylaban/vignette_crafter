Kwame is a 46-year-old married man who works as a curriculum designer for a large urban school
district. He came to therapy reluctantly, at his wife’s urging, after months of what she called
“living like a soldier in a war zone.” Kwame himself would not have used that language. He would
have said that he was simply paying attention, that the world required it.

The event that changed everything happened eighteen months ago, on a cool October evening. Kwame was
driving home from a late meeting at the district office, taking his usual route through a
residential neighborhood. He noticed a police cruiser behind him, then the flashing lights. He
pulled over immediately, hands on the wheel at ten and two, exactly as he had always done. The
officer approached, asked for his license and registration. Kwame complied. What happened next, he
still cannot fully describe. The officer’s voice sharpened. Kwame was ordered out of the car, then
onto the ground. He remembers the gravel pressing into his cheek, the weight of a knee on his back,
the cold metal of handcuffs. He remembers the officer saying something about a description matching
a suspect. He remembers the utter certainty, in that moment, that he might not survive this
encounter. The mistake was realized after twenty minutes. The officer helped him up, apologized,
said it was routine. Kwame drove home in silence. He did not tell his wife that night. He did not
tell anyone.

In the weeks that followed, Kwame found himself unable to stop scanning. Driving to work, he checked
his rearview mirror constantly, noting every police cruiser within sight. If one appeared behind
him, his heart would slam against his ribs, his palms would sweat, and he would pull over at the
next opportunity even if he had not been signaled. He began taking longer routes that avoided the
neighborhood where he had been stopped. Then he started leaving the house earlier to avoid traffic
altogether, reasoning that fewer cars meant fewer chances of being pulled over. His colleagues
noticed he was always the first to arrive and the last to leave. He told them he was being
productive. In truth, he was trying to control a world that felt increasingly dangerous.

The memories of that night did not come as a story he could tell. When his wife asked what had
happened, he found himself unable to produce words that felt adequate. He would say “I was stopped
by the police” and then stop, the sentence hanging unfinished, because the rest of it—the terror,
the helplessness, the grinding certainty that his life could end over a mistake—felt impossible to
translate into language. Sometimes, without any warning, he would be seized by the full emotional
force of that night: the pressure on his back, the officer’s voice, the cold. He would be sitting at
his desk reviewing a lesson plan, and suddenly his chest would tighten, his breath would come
shallow, and he would have to excuse himself to the bathroom, where he would stand with his hands on
the sink, waiting for the feeling to pass. There was no image with it, no clear picture in his
mind—just the raw, overwhelming sense that he was in danger, that something terrible was about to
happen.

His wife noticed other changes. He flinched when she touched him unexpectedly from behind. He could
not sit with his back to the door in a restaurant. He stopped going to the grocery store after dark.
He stopped going anywhere after dark. His social life contracted. He had always been a calm, steady
presence; now he was watchful, easily startled, quick to interpret a stranger’s glance as a threat.
At work, he found himself unable to concentrate on the detailed curriculum revisions that had once
absorbed him. He was too busy scanning, too busy preparing for the next threat.

What finally brought him to therapy was a moment at a red light. A police cruiser pulled up beside
him. Kwame’s hands began to shake. His vision narrowed. He was certain, for a few seconds, that the
officer was going to get out, that he was going to be pulled from his car again. He did not move
when the light turned green. The driver behind him honked. Kwame drove away, but he was trembling
for the rest of the day. That night, he told his wife he needed help. He is afraid of what will
happen if he does not get it—afraid that he will lose his job, his marriage, himself. But what he is
most afraid of, what he cannot say out loud, is that the world he once moved through with ease is
now a place of constant threat, and he does not know how to find his way back.
