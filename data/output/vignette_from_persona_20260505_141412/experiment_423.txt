Before it happened, Lena had loved the feeling of being on her bicycle. She lived in a midsized city
with decent bike lanes, and she commuted to the graphic design studio where she worked, enjoying the
rhythm of the ride. The trouble started when a client from a past freelance project began leaving
notes on her bike. At first, the notes were complimentary—he liked her work, he had seen her riding.
Then they became more personal. He knew her route. He knew her schedule. He began appearing on the
sidewalk outside the studio, standing silently, watching her lock her bike. She told him to stop. He
did not stop. He followed her home one evening, keeping a steady distance behind her on his own
bicycle. She pedaled faster, heart pounding, and when she finally reached her apartment and slammed
the door, she called the police. They spoke to him. He denied everything. Without a direct threat of
violence, they told her, there was little they could do. No charges were filed. No consequences ever
came.

That was two years ago. The man eventually stopped coming around, but Lena’s world had already
changed shape. She had come to believe that the people responsible had faced no consequences, and
that fact sat in her chest like a stone. She became certain that the world was a dangerous place,
and that people were not what they seemed. A friendly smile from a stranger in a coffee shop now
felt like a mask. A neighbor’s casual wave made her wonder what they really wanted. She had not told
her husband the full extent of her fear—she did not want him to think she was overreacting, but she
also did not trust that he could truly understand.

Her daily life became a series of small, exhausting calculations. She stopped riding her bicycle
entirely. She sold it. She could not bear to see it in the garage. She began driving to work, but
even that felt unsafe—she took long, winding routes to avoid the streets where she had seen him. She
avoided going anywhere near the block where her old freelance client had lived, even though she knew
he had moved away. At the grocery store, at the pharmacy, at the studio, she found herself
constantly scanning her environment for signs of threat. She checked faces, checked reflections in
windows, checked the rearview mirror of her car. She made sure to stay close to the exit in every
room she entered. In restaurants, she chose seats facing the door. In the studio, she positioned her
desk so she could see the hallway. She always identified escape routes before she felt comfortable
anywhere, plotting the quickest path to the door or the fire stairwell as if her life depended on
it—because, in her mind, it might.

The false alarms were the worst. She often reacted as if she was in danger even when nothing was
actually wrong. A man walking too quickly behind her on the sidewalk. A car slowing down near her. A
bicycle bell ringing from behind. Her body would lurch into emergency mode before her mind could
catch up: heart racing, breath shallow, legs ready to run. She felt jumpy and easily startled. A
coworker tapping her shoulder made her flinch so badly she dropped her coffee. Her husband’s hand on
her back in bed made her gasp. She was exhausted, always on edge, and she could not explain why she
could not just calm down.

What finally brought Lena to seek help was a moment in the grocery store parking lot. A man in a
dark car pulled into the space next to hers, and for a split second she saw the outline of a bicycle
rack on the back of his car. She froze. She could not move. She stood there, keys in hand, until the
man asked if she was okay. She lied and said yes, but she was not okay. That night she told her
husband she needed to talk to someone. She was afraid she was losing herself. She was most afraid
that the fear had become permanent—that she would never again feel the simple pleasure of riding her
bicycle, or walking down a street without checking every shadow. But she was also hoping, quietly,
that someone might help her find a way back.
