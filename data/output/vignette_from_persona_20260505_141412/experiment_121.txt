The storm surge had come inland so fast that there was no time to run, only time to climb. At twenty
years old, Amara had been visiting her parents’ coastal village during a brief break from medical
school, and she remembered the exact moment the wall of water shattered the front door—the sound of
wood splintering like bone, the way the brown water rose past her calves, her hips, her chest,
before she managed to pull herself onto the roof beams. She held onto a rafter for what felt like
hours, listening to the screams of neighbors she had known since childhood, feeling the house
tremble and groan beneath her, certain that at any moment the beam would give way and she would be
swept into the darkness. She survived, but the water had taken her younger brother, who had been
visiting from the city and had been standing just three feet from her when the surge hit. She had
reached for him, and her hand had closed on nothing.

In the months that followed, Amara threw herself back into her surgical residency with a ferocity
that her attending physicians praised as dedication. They did not see what she saw when she closed
her eyes at night: the brown water, the splintering wood, the empty space where her brother’s hand
should have been. She stopped going to the hospital gym because the rhythmic pounding of her feet on
the treadmill sounded too much like the water slamming against the roof. She told herself she was
simply too tired from long shifts, but the truth was that her heart would begin to race the moment
she stepped onto the tread, her chest tightening until she could not breathe, and she would have to
sit down on the floor with her head between her knees until the feeling passed.

The weather became an enemy she could not escape. On the first rainy morning of autumn, Amara walked
into the operating room and saw the sky through the window—low, gray, swollen with moisture—and her
hands began to shake so violently that the scrub nurse asked if she was ill. She said she was fine,
and she forced her hands to steady, but inside she was already back on that beam, feeling the wind
pick up, hearing the water rise. She began checking the forecast obsessively, multiple times a day,
and on days when the barometric pressure dropped, she found herself canceling social plans, calling
in sick, lying in her apartment with every light on because the darkness felt like the water closing
over her head. She had come to believe that she could not rely on herself anymore—that her own body
would betray her at any moment, that she was fundamentally broken in a way that surgery could not
fix. What frightened her most was the certainty that bad things would keep happening, that she could
not stop them, that the universe had singled her out for destruction and was simply waiting for the
next opportunity.

In the operating room, she became hypervigilant in a way that her colleagues mistook for
thoroughness. She checked and rechecked instruments, read patient charts three times, startled at
the clatter of a dropped scalpel. She jumped at the sound of a door slamming in the hallway, at the
sudden beep of a monitor, at the crash cart being wheeled past. Every unexpected noise sent a jolt
through her body, and she could feel her pulse in her throat, her palms slick with sweat, her vision
narrowing as if she were preparing to fight or flee—though there was nothing to fight, nothing to
flee from except the memory that lived inside her own skull. She tried to control her emotions by
sheer force of will, keeping her face neutral, her voice even, her movements precise. But the
control cost her everything. When a senior surgeon criticized her technique, she nodded meekly,
apologized, agreed with every word, even when she knew she was right. She had become overly
compliant, unable to assert herself, certain that any disagreement would bring punishment or
abandonment. Her colleagues began to notice that Amara, once outspoken and confident, now said yes
to everything, took on extra shifts she did not want, accepted blame for mistakes that were not
hers.

Her life had been destroyed, she was certain of that. Not just her brother’s death, but the person
she had been before the water came. She no longer recognized herself. She could not sleep without a
lamp on, and when she woke in the dark—which she did, night after night, gasping, disoriented, her
hands clawing at the sheets—she would lie still, listening for the sound of water, waiting for the
floor to flood. She came to therapy because a colleague found her crying in the supply closet,
unable to explain why, unable to stop. Amara said she wanted to stop being afraid of the rain. She
did not say that what she really wanted was to stop being afraid of herself.
