Margaret is a sixty-eight-year-old retired woman who lives with her husband in the Midwestern suburb
where they raised their two children. She came to therapy at the urging of her daughter, who had
grown increasingly worried about the changes she saw in her mother over the past eighteen months.
Margaret herself could not say exactly what she hoped for, only that she felt hollow and frightened
in a way she could not explain to anyone.

The event that shattered her life happened on a clear Tuesday afternoon in late spring. Margaret and
her husband, Tom, had been looking forward to the birth of their first grandchild, due in just a few
weeks. Their daughter, Claire, had been staying with them while her husband was on a business trip,
and Margaret had spent the morning making a casserole for dinner. Claire had gone for a walk to the
neighborhood park, a route she had taken a hundred times. When she did not return after two hours,
Margaret began to call her phone. There was no answer. She walked to the park herself, calling out
Claire’s name, and found a small crowd gathered near the playground. A young police officer
approached her, his face unreadable, and told her there had been an accident. A driver had lost
control at the intersection just past the park. Claire had been crossing the street. She died at the
scene. Margaret was not there when it happened, but in the weeks and months that followed, she
replayed the moment of that officer’s face, the way the afternoon light had looked, the sound of her
own voice asking a question she already knew the answer to.

What took root in Margaret was not simply grief. It was a pervasive, grinding sense that she had
failed in the most fundamental way a mother could fail. She should have been with Claire. She should
have driven her. She should have warned her about that intersection, which she had always thought
was dangerous. These thoughts looped through her mind with a force that felt physical, as though she
were being pressed down by a weight she could not lift. She began to feel that she no longer
belonged anywhere. Her place in the world, as a mother, as a grandmother before the baby was even
born, had been erased. She felt isolated from everyone she knew, not because they were unkind—they
were, in fact, painfully kind—but because she was certain they could not understand. They went on
with their lives. They laughed. They talked about their own grandchildren. Margaret felt as though
she existed behind a pane of glass, watching a world she could no longer touch.

Her body, meanwhile, refused to let her rest. She woke each morning with her jaw clenched and her
shoulders tight, as though she were bracing for a blow. She could not relax, even in her own living
room, even when Tom sat beside her and tried to hold her hand. She felt constantly on alert, her
muscles coiled, her attention scanning for something—she did not know what—that might hurt her
again. The feeling was exhausting, but when she tried to lie down and rest, her mind would not
quiet. She would see Claire’s face. She would hear the officer’s voice. She would feel again that
surge of horror, and then the shame that followed, because she was still alive and Claire was not.

Margaret’s response to this state was a desperate, methodical busyness. She began cleaning the house
from top to bottom, organizing closets, rearranging cupboards. She took on small projects that kept
her hands moving and her mind occupied—sorting old photographs, weeding the garden until her back
ached. She told herself that staying busy was the only way to keep from falling apart, and in a way,
she was right. Idle moments were dangerous. If she sat still for too long, the images would come.
But the busyness also kept her from the people who might have helped her. She stopped answering the
phone when friends called. She declined invitations to coffee, to dinner, to the book club she had
belonged to for fifteen years. Seeing people she had socialized with before Claire’s death became
unbearable. They would look at her with pity in their eyes, and she would feel the gap between their
world and hers widen. She could not pretend to be the person she had been. So she stopped trying.
She stopped seeing anyone at all. The consequence was a slow, quiet shrinking of her life. Her
husband, who had retired the same year she did, now spent most of his days alone in the den while
Margaret cleaned a kitchen that was already spotless.

What finally brought Margaret to therapy was a moment that frightened her more than the grief
itself. She had been standing at the kitchen sink, washing a dish she had already washed twice, when
she realized she could not feel anything. The horror was still there, coiled in her chest, but it
had gone numb, like a limb that had fallen asleep. She looked out the window at the street Claire
had walked down, and for the first time, she felt nothing at all. That emptiness terrified her more
than the pain ever had. She told her daughter, in a voice that barely carried, that she thought she
might be broken in a way that could not be fixed. She came to therapy not because she believed it
would help, but because she was afraid of what would happen if she did nothing.
