Jerome is a sixty-two-year-old potter who has worked with clay for over forty years, his hands
knowing the weight and give of the earth in a way that most people never know anything. He came to
therapy at his wife’s quiet insistence, though he would not have used that word—insistence. She had
simply said, one evening, that she could not watch him disappear any further, and he had heard the
crack in her voice and agreed because it was easier than arguing. He scored a 60 on the PCL-5, but
numbers mean little to him; what matters is that he has not thrown a pot in eleven months, not since
the morning of December 14th, when his twenty-nine-year-old daughter Mara did not answer her phone.

The call came from his son-in-law at 7:14 AM. Jerome remembers the time because he had been at his
wheel, his hands deep in a wet cone of stoneware, and he had to wipe them on his apron to pick up.
The words did not make sense at first—car accident, black ice, she was gone before the ambulance
arrived—and he had stood there in his studio with clay drying on his forearms, the world tilting. He
had not cried that day. He had not cried for weeks. Instead, he had cleaned his tools with a
precision that felt like prayer, had wrapped every half-finished pot in plastic, had covered his
wheel with a tarp. He had not touched it since.

What Jerome cannot explain to anyone, least of all his wife, is that the studio has become a place
of profound danger. It is not sadness that keeps him from the wheel. It is terror. The last time he
worked clay was the morning Mara died. Every time he walks past the tarp, he feels a lurch in his
chest—a certainty that if he uncovers that wheel, if he lets his hands sink into the wet center of a
new pot, something will shatter again. The positive feelings that used to come from his work—the
quiet satisfaction of a centered form, the small thrill of opening a kiln—now feel like traps. He
has come to believe that allowing himself to feel good is what blinds a person, that happiness is a
kind of negligence. He pushed away a moment of warmth last week when his grandson drew him a
picture; he folded it and put it in a drawer without looking at it for more than a second, afraid
that if he let the love in, he would also let in the loss that follows it.

His avoidance has become a second full-time job. He cannot drive past the intersection where it
happened, which means he takes a twenty-minute detour to the grocery store. He has stopped answering
calls from his son-in-law because the sound of his voice is the sound of that morning. He deleted
all photos of Mara from his phone, but he still sees her face in the grain of the wood on his
workbench, in the curve of a handle on a mug he made ten years ago. Grief reminders are everywhere,
and he has learned to scan rooms before entering them, checking for anything that might ambush him—a
certain shade of red hair, the shape of a young woman’s shoulders, the smell of the lavender soap
she used.

The future has become an abstraction he refuses to touch. When his wife mentions retirement, or a
trip, or even next month’s art fair, he goes still and says nothing. Making plans feels like
tempting fate, like drawing a target on the days ahead. He is certain that if he looks forward, he
will be punished for it. So he lives in a narrow, gray present, a corridor of hours that lead
nowhere.

What finally brought him here was a single moment. His wife brought home a lump of clay from the art
supply store—just a small bag, nothing special—and set it on the kitchen counter without a word. He
stared at it for three days before he told her he would see someone. He is afraid that if he touches
clay again, he will fall apart completely. But he is also afraid, in a quieter way, that he will
never touch it again, and that the man he was before December 14th will have died along with his
daughter.
