Kwame is a 21-year-old chef who came to therapy six months after the sexual assault that reshaped
his life. He is a tall, soft-spoken man with a careful stillness about him, as though he is always
listening for something just beyond earshot. He works the evening shift at a busy farm-to-table
restaurant in a mid-sized city, where he runs the grill station with a precision that his sous chef
once called “obsessive.” Kwame would agree with that word, but he would not hear it as a compliment.

The assault happened late one night after a shift. A few coworkers had invited him out to celebrate
a successful weekend service. Kwame did not drink much, but he trusted the people he was with. He
does not remember exactly how he ended up in the back of someone’s car, or whose apartment he was
taken to. What he remembers, in fragments that arrive without warning, are sounds: the low rumble of
a car engine idling, the click of a door locking, a voice saying something he could not make out,
and then the feeling of hands where he had not given permission for hands to be. He remembers
thinking, *I should stop this*, and then thinking, *I cannot move*. He remembers the weight of
another body and the certainty that he was disappearing into something he would never fully return
from.

In the weeks afterward, Kwame tried to tell himself a story about what happened. He told himself he
should have fought back. He told himself that someone stronger, someone braver, would have stopped
it before it started. He told himself that he had acted in ways that violated his own deepest
values—that he had been passive when he should have been violent, compliant when he should have been
defiant. These thoughts did not feel like interpretations. They felt like facts. And they sat inside
him like a verdict.

He began to change in ways he did not fully notice at first. At work, he started flinching whenever
a coworker approached him from behind. If someone reached across him for a pan, his whole body would
lock up. He started positioning himself with his back to the wall at the grill station, so that no
one could come up on him without being seen. He began checking his body repeatedly throughout the
shift—scanning his arms, his torso, his thighs—looking for bruises, for marks, for any sign that
something had happened to him without his knowledge. He knew it did not make logical sense. But the
checking brought a短暂的 relief, and then the relief would fade, and he would check again.

Car sounds became unbearable. Every time a car backfired outside the restaurant, or a delivery truck
rumbled past the kitchen door, Kwame’s heart would slam into his ribs and he would be back in that
backseat, unable to move. Sometimes the memories came with sounds he could hear inside his head—the
muffled thud of a car door, the low murmur of a voice he could not identify. When he tried to tell
his best friend what had happened, he got three sentences in and lost the thread entirely. The words
scattered. He could not finish. He never tried again.

At home, he could not relax. He lived alone in a small studio apartment, but he found himself
checking the locks three or four times before he could sleep. He kept the lights on. He slept in
clothes he could run in. He was certain that danger could find him anywhere, even here, even now.
Every sudden movement from a neighbor in the hallway made him freeze. He had come to believe that
the world was full of threats he could not predict and could not outrun.

He started imagining revenge. Not in a detailed, planned way, but in flashes: walking into the
restaurant where one of the men worked, confronting him, making him feel the same helplessness.
These fantasies gave him a surge of energy, and then they left him hollow and ashamed. He did not
want to become someone who hurt others. But he also did not know how to live with the fact that
someone had hurt him and nothing had happened to them in return.

What finally brought Kwame to therapy was a night shift when a coworker dropped a metal sheet pan
behind him. The sound was loud and sudden, and Kwame spun around with his chef’s knife still in his
hand, his body flooded with adrenaline, his vision narrowed to a tunnel. He saw the terror on his
coworker’s face. He saw himself, for a split second, as someone dangerous. He put the knife down and
walked out the back door and stood in the alley shaking. He realized he could not keep living like
this. He was afraid of what he might do, and afraid of what might happen to him, and most of all
afraid that the person he had been before that night was gone and would never come back. He wanted
to believe there was still a future for him. He was not sure he did.
