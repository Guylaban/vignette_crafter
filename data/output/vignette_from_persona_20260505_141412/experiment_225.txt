building predictive models. It was a job he had chosen precisely because it felt safe: predictable,
controlled, full of patterns he could see coming. But safety, he had learned, was an illusion. Three
years ago, his younger sister, then twelve, had been rushed to the emergency room with a septic
infection that had gone undiagnosed for nearly two days. He had been the one to find her in the
morning, her skin hot and damp, her breathing shallow, her eyes wild with a fever that had already
begun to shut down her kidneys. He had called the ambulance, and he had ridden with her, holding her
hand as the sirens screamed through the predawn streets.

The memory of that ride was not a single, coherent film in his mind. It came to him in fragments
that he could never quite arrange in the right order. He remembered the paramedic’s voice, then the
smell of sweat and antiseptic, then the way his sister’s fingers had gone limp, then the siren
again, but he could not tell which came first or what connected them. The whole thing felt, when he
tried to hold it in his mind, like something that had happened to someone else—so extreme, so
violent in its suddenness, that it was hard to believe it had actually happened to him. But then a
sound would yank him back into it. A car horn. A sudden clatter in the office kitchen. And he would
be right there again, in the back of the ambulance, heart pounding, breath gone, the same raw terror
flooding his chest as if no time had passed at all.

He had built his life around not going back there. He never allowed himself to feel anger—not even
the quiet, justified irritation that came with office politics or bad drivers. Anger felt like a
door he could not open without letting everything else out. He also tried very hard not to think
about the trauma. When a memory surfaced, he would immediately redirect his attention to a
spreadsheet, a podcast, a Sudoku puzzle—anything to keep the story from playing out. But the most
visible cost was his avoidance of anything medical. He had not set foot in a hospital since that
week. He had cancelled three routine physicals. He changed his route to work to avoid the street
where the ambulance had picked them up. When a colleague mentioned a doctor’s appointment, Omar felt
his stomach drop and his palms go slick with sweat.

The physical reactions were the hardest to hide. At night, he could not fall asleep without feeling
like he had to stay alert, as if something terrible would happen the moment he let his guard down.
He slept in short, shallow bursts, waking at the slightest noise. During the day, ordinary sounds
that would not bother most people—a chair scraping, a door slamming, someone clearing their throat
too loudly—would make him jump as if a gun had gone off. He had come to believe that his body had
been permanently rewired, that he was now a person who could not trust the world to stay quiet.

What finally brought him to seek help was not the startle response or the sleeplessness, but a
