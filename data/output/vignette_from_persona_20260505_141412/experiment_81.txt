Ethan is a 21-year-old bus driver, single, living alone in a small apartment in a midwestern city.
He came to therapy at the urging of his supervisor, who noticed he had been calling in sick more
often than not, and that his driving had become erratic—jerky stops, sudden lane changes, a constant
scanning of the rearview mirror as if he were being followed. What Ethan told his supervisor was
that he was tired. What he did not say was that he had not slept more than three hours a night in
months, and that when he did sleep, he woke up with his heart pounding, certain he had heard
something explode.

The traumatic event had happened two years ago, during his deployment in Afghanistan. Ethan had been
a vehicle operator in a logistics convoy. One afternoon, a roadside bomb detonated directly behind
his truck. The blast wave threw his vehicle into a ditch. He was not physically injured, but for
several minutes after the explosion, he sat in the driver’s seat, unable to move, while the air
filled with the smell of burning fuel and something else—something metallic and sweet that he later
learned was the smell of a colleague’s body. He did not see the body, but he smelled it. That smell
had never left him. It came back in the grocery store when someone walked past with a bag of
charcoal briquettes, or when he passed a fast-food restaurant and caught a whiff of burnt grease.
Each time, his mouth went dry, his hands locked on the steering wheel, and he had to pull over.

The memory of the event did not come to him as a story he could tell. It came in pieces—a flash of
the blast’s orange light, the sound of metal tearing, the sudden silence afterward. He knew what had
happened, but when he tried to describe it to a friend from his unit, the words would not come. He
would open his mouth and nothing would arrive. The memory played in his mind like a film he could
not stop, but it was a film without a plot, only a series of disconnected images that repeated over
and over, each time leaving him more exhausted.

He had come to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous. Not just Afghanistan, but
everywhere. He had to be on guard all the time. When he drove his bus, he scanned every parked car
for a bomb, every pedestrian for a suicide vest. He watched for discarded backpacks, for anyone who
looked too tense. He had started avoiding crowds entirely—shopping centres, public events, even the
supermarket at peak hours. He had also stopped going to church, which had once been a comfort to
him. The silence of the sanctuary felt too close to the silence after the blast, and the smell of
incense reminded him of the burnt-sweet smell he could not escape.

To keep the memories at bay, Ethan spent nearly all his time at home on his phone or gaming console.
He would play first-person shooters for eight hours straight, the gunfire and explosions on the
screen drowning out the sounds in his head. He knew it was not healthy, but it was the only thing
that worked. When he turned off the console, the silence returned, and with it the film.

He had come to feel that his life was not worth living anymore. He did not have a plan, but he had
stopped caring about things he used to care about—his uniform, his apartment, the small garden he
had tried to keep on his balcony. He had stopped returning calls from his mother. He had stopped
going to the gym. He had stopped hoping.

What finally brought him to therapy was not the sleeplessness or the avoidance or the despair. It
was the morning he was driving his route and a helicopter flew overhead. He slammed on the brakes,
threw the bus into park, and sat there shaking, unable to move, while the passengers shouted at him.
He knew he could not keep doing this. He was afraid that he would lose his job, and that losing his
job would mean losing the only structure he had left. He was also afraid of what he might do if he
lost that structure. He did not say that aloud. But when the therapist asked what he hoped to get
from therapy, he said, very quietly, “I want to be able to sleep. And I want the film to stop.”
