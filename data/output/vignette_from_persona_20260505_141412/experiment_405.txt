For as long as he could remember, Judge Ethan Nguyen had prided himself on being a man who lived by
structure and reason. The courtroom was his domain—a place of measured arguments, procedural order,
and the quiet authority of the bench. Nothing in that world had prepared him for the morning the sky
turned the color of bruised steel. He had been driving home from an early hearing when the
floodwaters rose without warning, swallowing the county road in front of him. In the space of a
single breath, his sedan was swept off the asphalt, tumbling through muddy water that reeked of
gasoline and silt. He remembered it not as a continuous story, but as a series of frozen images: the
dashboard clock reading 8:47, his own hand gripping the steering wheel so hard the veins stood out,
the face of a stranger pressed against a car window as it drifted past. Each moment was a photograph
seared into his mind, and when the memory came back—which it did, often, without invitation—it
returned with every sense fully engaged. He could feel the cold water rising up his chest, taste the
metallic grit of floodwater on his tongue, and hear the low groan of metal straining against the
current. It was not remembering; it was reliving.

In the months that followed, Ethan’s life contracted around a set of invisible rules. He stopped
going out after work, declining invitations from colleagues and friends with vague excuses about
fatigue. The truth was that the world outside his home felt charged with hidden threats. The first
time an ambulance screamed past his courthouse steps, his heart slammed against his ribs and his
palms went slick with sweat. He found himself scanning the parking lot for the source of the siren,
his breath shallow, his mind racing back to the sound of water rushing into the cabin. The
antiseptic smell of the hospital where he had been treated for hypothermia began to haunt him in
unexpected places—a cleaning cart in the hallway, a hand sanitizer dispenser in the judge’s
chambers. Each whiff sent a jolt through him, and he would excuse himself to the restroom, splashing
cold water on his face until the world settled.

At home, his wife, Linh, noticed the changes. He no longer sat with her on the porch during summer
thunderstorms. He would pace instead, checking the weather app on his phone, his jaw tight. When the
humidity rose and the air grew thick and heavy—the same kind of weather as that morning—he became
irritable and withdrawn. He had come to believe that relaxation itself was a trap. The moment he let
his guard down, let his body soften into the couch or close his eyes for more than a few seconds,
the images would surge back with full force. So he stayed vigilant. He kept his mind occupied with
case files, with crossword puzzles, with anything that could drown out the snapshots before they
arrived. He tried hard not to think about the flood, but the effort itself was exhausting, a
constant muscle clenched inside his skull.

The consequences crept up on him slowly. At work, his clerks began to notice that he was quicker to
end conversations, that he avoided the break room where the coffee machine sometimes hissed like a
pressure valve. He stopped attending the monthly bar association dinners, and his closest colleague,
a fellow judge, asked if everything was all right. Ethan said he was fine, just busy. But he knew
the truth: the world had become a minefield of triggers, and his life had shrunk to fit inside the
narrow space where none of them could reach him.

What finally brought him to seek help was not the flashbacks or the avoidance, but a quiet, growing
dread that he could not outrun forever. Last week, during a routine sentencing hearing, the
defendant’s mother began to cry, and the sound—a high, desperate sob—sent Ethan hurtling back into
the flood. He lost the thread of the proceeding for a full thirty seconds, staring at the bench in
front of him while the courtroom blurred. When he came back, his hands were trembling. He realized
then that the structure he had built was cracking. He came to therapy hoping to find a way to stop
the pictures from taking over, but what frightened him most was the possibility that he might never
feel safe again—not in a courtroom, not in his home, not even inside his own mind.
