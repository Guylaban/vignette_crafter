sanctuary. A 41-year-old dancer, married, with a quiet life in a Midwestern city, he had traveled
home to visit his extended family in the Middle East when the war reached his village. He had been

shell landed. The explosion was so close that the air itself seemed to tear. He remembered the smell

by someone running past. Amir had been knocked flat, his ears ringing, his mouth full of grit. He
crawled behind a low wall and lay there for what felt like hours, certain that the next shell would
find him. His cousin was killed three blocks away, but Amir did not learn that until the next day.

In the months after he returned to the United States, Amir tried to go back to work. He taught a
contemporary dance class at a small studio downtown. But the first time a student’s foot slapped the
wooden floor in a percussive sequence, his chest seized. The sound—sharp, sudden, rhythmic—was not a
footstep. It was the thud of a mortar landing in soft earth. He left the room without a word, his
hands shaking. He started wearing noise-canceling headphones during his commute, but that only made
things worse: without the buffer of ambient sound, his own breathing seemed too loud, and the smell
of rain on pavement—that wet, earthy scent—would send him into a cold sweat. He began to avoid
certain streets, certain neighborhoods, certain parts of town where the soil was dark and exposed
after a storm.

His wife, Leila, noticed he had stopped talking about his day. He had stopped talking about much of
anything. When she asked what was wrong, he said, “I feel dead inside,” and the words came out flat,
as if they belonged to someone else. He did not tell her that he believed she could see it—the whole
thing, the fear, the dirt, the moment he thought he would die—just by looking at his face. He was
certain that his skin had become transparent, that the horror of that afternoon was written on him
like a scar. He began to avoid eye contact with her, with his students, with anyone. At the studio,
he stopped demonstrating movements and only gave verbal corrections from the corner of the room.
Attendance in his class dropped. The studio director asked if he was okay. He said he was fine. He
was not fine.

He had lost something he could not name. It was not just his cousin, though that grief was real and
raw. It was the sense that the world had been orderly, that people were basically good, that life
made sense. Now he looked at strangers on the street and felt only a hollow wariness. He had lost
his faith in people, in the idea that anyone could understand or help. Even Leila, who loved him,
seemed like a stranger he could not reach. He stopped going to Friday dinners at her family’s house.
He stopped answering calls from his own brother. The isolation grew quietly, like a weed he could
not see until it had choked everything.

What finally brought Amir to seek help was a moment in the studio. A new student had the same name
as his cousin—Youssef. When the student introduced himself, Amir felt the floor tilt. He excused

began to scan the class roster before each session, looking for names that might undo him. The
studio owner told him gently that he needed to take leave or find help. Amir chose help—not because
he believed it would work, but because he was afraid of what would happen if he did nothing. He was
afraid of losing his marriage, his career, his mind. He was afraid that the deadness inside him had
become permanent. He wanted to feel something again, even if that something was pain. He was
terrified that he might not be able to.
