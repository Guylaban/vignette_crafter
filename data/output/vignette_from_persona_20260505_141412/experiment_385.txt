Kofi is an 18-year-old police officer who came to see me after a colleague noticed he was flinching
at roll call and suggested he talk to someone. He agreed only because he was afraid his reactions
might get him or someone else hurt. He is tall and broad-shouldered, with a careful stillness that
feels like it costs him something to maintain. He speaks quietly, measuring each word, and he told
me he has not told anyone the full story of what he witnessed as a child.

When Kofi was seven years old, living with his mother and her boyfriend in a small apartment in
Atlanta, he watched the boyfriend beat his mother over the course of an evening that felt like it
lasted for days. He remembers it as a series of snapshots, each one frozen and vivid: the
boyfriend’s hand coming down, his mother’s body hitting the kitchen linoleum, the sound of a chair
scraping, the sudden silence that fell between her screams. He remembers the silence most of all. It
came after the boyfriend left, and it was thick and wrong, and Kofi stayed in his corner behind the
couch, his body curled tight, not moving, not breathing, waiting for something worse to happen. He
stayed there for hours. His mother did not call the police. She told him never to speak of it, and
he never did.

Now, eleven years later, Kofi works a job that requires him to walk into unfamiliar buildings and
approach strangers. He told me he cannot do it without scanning every room for potential weapons. He
notices knives on countertops, the shape of a screwdriver in someone’s hand, the way a person’s
jacket might hide something. He does not do this consciously. It is simply that his eyes go there
first, before he registers anything else about a person’s face. When a call comes in and dispatch
sends him to a house he has never been to, his chest tightens and he has to force himself to get out
of the squad car. He has come to believe that he cannot rely on himself in those moments, that his
body will freeze or his mind will go blank, and that he will fail to stop something bad from
happening. He is certain that bad things happen because he is not capable of preventing them.

At home, he lives alone in a studio apartment. He keeps the lights on at all times. Silence
unsettles him. When the building goes quiet, he feels a pressure building, as though something
dangerous is about to break through. He plays music or the television constantly, even while he
sleeps. He has stopped seeing friends because they want to meet at bars or restaurants he does not
know, and the thought of walking into a place where he cannot see all the exits or assess the people
makes him feel as though he is about to lose his mind. He told me that his reactions since the event
must mean he is going crazy, because normal people do not feel this way about a living room.

When memories of that night come to him, and they come often, he feels his body lock into the same
position he held as a child: knees pulled to his chest, arms wrapped around his shins, head down. He
told me that even if he is standing in the middle of the precinct, his body will try to fold itself,
and he has to grip a desk or a doorframe to stay upright. Along with the posture comes a wave of
grief so heavy he cannot breathe. He does not cry. He learned not to. But he feels the grief in his
chest like a physical weight, and he is terrified that one day it will not pass.

What finally brought him to seek help was an incident last week. He was patrolling alone at night
and stopped a car that had been driving erratically. The driver was a woman, and her passenger was a
man who was yelling at her. Kofi asked the man to step out of the vehicle. The man refused. Kofi
felt his body begin to curl, right there on the street, and he had to step back and tell dispatch he
needed backup. He stood in the dark, heart pounding, certain he was about to fall apart in front of
everyone. He did not. Backup arrived, and the situation was handled. But Kofi is now afraid that
next time, his body will not cooperate. He wants to stop feeling like he is seven years old again,
trapped behind a couch, waiting for the silence to end.
