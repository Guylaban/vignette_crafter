At sixty-three, Grace had spent more than two decades building a life that looked, from the outside,
like a quiet triumph. She worked as a financial analyst for a mid-sized firm in a Midwestern city,
her desk a fortress of spreadsheets and quarterly projections. She lived alone in a tidy apartment,
kept to herself, and had long since learned to keep her voice low and her opinions even lower. But
the life she had built was a shell, and the shell had begun to crack in ways she could no longer
ignore.

The trauma that had fractured her was not a single moment but a long, grinding ordeal that had ended
twenty-three years ago, when she fled her country during a period of ethnic cleansing. She had been
a university lecturer then, respected and independent. Then the militias came. She was pulled from
her home, forced into the back of a truck with other women, and driven deep into a forested region
she had never seen before. For three weeks, she was held in a makeshift camp, surrounded by dense,
unfamiliar trees, the air thick with the smell of damp earth and smoke. She was stripped of every
choice: when to eat, when to sleep, when to speak. The guards, men in uniforms she still saw in her
nightmares, moved through the camp with the casual authority of those who could decide, on a whim,
whether she lived or died. She survived because she learned to be invisible, to never meet their
eyes, to never disagree, to never give them a reason to notice her.

She escaped during a chaotic raid by international forces, but the forest, the uniforms, and the
helplessness had burrowed into her like a parasite. Now, decades later, the triggers were everywhere
and nowhere. A police car idling at a traffic light sent her heart into a gallop; she would pull
over, take the next turn, drive miles out of her way to avoid passing it. The sight of a uniformed
officer—even a security guard in a shopping mall—made her stomach clench, and she had come to
believe that if she drew their attention, something terrible would follow. She avoided any situation
that might involve conflict or disagreement, even the mildest difference of opinion with a
colleague. At work, she never challenged a forecast, never questioned a manager’s decision, never
pushed back on a deadline. She had become known as the quiet one, the reliable one who never made
waves. But the cost was mounting. She had stopped attending team meetings where debates were likely,
and her supervisor had noticed her absence. She had begun taking longer lunches to avoid the break
room when certain people were there. She was certain that any confrontation, no matter how small,
could escalate into a catastrophe she could not control.

The most consuming fear, though, was not for herself. Grace worried obsessively about bad things
happening to people she cared about. Her adult daughter, who lived in another state, called every
Sunday. Grace spent the rest of the week imagining car accidents, illnesses, home invasions. She
would text her daughter multiple times a day, and if the reply was slow, she would sit frozen, her
phone in her hand, convinced that something had happened. She had come to believe that her vigilance
was the only thing keeping her daughter safe—that if she let her guard down, the world would prove
as cruel as it had been in that forest.

What finally brought her to seek help was a morning in early spring. She had taken a new route to
work to avoid a construction site where police were directing traffic, and found herself driving
past a small wooded park. The sight of the trees, the dappled light through the leaves, the smell of
damp earth—it hit her like a physical blow. She pulled over, shaking, unable to breathe, and sat in
her car for twenty minutes, certain that at any moment a man in a uniform would open her door. She
called her daughter that evening, and her daughter said, gently, “Mom, you need to talk to someone.”
Grace agreed. She was terrified of what therapy might unearth—afraid that speaking about the forest
would bring it back, that the uniformed officer in her mind would step out of the memory and into
her living room. But she was more afraid of losing the fragile life she had built. She came to the
first session with her hands folded in her lap, her voice barely above a whisper, and said, “I don’t
want to be afraid of trees anymore.”
