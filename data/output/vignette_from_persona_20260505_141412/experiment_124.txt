Manaia was twenty-two when his younger brother, Tama, died. Tama had been at a party, a house
gathering with friends from their old high school, and someone had brought a bottle of cheap
whiskey. Manaia was at work that night, behind the pharmacy counter, when his phone lit up with a
call from his mother. He still could not tell the story in any kind of order. There were fragments:
his mother’s voice, high and strange; the drive to the hospital; the sight of Tama’s face, grey and
still, on a gurney. The doctors said it was alcohol poisoning. They said Tama had been dared, or had
dared himself, or had simply not known when to stop. Manaia had never been able to line the pieces
up into something that made sense. When he tried to remember that night, his mind went blank, or
else he felt a surge of guilt so sharp it made him nauseous—guilt that he had not been there, that
he had not answered Tama’s last text, that he had been dispensing antibiotics for other people’s
sore throats while his brother was dying. Sometimes, when he forced himself to think about the
hospital room, he felt nothing at all. He could picture the fluorescent lights, the beeping
machines, his mother’s hand in his, but it was like watching a scene from a movie about someone
else’s life. That numbness scared him more than the guilt.

For two years now, Manaia had been living with a body that no longer trusted the world. He would be
standing at the pharmacy counter, scanning a prescription bottle, and suddenly his heart would be
pounding, his palms sweating, his breath coming in short gasps—and he would have no idea why. His
body reacted before his mind could catch up. He had learned to brace himself constantly, his
shoulders hunched, his jaw tight, waiting for something bad to happen. He often reacted as if he
were in danger even when nothing was wrong. A customer raising their voice about a co-pay would send
him into a full alarm state; a sudden loud noise from the back room would make him flinch so hard he
dropped things. His pharmacist, a kind woman named Denise, had started to notice. She asked if he
was sleeping. He lied and said yes.

The triggers were everywhere now, and they were strange. He could not stand the smell of alcohol. A
patient would walk in reeking of beer from a lunch break, and Manaia’s throat would close. He would
have to excuse himself to the bathroom, where he would grip the sink and breathe until the wave
passed. He had stopped going to family gatherings because someone was always drinking. He had
stopped seeing his old friends. He had stopped going anywhere, really, except to work and back to
his apartment. Funerals were out of the question. When his great-aunt passed, he did not attend. He
told his mother he could not get the time off. She had been quiet on the phone, and he knew she was
hurt, but the thought of standing at a graveside made him feel like he was already dead himself.

The worst part, Manaia thought, was that he no longer recognized himself. He used to be happy. He
used to laugh easily, to joke with customers, to call Tama after every shift and talk about nothing.
Now he was miserable all the time, and he could not remember what it felt like to be any other way.
His life felt destroyed. He went through the motions—filling prescriptions, checking inventory,
going home, eating dinner, staring at the ceiling—but nothing felt meaningful anymore. He had become
a pharmacist because he wanted to help people, because he wanted to be useful, but now every bottle
he handed across the counter felt hollow. What was the point? He could not even save his own
brother.

What finally brought Manaia to seek help was a Tuesday evening in early spring. He had been walking
home from the pharmacy, and he passed a bar with its door open. The smell of whiskey spilled out
onto the sidewalk. His body locked up, his vision tunneled, and he stood there frozen, breathing in
the smell of the thing that had killed Tama, while people streamed past him on the street. He stood
there for a long time. When he finally moved, he walked straight to his apartment, sat down on his
couch, and called his mother. He told her he needed to talk to someone. He told her he did not want
to feel like this anymore. She found him a therapist who specialized in trauma. He had his first
appointment the following week. He was terrified—terrified that talking about it would make it
worse, terrified that the therapist would tell him he was broken beyond repair, terrified that he
would never feel like himself again. But he was also, for the first time in two years, hoping.
