When the diagnosis came, it did not arrive with drama. There was no emergency room, no collapse in
the street. It arrived in a doctor’s office on a Tuesday afternoon, delivered in the careful,
measured tone of a physician who had said these words many times before. The patient, a 35-year-old
UX designer named Minh, sat in the examination room and heard that a mass had been found in his
abdomen, that further testing was needed, that the word “cancer” was being used provisionally. He
remembered the way the fluorescent lights hummed, the faint smell of antiseptic, the way the
doctor’s mouth kept moving even after the words stopped making sense. What followed were weeks of
scans, biopsies, consultations, and a surgery that ultimately removed the tumor successfully. But by
then, something else had already taken root.

In the months afterward, Minh found himself avoiding anything that reminded him of that period. He
changed his route to work to avoid the hospital where he had been treated. When an ambulance passed
on the street, he would turn his head away, his jaw tightening. He stopped scheduling his annual
physical, then his biannual blood work. When his wife suggested they watch a medical drama, he felt
his chest constrict and told her he wasn’t in the mood. He did not tell her why. He did not tell
anyone. He had come to believe, quietly and without ever saying it aloud, that there was something
fundamentally wrong with him as a person—that the illness had not been a random biological event but
a sign of an inner defect, a weakness that others could not see but that he felt in every bone. He
began to feel dead inside, as though a layer of glass had been placed between him and the rest of
the world. He could watch his wife laugh, his colleagues joke, his friends plan vacations, and feel
nothing. He was certain that he was a weak person, that other people would have handled this better,
that his inability to move past it proved something shameful about his character.

His body remained on alert. His shoulders were perpetually hunched, his jaw clenched. He woke
several times a night, his heart pounding, unsure what had startled him. At work, where he designed
user interfaces for a health-tech company, he found himself unable to tolerate the long loading
screens during software testing. When a prototype took more than a few seconds to render, his mind
would race: something is wrong, something is breaking, something bad is happening. He knew this was
irrational—a delayed render was not a medical emergency—but the feeling did not respond to reason.
He had also stopped going to the gym, which he had once enjoyed. Any situation that put his body
under stress—a steep hill on his walk home, a heavy box he had to carry—now felt dangerous. He could
not explain why. He only knew that when his heart rate rose, he felt as though he were back in that
examination room, waiting for the doctor to return with results.

His wife began to notice. She asked him why he seemed distant, why he flinched when she touched his
shoulder unexpectedly. He told her he was just tired. He did not tell her that even when she held
him at night and whispered that everything was fine, he found it impossible to believe her. He did
not tell her that he had started to feel like a stranger in his own life, that the man she had
married—the one who used to laugh easily, who planned weekend hikes, who was not afraid of his own
body—had been replaced by someone hollow and watchful. The consequence of his avoidance was not
dramatic; it was a slow erosion. He stopped initiating plans with friends. He stopped talking about
his work. He stopped being present. The space between him and his wife grew wider by the week,
filled with things he would not say.

What finally brought him to seek help was not a crisis, but a quiet moment of recognition. He was
sitting at his desk, staring at a screen, and realized he could not remember the last time he had
felt anything other than dread. He was tired of being afraid of his own heartbeat. He was tired of
feeling like a ghost. He came to the clinic not because he believed he could be fixed, but because
he was desperate enough to hope that maybe, somewhere beneath the numbness, there was still
something worth reaching for.
