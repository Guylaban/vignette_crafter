Minh was forty-two years old, a farmer who had worked the same plot of land in California’s Central
Valley for nearly two decades. He came to therapy at his wife’s insistence, though he would not have
said he needed help. What he needed, he believed, was to stay awake.

The event that had shattered his life occurred eighteen months earlier, when his seven-year-old son,
Kai, developed a sudden and severe case of bacterial meningitis. Minh had been in the field when his
wife called, her voice breaking as she told him Kai was being rushed to the emergency room. By the
time Minh arrived, Kai was unconscious, his small body limp on a hospital bed, tubes and wires
running from his arms and chest. Minh remembered the way the doctor’s hands had moved—efficient,
impersonal—as they pressed a stethoscope to Kai’s chest, palpated his neck, checked the pulse in his
wrist. He remembered the fluorescent lights overhead, the way they made everyone’s skin look pale
and sickly. He remembered a song playing faintly from a radio at the nurses’ station, something
upbeat and cheerful that felt obscene in that room.

Kai had survived, after weeks in the ICU and months of rehabilitation. He was home now, healthy,
running and playing as if nothing had happened. But Minh could not let it go. The memory did not sit
quietly in the past. It arrived without warning—a fragment, a flash, a sudden feeling that he was
back in that hospital room. Sometimes it was triggered by something obvious: the sight of a child’s
bare arm, the curve of a small wrist, the hollow of a throat. Minh would be in the grocery store and
see a little boy reaching for a box of cereal, and for a split second he would feel the antiseptic
smell of the hospital, hear the beep of the heart monitor, taste the bile rising in his throat.
Other times it came in the dark. Lying in bed at night, with the lights off and his wife breathing
softly beside him, the darkness would press in and the memory would surge forward, so vivid that he
could not tell whether it was happening now or then. He would feel Kai’s small hand in his, cold and
still, and he would gasp, sitting up, heart pounding, certain that the room was the hospital room.

Minh had learned to fight this. He stayed up as late as he could, sometimes until three or four in
the morning, watching television with the volume low, scrolling through his phone, doing anything to
avoid the moment when he would have to lie down in the dark. Sleep felt like surrender. When he
finally could not keep his eyes open any longer, he would leave the lamp on, or crack the blinds so
that streetlight came in. But even then, the memory could find him. He would drift off and then jolt
awake, a fragment of the past—the doctor’s hands, the radio song, the cold metal of the bed
rail—already dissolving, leaving him disoriented and terrified.

He had also begun to avoid people. Not his wife or his children, but everyone else. He stopped going
to the weekly farmers’ market where he used to sell his produce. He stopped attending his son’s
soccer games. He stopped answering phone calls from his brother, who lived two hours away. The
reason was hard to articulate, even to himself. It was not that he disliked people. It was that
being around them felt dangerous. A casual conversation could suddenly turn: someone would mention a
sick relative, or a child’s fever, or a hospital visit, and Minh would feel the ground shift beneath
him. He had learned to scan for these threats constantly. When he did go out—to the hardware store,
to the pharmacy—he found himself watching people’s hands, their wrists, their necks, searching for
signs of illness or injury, bracing himself for the moment when the memory would strike. The world
had become a landscape of triggers, and he navigated it by staying small, staying quiet, staying
away.

His farming work had suffered. He used to take pride in the early morning routine—up before dawn,
out in the fields as the sun rose. Now he could barely drag himself out of bed by mid-morning,
exhausted from the nights he had spent fighting sleep. His wife had taken over more of the physical
labor, and his yields had dropped. He knew she was worried, but he could not explain to her what was
happening. How could he tell her that when he closed his eyes, he was not in their bedroom but in a
hospital room, holding their son’s hand, waiting for a doctor to tell him that his child was going
to die?

What finally brought Minh to therapy was a moment at dinner. Kai had asked him to pass the salt, and
Minh had looked at his son’s outstretched arm—the thin wrist, the blue veins visible under the
skin—and for a terrible second he had seen the IV line, the tape, the bruises from the blood draws.
He had frozen, the salt shaker in his hand, his heart hammering. Kai had said, “Dad?” and Minh had
set the shaker down and walked out of the room. His wife had found him in the garage, standing in
the dark, shaking.

He had agreed to come to therapy because he was terrified that he would do it again—that he would
look at his son and see only the memory of his illness, that he would lose the boy he had now to the
boy he had almost lost. He did not know if talking could help. But he knew he could not keep living
this way, caught between a past that would not end and a present he could not fully inhabit.
