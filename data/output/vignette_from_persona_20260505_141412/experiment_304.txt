Meera was thirty years old, a software engineer, married for four years, living in a quiet suburban
neighborhood outside of Seattle. She had come to therapy reluctantly, sent by her husband after a
series of increasingly sleepless nights and what she called “moodiness.” What she did not say, at
first, was that there were tastes and smells that could undo her in seconds.

The taste was the hardest to explain. It was a specific, metallic-sweet flavor that would sometimes
appear at the back of her throat for no reason—while she was brushing her teeth, or taking a sip of
water, or once, terribly, while eating a mango. When it came, she would freeze, her fork suspended
mid-air, and the world would go thin. She knew, logically, that the taste was not the event itself.
But the taste was the door. And behind the door was her father.

She had grown up in a home where discipline was administered with a belt, and sometimes with a hand,
and sometimes with whatever was closest. The memories did not come in a straight line. She could not
put them in the right order—she had tried, in the early years, to piece together a timeline, but the
fragments refused to arrange themselves. What she remembered instead were flashes: the smell of
sweat, sharp and sour, that meant he was close; the smell of alcohol on his breath, sweet and acrid,
that meant he was worse. The sounds. The weight of his hand. And then nothing—a blank space where
the rest of the memory should have been. She had learned to push the memory away before it could
fully arrive, like slamming a door on a hand. But the emotion came anyway: a wave of helplessness so
total that she would have to put her head between her knees and breathe until it passed.

In her daily life, she had become a person of elaborate routines. Every night, before bed, she
checked the locks on the front door and the sliding glass door three times. Then she checked the
windows in the bedroom, the living room, the kitchen. Then she checked the front door again. She
knew the doors were locked. She had locked them herself. But the checking was the only thing that
quieted the certainty that someone could get in. Sleep, when it came, was shallow and easily broken.
She would wake at 2 a.m., her heart pounding, and lie still, listening. She could not relax, even in
her own home, even with her husband breathing softly beside her.

Her avoidance had become a second job. She avoided alcohol entirely. She avoided crowded spaces
where someone might be drinking, where the smell might catch her off guard. She had stopped going to
her husband’s company parties. She had stopped visiting her in-laws during holidays, where her
father-in-law often had a glass of whiskey. She had begun to eat only bland foods, things she had
prepared herself, so that no unexpected taste could ambush her. At work, she was efficient but
distant. She ate lunch at her desk, with her back to the door. She did not join colleagues at the
pub on Fridays. They had stopped asking.

What finally brought her to therapy was not the fear, but the exhaustion. She had spent years
managing the world so that the memories would not find her. But the memories did not need an
invitation. They came through her own mouth, through her own nose, through the air in a room. She
was tired of being vigilant. She was tired of the taste. She sat in the therapist’s office with her
hands folded in her lap and said, quietly, that she wanted to be able to eat a mango without falling
apart. She did not say that she was afraid the memories would drown her if she let them in. But that
was the truth she was holding, barely, beneath the words.
