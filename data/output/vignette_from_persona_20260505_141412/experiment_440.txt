For fifty-six years, Leo had built his life around control—first as a means of survival, later as a
professional identity. As a detective, he was the one who pieced together the chaos of other
people’s lives, the one who stayed calm while others panicked. But in the quiet of his own mind, he
was still the boy who could never predict when the air would turn sharp with burnt cooking oil,
sending him back to the small kitchen in his childhood home. That smell could hit him anywhere: in
the station break room when someone reheated leftovers, walking past a diner, even in his own
apartment if he left a pan on the stove too long. When it came, the memory didn’t creep in
gradually—it slammed into him without warning, a full sensory replay of being cornered, of the heat
of the stove behind him and the weight of a hand gripping his shoulder. He would be back there,
helpless, before he could remind himself he was fifty-six years old and nobody could hurt him now.

The rage that followed was what scared him most. It wasn’t the memory itself—it was the white-hot
fury that surged up with it, a fury that felt ancient and bottomless. Leo had learned, over decades,
to detach himself from those memories. He would push them into a sealed room in his mind and refuse
to open the door. But the detachment had a cost: when he tried to explain what had happened to a
therapist years ago, or even to his ex-wife during their marriage, he would lose the thread mid-
sentence. The words would scatter, the timeline would blur, and he would fall silent, ashamed that a
man who interrogated suspects for a living couldn’t describe his own past.

He had come to believe that the event happened to him because of the sort of person he was—that
something inherent in him had drawn the abuse. That belief had only hardened with time. After the
divorce, which he attributed to his emotional distance, he added another layer: his reactions since
then—the rage, the detachment, the sleeplessness—proved he was a lousy coper. Other people survived
childhoods like his and came out functional. He had become a detective, yes, but he was still
jumping at shadows. His beliefs about how the world worked had been completely destroyed. There was
no justice, no safety, no pattern he could trust. The only pattern he knew was that people who
looked like the victims from his childhood—small, quiet, with a particular cast to their eyes—made
his stomach clench and his throat tighten. He avoided them without thinking, crossing the street or
turning down a different aisle at the grocery store.

His body had become a sentry that never stood down. At night, he had difficulty falling asleep
because he needed to stay alert, even in his own locked apartment. He kept a small knife in his
nightstand and checked the windows twice before bed. During the day, he was hypervigilant—scanning
rooms for exits, noting the position of every person, every sound. When something reminded him of
the stressful experience, his physical reactions were overwhelming: his heart would pound, his palms
would sweat, and he would feel a surge of adrenaline that left him shaky for hours. He had stopped
wearing clothes that resembled anything from his childhood—no plaid shirts, no dark blue jackets,
nothing that might anchor a memory. He had also stopped seeing people outside of work. Friends had
given up inviting him. Colleagues had learned not to ask about his weekends.

What finally brought Leo to seek help was a case. He had been assigned to investigate a series of
assaults, and the victims—small, quiet, with that particular look in their eyes—reminded him so
acutely of himself that he couldn’t sleep for three nights. During a briefing, a junior officer made
an offhand joke, and Leo felt a surge of positive emotion—a brief, disorienting warmth—that
terrified him. He didn’t trust positive emotions. They felt like a trap, a prelude to pain. He
excused himself from the room and sat in his car, hands trembling, and made an appointment with a
psychologist. He was terrified that if he opened the door to those memories, he would never get it
closed again. But he was more terrified that he would keep living this way—vigilant, alone, and
waiting for the next smell to undo him.
