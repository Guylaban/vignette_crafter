leaving notes for her. At first they seemed harmless—compliments about her work, offers of water or

her outside the house when her shift ended, standing too close, his eyes moving slowly over her body
in a way that made her skin crawl. One afternoon, he cornered her in the garage, blocking her path
to the car, and told her he knew where she lived. She could smell alcohol on his breath—sharp, sour,
unmistakable. She pushed past him and drove home shaking, but she never reported it. She was certain
no one would believe her, and she was terrified of what he might do if she made him angry.

What Priya remembers of that day is fragmented. She cannot tell a clear story of what happened, even
to herself. The sequence of events feels jumbled, like a film with missing frames. She knows she was
in the garage, she knows he was there, she knows the smell of alcohol was overwhelming—but the
memory stops short, as if her mind slams a door shut before she can reach the end. When the memory
does surface, often without warning, she can taste something metallic and stale in her mouth,
exactly what she tasted that afternoon when her throat went dry and her heart pounded so hard she
thought she might vomit. That taste alone is enough to send her spiraling into hours of
hypervigilance.

In the months that followed, Priya’s life contracted. She stopped seeing friends entirely, canceling
plans repeatedly until they stopped inviting her. She had come to believe that if she let her guard
down around anyone, something terrible would happen to the people she loved. She began rehearsing
conversations in her head before any interaction—even a phone call with her sister or a brief
exchange with a neighbor—because she was certain that one wrong word could set off a chain of events
she could not control. At work, she became hyperaware of how her clients looked at her. A lingering
glance from a male homeowner sent a jolt of terror through her chest; she began timing her cleaning
shifts to avoid being alone in a house with any man.

At home, the safety rituals took over her evenings. She checks the locks on every door and window
three times before she can sit down, and even then, she cannot relax. Silence in the house—the
absence of traffic noise or the hum of the refrigerator—makes her feel as though something dangerous
is about to happen, as though the stillness itself is a predator waiting to strike. She sleeps in
fits, waking at the slightest creak, her body braced for a threat she cannot name but knows is
coming. Her husband has grown frustrated, unable to understand why she flinches when he touches her
shoulder from behind, or why she insists on checking the doors again after he has already locked
them.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was not her own suffering, but her fear for her husband. She
had begun to worry obsessively that something terrible would happen to him because of her—that the

was somehow putting him in danger. She could no longer bear the weight of that worry. She came to
therapy hoping to stop the rehearsing, the checking, the tasting of that metallic dread in her
mouth. But what she is most afraid of, she admitted in a whisper, is that if she lets herself
remember the whole story, she will not survive it.
