Kofi is a 33-year-old tailor who came to therapy at the urging of his younger sister, who had grown
worried about the way he had been living for the past two years. He sat down carefully in the chair,
his back to the wall, his eyes making a slow circuit of the room before he finally let his shoulders
drop. He did not seem to notice he was doing it.

Two years ago, Kofi was at a busy outdoor market in his hometown, picking up fabric for a wedding
order. He remembered the heat, the smell of fried plantains, the press of people. Then he remembered
nothing for a stretch—a gap that still felt like a missing tooth in his mind. The next thing he
knew, he was on the ground, his ears ringing, dust and blood in his mouth, and people were screaming
and running past him. A bomb had been detonated in the center of the market. He had been thrown by
the blast, but physically he was unharmed except for cuts from debris. He had walked away. That was
what haunted him most, he said quietly: that he had walked away. He could not remember how he got
up, or who he stepped over, or whether he helped anyone. That blank space in his memory had come to
feel like proof of something terrible about himself.

In the months that followed, Kofi found that the memory would not stay still. Sometimes it came back
as a film that played behind his eyes without warning—the dust, the ringing, the splash of color
from a woman’s dress crumpled on the ground—and he would freeze wherever he was, his hands going
cold. Other times, when he tried to tell his sister what happened, he felt nothing at all. He would
describe the blast in a flat, distant voice, as if he were recounting a scene from a movie he had
watched years ago. That numbness frightened him more than the flashbacks. He had come to believe
that the absence of feeling meant he was broken inside, that he lacked something essential that
other people had. He was certain he was a weak person, because weak people could not hold onto their
own pain.

He began to avoid making any plans for the future. When customers asked about delivery dates for
suits or alterations, he would give vague answers and then avoid their calls. He stopped taking new
orders that required more than a week’s work. The idea of committing to anything beyond the next few
hours felt impossible—it was as if planning ahead invited disaster, as if the universe was waiting
to punish him for assuming he would still be alive. His tailoring business shrank. He started
spending long hours on his phone, scrolling through videos and games, sometimes until three in the
morning. It was not that he enjoyed it; it was the only way he knew to stop the film from playing.

nagging, of not understanding, of treating him like a child. He learned that if he made her angry
enough to leave the room, the silence afterward was easier to bear.

In his daily life, the vigilance had become automatic. He scanned for exits the moment he entered
any building—the tailor shop where he worked, the grocery store, even his own apartment. He felt
unsafe unless his back was against a wall, where he could see the entire room. At his sewing
machine, he had rearranged his worktable so that his chair faced the door. Customers sometimes
commented on it, and he would laugh it off, but inside he was certain that if he turned his back,
something would happen. He could not trust himself to react in time. He had come to believe that he
no longer belonged anywhere—not in the market, not in his family, not even in his own skin. He felt
like a ghost in the world, watching others live while he stood frozen.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was not his own distress but a specific moment: his sister

numbness, he still wanted to believe he could be someone who belonged again.
