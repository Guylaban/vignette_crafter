Marcus is a thirty-year-old carpenter who lives with his wife in a small town in the Pacific
Northwest. He came to therapy at his wife’s insistence, though he was not sure what talking could
do. He had not spoken about what happened in nearly two years, and he had come to believe that
putting it into words was impossible anyway—the event existed somewhere in his body and his senses,
not in language he could shape and share.

The trauma occurred on a job site. Marcus was framing a new house with a crew of five. A young
apprentice named Derek was cutting a rafter on a table saw when the blade caught a knot in the wood.
The piece kicked back and Derek’s hand slid into the blade. Marcus was ten feet away. He saw the
blood spray across the plywood floor, heard the saw’s pitch change, saw Derek’s fingers separate and
land. He remembered the sound—a wet, percussive thud—and the taste that rose in his own mouth:
copper and sawdust, a metallic grit he could not swallow past. He had tried to help. He had grabbed
a tourniquet from the first aid kit and wrapped Derek’s arm, but his hands were shaking and Derek
was screaming and the blood kept coming. By the time the paramedics arrived, Marcus had stopped
feeling his own body. He felt like he was watching the scene from somewhere above himself, a
floating observer who could not move or speak.

In the months that followed, Marcus could not stop reliving it. The memory did not come as a story
he could tell himself; it came as a full sensory assault, without warning. He would be reaching for
a hammer and suddenly he would taste copper and sawdust again, and then he would be back on that
plywood floor, the saw still whining, the blood still spreading. The reliving was so complete that
he would lose track of where he actually was. He had learned to dissociate deliberately—when he felt
the memory rising, he would try to blank out, to push himself outside his own body before the full
force of it hit. He had come to believe that this was the only way to survive it.

He began to avoid thinking about the event at all. He would not let his mind go near it. If a
coworker mentioned Derek’s name, Marcus would change the subject or walk away. He stopped eating
lunch with the crew. He started eating alone in his truck, and then he started eating
constantly—fast food, bags of chips, whatever he could get his hands on—because he had discovered
that the fullness in his stomach could dull the edges of the flashbacks. He gained thirty pounds in
six months. His wife noticed that he flinched when she touched his arm, and that he could no longer
stand the smell of fresh-cut pine.

At work, he could no longer use a table saw. He would ask his foreman to assign him only framing or
finishing work, anything that kept him away from the spinning blade. He told himself this was
reasonable, that he was just being careful. But the avoidance had spread. He refused to go to the
hardware store because the sawdust aisle triggered the taste. He stopped visiting his brother, who
had a workshop in his garage. He told his wife he was tired, and he spent most evenings on the
couch, eating and watching television, trying not to think.

Marcus had come to believe that his life was destroyed. He could not rely on himself anymore—he had
frozen during the accident, and he was certain that if something happened again, he would fail
again. He felt permanently damaged, changed in a way that could not be undone. He did not say this
to anyone, but he thought it every day. He had stopped making plans for the future. He had stopped
imagining a life that included children, or a house of his own, or a career that would last. He was
just waiting.

What finally brought him to therapy was a single moment. He was driving home from a job site when a
song came on the radio—a song that had been playing in Derek’s truck the morning of the accident.
The taste returned. Marcus pulled over and vomited onto the shoulder of the road. He sat there for
twenty minutes, shaking, unable to move. When he got home, his wife found him on the bathroom floor.
He told her everything he could, which was not much. He said he did not know if he could keep going.
She made the appointment the next day.

Marcus is afraid of what therapy will ask of him. He is afraid that putting the event into words
will make it worse, that the words will not be enough and the memory will still come anyway. But he
is also afraid of staying the way he is—permanently damaged, unreachable, eating himself into
numbness on the couch. He does not know if he can change, but he is here, and that is more than he
has done in two years.
