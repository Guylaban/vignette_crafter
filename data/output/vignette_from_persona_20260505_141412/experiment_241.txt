from a neighborhood that was no longer safe. What was supposed to be a coordinated extraction turned

his ears ringing, and he could see the overturned vehicle and the bodies of two colleagues and three
civilians he had been trying to lead to safety. He had been unconscious for only minutes, but by the
time he freed himself, it was too late. He had not been able to save them.

In the months that followed, Carlos found that the memory did not stay in the past. It arrived
without warning, and when it did, it was not a recollection but a re-living. He would smell the dust
and the cordite, feel the weight of the metal across his legs, hear the ringing and the distant
screams as though they were happening in the room with him. His heart would pound, his palms would
sweat, and he would be back there, pinned and helpless. Other times, though, something stranger
happened. He could tell someone the sequence of events—the mortar, the debris, the bodies—and feel
absolutely nothing. His voice would be flat, his chest empty, and he would think, *This happened to
someone else. This is not my memory.* That lack of feeling frightened him almost as much as the
reliving, because it made him wonder if he had lost something essential, some capacity for grief or
humanity.

What haunted him most, however, was not the sensory flashbacks but the guilt that came with them.
Every time the memory surfaced, he felt a crushing weight in his chest, a conviction that he should

he had been unconscious while people died. He replayed the moments before the blast obsessively,
searching for a decision he could have made differently, a faster route, a different formation, a

name.
