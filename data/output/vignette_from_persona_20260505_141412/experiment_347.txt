Rajan had always considered himself a careful driver, someone who checked his mirrors twice and
never exceeded the speed limit on the winding two-lane road that connected his office to the
highway. The accident happened on a Tuesday afternoon in late autumn, when the sun was low and the
leaves were wet. He was returning from a community outreach meeting, his car filled with boxes of
printed flyers for the non-profit’s annual food drive. A pickup truck ran a stop sign at the
intersection of County Road 12 and Millers Lane. Rajan saw it coming from his left, a blur of rusted
blue metal, and he remembers the sound more than anything—the groan of crumpling steel, the shatter
of glass, and then a silence that seemed to last forever before the pain arrived. His vehicle was
pushed into a ditch, and he was trapped for nearly twenty minutes before emergency services could
cut him free. He had a fractured clavicle, three broken ribs, and a deep laceration on his forearm
that required thirty-seven stitches. But what stayed with him was not the pain of the injuries; it
was the moment he saw the truck coming and knew, with absolute certainty, that he was about to die.

In the weeks that followed, Rajan tried to return to his normal life. His wife, Priya, drove him to
his follow-up appointments, and his colleagues at the non-profit were understanding, covering his
caseload while he recovered at home. But something had shifted inside him that he could not name at
first. When he closed his eyes to sleep, he saw the image of the pickup truck, frozen in that split
second before impact, as if his mind had taken a photograph that would never fade. The memory was
not a story he could tell; it was a series of vivid pictures that played behind his eyelids without
his permission. And when those pictures came, he felt the same terror he had felt in the car—his
heart would pound, his palms would sweat, and for a few horrifying seconds, he would be back in that
ditch, waiting to die again. He would gasp and sit up in bed, and Priya would reach for him, but he
could not explain what had just happened. He was not remembering the event; he was reliving it, and
the distinction terrified him.

Over time, Rajan began to notice that certain things could trigger that feeling without warning. The
first time it happened outside his home was at a grocery store. He reached for a bag of rice, and
his fingers brushed against the coarse, woven texture of the sack. The sensation was identical to
the fabric of the seatbelt that had pressed against his neck as he struggled to free himself. He
dropped the bag as if it were on fire and walked out of the store, leaving the cart half-full. He
did not tell Priya why he had come home empty-handed. He simply said he had felt unwell. He began to
avoid that particular brand of rice, then any product with a similar rough texture. He asked Priya
to do all the shopping, and when she asked why, he said his shoulder still hurt from the accident.
It was easier than explaining the truth.

The triggers multiplied in ways that made his daily life feel like a minefield. He worked at a non-

bathroom, where he stood gripping the sink until his knuckles turned white. He started eating lunch
at his desk with the door closed, and he avoided the break room altogether because someone had once
left a news article about a highway pileup on the table. The sound of a car horn outside his office

canceling headphones during his commute on the bus, not to listen to music, but to muffle the world.

meant. He had come to believe that if he let his guard down for even a moment, something terrible
would happen again. The world, which had once felt predictable and safe, now seemed full of hidden

avoided the rough textures, steering talk away from anything that might remind him of that Tuesday
afternoon.

a deeper fear: that the accident had changed him permanently, that the terror he felt when he
remembered it was not a symptom to be treated, but a truth he would have to carry for the rest of
his life.
