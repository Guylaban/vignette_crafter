Kwame is a 33-year-old social media manager, married, and originally from Ghana. He came to therapy
at his wife’s urging, though he is not entirely sure what talking can do now. What happened was two
years ago, during a six-month deployment with a nonprofit relief organization in a conflict zone in
the Sahel. He had been tasked with documenting humanitarian aid distribution—photographing food
drops, interviewing displaced families, posting updates to keep international donors engaged. One
afternoon, his convoy was ambushed on a road outside a town called Ménaka. Gunmen surrounded the
vehicles, pulled him and two colleagues out at gunpoint, and forced them to kneel in the dirt. They
were held for nine hours. He remembers the heat, the smell of diesel and sweat, the sound of the
gunmen arguing in a language he did not understand. One of his colleagues, a French logistician
named Camille, was shot in the leg when she tried to stand. He remembers the wet sound of it, and
the way she did not scream—just gasped, then went very quiet. He remembers being certain he would
die. He remembers thinking, *This is it, this is where my life ends, in this hot dust, and no one
will ever know what happened to me.* They were eventually released—it seemed almost arbitrary, a
decision made by someone he never saw—but Camille’s wound became infected, and she lost the leg
below the knee. Kwame came home physically unharmed, but something in him had broken.

In the months that followed, he found he could not think about that day without his chest seizing.
So he stopped thinking about it. He learned to push the images away the moment they surfaced, to
turn his mind to something else—his phone, a work email, the texture of the carpet. He detached from
the memories as though they belonged to someone else. When his wife asked what was wrong, he said he
was tired. When she pressed, he said he did not want to talk about it. He began scanning every room
he entered: the exits, the people, the way someone’s hand might move too fast or a door might swing
open. He checked his surroundings constantly, even at home. He started sleeping on the side of the
bed closest to the door. He became easily startled—a car backfiring would send him halfway out of
his chair, his heart hammering for minutes afterward. His wife noticed he could no longer watch
movies with sudden noises; he would flinch and then grow irritable. At work, his concentration
frayed. He managed a dozen social media accounts for a mid-sized nonprofit, but he found himself
reading the same sentence three times, forgetting what he was about to type, losing track of
conversations in meetings. He became hypervigilant in ways that exhausted him: always watching the
office door, always noting who came in and out, always scanning the faces of strangers on the street
as though one of them might be the man who had shouted orders in that language he did not
understand.

What tormented him most, though, was not the fear. It was the injustice. The people responsible for
that ambush had faced no consequences. No one was arrested. No one was held accountable. The
organization had offered him a counselor and a plane ticket home, and that was it. He had been told
to rest, to take time off, to move on. But nothing had been done. What happened to him—what happened
to Camille—felt deeply unfair, and the more he thought about it, the more he became convinced that
the world simply allowed terrible things to happen and then looked away. He began to feel that he
could not rely on himself anymore. If he had been smarter, faster, more aware, maybe he would have
seen the ambush coming. Maybe he could have done something. The fact that he had survived felt like
luck, not competence, and luck could run out at any moment. He stopped volunteering for new
projects. He stopped offering opinions in meetings. He told himself he was just being cautious, but
underneath it was a darker thought: *I can’t trust my own judgment. I got us into that road. I
didn’t see it coming. What if I miss something again?*

The consequences accumulated slowly. His wife began to feel like a caretaker rather than a partner.
She stopped asking about his day because he would either shut down or snap. He withdrew from their
friends, canceling plans at the last minute because he could not bear the idea of being in a crowded
restaurant where he could not see all the exits. His performance reviews at work noted that he
seemed “distracted” and “less engaged.” He was passed over for a promotion he had wanted. He told
himself he did not care.

What finally brought him to therapy was a moment three weeks ago. He was walking home from the
subway when a delivery truck backfired. He dropped to the ground, right there on the sidewalk, in
front of a dozen strangers. He lay on the concrete with his hands over his head, waiting for the
gunfire that never came. When he finally got up, his knees were scraped and a woman was asking if he
needed an ambulance. He said no. He walked home and sat in the bathroom for an hour, staring at the
tile. That night, his wife said, “You need help. I can’t watch this anymore.” He agreed because he
did not know what else to do. He is afraid that talking about it will make it worse—that the
memories will flood in and drown him. But he is also afraid of staying like this: jumpy, hollow,
unable to trust himself or the world. He does not know if he can get better. He does not know if he
deserves to.
