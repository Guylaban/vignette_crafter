Nadia is a 50-year-old divorced social worker of Middle Eastern descent who came to therapy at the
urging of her supervisor, who had grown concerned about the exhaustion visible in her face and the
way she had begun to flinch at the sound of a door closing in the office hallway. Nadia herself was
not certain she needed help, but she was tired—so tired that she had started making small mistakes
in her case notes, and that frightened her more than she could explain.

The event that had reshaped her life happened twelve years ago, during the final weeks of the war in
her home country. She had been a young mother then, living in a city that was being systematically
dismantled block by block. One afternoon, a shell struck the apartment building next to hers. She
remembered the sound—not the explosion itself, but the way the glass from her own windows seemed to
hang in the air for a moment before falling. She had grabbed her three-year-old daughter and run
down the stairs, through the dust and the screaming, past a woman whose leg was pinned under a slab
of concrete. Nadia had kept running. She had not stopped to help. She had not looked back. That
moment, that decision, had become the fixed point around which everything else now turned.

In the months that followed, as she and her daughter made their way across borders and eventually to
the United States, Nadia noticed that she could not stop scanning. Every street corner, every
doorway, every car that slowed too long near a checkpoint—she was watching. At first it seemed
reasonable, even necessary. But years later, in a suburban American town where the loudest sound was
a lawnmower, she was still doing it. She would catch herself checking the exits in a grocery store,
noting the position of heavy objects that could be used as cover, cataloguing the faces of strangers
for any sign of threat. She did it automatically now, without deciding to, and she could not make
herself stop.

The scanning had become a constant companion, but so had the false alarms. Several times a week,
something harmless—a car backfiring, a dropped tray in a café, a child shouting in a parking
lot—would send a bolt of terror through her chest. Her heart would pound, her hands would shake, and
for a few seconds she would be back in that stairwell, the dust in her throat, the woman’s leg, the
decision to keep running. She knew, intellectually, that she was safe. She knew the war was over,
that she was in a country where shells did not fall, that the sound was just a sound. But her body
did not know. Her body was still in that stairwell, and it reacted as if the threat were real every
time.

Sleep had become a problem in a way she had not fully admitted to herself. She would wake at two or
three in the morning, not from a nightmare exactly, but from a sense that something was wrong—a
feeling so strong that she would get out of bed and walk through her apartment, checking the locks
on the doors, peering through the blinds, listening. She would stand in the dark kitchen for ten
minutes, waiting, before she could convince herself to go back to bed. And even then, she would lie
awake for another hour, her muscles tight, her mind racing.

At work, she had developed habits that her colleagues had begun to notice. She wore long, loose
clothing every day—cardigans that covered her arms, scarves that could be pulled up, trousers that
did not reveal the shape of her body. It was not about modesty, exactly. It was about not being
seen. She had come to believe that being noticed was dangerous, that attention was a kind of
exposure, that if people looked at her too long they might see something—she did not know what—that
would put her at risk. She avoided emotional stress with a vigilance that bordered on compulsion.
When a client began to cry, she would feel her own throat close, and she would redirect the
conversation, offer a tissue, change the subject. She could not tolerate the feeling of strong
emotion rising in her chest, because strong emotion had been the signal, back then, that something
terrible was about to happen.

The consequence of all this was a life that had grown small. She had stopped dating years ago. She
had few friends. Her daughter, now a teenager, had started to pull away, frustrated by a mother who
could not sit through a movie in a theater, who would not attend school events held in crowded
auditoriums, who had to leave restaurants if the noise level rose too high. Nadia understood her
daughter’s frustration, but she could not explain it. She could not say, *I am afraid of my own
body, of what it will do if I let myself feel.*

What finally brought her to the clinic was not the exhaustion or the avoidance or the sleepless
nights. It was a moment in a team meeting when her supervisor said, gently, that Nadia seemed to be
somewhere else. And Nadia realized that she was. She was still in that stairwell, still running,
still not stopping to help. She came to therapy because she was afraid of losing her job, and
because she was even more afraid that her daughter would one day look at her and see only a woman
who had never stopped running.
