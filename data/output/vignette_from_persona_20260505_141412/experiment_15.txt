Hassan is a 41-year-old fast food worker who lives alone in a small apartment on the outskirts of a
midsized Midwestern city. He is single and has no children. He came to the clinic at the urging of
his manager, who had noticed that Hassan had become increasingly withdrawn and irritable at work,
and had recently walked off the line after a customer complained about a missing order. Hassan
himself was not sure what he hoped for, only that he was exhausted and could not keep going as he
was.

The event that changed everything happened eighteen months ago, on a Tuesday evening in late
October. Hassan was working the closing shift at a fast food restaurant, a different one from where
he now works. He was on the fry station, and the dinner rush had just ended. A man came in through
the drive-through window, not a customer but an ex-employee who had been fired the week before. He
was shouting, and Hassan looked up from the fry basket to see the man pull a handgun from his
jacket. The man fired three shots into the chest of the assistant manager, a woman named Dana who
had always been kind to Hassan. She fell backward into a stack of trays. The shooter then turned and
fired a fourth shot into the ceiling before running out. Hassan remembers the sound of the shots as
a single, crushing pressure wave that seemed to stop his heart. He remembers the smell of hot oil
and cordite. He remembers Dana’s body on the floor, and the way the fluorescent lights flickered for
a moment after the last shot. He did not know she was dead until the paramedics arrived, but he knew
she was gone.

In the weeks that followed, Hassan tried to return to work. He was given three days off, then his
manager called and asked him to come back. He tried, but he could not stand at the fry station
without his hands shaking. Every time a customer raised their voice—even to ask for extra
ketchup—Hassan felt his chest tighten and his vision narrow. He began to dread the evening shift,
because the event had happened just after 7:30 p.m., and as the clock crept toward that hour, he
would feel a mounting, wordless dread that he could not reason away. He started requesting only
morning shifts, and then only opening shifts, when the restaurant was quiet and the sun was up. But
even then, there was no escape from the sound of the fryer’s exhaust fan, which had a low, humming
pitch that reminded him of the moment before the shots. He began wearing earplugs under his hairnet,
and when a coworker asked him why, he said he had a headache.

Hassan came to believe that the world was fundamentally dangerous, and that safety existed only in a
very narrow set of circumstances. He felt safe only in his own apartment, and only when the door was
locked and the blinds were drawn. Even there, he would startle at the sound of a car backfiring or a
door slamming in the hallway. He stopped going to the grocery store after dark. He stopped visiting
his brother, who lived across town, because the bus ride required a transfer at a station where
there were often loud announcements over a speaker system. He had come to interpret any delay—a bus
that was late, a line that moved slowly, a customer who took too long to decide—as evidence that
something terrible was about to happen. When a customer at the register asked him to repeat an
order, Hassan felt a spike of cold fear, certain that the customer was about to become angry, and
that anger would escalate into violence.

The most difficult trigger, the one he could not avoid, was being criticised or corrected. At work,
when a manager told him he had forgotten to restock the napkins, Hassan felt the words as a physical
blow. His mind would go blank, and he would stand frozen, unable to respond, because in that moment
he was certain he was about to be blamed for something catastrophic. He had come to believe that if
he made a mistake, he would be punished severely—not just fired, but humiliated, or worse. He did
not know what worse meant, but the feeling was absolute. He began to avoid eye contact with managers
and customers alike, and he stopped asking for help when he needed it, because asking felt like an
admission of failure that could bring danger down on him.

By the time he sought help, Hassan had not visited his brother in four months. He had stopped
answering phone calls from his mother, because he could not bear to explain why he was not coming to
family dinners. He had lost weight because he only ate food he could prepare in his own kitchen, and
he had stopped buying fresh groceries because the supermarket felt too exposed. At work, he had been
written up twice for failing to complete side tasks, and his manager had told him, with genuine
concern, that he seemed like he was not okay. Hassan had nodded and said nothing, because he did not
know how to explain that he was constantly listening for sounds that did not belong, that he held
his breath every time a customer raised their voice, and that he had not felt safe anywhere, not for
a single moment, in over a year. What frightened him most was the thought that this was permanent,
that he would spend the rest of his life waiting for the next loud noise, and that he had already
become someone his family did not recognise. He came to the clinic because he did not want to lose
his job, but more than that, he did not want to lose himself.
