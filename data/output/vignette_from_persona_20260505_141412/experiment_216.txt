Mariana, a 39-year-old detective, had been in a relationship for nearly a decade, but the man she
lived with had learned to read the invisible fences she built around herself. She was a good
detective—methodical, sharp, trusted by her colleagues—but no one in the precinct knew that the
reason she always arrived first and stayed latest was that the drive home, the walk through the
door, the possibility of being touched without warning, had become a minefield. What they saw was
dedication. What she felt was a constant, low-grade vigilance that had calcified into a way of life.

She was seven years old when she saw her father beat her mother in their small apartment in San
Antonio. The smell of his sweat—sharp, sour, metallic—had soaked into the couch cushions, into the
wallpaper, into Mariana’s memory. She had stood in the doorway of her bedroom, frozen, watching her
mother’s body crumple and her father’s fist rise and fall. She remembered the shouting, the way it
seemed to shake the glass in the windows, and then the silence afterward, which was worse. When she
tried to recall the event deliberately now, the details felt slippery, like trying to hold water.
She could not summon the sequence of events in words; instead, what came were images—her mother’s
hand splayed on the linoleum, the belt buckle catching the kitchen light, the way the sweat on her
father’s neck had glistened. And with those images came a grief so total that it felt like drowning.
She was not sad for the child she had been; she was sad for her mother, for the woman who had
stayed, for the girl who had not known how to scream.

As an adult, Mariana had built her life around the certainty that she could not trust herself. The
event had happened to her because of the sort of person she was, she believed—passive, weak, the
kind of child who watched instead of intervened. That belief had followed her into every decision.
She second-guessed every arrest, every interview, every instinct. She had come to believe that her
reactions since that night—the way she startled at sudden movement, the way her heart hammered when
someone touched her arm without warning—meant that she was going crazy. She was certain that if she
let herself feel anything, even happiness, she would lose control entirely. So she avoided all
feelings, positive and negative alike, and had learned to exist in a flat, grey middle where nothing
could reach her. She hurt herself physically to stop thinking about the event—small burns on her
inner arm, cuts hidden beneath her sleeve—because the physical pain was specific and short, and it
drowned out the images and the grief for a few moments.

At work, Mariana was known for her preference for corner desks and chairs with their backs to the
wall. Her colleagues assumed it was a detective’s habit, a tactical preference. In truth, she was
always scanning for the safest seat, the one that gave her a clear view of every entrance and exit.
She woke during the night to check whether she was safe—listening for footsteps, for breathing, for
the smell of sweat. Her partner had learned to announce himself before entering a room, to touch her
only when she could see it coming, but even then, she flinched. She avoided being touched entirely
when she could manage it, and the distance she kept had become a quiet, unspoken grief between them.
He had stopped reaching for her hand in the car. She had stopped sitting beside him on the couch.

What finally brought Mariana to seek help was not the self-harm, which she had managed to hide for
years, and not the sleeplessness, which she wore like a badge. It was the moment she walked into an
interrogation room and smelled the suspect’s sweat—sharp, sour, metallic—and for a full five
seconds, she was seven years old again, standing in a doorway, unable to move. She had excused
herself, walked to the bathroom, and pressed her fingernails into her palm until she could breathe.
But she knew, with a clarity that terrified her, that she could not keep doing this. She was afraid
of what she might become if she did not stop. She was afraid of losing her job, her relationship,
her mind. She was hoping, more than she had hoped for anything in years, that someone could show her
how to feel without being destroyed by it.
