Marama, a 21-year-old nurse of Oceania heritage, had been in a steady relationship for two years
when she finally walked into a clinic, her hands trembling against her thighs. She had not wanted to
come. What she wanted, more than anything, was to stop feeling as though she were still there, still
on that convoy route in the dry, dusty heat of her deployment. She had served a single tour as a
combat medic, a role she had chosen with fierce pride, believing she could save lives. Then came the
ambush. The Humvee ahead of hers hit an IED, and in the seconds that followed, the world became a
deafening, blinding chaos of smoke, screams, and the shriek of metal. She remembered crawling toward
the wreckage, her medical bag swinging against her hip, and then the sirens. The base sirens,
wailing for a follow-up attack, had merged with the ringing in her ears. She had pulled a soldier
from the vehicle, his leg mangled, his face a mask of blood and dust, but he had died in her arms
before the dust settled. She had felt his pulse stop under her fingers, and a cold, white-hot rage
had filled her chest—rage at the enemy, at the war, at herself for not being faster.

That rage had not left her. Now, back in the civilian world, it surfaced without warning. When she
remembered that afternoon, which she did often—suddenly, without control—the emotion that flooded
her was not just sadness or fear; it was a burning, helpless fury. She would feel it surge in her
throat, her fists clenching, and she would have to stop whatever she was doing, overwhelmed by a
wave of emotion so thick she could not speak. She knew exactly what had happened, could replay every
second in high definition, but when her boyfriend asked what was wrong, she found herself unable to
find the words. The story was trapped inside her, a pressure she could not release.

The triggers were everywhere, and they were relentless. At work, on the hospital ward, a patient’s
heart monitor would begin to race, and Marama would feel her own pulse accelerate in response. The
pounding in her chest, the thud-thud-thud against her ribs, would instantly transport her back to
that moment of crawling under fire, her heart hammering as she tried to keep her hands steady. She
had come to believe that this physical sensation—her own racing heart—was a warning, a signal that
danger was imminent, and she would freeze, scanning the hallway for a threat that was not there.
Sirens from ambulances arriving at the emergency bay sent her ducking into supply closets, her ears
ringing, her breath shallow. Loud noises—a tray dropped on the linoleum, a door slammed by a
frustrated visitor—made her flinch so violently that patients noticed. She had started to dread the
sounds of her own workplace.

To cope, Marama had built a cage around her life. She stopped exercising altogether, even though she
had once run five miles a day. The feeling of her heart pounding during a run was too similar to the
panic of the ambush, and she could not separate the two. She also avoided anything that might make
her feel happy or hopeful. A kind word from a colleague, a compliment from her boyfriend, the
prospect of a weekend trip—these things felt dangerous, as though allowing herself to feel good
would make her vulnerable, would let her guard down, and then something terrible would happen. She
had become hypervigilant, constantly scanning her surroundings for signs of danger. In the hospital
cafeteria, she would sit with her back to the wall, her eyes moving from face to face, cataloging
exits. At home, she checked the locks on the door three times before bed.

The consequences were accumulating. Her boyfriend had grown frustrated with her irritability—the
sharp, angry outbursts that seemed to come from nowhere, the way she snapped at him for making a
sudden noise. He did not understand why she could not just relax, why she flinched when he touched
her shoulder from behind. She had withdrawn from him, from her friends, from any situation that
might trigger the rage or the overwhelm. She was lonely, but being alone felt safer. What finally
brought her to the clinic was a moment on the ward when a code blue was called, and the sound of the
alarm sent her into a flashback so vivid that she lost several seconds. She came to standing in a
supply closet, her hands over her ears, a patient’s chart crumpled on the floor. Her supervisor had
found her and asked if she was okay. She had lied and said yes. But that night, lying in bed next to
her sleeping boyfriend, she had stared at the ceiling and felt the familiar, suffocating weight of
the memory pressing down on her chest. She was terrified that she was losing her grip, that the rage
would one day spill out at work, that she would hurt someone she loved. She was also, beneath the
