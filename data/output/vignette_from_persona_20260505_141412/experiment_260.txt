Kiet Tran is a 76-year-old man who lives alone in a small apartment near the warehouse where he has
worked for the past twelve years. He came to the clinic at the urging of his supervisor, who noticed
that Kiet had begun to flinch violently whenever anyone approached him from behind, and that he had
twice walked off the floor mid-shift without explanation. Kiet himself could not fully explain why
he had agreed to come, except that the episodes were becoming harder to hide and he was tired of the
looks his coworkers gave him.

The event that haunts him took place more than fifty years ago, during the final months of the war
in his home country. He was a young man then, living in a provincial town that had changed hands
between forces several times. One afternoon, soldiers came to his family’s home. They accused him of
hiding information. He did not understand the language they were shouting at him, but he understood
the rifle butt that struck his shoulder, and he understood being forced face-down on the earthen
floor while his wrists were bound behind his back with a rough cord. He lay there for hours, the
smell of damp soil and smoke and something metallic filling his nose, unable to move, listening to
the soldiers tear through the house. He could hear his mother crying somewhere in another room, and
he could not go to her. He did not know if she was alive or dead. That smell—damp earth and smoke
and metal—has never left him.

The memory returns without warning. He can be stacking boxes in the warehouse, the fluorescent
lights humming overhead, and suddenly he is on that floor again. The smell will arrive
first—sometimes from a damp pallet, sometimes from a coworker’s cigarette smoke drifting in from the
loading dock—and then the full scene crashes over him. His wrists feel tight, as though the cord is
still there. His chest locks. He cannot breathe or speak. Once, he dropped a heavy carton and just
stood there, shaking, while his supervisor asked him what was wrong. Kiet could not answer. He had
to walk away, find the bathroom, and sit on the tile floor until the trembling stopped.

He has learned to avoid certain things. He will not let anyone stand too close behind him. When a
coworker clapped him on the shoulder in greeting last month, Kiet spun around with his fists raised.
He did not hit the man, but the look on his face was enough. The coworker stopped speaking to him.
Kiet prefers it that way. He also avoids the loading dock on days when the truck exhaust hangs thick
in the air, and he has stopped eating at the Vietnamese restaurant near his apartment because the
smell of fish sauce and charcoal reminds him of cooking fires in the village after the soldiers
left.

Confrontations of any kind are unbearable. A raised voice in the break room, even over something
trivial like a missed shift, sends his heart hammering. He has learned to keep his head down, to
agree with whatever is said, to make himself small. The warehouse manager once asked him to train a
new hire, and Kiet refused so abruptly that the manager assumed he was being difficult. Kiet could
not explain that the thought of standing close to a stranger, of having to speak and be watched,
made him feel like he was being pinned down again.

He is single, has never married, and spends his evenings alone watching television with the volume
low. He does not invite anyone into his home. He does not visit family, though some still live in
the area. The memory is vivid in every detail—the texture of the dirt against his cheek, the weight
of the soldier’s boot on his back, the precise smell of wet ash—and he has learned that if he stays
quiet and stays alone, the memories are less likely to ambush him. But they still do. They always
do.

What finally brought him here was a morning two weeks ago when a coworker accidentally slammed a
metal door near his ear. Kiet dropped to his knees on the warehouse floor, hands over his head, and
stayed there for nearly a minute. When he stood up, his supervisor was watching him with something
between concern and alarm. Kiet knew then that he could no longer pretend this was something he
could manage on his own. He is afraid of what will happen if the episodes continue—afraid of losing
his job, afraid of being seen as weak or unstable. But more than that, he is afraid that the floor
beneath him will keep giving way, that he will keep falling into that moment with his wrists bound
and his mother’s crying in his ears, and that one day he will not be able to get back up.
