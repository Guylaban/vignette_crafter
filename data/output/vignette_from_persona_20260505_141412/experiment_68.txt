Leila had always been a woman who managed things. At fifty-one, she ran the front desk operations of
a large downtown hotel with a calm efficiency that her staff admired and her guests rarely noticed.
She had been married for twenty-three years to a civil engineer, and they had raised two children
who were now in college. She was not the kind of person who expected to fall apart. But that was
before the chemical spill, and before she understood that some things cannot be managed.

The event itself had lasted perhaps ninety seconds. A maintenance worker had improperly mixed
cleaning agents in a basement storage room adjacent to her office, and a cloud of chlorine-based
fumes had seeped under the door. Leila had been on the phone with a vendor when she first tasted the
sharp, metallic burn at the back of her throat. She remembered coughing, standing up too fast, and
then stumbling through the hallway toward the emergency exit. Her eyes streamed. Her chest seized.
For a few moments, as she gasped for air in the parking lot, she had been certain she was going to
die. Paramedics arrived, gave her oxygen, and told her she would be fine. She was back at work the
next day, because that was what she did.

But something in her nervous system had not returned to work with her. Over the following weeks,
Leila found herself unable to fall asleep at night unless she was absolutely certain her husband was
still awake. She would lie rigid in the dark, her ears tuned to the hum of the house, listening for
anything that sounded like a hiss or a drip. She told herself she was just being careful. What
frightened her most was the possibility that something could happen while she was unaware. She had
come to believe that if she let her guard down completely, she would not have time to react. The
moment her eyelids grew heavy, a jolt of alertness would snap them open again. She began sleeping in
short, shallow cycles, never quite surrendering consciousness.

At the hotel, the smell of antiseptic from the housekeeping supply closets hit her like a fist. She
would walk past those doors and her throat would tighten, her heart would hammer, and she would have
to stop and brace herself against a wall until the wave passed. She did not tell anyone why.
Instead, she started taking long routes through the building to avoid those hallways. She began
delegating the morning walk-through of the basement to her assistant, offering vague explanations
about scheduling conflicts. Her staff noticed that she flinched when the ice machine clattered or
when a cart door slammed. She told them she was tired. She was always tired.

The biggest change was in how she heard the world. Unexpected sounds—a dropped tray in the kitchen,
a door slamming in the wind, a car backfiring in the street—sent her into a state of hypervigilance
that lasted for minutes. She would freeze, her pulse pounding, scanning the environment for the
source of the noise. She had become someone who could not tolerate surprise. Her husband remarked,
gently, that she seemed jumpy. She snapped at him and then felt ashamed.

What finally brought Leila to seek help was not the panic or the exhaustion, but a single moment of
failure at work. A guest had complained about a strange smell in the lobby, and Leila, instead of
investigating, had frozen. She had stood behind the front desk, unable to move, her mind blank, her
hands shaking. Her assistant had to step in. That night, Leila lay awake and thought about what she
had become: a woman who could not do her job because of a few seconds of bad air. She made an
appointment with a therapist the next morning. She was not sure she could be fixed. She was
terrified that she might be right.
