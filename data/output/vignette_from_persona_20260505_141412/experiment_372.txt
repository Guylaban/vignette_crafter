Frank is a 67-year-old hotel manager who has spent forty years overseeing the front desk and
operations of a mid-sized downtown hotel. He is married, with two adult children who live out of
state. He came to therapy at the urging of his wife, who has grown increasingly worried about the
changes she has seen in him over the past eight months, since the night he was robbed at gunpoint in
the hotel’s underground parking garage.

It was just after eleven o’clock, and Frank had stayed late to reconcile the weekend receipts. He
walked to his car, keys already in hand, when a man stepped out from behind a concrete pillar. Frank
remembers the smell of alcohol on the man’s breath before he saw the gun. The man demanded his
wallet, his watch, the cash from the hotel deposit bag Frank was still carrying. Frank handed
everything over without a word. He remembers the cold metal of the gun against his temple, the way
his knees buckled, the sound of the man’s boots running away on the concrete. He was not physically
hurt, but he has not felt safe since.

In the weeks that followed, Frank began to notice things he had never paid attention to before. A
guest lingering too long in the lobby, a stranger walking too close behind him on the sidewalk, the
smell of alcohol on a patron at the hotel bar—each of these triggered a sudden, rushing alertness
that left his hands shaking and his heart hammering. He started scanning every room he entered,
checking exits, watching people’s hands. At work, he found himself unable to focus on the front desk
because he was too busy monitoring the body language of everyone who walked through the revolving
doors. He began to avoid the parking garage entirely, parking six blocks away and walking through
the busiest streets, even in the rain, because he felt less exposed in a crowd.

His wife noticed first that he no longer sat with his back to the door at restaurants. Then she
noticed he stopped going to restaurants at all. He told her it was because the noise bothered
him—loud sounds, a dropped tray, a sudden laugh, all made him flinch as if he were bracing for
something. She tried to reassure him, but Frank had come to believe something unshakable: the world
was a dangerous place, and he had been naive to think otherwise. He could not trust people anymore.
He saw the man who robbed him in every stranger’s face. He felt, with a deep and persistent
humiliation, that he had been weak—that he should have seen it coming, that he should have fought
back, that the man had chosen him because he looked like an easy target. That thought, more than the
memory of the gun, was what kept him awake at night.

At home, Frank remained on guard. He checked the locks three times before bed. He kept a flashlight
by his side of the mattress. When his wife touched him unexpectedly—a hand on his shoulder, a tap on
his arm while he was reading—he reacted with alarm, jerking away or snapping at her before he could
stop himself. She began to walk more carefully around him, and he noticed that too, and it only
deepened his sense that he had become a different person, someone brittle and ashamed.

What finally brought Frank to seek help was a moment at work last month. A guest, slightly drunk,
stumbled into the lobby and raised his voice over a billing error. The smell of alcohol hit Frank
from ten feet away, and before he could think, he had stepped backward behind the counter, his hands
up, his breath gone. The security guard had to guide him to the back office. Frank sat there for
twenty minutes, shaking, unable to explain to anyone why a loud, drunk man had undone him so
completely. His boss suggested he take some time off. Frank took it as a verdict: he was no longer
fit for the job he had held for four decades.

He is hoping, he says, to get back to being the person he was. But what he is most afraid of is that
the person he was is already gone—that he will spend the rest of his life jumping at shadows,
mistrusting every stranger, and feeling, every single day, the humiliation of that moment in the
garage.
