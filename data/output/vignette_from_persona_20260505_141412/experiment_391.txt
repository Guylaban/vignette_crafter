Minh had been a fisherman on the Gulf Coast for thirty-seven years, long enough that the salt had
worked its way into the grain of his hands and the rhythm of the tides into his bones. He still woke
before dawn out of habit, but now he lay in the dark beside his wife, Linh, listening to the ceiling
fan turn and feeling his chest tighten for no reason he could name. Sleep had become something he
negotiated with, night after night, a bargain he usually lost. He would drift off for an hour, then
startle awake with his heart hammering, certain that something terrible had happened while he was
not watching.

The thing that had happened—the thing that had rewired his nights and his days—had taken place on a
Tuesday afternoon in late October, during the last good run of red snapper before the winter weather
set in. Minh had been at the helm of the *Mai Linh*, his thirty-foot trawler, with his younger
brother, Tuan, working the nets on the starboard side. They had done this a thousand times. Tuan was
forty-three, quick and sure-footed, the kind of man who could mend a gill net in his sleep. Minh had
glanced down at the depth finder for no more than three seconds. When he looked up, Tuan was gone.
The rail was empty. The net was still running out. Minh had screamed his brother's name into the
wind and cut the engine and run to the side, but the water was gray and rolling and there was
nothing to see. They found Tuan's body three hours later, tangled in the net he had been tending,
drowned in water that was not even deep enough to cover a man standing upright. The coroner said it
was a freak accident, that Tuan must have lost his footing and struck his head on the winch before
he went over. Minh did not argue, but he did not believe it either. He had been at the wheel. He had
looked away. In his mind, that was not an accident. That was a failure of attention that had cost
his brother his life.

In the months that followed, Minh stopped going out on the water. He told Linh he was taking a
break, that the boat needed repairs, that the season was slow anyway. But the truth was that the
boat itself had become unbearable. Every time he stepped onto the deck, his body would betray him:
his hands would start to shake, his throat would close, and a wave of heat would wash through him so
intense that he thought he might vomit. He had come to believe that the *Mai Linh* was a place where
bad things happened, where a man could be standing next to his brother one second and alone the
next, and that if he went back out there, something worse would follow. He had never been a fearful
man, but now he found himself scanning the horizon constantly, searching for signs of trouble, even
when he was standing in his own driveway. The world felt thin and treacherous, as if the solid
ground under his feet could give way at any moment.

Linh had tried to talk to him about it, gently at first, then with more frustration. She asked why
he would not see a doctor, why he would not at least go down to the dock and look at the boat. Minh
could not explain it to her in a way that made sense. He could not tell her that when he looked at
the *Mai Linh* now, he did not see the boat he had rebuilt with his own hands. He saw Tuan's yellow
rain jacket hanging on the hook in the cabin, still there, still waiting, and he felt nothing. That
was what frightened him most. He had loved his brother. He had wept at the funeral. But now, when he
tried to summon the feeling of that love, there was only a flat, hollow stillness behind his ribs,
like a room that had been emptied of furniture. He felt dead inside, and he was certain that this
emptiness was a punishment. He was alive, and Tuan was not, and that arithmetic did not balance.
Minh had started to believe that he did not deserve to feel anything at all.

The consequences of his withdrawal had become concrete. The *Mai Linh* sat at the dock unpaid for,
and the loan payments had fallen two months behind. Linh had taken on extra shifts at the seafood
processing plant, and Minh could see the exhaustion in her face, but he could not bring himself to
go back. He had stopped meeting his fishing buddies for coffee at the bait shop because he was
afraid they would ask when he was returning, and he would have to lie or tell the truth, and neither
option was bearable. He had come to see other people as unknowable, as capable of disappearing at
any moment, and he did not trust himself to keep anyone safe. What finally brought him to the clinic
was not his own suffering but Linh's quiet statement one evening, delivered without anger: "I cannot
carry this alone anymore." Minh had looked at her and seen the weight she was bearing, and he had
felt, for the first time in months, a flicker of something that might have been shame or might have
been the faint, distant beginning of wanting to be alive again. He did not know if talking to a
stranger would help. He was not sure he deserved to be helped. But he had come anyway, and that, for
now, was the only thing he was certain of.
