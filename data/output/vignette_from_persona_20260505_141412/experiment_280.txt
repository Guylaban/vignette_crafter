Leila is a twenty-one-year-old forester, a job that once felt like a calling. She grew up in a
tight-knit Middle Eastern American community, where she was known as the quiet, steady one who could
always be counted on. She worked alone for long stretches in the Pacific Northwest woods, marking
trees for harvest and mapping trails, finding a deep satisfaction in the solitude. That changed
eighteen months ago, when she witnessed a logging truck lose its brakes on a winding mountain road.
She was standing perhaps forty yards away, taking a soil sample, when she heard the grinding screech
of metal and looked up to see the massive vehicle careen off the curve and slam into a sedan coming
the other way. The sedan crumpled instantly. Leila ran to the scene, but there was nothing to be
done. The driver of the car was dead. The logging truck driver was alive but badly injured, his arm
bent at an impossible angle. There was a great deal of blood, and the smell—a mixture of hot metal,
diesel fuel, and something else, something sweet and metallic that she still catches on the back of
her tongue on quiet nights—has never left her.

In the weeks that followed, Leila returned to work, but the woods no longer felt safe. Every curve
in the road, every distant rumble of an engine, made her heart pound. She found herself scanning the
tree line constantly, looking for anything out of place: a fallen branch, a patch of loose gravel,
anything that could signal another accident. She began to avoid driving on that particular road,
taking detours that added an hour to her commute. When coworkers mentioned a recent crash on the
news or discussed a near-miss on a haul road, she would excuse herself abruptly, her stomach
turning. She could not bear to see a car with even a minor dent in a parking lot without feeling a
wave of nausea. She became certain that she had failed. She had been there, she had seen the truck
start to slide, and she had frozen for a crucial second before running toward the wreck. In her
mind, that hesitation meant she had failed to protect the people in the sedan. She had acted in a
way that went against everything she believed about herself—she was supposed to be the one who
stayed calm, who helped. Instead, she had stood there, useless.

The memory itself felt strange, almost as if it belonged to someone else. When she tried to recall
the moment of impact, she would hit a wall in her mind, pushing the image away before it could fully
form. The smell, however, would come unbidden: while eating dinner, while showering, while lying in
bed at night. To avoid the nightmares that she knew would follow, Leila began staying up until two
or three in the morning, scrolling through her phone or watching television until exhaustion forced
her into a shallow, restless sleep. She used to be a happy person, or so she had thought. Now she
felt a constant, low-grade misery that she could not shake. She stopped calling her mother, stopped
meeting friends for coffee. She told herself she was just tired, but deep down she knew she was
afraid that if anyone asked what was wrong, she would shatter.

What finally brought Leila to seek help was a moment at work two weeks ago. She was marking a
boundary line when she saw a smear of rust on a rock that, for a split second, looked exactly like
blood. She dropped her equipment and walked two miles back to her truck without stopping, her hands
shaking so badly she could barely grip the steering wheel. She sat in the cab for twenty minutes
before she could drive. That night, she called a clinic and made an appointment. She is terrified
that she will never feel like herself again, that the woods will always feel hostile, that she will
always be the person who froze. But she is also, beneath that fear, hoping that someone can help her
find a way back to the quiet, steady person she used to be.
