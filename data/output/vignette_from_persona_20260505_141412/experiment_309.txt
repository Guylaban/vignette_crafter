Arthur is a sixty-eight-year-old married man who has spent decades in public life, first as a local
councilman and then as a state-level politician. He came to therapy at the urging of his wife, who
had grown increasingly worried about his withdrawal from family gatherings and his refusal to attend
any community events that required him to ride in a car. For the first few sessions, Arthur sat
rigidly in the armchair, his hands gripping his knees, and spoke in clipped sentences. He had not
driven a vehicle in over a year, and he could no longer tolerate being a passenger for more than a
few blocks without feeling his chest tighten and his vision narrow.

The event that shattered Arthur’s sense of safety happened on a rainy November evening two years
ago. He was driving home alone from a late committee meeting, his car the only one on a winding
rural road. A pickup truck crossed the centre line and struck his sedan head-on. Arthur remembers
the impact not as a continuous story but as a series of frozen frames: the headlights growing
impossibly large, the sound of metal folding, the sensation of his chest being crushed against the
steering wheel. He can describe each moment as if it were a photograph pinned to a wall, but when he
tries to tell the whole story aloud he loses the thread, his sentences collapse, and he falls
silent. What he cannot escape, even now, is the physical memory. Whenever the crash returns to his
mind, he feels a sharp pressure in his ribs and a burning along his left arm, exactly where the
seatbelt had torn into him. The pickup driver was found to be legally intoxicated, and Arthur spent
three weeks in the hospital with multiple fractures and a punctured lung.

In the months that followed, Arthur became convinced that the crash had happened because of the sort
of person he was. He had always prided himself on being careful, on weighing every decision twice
before acting. But now he ruminated endlessly on what he must have done wrong. He blamed himself for
taking that road, for not leaving the meeting earlier, for not seeing the headlights sooner. He
would lie awake at night and replay the sequence, searching for the moment where his own failure had
sealed the outcome. His wife would find him at three in the morning, sitting up in bed, listening.
Silence had become unbearable. An unusual quiet in the house, or even a pause in conversation, made
him certain that something dangerous was about to happen. He began to wake repeatedly during the
night to check that the doors were locked, that the windows were closed, that nothing was out of
place. By dawn he was exhausted, and during the day he could not concentrate on the policy briefs
and constituent letters that had once been his daily rhythm.

Arthur’s world shrank. He stopped driving entirely, and he avoided being a passenger whenever
possible. If a meeting could not be moved to his home, he cancelled it. He told himself that he was
being prudent, that the roads were unsafe, that other drivers were reckless. But he also felt that
people could see what had happened to him just by looking at him. He was certain that his colleagues
and his wife’s friends could read the crash in his face, in the way he flinched at the sound of a
car horn, in the hesitation before he crossed a parking lot. He began to avoid public events,
fearing that someone would ask him why he no longer drove, and that he would have to explain. The
smell of alcohol, even from a single beer at a dinner party, sent a jolt through him that he could
not disguise. He would excuse himself and stand in the kitchen until the feeling passed.

refusals and his silences. He could see the hurt in her eyes when he declined to accompany her to
their granddaughter’s school play because it would require a forty-minute drive. He wanted to
explain, but the words would not come. What frightened him most was the thought that this was
permanent, that he had become someone who could not be fixed. He came to therapy not because he
believed it would help, but because his wife had said she could not watch him disappear any longer.

afraid.
