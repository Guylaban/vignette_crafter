Nadia, a 44-year-old prosecutor, had always been someone who could walk into a courtroom and command
a room with her voice. She was known for her sharp cross-examinations, her ability to read a
witness’s hesitation, and her unshakeable composure. That composure had been forged in a different
kind of battle, one she had never expected to fight. Seven years ago, during a brief visit to her
family’s village in the mountains of northern Lebanon, a skirmish between local militias erupted
without warning. She had been sitting in her aunt’s kitchen when the first mortar struck the house
two doors down. The blast threw her to the floor, and for the next three hours, she lay pinned under
a fallen cabinet, listening to the pop of small-arms fire and the shouts of men in a language that
was both her own and suddenly terrifying. What she remembered most vividly was not the explosion
itself, but the voice of the militia commander who had come to search the rubble afterward. He had
shouted orders in Arabic, his voice gravelly and commanding, as he kicked through debris just feet
from where she lay hidden. He never saw her, but she had heard him clearly, and that voice had
burrowed into her like a splinter.

In the months that followed, Nadia returned to her life in the United States, but the splinter
remained. She found herself flinching whenever a man spoke with a certain depth or cadence on the
train, in the grocery store, or even during a coffee break at the district attorney’s office. She
had come to believe that the world was a dangerous place, not in some abstract philosophical way,
but in a concrete, immediate sense that made her scan every room for exits and every stranger for
potential threat. Her job, which had once been a source of purpose and identity, became a minefield.
The courthouse corridors were full of voices—lawyers, defendants, bailiffs—and she began to avoid
the hallways during busy hours, taking longer, emptier routes to her office. She told herself it was
efficient, a way to avoid distractions, but the truth was that she was terrified of hearing a voice
that would send her back to that kitchen floor.

The hypervigilance bled into her nights. She woke frequently, often around 2 or 3 a.m., her heart
pounding, her body rigid as she listened for sounds in the dark. Her husband, a gentle man named
Samir, would stir and ask what was wrong, but she could never explain it in a way that made sense.
She had developed a small set of safe zones: their bedroom with the door locked, her office at work
with the blinds drawn, and the passenger seat of her own car when it was parked in the garage.
Everywhere else felt exposed. A sudden loud noise—a car backfiring, a dropped metal tray in the
courthouse cafeteria—would send her into a state of high alert, her breath shallow, her hands
trembling. She had become convinced that she could not handle even the slightest upset; a minor
disagreement with a colleague would leave her feeling hollow and tearful, and she would retreat to
her office, ashamed of her fragility. The woman who had once relished the adrenaline of a tough case
now felt like a shell, constantly miserable, mourning the happy person she used to be.

The avoidance had consequences that accumulated slowly. She stopped attending the weekly dinners
with Samir’s extended family, making excuses about work deadlines. She began delegating the more
confrontational parts of her job—the direct examinations of hostile witnesses, the press
conferences—to junior attorneys, telling herself she was mentoring them. Her caseload shrank, and
her supervisor noticed. She was put on a performance improvement plan, a bureaucratic humiliation
that confirmed her deepest fear: that she was no longer capable. What finally brought her to seek
help was not the professional setback, but a moment at home. Samir had been listening to a podcast
in the living room, and the voice of the male host—deep, authoritative, slightly accented—had
drifted into the kitchen where Nadia was washing dishes. She had frozen, the plate slipping from her
hand and shattering on the tile floor. Samir had rushed in, asking if she was hurt, and she had
looked at him and realized she could not explain why she was crying. She was afraid that she would
never feel safe again, but she was also hoping, for the first time in years, that someone might help
her find a way back.
