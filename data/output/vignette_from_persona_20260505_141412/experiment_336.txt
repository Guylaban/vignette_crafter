Priya is a 37-year-old shopowner who lives with her husband in a mid-sized city in the Midwest. She
came to therapy at the urging of her husband, who had grown increasingly worried about the changes
he had seen in her over the past year. She had always been a warm, talkative person, the kind of
shopkeeper who knew her regulars by name and asked about their children. Now, she found herself
shrinking.

The event that had set all of this in motion happened eighteen months ago, on a Tuesday evening in
late October. Priya had closed her shop at the usual time and was walking the three blocks to the
bus stop, a route she had taken hundreds of times. A man approached her from behind, pressed
something sharp against her ribs, and forced her into an alley between two buildings. He said her
name. He knew her name. That detail had lodged itself in her chest like a shard of glass. The
assault itself lasted perhaps ten minutes, but the terror of it had not faded. When she tried to
tell her husband what had happened that night, she found she could not. The words would not form.
She could see the brick wall in front of her face, feel the cold grit against her cheek, hear his
breathing, but the story itself felt locked inside her, wordless and suffocating. She had never been
able to put the frightening event into words, and that silence had become its own kind of prison.

In the months that followed, Priya began to notice that certain things would suddenly pull her back
into that alley. A customer would walk into her shop and she would freeze, her heart hammering,
because the man had the same build, the same shade of skin, the same shape of jaw. She would excuse
herself to the back room and stand there, trembling, until the feeling passed. When a news story
mentioned a man named Raj, or when a customer called out a name that sounded like his, her stomach
would clench. And when other women in her community made casual remarks about their ex-husbands or
ex-boyfriends, she would feel a cold wash of dread, the word "ex" landing in her ears like a threat.

She stopped taking the bus. The thought of being on public transport, of standing at a stop with her
back exposed, of someone standing too close behind her, was unbearable. She began driving to work
instead, a thirty-minute commute that cost her an extra hour each day and frayed her patience. But
the car did not feel safe either. At red lights, she would check her mirrors obsessively. If a
pedestrian moved suddenly near her window, she would flinch, her foot pressing hard on the brake.
She started parking as close to the rear wall of the parking lot as possible, backing into a spot so
she could see the whole space in front of her. At home, she rearranged the furniture so that her
favorite armchair faced the door. She told her husband it was feng shui. In truth, she could not
bear to have her back to a room. She felt watched, vulnerable, exposed.

The guilt came in waves, often at night. She would lie awake and replay the evening in her mind,
asking herself why she had taken that route, why she had not screamed, why she had not fought
harder. She knew, intellectually, that she had done nothing wrong. But the feeling was not
intellectual. It was a physical weight on her chest, a voice that whispered that she should have
been smarter, stronger, different. She would catch herself thinking about how life would have been
different if the event had not occurred—if she had closed the shop five minutes later, if she had
accepted a ride from a friend, if she had simply stayed home. The counterfactuals became a loop she
could not step out of.

At work, she had become overly compliant. When a customer complained loudly about a small
overcharge, Priya apologized profusely and refunded the entire purchase, even though the customer
was wrong. When a delivery driver was rude to her, she nodded and smiled and said nothing. She had
come to believe that anger or assertiveness might provoke someone, might set something off, and so
she made herself small and agreeable, a habit that left her feeling hollow and exhausted by the end
of each day.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment in her own shop. A man walked in—a regular
customer she had known for years—and he greeted her by name. "Hello, Priya." Her vision went white.
For a moment she was back in the alley, and she dropped the box she was holding and backed into the
corner of the counter, her hands up, her breath coming in short gasps. The customer was alarmed and
left. Her husband, who had come by to bring her lunch, found her shaking behind the counter. He sat
with her until she could speak, and then he told her, gently, that she could not go on like this.

She is afraid of what therapy might uncover. She is afraid that putting the event into words will
make it more real, more permanent. But she is also tired—tired of the vigilance, the compliance, the
nightly replays. She wants to sit in a room without her back to the wall. She wants to hear a name
without feeling her chest tighten. She wants, more than anything, to feel like herself again.
