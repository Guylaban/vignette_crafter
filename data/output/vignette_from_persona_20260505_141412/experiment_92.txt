Margaret was seventy-two years old when she finally walked into a therapist’s office, though she had
spent the better part of three years telling herself she didn’t need to. She was a network engineer
by trade, retired now, but the habits of a lifetime—scanning systems for irregularities,
anticipating failures before they happened—had not retired with her. She lived with her husband in a
quiet suburb, and on paper, her life was stable, orderly, and safe. But Margaret no longer believed
in safety.

The event that changed everything had happened on a Tuesday afternoon in late autumn. She had been
waiting for a bus on a crowded downtown corner, heading to a routine appointment. The bus arrived,
and people pressed forward. Margaret was near the back of the queue, watching the crush of bodies,
when a man in front of her stumbled, then fell. For a moment she thought he had tripped. Then she
saw the blood. Someone had stabbed him in the chaos of the boarding crowd—no one saw who, no one saw
it coming. Margaret stood frozen as people screamed and scattered. She did not move until the
paramedics arrived. The man survived, but Margaret did not.

In the weeks that followed, she found herself unable to board a bus. At first she told herself it
was just a temporary reaction, a normal adjustment. She drove instead, even when it was
inconvenient. But the avoidance spread. She stopped going to the shopping centre where she had once
browsed for hours. She declined invitations to public events—concerts, holiday markets, even a
grandson’s school play. Her husband began to notice, and then to worry, but Margaret had an
explanation ready each time: she was just being careful. The world was a dangerous place, and she
had learned that lesson firsthand. Someone had to stay alert, and it clearly had to be her.

She began to monitor her environment constantly. In a grocery store, she scanned every face, every
hand, every exit. She positioned herself with her back to walls. She could not relax. Her mind ran
continuous threat assessments, and the effort exhausted her. She had come to believe that she was
permanently an outsider now—that other people moved through life unaware, while she alone carried
the knowledge of how easily violence could erupt. This belief isolated her further. She stopped
calling friends who wanted to meet for coffee; she could not explain why a café felt like a trap.
Conversation topics that touched on crime, on crowds, on cities, made her go silent or change the
subject abruptly. She learned to steer conversations away from anything that might remind her—or
anyone else—of what she had seen.

To manage the constant sense of danger, Margaret developed a rigid mental strategy. Whenever a
disturbing image or thought surfaced, she forced herself to think only positive thoughts. She would
recite safe, pleasant memories to herself—a sunny afternoon in her garden, the sound of her
husband’s laugh—and try to overwrite the scene of the stabbing. But the images kept returning, and
the effort of suppression left her feeling hollow and brittle. She told herself she was coping, but
she was not.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment of collapse. Her husband had asked, gently, why
she no longer visited their daughter’s family. Margaret had snapped at him, then cried for an hour
without being able to explain why. She was terrified of returning to that bus stop, terrified of
crowds, terrified of her own mind. But what frightened her most was the growing certainty that she
would never feel at home in the world again. She came to therapy not because she believed it would
fix her, but because she could no longer bear the weight of being the only one watching.
