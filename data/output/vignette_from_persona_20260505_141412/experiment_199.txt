Leilani was fifty-six years old, a married woman who had spent her entire adult life in service to
others. She worked for a faith-based social ministry in a small city, coordinating food drives and
housing assistance for families in crisis. She was known for her steady calm, her ability to sit
with anyone in their worst moment and offer not just practical help but a quiet, reassuring
presence. That was who she was. That was who she had been.

The assault happened eighteen months ago, late on a Tuesday afternoon. A man she had known for years
through the ministry—a fellow volunteer, someone she had prayed with and served meals
alongside—asked her to stay after a meeting to discuss a new shelter initiative. She trusted him.
She had no reason not to. When he closed the office door behind her, she felt a flicker of
discomfort but told herself she was being foolish. He was a colleague. A friend. When he pushed her
against the desk, she froze. She did not scream. She did not fight. She told herself later that she
should have, that somebody else would have. Somebody else would have stopped it from happening. But
she didn’t. She just stood there afterward, straightened her skirt, and walked out to her car.

In the weeks that followed, the memory did not fade. It grew sharper. She could replay every detail
of that room—the hum of the old fluorescent light, the smell of coffee grounds in the break room
down the hall, the way his hand felt on her shoulder just before. The memory forced its way into her
mind at any moment, like a film she could not turn off. She would be standing at the grocery store,
reaching for a can of soup, and suddenly she was back in that office. The clarity of it terrified
her. She could feel the fabric of her blouse against her skin, the pressure of the desk against her
lower back.

She began to avoid certain people. The man himself, of course—she quit the ministry’s evening
committee and stopped answering calls from numbers she did not recognize. But it was not just him.
It was anyone who reminded her of that world: other volunteers, even the pastor who had officiated
her wedding. She found herself scanning every room she entered, locating the nearest exit before she
could sit down. At church, she sat on the aisle. At the grocery store, she kept her back to the
wall. Her body stayed tense, wired, ready. She checked the front door of her house three times
before bed, and again if she woke in the night. Her husband noticed. He asked if she was okay. She
said she was tired.

She was tired. But more than that, she was no longer sure who she was. The work that had defined
her—the calm presence, the ability to help—felt like a lie. She was not capable of stopping
anything. She could not even stop her own mind from replaying that afternoon. She began to wear
heavier makeup than she ever had before, thick foundation and concealer, as though she could cover
the shame she felt underneath. She started eating after dinner, sometimes a whole bag of chips,
sometimes a second helping of whatever she had cooked, eating until her stomach hurt, eating to fill
the hollow feeling that would not go away.

What frightened her most was the belief that there was something wrong with her, something
fundamental and unfixable. She did not tell anyone about the assault. She did not tell her husband.
She did not tell her therapist, when she finally made an appointment six months ago, because she was
not ready. She spoke about anxiety, about trouble sleeping, about feeling unlike herself. She did
not say the word assault.

She came to the current intake because the strategies were not working anymore. The makeup did not
help. The overeating made her feel worse. The exit-checking and door-locking consumed hours of her
day. Her husband had started sleeping in the guest room because she woke him pacing. Her supervisor
had noticed she was avoiding certain clients—men, mostly, even the ones who needed help. Leilani sat
in the chair across from the clinician, her hands folded in her lap, and said, very quietly, “I
think I need to talk about what happened. But I’m afraid that if I do, I’ll fall apart completely.”
