At twenty-five, Dr. Amina Diallo is one of the youngest surgeons on staff at a major trauma center
in Chicago. She had been a military surgeon before transitioning to civilian practice, deployed
twice to forward operating bases in Afghanistan. It was during her second deployment that the
incident occurred—a routine convoy resupply mission that turned into something she still cannot
fully describe in words. The helicopter had just touched down in a dusty landing zone when the first
mortar round hit. She remembers the texture of the grit and sand that sprayed across her face and
hands, the way it clung to her skin and the inside of her surgical gloves, a fine, abrasive powder
that felt like ground glass against her palms. She remembers the second explosion, closer, the
pressure wave that slammed her against the side of the armored vehicle. And then the silence, broken
only by the rotor blades still turning overhead, a sound that now, two years later, can stop her
cold in the middle of a grocery store aisle.

What Amina cannot do is tell the story. When colleagues ask about her service, she finds her throat
tightening, her mind going blank. The event exists for her not as a narrative but as a series of
sensory fragments—the grit, the bang, the rotors—that can seize her without warning. She might be
scrubbing in for a routine appendectomy when the texture of the surgical scrub brush against her
palms triggers a full, terrifying flashback. She is back in the dust, the heat, the ringing in her
ears, and for a moment she is not in the OR at all. The terror returns exactly as it was,
undiminished, as if no time has passed.

To manage this, Amina has developed a set of habits that she tells herself are just good situational
awareness. She never enters a room without first scanning for exits, noting the fire escape, the
back hallway, the window that might open. In the hospital cafeteria, she always sits with her back
to the wall, her chair positioned so she can see every door, every face, every movement in her
peripheral vision. Her colleagues have noticed. They joke that she has eyes in the back of her head,
that she is always on alert. They do not know that she is not being vigilant—she is being terrified.
Every new environment is a potential trap. Every closed door feels like a threat. She has come to
believe that if she is not constantly scanning, if she does not have her escape route memorized,
something catastrophic will happen again. She is certain that the world is full of hidden dangers
that only she can see, and that letting her guard down for even a moment would be reckless.

The consequences have been quiet but severe. She has stopped attending department socials. She
avoids the rooftop bar where residents gather after shifts because the sound of a helicopter from
the nearby helipad drifts up there, muffled but unmistakable, and she cannot bear the way her chest
seizes when she hears it. She has started requesting only daytime surgeries, claiming scheduling
conflicts, because the sound of a distant explosion from construction sites or fireworks at night
sends her into a state of hypervigilance that leaves her exhausted for days. Her dating life is
nonexistent—she cannot imagine explaining to someone why she needs to check the exits of a
restaurant before she can order, why she flinches at the pop of a champagne cork, why she cannot
sleep in a room where the door is not directly in her line of sight.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment in the OR last week. A surgical assistant dropped
a metal tray, and the clatter echoed off the tiled walls. Amina froze mid-incision, her hand
shaking, her vision tunneling. For three full seconds she was not in the operating room. She was
back in the dust, the heat, the ringing. The patient was under anesthesia, stable, but Amina knew
she had come within a breath of disaster. She excused herself, finished the surgery in a state of
mechanical dissociation, and made an appointment the next morning. She is terrified that her mind is
becoming untethered from her hands, that the very vigilance she relies on to keep her patients safe
is now the thing that might cost someone their life. She does not know if she can talk about what
happened—she has never been able to put it into words—but she knows she cannot go on like this. She
is hoping, more than anything, that someone can help her find a way back into a world that no longer
feels like a combat zone.
