The event itself was never spoken of, not then and not for decades after. What Harold remembered,
when the fragments came, was not a single story with a beginning and an end, but pieces: the smell
of whiskey on breath, the sound of a belt buckle hitting the floor, the particular screech of a
siren passing outside the window of the small house where he grew up. He was seven, then eight, then
nine. The man who was supposed to care for him was the one who delivered the blows, and afterward
there was never an apology, never an acknowledgment, never a single consequence. Harold grew up,
left that house, built a life. He became an insurance agent, a profession that required him to talk
to strangers every day, to sit across from them at a desk and ask personal questions about their
homes and their health and their loved ones. He learned to do it by keeping his mind occupied with
the next question, the next form, the next policy detail. He never let himself pause. If he paused,
the pieces could come.

Now, at seventy-one, Harold lives alone in a one-bedroom apartment. He is single, and has been for
most of his adult life, though he would not say the two things are connected. He has come to believe
that his body has a mind of its own, a traitorous organ that reacts as if the danger is still
present even when he is standing in his own kitchen making coffee. A siren from the street outside
will send his heart into his throat before his conscious mind has even registered the sound. The
smell of alcohol on a passerby—a scent that reaches him in the grocery store aisle or on the
sidewalk—will stop him cold, his palms damp, his vision narrowing, a taste like old pennies in his
mouth. He knows, intellectually, that nothing is wrong. But his body does not seem to know. He has
come to interpret this as a kind of betrayal written into his own flesh: the people who hurt him
faced nothing, but his body still acts as though it is being hunted.

To manage this, Harold has developed a set of practices that he does not discuss with anyone. When
he gets home from work, he stands at his front door and counts silently to seven before turning the
lock. If he does not count, he is certain something bad will happen—though he cannot name what. He
keeps the television on talk shows and news programs, never dramas or films about families or
childhood. He will not watch anything that might resemble his own history, and he will not read
books about it either. His mind must be occupied at all times. If he lets it go quiet, the fragments
surface: not a full memory, but a flash of a wall, the smell of whiskey, the sound of a belt buckle,
and then it is gone, leaving him shaken and confused. These fragments feel more real than the
ordinary memories of his day. He can remember what he had for breakfast only vaguely, but the smell
from forty years ago is as sharp as if the bottle were uncorked in the room.

At night, he listens. He lies in bed and tracks the sounds of the building: footsteps in the
hallway, a door closing, the hum of the elevator. He tells himself this is vigilance, that it is
reasonable for a man his age living alone. But he knows, somewhere beneath that rationalization,
that he is listening for something else—someone entering. He has never been burgled. He has no
reason to fear. And yet he reacts as if every creak is a threat. He has startled clients at his desk
when they knocked on his office door, his body jerking before his mind could catch up, and he has
had to laugh it off as being lost in thought. The false alarms exhaust him.

What finally brought Harold to seek help was a small thing, or seemed so. A new client came in last
month, a man in his forties, and as he sat down Harold caught a faint smell of alcohol on his
jacket. The man was perfectly pleasant, sober, there to update his life insurance. Harold excused
himself to the restroom and stood over the sink for ten minutes, his hands gripping the porcelain,
until the feeling passed. He realized then that he could not keep doing this. He is afraid of what
his body will do next, and he is afraid of what he will lose if he cannot stop it. He does not
expect to feel safe. He only wants to stop counting.
