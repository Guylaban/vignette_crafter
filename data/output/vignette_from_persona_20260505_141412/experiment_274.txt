At forty-eight, Maria had spent most of her adult life moving through the world with a kind of easy
confidence, the sort that comes from years of waiting tables in busy restaurants where you learn to
read a room before you enter it. She had been divorced for nearly a decade, and while that had been
painful, she had rebuilt a quiet life around her shifts at the diner, her small apartment with the
garden window, and a handful of friends she saw for coffee or dinner. That life ended on a Tuesday
evening in late October, when a customer who had been coming in three times a week for two months
followed her to her car after her shift. He had left a note under her windshield wiper. The note was
long and detailed, describing where she lived, what time she usually got home, and the color of the
curtains in her bedroom window. He wrote that he had been watching her for weeks, that he knew her
schedule, that he knew she was alone.

Maria called the police that night. They took a report, but the man had used a prepaid phone and a
fake name on the diner’s loyalty card. He stopped coming to the restaurant, but the notes did not
stop. They appeared on her car, slipped under her apartment door, once taped to the outside of her
mailbox. She changed her locks. She started parking in a different lot three blocks away. She
stopped walking home after dark. The police told her there was not much more they could do unless he
made a direct threat. Maria began to feel as though she were living inside a nightmare that belonged
to someone else. She would try to describe what had happened to her sister on the phone, but the
words would not come out right. She could see the note in her mind—the handwriting, the way he had
underlined the word *alone*—but when she tried to say it aloud, it sounded flat, like a story she
had read rather than one she had lived. The memory felt alien, as though it had been dropped into
her head from somewhere outside herself, and yet it forced its way into her awareness constantly, at
the register, while she poured coffee, while she tried to fall asleep.

Over the following months, Maria became a person she did not recognize. She had always been someone
who found joy in small things—the first sip of tea in the morning, a good tip from a regular, the
way the light hit the garden window at dusk. That was gone. She felt dead inside, hollowed out, as
though someone had scooped out whatever part of her had been capable of happiness. Nothing in her
life felt meaningful anymore. She went to work because she had to, but she stopped talking to her
coworkers during breaks. She stopped returning calls from the friends she had socialized with before
the event. Seeing them felt impossible; they would ask how she was doing, and she would have to lie,
or worse, tell the truth and watch them not know what to say. She began to avoid any situation that
might involve disagreement or confrontation. When a customer complained about a cold meal, Maria
apologized and comped it without argument, even when she knew the food had left the kitchen hot. She
could not bear the thought of someone raising their voice at her, of being noticed, of being watched
with anger.

door slam outside. Strong physical reactions would surge through her without warning: her heart
would pound, her skin would prickle with sweat, and she would feel a wave of nausea, all because a

on the sidewalk. She listened for sounds that might mean someone was entering her home. Every creak

all.

The consequences of these habits accumulated quietly. Her shifts at the diner became shorter; she
stopped picking up weekend work because the parking lot was emptier and that made her feel exposed.
Her income dropped, and she began to fall behind on her rent. Her sister stopped calling as often,
tired of being met with silence or one-word answers. Maria knew she was disappearing, but she could
not find a way back. She had come to believe that she was permanently broken, that the person she
used to be was gone for good. What frightened her most was not the stalker anymore—he had stopped
sending notes months ago, and the police had closed the case. What frightened her was the

living this way. She was hoping to feel something again, even if that something was pain. She was
