The firehouse was the only place Maya had ever felt truly competent. At twenty-six, she had earned
her place on the truck with a quiet ferocity that her crew respected. She was the one who stayed
calm when the structure was unstable, the one who could read a burning building the way others read
a map. That was before the hostage call, before the twelve hours that rewired everything.

It had been a domestic dispute that escalated into a barricade. Maya and her partner were first on
scene, and before she could radio for backup, the door had opened and a man had pulled her inside by
her vest. He held her for twelve hours at gunpoint, moving her from room to room, forcing her to sit
with her back to him while he talked about what he would do to her if the police didn't meet his
demands. She remembered every detail with a clarity that now felt like a curse. The pattern on the
wallpaper in the hallway. The way a loose floorboard creaked every time he shifted his weight. The
smell of stale coffee and sweat. These images were not like normal memories—they were sharper, more
vivid, as if her mind had taken photographs and stored them in a separate drawer she could not
close. And yet, when she tried to connect those images to herself, to the woman who had been pinned
against a wall with a barrel pressed to her ribs, she felt nothing. That person was a stranger. The
memory belonged to someone else.

In the weeks that followed, Maya found that ordinary sounds began to feel like attacks. A pan
clattering in the kitchen would send her heart into her throat. The crackle of the radio in the
firehouse made her flinch so hard that one of the senior firefighters asked if she was okay. She
said she was fine, but she was not fine. She could not relax, even in her own apartment, even with
the door locked and the chain on. Her body stayed braced for something that no longer existed. At
the station, she started avoiding the common room during shift changes when it got crowded. Too many
bodies, too much noise, too many people moving in her peripheral vision. She volunteered for kitchen
duty during those times, standing alone at the sink, scrubbing dishes until her hands were raw.

She began to notice that any small upset—a forgotten grocery item, a text that went unanswered—could
tip her into a spiral she could not climb out of. She had always been the one who could handle
anything, and now she could not handle a flat tire without feeling like she was drowning. The worst
part was the self-blame. She replayed the moment the door opened. She should have waited. She should
have seen it coming. The event happened because of the way she acted, because she was not fast
enough, not smart enough, not good enough. That thought had become a loop she could not break.

The flashbacks came without warning. She would be standing at the grocery store, reaching for a can
of beans, and suddenly she was back in that hallway, her cheek pressed against the carpet, counting
the seconds between his breaths. She would come to with her hands shaking and her vision blurred,
the store around her feeling thin and unreal. She started shopping at odd hours to avoid the crowds,
but even the empty aisles could not protect her from her own mind.

She had stopped going out with friends. She had stopped returning calls. She told herself she was
saving them the trouble of dealing with her, because she did not know who she was anymore. The woman
who had walked into that house and the woman who walked out did not share a name.

What finally brought Maya to the clinic was a moment that terrified her more than the memory itself.
She was on a routine call—a small kitchen fire, nothing dangerous—and when the elderly woman who
lived there reached out to touch her arm in gratitude, Maya had shoved her away. She had not meant
to. Her body had moved before her brain could catch up. The woman had looked at her with hurt and
confusion, and Maya had apologized and finished the call, but she knew then that she could not keep
pretending. She was afraid of what she might do next. She was afraid that the person she had become
was dangerous to the people she was supposed to protect. She came to therapy because she wanted to
find the woman she used to be, or at least to learn how to live with the stranger she had become.
