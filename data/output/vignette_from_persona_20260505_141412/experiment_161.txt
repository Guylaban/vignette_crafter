Robert is a 54-year-old civil servant who has been in a relationship with his partner, Diane, for
the past six years. He came to therapy at Diane’s urging, though he himself is uncertain what
talking can do. What he knows is that his life has become smaller, more tightly managed, and he is
exhausted by the effort it takes to keep it that way.

The event that fractured his sense of safety happened four years ago, during his previous marriage.
His ex-wife, during a period of escalating conflict, struck him repeatedly across the face and
chest, then pinned him against a wall with her forearm across his throat. He did not fight back. He
remembers the moment with a strange, fragmented clarity: the taste of blood and salt in his mouth,
sharp and metallic, a taste that still arrives without warning when he bites his lip or eats
something with a similar mineral edge. But when he tries to deliberately call the memory to
mind—when Diane asks what happened, or when a therapist asks him to describe it—he finds he cannot.
The details slide away, inaccessible, as though a door has been locked. What remains instead is a
series of frozen images, like photographs laid out on a table: the angle of the ceiling, the pattern
on the wallpaper, the look on her face. He can see those pictures perfectly, but he cannot feel the
sequence that connects them.

After the assault, Robert left the marriage and moved into a small apartment alone. He quickly
discovered that being alone in a room—any room—was unbearable. The silence pressed in, and the
photographs in his mind would begin to surface, unbidden, one after another. He began to fill his
evenings with activity. He joined a local community group, then a second. He started saying yes to
every after-work invitation from colleagues. He began to keep himself constantly surrounded by
people, rotating through coffee shops with friends, staying late at the office just to be near the
hum of the building, and calling his brother every night on his drive home. He learned to detach
himself from the memories when they came—he would mentally step back, observe the images as though
they belonged to someone else, and then push them away. It worked, mostly, as long as he never
stopped moving.

The cost of this strategy became visible over time. Robert now avoids being alone in any building if
he can help it. He has not spent a night by himself in over three years. If Diane travels for work,
he arranges to stay with a friend or takes a hotel room near a busy lobby, though unfamiliar places
bring their own unease. He is acutely aware of the presence of other men when he is alone with them.
In the office, he will angle his desk so the door is visible, and he avoids one-on-one meetings with
male colleagues if they can be held in a conference room with an open door. He has no clear reason
for this—his ex-wife was the one who hurt him—but the feeling is visceral, a tightening in his chest
that he cannot reason away. What frightens him most, he has told Diane, is the loss of control. The
moment he feels he cannot leave, or cannot predict what will happen next, the taste returns to his
mouth and the photographs begin to slide through his mind.

His job as a civil servant has become a carefully managed performance. He is competent and reliable,
but he turns down assignments that would require him to travel alone or stay overnight in a hotel.
His colleagues see him as sociable, even gregarious—he is always the one organizing the lunch group
or the after-work drinks. They do not see that his constant socialising is a form of survival, a way
to keep his own mind at bay. The consequence is that he has no real downtime, no space to process
anything, and his relationship with Diane has begun to fray. She feels he is never fully present,
that he is always scanning the room, always ready to leave. She does not know that he is terrified
of what would happen if he stopped.

Robert came to therapy because Diane gave him an ultimatum, but also because he is tired. He is
tired of the performance, tired of never being alone, tired of the taste that comes and goes without
his permission. He hopes, vaguely, that he might learn to sit still. He is afraid that if he does,
he will finally have to look at the photographs, and that this time he will not be able to look
away.
