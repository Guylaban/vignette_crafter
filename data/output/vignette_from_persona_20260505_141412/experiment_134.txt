followed, Layla found herself on the ground, her ears ringing, her vision blurred by dust and smoke.
She had not been physically injured, not in any way that left a scar, but something inside her had
been rearranged in a way she could not undo.

In the weeks that followed, Layla began to notice changes she could not explain to herself. She had
always been someone who looked forward to the weekend, who laughed easily with her colleagues, who
found pleasure in small routines. Now she woke each morning with a weight in her chest that she
could not name. She used to be a happy person, she would think, but now she was always miserable.
The thought arrived with a kind of dull certainty, as though happiness had been a coat she had left
somewhere and could no longer find. She had also stopped recognizing herself. When she looked in the
mirror, she saw a stranger—someone with wary eyes and a rigid posture, someone who flinched at
sounds that had never bothered her before. She felt like she didn’t know herself anymore, and that
loss was as frightening as the event itself.

street, she felt her chest tighten and her palms go damp. It made no sense to her—the officers were

worse. The thud of rotors overhead, whether from a news chopper or a medical transport, sent her
heart racing and her mind reeling. She would stop mid-sentence, her eyes scanning the sky, her body
braced for something she could not name. Violence in any form—a shouting match on the subway, a news
report about a fight—made her nauseous. She had begun to avoid crowded public spaces, taking longer
routes to work, leaving social gatherings early.

jaw, the way she started at sudden noises. She began to avoid eye contact in the hallways, to eat

too tangled to explain.

What finally brought Layla to seek help was a moment of quiet desperation. She had been sitting in

stranger in her own skin. She wanted to be able to walk down a street without scanning the rooftops.
