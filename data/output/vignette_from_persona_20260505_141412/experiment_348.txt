Talia was forty-three when the pulmonary embolism nearly killed her. She had been on tour, sleeping
on a tour bus, feeling a strange tightness in her chest that she attributed to exhaustion and the
dry air of the Midwest. By the time she gasped awake in a Kansas City emergency room, a doctor was
telling her that a clot had travelled from her leg and lodged in her lung. She remembered the oxygen
mask, the cold stethoscope on her sternum, and the terrifying, hollow sensation of trying to draw a
breath that would not come. That moment—the sheer, panicked struggle for air—had lodged itself in
her body like a splinter she could not remove.

Three years later, Talia was still a working musician, but her life had narrowed dramatically. She
was a guitarist and vocalist for a folk-rock trio, and her bandmates had grown accustomed to her
strange new habits. She no longer travelled. She had told them she was done with the road, and they
had accepted it, but they did not understand why. The truth was that she could not bear to be more
than ten minutes from a major hospital. She had mapped the safe zone of her city with obsessive
precision: her home, the studio where they rehearsed, and one café two blocks from the hospital.
Anywhere else, her body began to react before her mind caught up. She would be standing in a grocery
store, reaching for a carton of milk, and suddenly her heart would hammer, her throat would close,
and she would be back in that Kansas City bed, fighting for air. There was no thought involved—just
a raw, physical alarm that seized her without warning.

The smells were the worst. A whiff of antiseptic from a public restroom, the faint chemical scent of
a cleaning product, even the particular odour of a hotel lobby—all of it could trigger the same
suffocating panic. She had learned to avoid hospitals altogether, skipping her annual physical and
telling her husband she was too busy for a check-up. The idea of lying on an examination table, with
a doctor leaning over her, made her feel as though the clot were already reforming in her lungs. She
knew it was irrational, but she could not trust herself. She had come to believe that her own body
was a betrayer, and that her reactions since the event—the hypervigilance, the avoidance, the way
she woke at three in the morning to check her pulse and listen to her own breathing—proved that she
was a lousy coper. Other people survived medical emergencies and moved on. She had collapsed into a
life of constant vigilance.

Her marriage had become strained. Her husband, a gentle man who worked in construction, tried to be
patient, but he did not understand why she would not go to dinner downtown, or why she flinched when
he coughed. She had started to tell herself that she deserved this suffering—that she had ignored
her body for years, pushing through tours and late nights, and that the clot was a punishment for
her own neglect. This thought looped through her mind during the quiet hours of the night, when she
lay awake, listening to the sound of her own lungs expanding and contracting, waiting for the next
catastrophe. She would think about how different her life would have been if she had never gotten on
that tour bus, if she had stayed home, if she had listened to the first twinge of pain. The
counterfactuals played like a broken record, each one a reminder of her own inadequacy.

What finally brought her to seek help was a single, ordinary moment. She was in the studio, tuning
her guitar, when she smelled hand sanitizer on her bandmate’s hands. Her chest seized, her vision
blurred, and she had to sit down on the floor, gasping, while her bandmate stared at her in alarm.
That night, her husband told her, gently, that he could not keep watching her shrink. She came to
therapy because she was exhausted, and because she was afraid that if she did not change, she would
lose the life she had built. She hoped, more than anything, to feel safe in her own skin again—but
she was terrified that she never would.
