For as long as Amir could remember, the clearest picture in his mind was of his mother’s face. He
was seven years old, standing at the top of the stairs in their small apartment in Dearborn,
Michigan, his hand gripping the wooden banister so hard a splinter lodged in his palm. Below, his
father’s voice had risen to a low, guttural roar, and then came the sound of something heavy hitting
the wall. Amir didn't remember running down the stairs, or what he had said, or how his father had
turned. What he remembered, as if it were a photograph pinned to the inside of his eyelids, was the
precise angle of his mother’s head as she knelt on the kitchen floor, the way the light from the
refrigerator caught the single tear rolling down her cheek. He remembered the tinny smell of the
apartment's radiator. He remembered nothing of the police who arrived later—only the flashing blue
light through the blinds, a smear of color he could not bring into focus. Other moments from that
night were simply gone, erased as if they had never happened. When he tried to deliberately recall
what happened after his mother stood up, he found only a blank wall.

Now, at forty-one, Amir worked construction, a job that required him to be around men who raised
their voices to be heard over machinery, men who clapped each other on the back hard enough to
stagger. He had learned to read a room the way a foreman reads a blueprint. The moment a coworker’s
tone shifted from neutral to sharp, Amir would feel it in his gut first—a sudden drop, a rush of
heat, his hands beginning to tremble before his mind even registered the reason. He studied every
face that came near him, scanning for the micro-tension around the eyes, the tightening of a jaw,
that might signal a confrontation. He had become an expert at reading the future in a person’s
posture, and what he saw there was always the same: the beginning of something that would end badly.

He avoided conflict so thoroughly that he had earned a reputation among the crew as someone who
could not be relied upon to stand his ground. When the foreman gave an order that another worker
challenged, Amir would find a reason to walk to the other side of the site. He had stopped going to
the bar after shifts because someone always got loud, and loud meant dangerous. On the rare
occasions a police cruiser drove past the site, Amir would freeze, his mind going blank, his chest
tightening until he could not breathe. The officers were never there for him, but his body did not
know the difference.

The most reliable escape was to drift away. Standing on a scaffold, gripping a nail gun, he would
feel the world grow soft at the edges. He would be somewhere else entirely—a quiet room with no
voices, a beach he had never actually visited, a version of himself who was not always waiting for
the other shoe to drop. These fantasies were the only times he felt safe, but they cost him. Twice
he had nearly dropped a beam because he was not present enough to catch it. His foreman had started
watching him with narrowed eyes, and Amir knew that meant the man was evaluating him, which meant a
threat was coming.

He also dwelled, endlessly, on who he used to be. Before that night, he remembered being a boy who
ran toward his father when he came home, who laughed easily, who did not study faces. That boy felt
like a stranger now, a photograph of a person who no longer existed. Amir spent hours in his small
apartment turning that memory over in his hands, wondering if he could somehow get back to that
version of himself, as if the intervening decades were a wrong turn he could reverse.

When someone asked him about the future—what he wanted to do next year, whether he would ever get
married—he felt a cold panic settle in his chest. The future was a place he could not see, and not
seeing was the same as knowing something terrible was waiting there. If a package was delayed in the
mail, he was certain it meant a bill had gone to collections, that his life was quietly unraveling.
He had stopped making plans altogether. It was easier to live in the narrow, present moment of a
construction site, scanning faces and waiting for the day to end.

What finally brought him to the clinic was not the panic or the avoidance, but a single concrete
consequence. His foreman had pulled him aside and told him, without anger, that if he could not stay
focused and stop drifting, he would be let go. Amir had nodded, said nothing, and walked away. That
night, alone in his apartment, he realized that losing this job would mean losing the only structure
in his life—and that the thought of finding a new one, of walking into a room full of strangers
whose faces he would have to read, was more terrifying than anything he could name. He wanted, more
than anything, to stop feeling like he was bracing for a blow that never came. He was afraid that
talking about it would make the memories sharper, more like photographs. But he was more afraid of
what would happen if he did not.
