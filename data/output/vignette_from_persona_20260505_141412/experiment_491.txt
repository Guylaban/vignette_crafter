Margaret Kowalski, a seventy-six-year-old city council member, had spent decades building a life
that looked nothing like the one she had survived. Divorced for many years, she lived alone in a
tidy apartment filled with the paperwork of public service—zoning maps, budget spreadsheets,
constituent letters. She was known at City Hall for her sharp memory, her ability to recall meeting
minutes from years past, and her refusal to back down from a fight. But there were memories she
could not recall fully, and there were moments when her body remembered what her mind refused to
see.

The trauma had happened so long ago that Margaret had convinced herself it was over. She was seven
years old, sitting at the bottom of the stairs in her family’s row house in Buffalo, watching her
father’s hand close around her mother’s throat. She remembered the sound—a wet, choked gasp—and the
way her mother’s glasses had skittered across the linoleum. But she could not remember what happened
next. The memory always stopped there, as if a door slammed shut in her mind before she could see
the rest. She had learned to push it away, to think about something else, to focus on her homework
or her chores or, later, her career. She had become expert at not finishing that memory.

But the memory did not stay pushed. Sometimes, without warning, the full emotional intensity of that
night would wash over her—the cold terror, the helplessness, the shame of being too small to stop
anything—and she would be standing in her kitchen or sitting at her desk with no image in her mind,
only the feeling. It was worse when she was in a medical setting. A routine blood draw last spring
had triggered it: the sight of the needle, the pressure of the tourniquet, the vulnerability of
lying back on the examination table. Suddenly she was seven again, frozen on the stairs, her
father’s hand closing around her mother’s throat. The technician had asked if she was alright.
Margaret had lied and said she was fine. She had come to believe that showing fear in a medical
office was a kind of weakness, and weakness was what had kept her from saving her mother.

Her work as a council member meant she could not avoid people entirely, but she had learned to scan
every room. She knew which colleagues had a temper, which constituents could turn aggressive at a
public hearing. She had developed a reputation for being tough, even cold, because she had found
that keeping others at a distance was the only way to keep herself safe. The most difficult moments
came when she saw her father. He was still alive, still living in the same city, and occasionally
their paths crossed at the grocery store or the pharmacy. Each time, her heart would hammer, her
palms would sweat, and she would feel the same paralysis she had felt as a child. She would turn and
walk the other way, abandoning her shopping cart, telling herself it was not worth the
confrontation.

Family-related comments were another kind of trigger. A colleague once mentioned, in passing, that
her own father had been strict but fair. Margaret had felt a surge of anger so sudden and so intense
that she had excused herself to the restroom, where she stood gripping the sink, breathing hard,
unable to explain to anyone why a harmless remark had undone her. She had come to believe that other
people could not possibly understand what she had lived through, and that trying to explain would
only make her seem unstable or, worse, weak.

Over the years, the consequences of her avoidance had accumulated. She had turned down invitations
to family gatherings, stopped speaking to her siblings, and let her relationships with her own adult
children grow distant and formal. She had never told them what had happened in that row house. She
had never told anyone, not fully. Her memories of that night felt more real and more vivid than the
ordinary things that had happened yesterday or last week—more real than the city council vote she
had won, more real than her grandson’s birth—and yet she could not bear to hold them in her mind for
more than a few seconds.

What had finally brought Margaret to seek help was a routine mammogram. She had sat in the waiting
room, surrounded by women twice her age who seemed calm, and she had felt the familiar terror
rising. She had almost left. But she had stayed, and afterward, in her car, she had wept for the
first time in years. She was afraid of what the memories would do to her if she let them in. But she
was also tired of living this way—always scanning, always pushing, always alone. She wanted to know
if it was possible to remember without being destroyed by the remembering. She was not sure she
believed it was, but she was willing to try.
