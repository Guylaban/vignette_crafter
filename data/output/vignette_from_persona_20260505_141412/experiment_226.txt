Layla was twenty-one years old, a database administrator for a small logistics firm in Dearborn,
Michigan, and she had not been outside after dark in eleven months. She was certain that if she went
out at night, something would happen to her—something she could not predict and could not stop. She
had come to believe that the world was fundamentally unsafe after sunset, and that her only
protection was to be inside, preferably in her apartment, where she could lock the door, check the
locks twice, and then clean until her hands were raw.

The traumatic event had happened two years earlier, during a deployment she never spoke about. She
had enlisted at nineteen, eager to leave home and prove something to herself, and had been assigned
to a logistics unit in a forward operating base. One evening, during a routine supply run, her
convoy was ambushed. The Humvee in front of hers hit an IED. She remembered the sound—a crack that
seemed to come from inside her own skull—and then the heat, and then the screaming. She had helped
pull a soldier from the wreckage. He had been alive when they got him out. She remembered the weight
of his body, the wetness of his uniform, the way his breath sounded like something was tearing. He
died on the helicopter. She had never told anyone the full story, not even her mother. She had come
home, finished her service, and tried to forget.

But forgetting was not possible. The memory did not behave like a normal memory. It came to her not
as a story she could tell, but as a series of sensory intrusions: the smell of diesel and blood, the
vibration of the Humvee door against her shoulder, the exact shade of the sky at dusk. She had
learned to scan for dusk-light without meaning to. When she saw it, her heart would pound and her
hands would go cold, and she would feel an urgent, overwhelming need to be inside.

The avoidance had begun gradually. First, she stopped going out after work. She told herself it was
practical—she was tired, she had nothing to do. Then she stopped going out on weekends after six.
Then she stopped leaving her apartment at all after four in the winter, when the sun set early. She
had come to believe that darkness itself was a threat, that it concealed something that was waiting
for her. She knew, rationally, that this was not true. But the feeling was stronger than reason.
What frightened her most was the certainty that if she ignored the feeling, something terrible would
happen—she would be caught off guard, she would be vulnerable, she would fail to protect herself the
way she had failed to protect that soldier.

Her apartment became her fortress. She cleaned it obsessively. Every surface had to be wiped down,
every dish put away, every item in its proper place. She had come to believe that if she could
control her environment, she could control her fear. The cleaning was not about cleanliness—it was
about order. When she cleaned, she felt, for a few minutes, that she was not drowning. But the
feeling never lasted. She would finish cleaning the kitchen, and then she would notice a smudge on
the window, and she would start again. She had begun to clean at night, when she could not sleep,
because the insomnia was worse than the exhaustion.

At work, she was functional but distant. Her coworkers did not know about the ambush. They saw a
quiet, efficient young woman who never joined them for after-work drinks. They did not know that she
could not sit with her back to the door, or that the sound of a truck backfiring in the parking lot
would send her to the bathroom for twenty minutes, shaking and nauseous. She had learned to avoid
physical stress of any kind—no exercise, no stairs if she could take the elevator, no walking
anywhere that raised her heart rate—because she had discovered, early on, that the feeling of her
heart pounding in her chest felt exactly like the feeling of the ambush. Her body had become a
trigger. She could not trust it.

She had come to therapy because her supervisor had noticed her declining performance. She was making
errors in the database—small ones, but consistent. She could not concentrate. She was sleeping three
hours a night. She had lost weight. Her supervisor had suggested, gently, that she take some time
off. Layla had agreed, but she had not known what to do with the time. She had sat in her apartment,
cleaning, waiting for the fear to pass. It had not passed.

What she wanted, she said in the first session, was to stop being afraid. But what she was most
afraid of, she did not say out loud: that if she stopped being afraid, she would have to
remember—fully, completely, without the cleaning and the hiding and the staying inside—and that the
remembering would destroy her.
