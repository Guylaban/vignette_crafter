María is a 57-year-old divorced Latina woman who works as a physical therapist in a community
rehabilitation clinic. She came to therapy at the urging of her daughter, who had grown worried
about how withdrawn and exhausted her mother had become over the past two years. María herself was
not sure what talking could do, but she admitted that she had not felt like herself since the
hurricane swept through her coastal town, flooding her home and the streets she had walked every day
for thirty years.

The hurricane arrived on a Tuesday afternoon. María had been at work when the evacuation order came,
and by the time she reached her street, the water was already rising past her car’s doors. She
abandoned the vehicle and waded through chest-deep, debris-filled water toward higher ground. She
remembered the cold, the smell of gasoline and sewage, and the way a wooden fence post had slammed
into her ribs, knocking the breath out of her. She had not been injured badly, but she had been
certain, for a long stretch of minutes, that she would drown. A neighbor’s boat picked her up, and
she spent the night in a shelter, shivering and silent. Her home was destroyed. She lost
photographs, furniture, her mother’s ceramic nativity set—things that could not be replaced. But she
told herself she was lucky. She was alive. She should have been fine.

She was not fine. In the months that followed, María noticed that she could not watch the evening
news without her stomach turning. If a reporter mentioned the name of the hurricane—or even a
similar name, like a person’s name that sounded close—she felt nauseous, sometimes to the point of
gagging. She began avoiding the television altogether. She stopped driving past the beach, then
stopped driving anywhere near the flood zone. Eventually, she stopped going out at all except to
work. She told herself it was practical: there was no reason to go to the supermarket when her
daughter could drop off groceries. But the truth was that the moment she stepped outside, her body
felt wrong. The air felt too heavy. A car horn or a slammed door made her flinch so hard she would
have to stop walking and brace herself against a wall. She started to believe that only her
apartment was safe—and even there, she slept poorly, waking at every creak and groan of the
building, her heart pounding as if the water had somehow found her again.

At work, María had always been steady and calm. But now, when a patient coughed unexpectedly or
dropped a metal crutch on the tile floor, she felt a spike of alertness that left her shaky for
minutes afterward. She could still do her job, but she did it on autopilot. She found herself
deliberately trying to blank out during the more tedious stretches of the day—pushing her awareness
away from her body, letting her mind go fuzzy, as if she could escape the constant vigilance by
simply not being fully present. It worked, in a way, but it also meant she sometimes forgot what she
had just said to a patient, or found herself standing in the supply closet with no memory of walking
there.

What troubled her most was the way the memory of the hurricane came to her. Sometimes, without
warning, she would feel the full force of that terror—the cold, the pressure on her chest, the
certainty of dying—but there was no image attached to it. She would be standing at the kitchen
counter, making tea, and suddenly her hands would be shaking and her breath would be short, and she
would have no idea why. Other times, she would remember the hurricane and feel a wave of grief so
deep it confused her. She had not lost anyone she loved. But the memory felt like someone
else’s—like she was watching a movie about a stranger who had nearly drowned, not about herself. And
yet the grief was hers, real and heavy, and she could not explain it to anyone.

She had begun to dwell on who she used to be: the María who had coffee with friends on Saturday
mornings, who drove to the coast just to watch the waves, who slept through the night without a
second thought. That woman felt like a ghost now. María did not know how to become her again. She
came to therapy because her daughter had said, gently, that she missed her mother. And María missed
herself too. But she was afraid that talking about the hurricane would bring back the nausea, the
shaking, the sense that she was drowning all over again. She was not sure she could survive that a
second time.
