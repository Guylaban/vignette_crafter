Javier had been a detective for four years, and he had always prided himself on his ability to read
a room, to see past the surface of what people showed him. He could spot a liar from across an
interrogation table. He had never imagined he could be so blind to what was happening in his own
home. The relationship had started with a kind of intensity that he mistook for passion—his ex-
partner, a man he had trusted completely, had a way of making Javier feel like the center of the
universe, and then, slowly, like the cause of every problem. The first slap came during an argument
about nothing, a Tuesday night, and Javier had been so stunned that he had convinced himself it was
an accident. But the accidents kept happening. The last night, the night that ended everything, had
started with the smell of whiskey on his partner’s breath. Javier had felt his stomach turn, a wave
of nausea that he now recognized as a warning his body had understood before his mind did. His
partner had grabbed him by the collar, and Javier had heard the fabric rip, a sound that now played
in his head whenever a memory forced its way in. He had ended up on the kitchen floor, a gash on his
forearm from a broken plate, and when he finally looked up, he saw his partner standing over him
with an expression of cold disgust. Javier had left that night and never gone back, but he had
brought the memory with him.

In the months that followed, Javier found that his own mind had become a hostile environment. The
memories were not vague or distant; they were startlingly clear and detailed, as if his brain had
recorded the entire event in high definition and was now playing it back without his permission. He
would be standing in the grocery store, and the smell of a man’s cologne—something like whiskey and
leather—would hit him, and suddenly he was back on that kitchen floor, hearing the rip of his shirt
and the sound of his own breathing. The nausea would rise in his throat, immediate and physical, and
he would have to abandon his cart and leave. At work, he was surrounded by violence. He saw injuries
on victims and suspects every day—bruises, cuts, broken bones—and each one was a mirror held up to
his own body. He found himself staring at a woman’s black eye during an interview, and he had to
excuse himself to the bathroom, where he vomited. He had come to believe that he was marked by what
had happened, that there was something fundamentally broken in him now that other people could
sense. He was certain that he was permanently an outsider, that the easy camaraderie he used to have
with his colleagues was gone. He had lost his faith in people entirely. They were not what they
seemed, he thought. Everyone was capable of cruelty; he had just been too stupid to see it.

Javier’s response to this internal chaos was to build a fortress. He stopped letting anyone touch
him. When his wife reached for his hand at dinner, he flinched, and he saw the hurt flicker across
her face before she pulled away. He could not explain it to her—how a simple touch felt like a trap,
like a prelude to a blow. He had learned to numb his feelings, to push everything down into a cold,
flat place where it could not hurt him. Even relaxation felt dangerous. On the rare evenings he
tried to sit on the couch and watch a movie, his heart would start racing within minutes, his body
bracing for an attack that was not coming. So he stopped trying. He worked longer hours, took the
night shifts no one else wanted, and filled every waking moment with the mechanical tasks of his
job. The consequence was that his marriage began to feel like a ghost of itself. His wife slept on
the far side of the bed, and they spoke mostly about logistics. He knew she was lonely, but he did
not know how to let her back in.

What finally brought Javier to seek help was a moment at his desk. He had been reviewing case files,
and a photograph of a victim’s facial injuries triggered a memory so vivid that he heard his own
voice pleading, a sound he had not known he had made that night. He sat frozen, staring at nothing,
for nearly ten minutes. When he came back to himself, he realized he was terrified—not of his ex-
partner, but of his own mind. He was afraid that the fortress was going to collapse, and that he
would be left with nothing but the memory and the nausea and the certainty that he would never feel
safe again. He hoped, quietly and without much conviction, that someone could show him a way out.
