Ethan sat in the passenger seat of his wife’s car, the late autumn sun low and golden through the
windshield, and felt the exhaustion wash over him like a tide he could no longer outrun. He was
thirty-three years old, a prosecutor in a mid-sized city, married for six years, and for the past
eighteen months he had been living with something he could not name. He had come to his first
appointment because his wife, Rachel, had finally said she could not watch him disappear anymore. He
had not argued. He was too tired.

The event that had shattered him had taken place on a weekday afternoon in October, two years ago.
Ethan had been walking through the central plaza of the county courthouse complex, a public space he
crossed every day, when a vehicle-mounted improvised explosive device detonated less than a hundred
yards from where he stood. The blast threw him to the ground. He had no memory of the sound
itself—only a pressure that seemed to come from inside his skull, and then the ringing, and the
smoke, and the bodies. He had scrambled to his feet and moved toward the wreckage. He had pulled at
debris. He had tried to help. But the man whose leg he had held while waiting for paramedics had
died before they arrived. The woman who had been thrown against a concrete planter had been
unconscious and bleeding from her ears. Ethan had stayed until the scene was cleared. He had gone
home that night and told Rachel he was fine. He had not been fine.

What troubled him most, he said, was that he could not tell a clear story of what had happened, even
to himself. The memory existed not as a narrative but as a series of sensory fragments that arrived
without warning and without context. He would be standing in the shower, and suddenly he would be
there again—the heat of the blast wave, the grit of pulverized glass on his tongue, the smell of
alcohol from a broken bottle of hand sanitizer that had been on a security guard’s desk. That smell,
specifically, had become a trigger. He could not walk past a bar without his chest tightening. He
could not sit next to someone who had been drinking without feeling his pulse spike. He had stopped
going to dinner with colleagues. He had stopped going anywhere.

The memories, when they came, were more real than normal. They had a vividness that ordinary
recollections lacked, a kind of brutal clarity that made the present moment feel thin and false. He
would be at his desk reviewing case files, and the memory of the blast would descend on him with
such force that he would lose track of where he was. The past felt present. It was not remembering;
it was reliving. And the worst part, the part that kept him awake at night, was the guilt. He could
not forgive himself for not doing more. He had been a prosecutor. His job was to protect people. He
should have seen the vulnerability of that plaza. He should have done something. He had failed.

This belief had grown roots. He had come to distrust others in a way he had not before. People
smiled, they shook his hand, they said they understood, but he was certain that beneath the surface
they were not what they seemed. He had stopped trusting his own judgment. He had stopped trusting
anyone’s. At work, he had become withdrawn, delegating trials he would once have fought to lead. His
supervisor had noticed. Rachel had noticed. He had noticed himself shrinking.

What finally brought him to seek help was not the nightmares, though they came most nights. It was
not the way he flinched at the sound of a car backfiring or a door slamming, though he did. It was
the exhaustion. The feeling of being tired all the time, bone-deep tired, as if he had been running
for two years without stopping. He had come to believe that he would never feel safe again, and he
was not sure he deserved to. But he was also afraid—afraid that if he let himself really think about
what happened, he would not be able to put himself back together. He did not know if he wanted to be
forgiven. He only knew he could not keep living this way.
