Ramon is a 36-year-old tax consultant who lives with his wife in a quiet suburb. He served a single
deployment in a combat zone a decade ago, and for most of that decade he has been
functioning—working, paying bills, keeping up appearances—but something has been eroding beneath the
surface, and now it has begun to crack. The event that haunts him happened during a routine foot
patrol in a village that was supposed to have been cleared. He and his squad were moving through a
narrow alley when an improvised device detonated inside a mud-walled home to his left. The blast
wave threw him against the opposite wall. He remembers the dust, the ringing in his ears, and then
the taste—a thick, metallic, gritty taste that filled his mouth, a combination of blood, dirt, and
something chemical from the explosive residue. He can still taste it now, without warning, when a
memory returns. Sometimes the memory hits him so hard that he has to stop whatever he is doing: he
stops mid-sentence at his desk, stops walking down the hallway at home, stops breathing for a
moment, overwhelmed by a wave of emotion that he cannot name or control. Other times, and these
moments frighten him in a different way, he can recall the scene perfectly but feels nothing at
all—as if he is watching a stranger’s life on a screen, numb and detached, and he wonders whether he
has lost something essential in himself.

In the years following his discharge, Ramon learned to manage. He married a kind woman who knew he
had served but did not push for details. He took a job at a mid-sized accounting firm, where he sits
in a cubicle with a clear line of sight to the emergency exit. He always identifies escape routes
before he feels comfortable anywhere. He does it automatically now: when he walks into a conference
room, when he enters a restaurant, when he sits down in his own living room. He has come to believe
that silence is dangerous. When the office goes quiet—when everyone is absorbed in their
spreadsheets and no one speaks—his chest tightens and he becomes certain that something terrible is
about to happen. He has learned to break the silence himself, asking a colleague a question just to
hear a voice, but he knows this looks odd and he worries people think he is anxious or unfriendly.

The triggers that undo him are not the obvious ones. He does not flinch at loud noises the way he
used to. What undoes him is conflict. A raised voice in a meeting, a tense exchange with a client,
even a sharp word from his wife over something trivial—these send him into a state he cannot
describe to anyone. Certain conversation topics also set him off: talk about the military, about
war, about the Middle East, about sacrifice or service. He steers conversations away from these
subjects so quickly that people notice, and he has learned to excuse himself to the bathroom when he
feels the topic approaching. Feeling physically unwell is another trigger. If he develops a headache
or a stomach ache, his mind immediately races back to that alley, to the moment just before the
blast when something felt wrong in his body but he ignored it.

Ramon has developed a set of habits that have quietly shrunk his world. He avoids being touched.
When a colleague claps him on the shoulder, he jerks away before he can stop himself. His wife has
learned not to reach for him from behind, because he reacts with alarm, his whole body stiffening.
She has stopped trying to hold his hand as often as she used to, and he knows this hurts her, but he
cannot explain to her that unexpected touch feels like an attack. He also avoids positive feelings.
When he feels happy—when he is laughing at a joke, when he is enjoying a meal, when he is feeling
close to his wife—he pushes the feeling away. He has come to believe that letting himself feel good
is dangerous, that the moment he relaxes is the moment something will go wrong. He avoids talking
about the event. He has never told anyone the full story, not his wife, not a friend, not a
therapist. He is certain that if he speaks the words aloud, he will lose control completely, and he
will never get it back.

The consequences have accumulated slowly. He has become distant at work, seen as competent but
unreachable. His wife has stopped asking him how he is feeling because his answers are always the
same: fine. She has started sleeping on the far side of the bed. Last month, she told him she felt
like she was living with a stranger. That is what finally brought him to seek help—not the
nightmares or the startle responses or the taste of grit in his mouth, but the look on her face when
she said those words. He is afraid that talking about what happened will make everything worse, but
he is also afraid that not talking about it has already cost him more than he can afford to lose.
