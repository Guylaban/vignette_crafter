Sovan was 74 years old, a retired welder who had spent forty years with the same fabrication shop in
a small Midwestern city. He was married to Maly, his wife of fifty-one years, and they had three
grown children who lived nearby. He came to therapy at his daughter’s insistence, though he was not
sure what talking could possibly change. The trauma had happened fifty-three years ago, during the
civil war in his home country, when he was a young man of twenty-one. He had been a farmer then, not
a welder, and he had been caught in the crossfire of a battle that swept through his village. He
remembered the crack of gunfire, the screaming, and the moment he had to run for cover across an
open rice paddy. What he remembered most vividly was the sensation of running until his lungs
burned, the air so thick with smoke and dust that he could not catch his breath. He had made it to a
ditch, but he had never stopped feeling that he had done something wrong—that if he had run faster,
or in a different direction, or if he had not been so afraid, the outcome might have been different.
He could not say what outcome he meant. The event itself was over in minutes, but it had never
really ended for him.

For decades, Sovan had managed. He learned to weld, he married, he raised children, he built a life.
But the world had remained a place where he needed to be watchful. He had difficulty concentrating
at work, especially in his later years, because his attention was always split between the task at
hand and scanning the environment for signs of danger. He could not fall asleep easily at night
because he felt he needed to stay alert, listening for sounds that did not belong. His wife Maly had
long since learned not to startle him, and she had accepted that he would wake several times during
the night to check the locks. He had come to believe that the people responsible for what happened
in his village had faced no consequences, and that this was a profound injustice—that what happened
to him was deeply unfair and nothing had been done about it. This belief sat in him like a stone,
and he had never spoken about it to anyone, not even Maly. He avoided talking about the war
entirely. When his children asked about his past, he changed the subject. When a documentary about
the conflict came on television, he left the room. He had developed a habit of redirecting his
thoughts whenever the memory surfaced, pushing it away by focusing on something else—a task, a
noise, a memory of something pleasant. But the memory did not feel like his own. It felt like a
story that had happened to someone else, so extreme that it was hard to believe it had actually
happened to him. He was only remembering parts of it, fragments that did not fit together into a
coherent picture, and this made him doubt his own mind.

The triggers that brought the past into the present were specific and relentless. Car sounds—the
backfire of a truck, the screech of brakes, the rumble of a diesel engine—could send him into a
state of hypervigilance that lasted for hours. The sensation of breathlessness, even from climbing
stairs or carrying groceries, would flood him with the same panic he had felt running across that
paddy. And any media content related to war or conflict, even a news report about a distant country,
was enough to make him feel as though he were back in that moment. His occupation as a welder had
placed him in an environment full of loud, unpredictable noises—metal clanging, machinery starting
up—and he had learned to brace himself constantly. The open floor of the fabrication shop, with its
high ceilings and exposed spaces, had always made him feel vulnerable. He avoided open areas
whenever possible, positioning himself near walls or machinery, and he had turned down promotions
that would have required him to supervise workers on the shop floor. His colleagues had thought he
was just a quiet, hardworking man who liked his corner of the shop. They did not know that he was
hiding.

What brought Sovan to therapy now was not a crisis, but a slow accumulation of exhaustion. He had
retired two years ago, and without the structure of work, the vigilance had become harder to manage.
He found himself avoiding going to the grocery store because the parking lot felt too open. He had
stopped taking walks with Maly because the sound of cars on the road made him jump. She had begun to
worry that he was becoming agoraphobic, and their daughter, a nurse, had recognized the signs of
PTSD. Sovan agreed to come because he did not want to be a burden, and because he was tired of
living this way. What he was most afraid of was that talking about the event would make it more
real, that the fragments would finally cohere into a memory he could not escape. What he was most
hoping for was something he could not put into words—perhaps just the permission to stop running.
