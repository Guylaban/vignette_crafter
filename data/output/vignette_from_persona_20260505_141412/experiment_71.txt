Carlos sat forward in the chair, his thick, scarred hands resting on his knees. At seventy, he was
still a powerfully built man, a lifetime of welding having left him with a frame that was all dense
muscle and stubborn sinew. But his dark eyes, the color of wet earth, held a constant, searching
vigilance. He had come to the clinic at the insistence of his daughter, who had found him, for the
third time that month, standing frozen in the middle of the hardware store, unable to move or speak.
He had been looking at a roll of chain-link fencing.

The event that had broken the world into a before and after happened forty-two years ago, in a small
town outside of San Salvador. Carlos had been a union organizer, a quiet but effective man who could
talk to the coffee workers in a way the landowners could not. One evening, three men in civilian
clothes had pulled him from his home. They did not take him to a station. They took him to a
concrete room beneath a warehouse, a room with a single, bare bulb and a drain in the center of the
floor. For three days, they asked him questions about names and meeting places. For three days, he
did not answer. They used electricity, they used water, and they used a thick, rusted length of
rebar. The worst part, the part that lived in him still, was not the pain itself, but the sound of
the rebar whistling before it hit, and the complete, suffocating certainty that he had no options.
There was no door he could open, no bargain he could strike, no god he could pray to that would stop
the next swing. He was a container for their violence, and the only choice he had was to endure.

The memory of that room did not feel like a memory. When it came, it was not a story he recalled; it
was a room he was suddenly inside. The smell of damp concrete would fill his nostrils while he was
checking the pressure on an acetylene tank. The particular weight of a hand on his shoulder from a
coworker would make his whole body lock, the present moment dissolving into the feeling of being
bound. He could never explain this to anyone. When his daughter asked him what was wrong, the words
would tangle in his throat. The thread of the narrative would vanish, and he would be left with
nothing but the image of the bare bulb and the sound of his own ragged breathing, and he would
simply stop talking.

He had developed a quiet, desperate system for survival. He tried hard not to think about it. He
would catch himself beginning to drift toward that room and he would mentally slam a door, focusing
instead on the precise heat of his welding torch or the number of stitches in a seam. He had also
become, over the decades, a man who said yes to everything. When a foreman barked an unreasonable
order, Carlos would nod and comply. When a neighbor asked for a favor that cost him his entire
Saturday, he would agree without a thought. He had learned that the safest way to exist was to be
invisible and agreeable. His body had learned that resistance was what got you beaten. So he smiled,
he complied, and he seethed. The rage was a constant, low-frequency hum beneath the compliance. When
he did remember—when the mental door failed to slam shut—the rage was not a flash of anger. It was a
deep, oceanic fury that terrified him. He was certain that if he ever let it out, he would not be
able to stop.

He lived in a state of permanent threat assessment. He had come to believe, with the bone-deep
certainty of a man who has been proven right, that you can never know who will harm you. The man in
the pickup truck at the gas station could be one of them. The new supervisor at the fabrication shop
had a certain way of standing that made Carlos’s skin prickle. Every stranger was a potential
interrogator. He was also certain that the whole thing was his fault. He blamed himself for the way
he had acted—for the fact that he had not fought back harder, that he had not found a way to escape,
that he had not done more to stop what happened. This guilt was a cold, hard stone in his gut. He
would never forgive himself for his own inaction, for the fact that he had survived while others, he
was sure, had not.

The consequences had accumulated slowly, like rust. He had outlived his wife by eight years, but he
had never been able to be present for her. She had learned not to touch him from behind. He had lost
contact with his old friends from the union; they reminded him of a person he no longer was. His job
as a welder, the one place he had felt competent and in control, had become a minefield. The high-
pitched hiss of a gas line could sound like a gunshot. The clang of a dropped steel beam was a
gunshot. The sound of a car backfiring in the parking lot was always, always a gunshot. He would
drop his helmet and stand perfectly still, his heart hammering, his mind returning to that concrete
room where the only sound was the one that preceded the pain.

What had finally brought him here was not the fear, but the loneliness. He had been standing in his
empty kitchen, staring at the stove, unable to decide whether to make coffee because the decision
felt as impossible as choosing which bone to break first. He had realized that he was not living. He
was just waiting. He was waiting for the next bad thing, the next trigger, the next time the past
would swallow the present. He was terrified of what he might find in therapy—terrified that he would
have to open the door to that room and look at it directly. But he was more terrified of the
alternative. He was hoping, with a small, fragile part of himself that he had thought was dead, that
someone could teach him how to stop holding his breath.
