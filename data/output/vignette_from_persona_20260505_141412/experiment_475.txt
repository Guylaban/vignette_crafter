When the mugging happened, Phan was coming out of the hospital chapel. It was late, nearly midnight,
after a long vigil with a family whose son had not survived surgery. He had been carrying a small
wooden cross in his jacket pocket, something he always did before night visits, and he remembered
thinking about the wife’s face as he walked toward the parking garage. The garage was nearly empty.
He heard footsteps behind him, and before he could turn, a man’s arm locked around his throat from
behind. Another man stood in front of him, breathing hard, the sound wet and close. They demanded
his wallet, his phone, his car keys. He gave them everything. But what stayed with him was not the
knife they pressed against his ribs, nor the moment they shoved him to the concrete. It was the
heavy breathing—loud, ragged, right in his ear—and the smell of cigarette smoke and sweat on the
jacket of the man holding him. He could still smell it sometimes, suddenly, without warning, as if
the man were standing behind him again.

In the weeks after, Phan began to notice things he could not explain to himself. The images came
first: not the whole event, but fragments. The glare of the parking garage lights on the concrete
floor. The scuffed boots of the man in front of him. The cross still in his pocket, untouched. These
pictures arrived without context, often when he was trying to pray or prepare a sermon. He would be
sitting in his office, reading scripture, and suddenly he was back on that cold floor, and the rage
would rise in his chest so fast it frightened him. He had never thought of himself as an angry
person. He had counseled grieving families, sat with the dying, held space for rage in others. But
this rage was different—it was hot and wordless, and it was directed at himself. He had come to
believe, quietly and without telling anyone, that the event happened to him because of the sort of
person he was. Somebody else would not have gotten into that situation. Somebody else would have
been more alert, more careful, more worthy of protection. He had been too trusting, too soft, too
naive. He could not stop bad things from happening to him. That was just the truth.

His work began to suffer in ways he could not fully name. As a chaplain, he moved through the
hospital constantly: ICU waiting rooms, oncology wards, the emergency department. But now, whenever
he walked into a crowded elevator or a full waiting room, his chest tightened. He started taking the
stairs, even to the seventh floor. He began to avoid the parking garage entirely, parking three
blocks away and walking through the main entrance, even in rain. He told himself it was practical.
He did not tell anyone that heavy breathing sounds—from a patient on oxygen, a visitor out of
breath, a nurse hurrying past—made him freeze mid-sentence. He started to dread the moments when a
family member would be crying, gasping, trying to speak through sobs. He could not separate that
sound from the sound in the garage.

The most concrete consequence came when his supervisor asked him to lead a monthly support group for
victims of violent crime. Phan had led the group for years. He was good at it. But now, the thought
of sitting in a room with people whose stories might echo his own, whose breathing might quicken as
they spoke, filled him with a dread he could not explain. He declined, citing a scheduling conflict.
His supervisor accepted it without question, but Phan knew what he had done. He had withdrawn from
the very work that had once given his life meaning. He had become someone who could not sit with
suffering because his own was too close.

What finally brought him to seek help was not the avoidance, not the rage, not the smells that came
back unbidden. It was a Sunday morning, during a service for the families of patients who had died
in the hospital that quarter. He stood at the podium, opened his mouth to speak, and saw the face of
the man in front of him—the one who had held the knife—and felt the rage again, so strong his hands
shook. He closed the service early and went to his office and sat alone for an hour. He was afraid
of what he might become if he did not find a way to live with what had happened. He was afraid he
would stop being a chaplain altogether. He was afraid that the person he had been before the
garage—the person who could hold another’s pain without crumbling—was already gone. He wanted to
believe that was not true.
