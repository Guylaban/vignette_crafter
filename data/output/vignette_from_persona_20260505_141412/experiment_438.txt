Leila is a 59-year-old police officer, a married woman who has spent over three decades on the
force, and she still cannot sit in the passenger seat of her own car. When her husband drives, she
grips the door handle, her eyes scanning the road ahead for any sign of trouble, her own heart
pounding so hard she can feel it in her throat. It is that pounding, that familiar surge of
adrenaline, that now terrifies her more than any suspect ever did. She had learned to trust her
body’s alarms on the job—that spike in heart rate that meant danger was near. But now, that same
signal feels like a betrayal. Her body keeps reacting as if she is still in danger, even when she is
sitting safely in her own driveway, and she cannot make it stop.

The event that broke this trust happened eighteen months ago, during a routine domestic disturbance
call. Leila and her partner arrived at a small apartment to find a man in the grip of a violent
rage. He was larger than she was, younger, and when he turned on her, she felt the full weight of
his fury. He struck her with a lamp, then with his fists. She remembers the sound of it—the crack of
the lamp base against her skull, the wet thud of his knuckles against her jaw. She can still hear
those sounds in her head when the memory comes, clear and detailed, as if she is back in that
cramped hallway. The terror she felt then returns undiminished, a wave that washes over her without
warning. She was saved by her partner’s intervention, but in the months that followed, something
inside her shifted permanently.

At first, Leila tried to carry on. She returned to work, but found herself watching every civilian
with suspicion. She became hypervigilant for dangerous people, scanning faces in the station, in the
grocery store, in her own neighborhood. She began to believe that she could not rely on other
people—not her colleagues, not her husband, not anyone. They had not been there when she needed
them. The assault felt deeply unfair, and nothing had been done about it. The man was arrested, but
the charges were reduced. He served six months. Leila saw no justice. The injustice sat in her chest
like a stone, and she carried it everywhere.

To manage the growing dread, Leila started to isolate herself. She stopped answering phone calls
from her sisters. She declined invitations to dinner with friends. She told her husband she was
tired, that she needed space. Over time, she cut off contact with almost everyone she had known. The
only person she still spoke to was her husband, and even that felt strained. She could not explain
to him why she flinched when he touched her shoulder, why she insisted on driving everywhere, why
she would not let him hold her at night.

The worst part, for Leila, was the feeling that her own mind was turning against her. She began to
monitor her thoughts constantly, checking for signs that she was going crazy. Every intrusive
memory, every flash of the hallway, every echo of the lamp cracking against her skull—she treated
these as evidence that she was losing her grip. She would catch herself thinking about the assault
during a quiet moment and immediately try to push it away, as if the thought itself were a symptom
of something worse.

Now, nearly a year and a half later, Leila has come to her first appointment because her husband
threatened to leave if she did not get help. She is afraid of that, too—afraid of losing the only
person she has left, and afraid of what she might become if she is alone. What she hopes for, though
she cannot quite say it out loud, is that someone can show her how to stop the pounding in her
chest, how to sit still without scanning the room for threats, how to remember what happened without
feeling like she is still there, still fighting for her life.
