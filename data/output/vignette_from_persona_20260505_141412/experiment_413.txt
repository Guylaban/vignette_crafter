Mariana, a 26-year-old school counselor of Latin American descent, still remembers the exact sound
of a glass ashtray hitting the living room wall. She was seven years old, hiding behind the sofa in
the small apartment she shared with her mother and her mother’s boyfriend, Manuel. She had been told
to stay in her room, but she had crept out to see why her mother was crying. What she saw was
Manuel, drunk, shoving her mother against the kitchen counter while shouting that she had "made him
do this." Mariana had tried to intervene, running into the room and pulling at Manuel’s sleeve,
begging him to stop. He had turned and backhanded her across the face, sending her sprawling. Her
mother had screamed, and then the police had come—called by a neighbor—but by the time they arrived,
Manuel was sitting calmly on the couch, and her mother, terrified and bruised, told the officers it
was nothing, that she had fallen. The officers left. Nothing changed. Manuel stayed. And Mariana
learned, at seven years old, that the people who were supposed to protect her—her mother, the
police—would not. She came to believe, with a certainty that never faded, that the event had
happened because of how she acted, because she had left her room, because she had tried to help. She
had made it worse.

Now, nearly two decades later, Mariana works in a middle school in a suburban district, guiding
children through their own confusions and hurts. She is good at her job, patient and kind, but she
carries a constant, exhausting vigilance. She scans every room she enters for exits, for angles, for
who might be angry. She cannot tolerate having her back to a door. In her own small apartment, she
feels no safety; she sleeps with the lights on in the hallway and has installed a chain lock she
checks three times before bed. The sound of a key in the lock, even her own, makes her heart stop.
At work, she has learned to sit with her desk facing the door, her chair pushed against the wall so
no one can approach from behind. When a student taps her shoulder unexpectedly, she flinches so hard
that the child sometimes steps back, startled, and Mariana has to force a smile and apologize.

The memory of that night does not come to her as a coherent story she can tell. When a colleague
asked, during a team-building exercise, about her "most challenging childhood experience," Mariana
opened her mouth to describe the ashtray, the shouting, the taste of blood from her lip—and found
herself unable to speak. The words scattered. She felt the same overwhelming heat and helplessness
she had felt as a child, and she had to excuse herself to the bathroom, where she sat on the floor
for ten minutes, shaking. The memory returns without warning: a certain smell of cooking oil, a
man’s raised voice in the hallway at school, even the particular quality of afternoon light slanting
through blinds. Each time, it is as if she is back behind that sofa, small and frozen.

She has developed a quiet, methodical set of strategies to manage this. She avoids the street where
her childhood apartment stood, taking a longer route home even if it adds twenty minutes to her
commute. She replays the night in her mind, over and over, trying to find the exact moment where she
could have acted differently—if she had stayed in her room, if she had not pulled on Manuel’s
sleeve, if she had screamed louder for the police to stay. These rumination sessions can last hours,
leaving her drained and no closer to an answer. She has come to believe, deeply, that people cannot
be trusted. She keeps her colleagues at a careful distance. She has not dated in three years; the
thought of being vulnerable with a man, of letting him into her home, feels impossible.

What finally brought Mariana to seek help was a moment at work last month. A father, angry about a
disciplinary action against his son, raised his voice in her office. It was not loud, not
threatening—but the tone, the cadence, the way he pointed his finger, sent her into a full
dissociative flashback. She could not respond. She sat frozen, staring, until the school principal
intervened. The next day, her supervisor gently suggested she take some leave. Mariana is terrified
of losing her job, the one place where she feels she has some purpose. She is hoping, though she
cannot quite say it out loud, for a way to stop feeling like a seven-year-old girl hiding behind a
sofa every time the world gets a little too loud.
