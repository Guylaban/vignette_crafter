Kofi was a 77-year-old software engineer who had lived in the United States for nearly forty years,
but the country he carried inside him was the one he had fled as a young man. He had been a
university student in a West African nation during a period of military rule, and one evening
soldiers had come for him. They had not explained why. They had simply shouted orders—*get down,
hands on your head, do not move*—and he had obeyed, because to disobey a command was to invite a
bullet. He remembered the moments as a series of snapshots: the boot on his back, the concrete floor
against his cheek, the smell of diesel and sweat, the sound of a radio crackling static. He
remembered the faces of the soldiers, bored and efficient. But he did not remember feeling afraid.
When he tried to recall that night now, he felt nothing at all—a kind of emotional deadness, as
though the person on that floor had been someone else, a stranger whose story he had once been told.
The images were clear, but they belonged to another life.

In the weeks after the event, Kofi had managed to escape the country and eventually resettled in the
United States. He married, raised children, built a career writing code for a financial firm. For
decades, he functioned well. But in the past few years, something had shifted. The snapshots had
begun surfacing unbidden, often at quiet moments—while he was waiting for the train, or lying in bed
at night. They came as pictures, not stories: a soldier’s hand reaching down, a door being kicked
open, the glare of a flashlight. And alongside the images came a creeping sensation of unreality, as
though the world around him were thinning, becoming transparent. He would be sitting at his desk,
and suddenly his hands would look unfamiliar, or the room would feel two-dimensional, like a stage
set. The feeling frightened him more than the memories themselves.

Kofi had developed a set of habits to manage these intrusions, though he did not think of them as
strategies. He avoided going out whenever possible. He had stopped attending the community center
where he used to play chess, and he no longer walked to the corner store for his morning paper. When
his wife asked him to accompany her to the grocery store, he found reasons to stay home—his back
hurt, he was tired, he had work to finish. In truth, he was afraid of encountering anyone who might
give him an order. A security guard telling him to move along, a cashier asking him to step forward,
even a neighbor calling out a greeting—any command, however benign, sent a jolt through him. His
pulse would spike, and the snapshots would press in, and he would feel that terrible detachment
creeping over him like a fog.

He had also begun avoiding people more broadly. He had retired from software engineering two years
earlier, but he still kept in touch with a few former colleagues. Now he let their calls go to
voicemail. He told himself he was busy, but the truth was that being around others required him to
stay vigilant, to monitor every word and tone of voice for the possibility of a demand. It was
exhausting. Instead, he kept his mind occupied constantly—listening to podcasts at full volume while
he did chores, working through complex puzzles, watching dense documentaries late into the night. If
his mind was full, there was no room for the pictures to break through. The cost of this constant
distraction was that he rarely felt present with his wife. She had begun to complain that he seemed
distant, that he was not listening. He knew she was right, but he did not know how to stop.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was a moment in the kitchen three weeks ago. His wife had
asked him to pass the salt—a simple request, nothing more—and he had felt his chest tighten, his
vision narrow, his hands begin to tremble. He had stood frozen, unable to move, while the snapshots
flickered behind his eyes: a soldier’s boot, a flashlight, a radio. His wife had looked at him with
concern and confusion, and he had not been able to explain. That night, he lay awake and understood
that he could no longer manage this alone. He was afraid of what the memories might do to him if he
let them in, but he was also afraid of losing the life he had built—of becoming a stranger to his
wife, a ghost in his own home. He wanted to feel real again. He wanted to be able to pass the salt
without falling apart.
