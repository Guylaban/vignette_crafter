The morning of the incident, Marcus had been finishing a late shift at the design studio, the only
one left on the fourth floor of the converted warehouse downtown. He was packing his tablet when he
heard the commotion from the street—shouting, then the screech of tires. He looked out the window
and saw a patrol car angled across the intersection, doors open. Two officers were struggling with a
man on the ground near a bodega. Marcus watched, frozen, as one officer’s knee pressed into the
man’s back while the other swung a baton. He saw the man’s face go slack, then a bloom of red on the
pavement beneath his head. Marcus didn’t look away. He couldn’t. He stood at that window for what
felt like an hour, his coffee going cold in his hand, until another officer noticed him and shouted
for him to step back. He stumbled to his desk and sat in the dark until the police tape came down.

That was eighteen months ago. Now, Marcus cannot see a police cruiser without his chest tightening.
He takes three different routes to work to avoid the station two blocks from his office, and if he
catches sight of a uniform in a coffee shop or on the sidewalk, he will turn around and walk the
other way, even if it makes him late. He has stopped going to the grocery store near his apartment
because a man with a scarred cheek works the register, and the sight of any visible injury—a
bandage, a bruise, a healing cut—drops him straight back to that night. He will be standing in the
cereal aisle, and suddenly he is at the window again, watching the blood spread, and his hands start
to shake and his vision goes narrow. He has learned to keep his eyes on the floor in public, to scan
only the lower halves of people’s bodies so he won’t catch a glimpse of a wound or a uniform.

At home, Marcus spends hours on his phone, scrolling through design feeds or playing matching games,
because the moment he puts it down the thoughts come. He has become an expert at keeping his mind
occupied. He listens to podcasts while he showers, has the television on while he eats dinner, and
falls asleep with earbuds in. His wife, Lisa, has stopped trying to talk to him after nine o’clock
because he cannot hold a conversation—he is too busy running a mental treadmill to keep the memory
at bay. When she asked him last week what he was thinking about, he said nothing, which was true in
the sense that he was working very hard to think of nothing at all.

But the memory does not wait for permission. It arrives without warning, triggered by something as
small as the sound of a car door slamming or the particular quality of evening light through
venetian blinds. When it comes, it is not a recollection—it is a full-body event. His heart hammers,
his palms sweat, and he feels the same helpless horror he felt standing at that window. If he is in
the middle of a sentence, he stops. He cannot finish. He has tried to tell Lisa what happened,
twice, and both times he lost the thread halfway through, the words dissolving into a kind of
static, and he ended up just shaking his head and walking away.

Marcus has come to believe that something essential in him was broken that night. He no longer finds
pleasure in the work he used to love—the layouts feel hollow, the colors meaningless. He goes
through the motions at his desk, meeting deadlines, but he cannot remember the last time he felt
proud of a project. He is certain that his life has been fundamentally destroyed, that there is no
version of himself that exists before the incident and no version that will exist after. What eats
at him most is the injustice of it. He was just a bystander. He did nothing wrong. And yet he is the
one who cannot sleep, who cannot sit in a restaurant without his eyes tracking the exits, who feels
his body hum with tension from the moment he wakes up until the moment he finally, exhaustedly,
passes out. No one has been held accountable for what he saw. Nothing has been done.

What finally brought Marcus to seek help was a moment two weeks ago, when his nine-year-old daughter
asked him to play catch in the backyard and he said no because he could hear a siren three blocks
away and knew he would not be able to concentrate on the ball. He watched her face fall, and he felt
a kind of terror that was different from the usual fear—a colder, clearer dread. He is afraid that
he is disappearing, that the man he used to be has already been replaced by this vigilant, hollow
version of himself. He does not know if he can get the old Marcus back. But he knows he cannot keep
watching his daughter’s face fall.
