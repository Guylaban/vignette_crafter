Kai was eighteen years old, working as a project manager for a small construction firm, a job that
required him to coordinate with crews of men on job sites every day. He had been with his girlfriend
for just over a year, and she was the only person he felt truly safe around. But that safety was
fragile, and he knew it. What brought him to therapy was a growing sense that he was losing control
of his own life—that the strategies he had developed to keep himself together were starting to
unravel.

When Kai was a child, he had witnessed his father beat his mother. He could not remember the whole
event. What stayed with him were pieces—the sound of a heavy hand connecting with flesh, the sharp
smell of beer on his father’s breath, the sight of his mother’s body crumpling against the kitchen
cabinets. The memory came in fragments that did not fit together like a normal story. There was no
beginning, no middle, no end. Just terror, raw and immediate, every time a piece surfaced. And when
it did, it was not like remembering something from the past. It was like being there again, his
small body frozen in the doorway, his chest tight, his throat closed. The same terror he had felt at
six years old would wash over him in the present moment, leaving him breathless and shaking.

In the years since, Kai had learned to manage. But his management had become a prison. He had come
to believe that men were dangerous, that being alone with a man meant being vulnerable to violence.
On job sites, he made sure he was never the last person in a room with a coworker. He would step out
for a cigarette, or pretend he had a phone call, or simply walk away. He smoked constantly—a pack a
day, sometimes more—because the ritual of lighting up gave him something to do with his hands and a
reason to step outside. He drank most evenings, not to get drunk but to take the edge off the
hypervigilance that hummed beneath his skin like a live wire. He had also started taking his
girlfriend’s anxiety medication when she was not looking, because it helped him sleep. He knew it
was not his prescription, but he told himself he needed it more than she did.

The avoidance had crept into every part of his life. He avoided people in general now—not just men.
He had stopped going to the grocery store after dark. He had stopped visiting his mother because she
still lived in the same house, and the smell of alcohol anywhere—even cooking wine, even hand
sanitizer—sent him into a spiral. He had also stopped riding his bicycle, the one thing that had
once made him feel free, because the sensation of being on two wheels, exposed and moving, reminded
him of the helplessness of childhood. He could not explain why. It just did.

At home, he checked the locks on the doors and windows three or four times before bed, sometimes
getting up again to check them once more. He jumped at ordinary sounds—the clatter of a dropped
tool, the slam of a car door, the creak of the house settling. His girlfriend had started to notice
the way he watched other people when they were out together, his eyes tracking strangers as if they
were predators. He watched their hands. He watched their faces. He was certain that if he let his
guard down for even a second, someone would hurt him.

What finally brought Kai to therapy was a panic attack on a job site two weeks ago. He had been
alone in a storage trailer with a subcontractor, a man he had worked with for months. The man had
said something ordinary—asked about a delivery—and Kai had frozen. His heart had pounded so hard he
thought he might pass out. He had fled without explanation, and his boss had told him to take some
time off. His girlfriend had found him crying in the bathroom when he got home. She had held him and
told him he needed help. He was terrified of what would happen if he did not get better—terrified of
losing his job, of losing her, of spending the rest of his life jumping at shadows. But he was also
terrified of what getting better might mean: facing the pieces of a memory he had spent years trying
to keep at bay.
