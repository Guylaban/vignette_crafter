Leilani was forty-three years old, a physical therapist who had spent nearly two decades helping
people rebuild strength after accidents and surgeries. She lived with her partner in a quiet suburb
and had always thought of herself as someone steady, someone who could handle pressure. That changed
in a single moment, ten months ago, in the outpatient rehabilitation gym where she worked.

A patient—a middle-aged man recovering from a hip replacement—had been struggling with a balance
exercise. Leilani had positioned herself beside him, one hand on his gait belt, the other ready to
correct his stance. She did not see the small puddle of water on the floor, left by the cleaning
crew. When the patient’s foot slipped, he lurched sideways, and Leilani instinctively reached to
catch him. But her footing was uncertain, and instead of steadying him, she pulled him off balance.
He fell hard, his head striking the corner of a treatment table. The sound was something she would
never forget. There was blood immediately, and the man’s eyes went blank. He survived, but the
injury left him with lasting cognitive deficits and a permanent limp. The hospital’s investigation
cleared Leilani of fault—it was ruled an accident—but that did not matter to her. She knew what she
had done. She had been the one holding him. She had not done enough to stop what happened.

In the weeks that followed, Leilani could not tell a clear story of the fall, even to herself. The
memory existed as fragments: the wet floor, the patient’s surprised face, the impact, the blood. But
the sequence was jumbled, and when she tried to piece it together, her mind would slide away. The
fragments that did surface felt more real than ordinary memories—sharper, louder, as if she were
still in that room. Sometimes, without warning, she would feel herself back there, her hand
outstretched, the weight of the patient pulling her forward. Her heart would pound, her palms would
sweat, and she would be certain for a terrifying second that it was happening again.

She quit her job within a month. The gym itself became unbearable, but more than that, she could not
face the possibility of another patient trusting her. She started staying home, and when she did go
out—to the grocery store, to a coffee shop—she always made sure to sit or stand near an exit. She
scanned every room she entered, not for exits only but for anything that could be used as a weapon:
a fire extinguisher, a heavy lamp, a glass bottle. She did not know why she looked, but she could
not stop. The world had become a place full of dangers she had not seen before.

She began drinking more than she used to, but it was the smell of alcohol that undid her. The
cleaning solution in the gym had been alcohol-based. Now, any whiff of it—from a hand sanitizer,
from a friend’s drink—sent her stomach clenching. She stopped going to restaurants where people
ordered cocktails. She asked her partner not to keep wine in the house. When that was not enough,
she started using marijuana in the evenings, not to get high exactly, but to blur the edges of her
mind so the memories would not press in so hard. She also started spending money she did not have.
Online shopping, mostly. Clothes, shoes, gadgets she did not need. The packages arrived, she opened
them, and for a few minutes she felt nothing but the small pleasure of something new. Then the shame
would return, heavier than before.

The shame was the worst part. When she remembered the event—and she remembered it many times a
day—she felt it wash over her like a hot wave. She was certain she would never forgive herself. She
had been the professional in that room. She should have seen the water. She should have held him
differently. She should have been stronger. Her partner tried to reason with her, tried to say it
was an accident, but Leilani could not accept that. The person she had become—this fearful, guilty,
hypervigilant woman who could not work, who avoided friends, who spent money she did not have to
escape thoughts she could not face—felt unrecognisable. She did not know who she was anymore. She
avoided talking about the event entirely. If someone brought it up, she changed the subject or left
the room. She was certain that if she let herself think about it fully, she would not be able to
handle it. The thoughts would swallow her whole.

What finally brought her to therapy was a confrontation with her partner, who had found credit card
bills and asked her directly what was happening. Leilani had no answer. She came to the first
session looking exhausted, her hands clasped tightly in her lap. She said she wanted to stop feeling
like a stranger to herself. But what she was most afraid of, she admitted quietly, was that talking
about the event would make it real in a way she could not undo. She was hoping, against hope, that
there was a way to carry what had happened without being destroyed by it.
