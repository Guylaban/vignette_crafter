therapy at the urging of her younger sister, who had grown alarmed by how isolated Leila had become
over the past two years. Leila sat forward in her chair, her hands clasped tightly in her lap, and
spoke in a low, careful voice about what had happened to her son, Adam.

Two years ago, when Adam was twelve, he developed a sudden, severe bacterial infection that spiraled
into sepsis within hours. Leila had been the one to notice the mottling on his skin, the way his
breathing had become shallow and fast. She drove him to the emergency room herself, and she
remembered every second of that drive—the stoplights she ran, the way her own voice had sounded
strange and far away as she screamed at the triage nurse. Adam spent eleven days in the pediatric
intensive care unit. He recovered fully, but Leila had not.

She now worked at a busy retail pharmacy in a strip mall, and the job that had once felt like a
calling had become a minefield. Every ambulance siren she heard through the storefront windows made
her drop whatever she was holding. A customer who mentioned a hospital visit would send her heart
racing so fast she had to excuse herself to the back room. She had begun to dread the monthly
inventory checks because they required her to walk past the first-aid aisle, where the sight of
sterile bandages and antiseptic wipes brought back the smell of the ICU. She had stopped scheduling
her own annual physical exam, and when a small cut on her finger became infected, she treated it
herself with over-the-counter ointment rather than see a doctor. The thought of lying on an
examination table, of someone pressing a stethoscope to her chest, made her feel as though she were
suffocating.

At home, Leila tried hard not to think about those eleven days. She had learned to steer her mind
away whenever a memory surfaced—the beeping monitors, the way Adam’s hand had felt so small and
still in hers. But the effort of suppression left her drained, and at night, when she lay in the
dark, her body refused to settle. She would startle at the creak of the building’s pipes, at the hum
of the refrigerator, at sounds she knew were harmless. Her mind would not stop. She lay awake
thinking about why this had happened to her, why her son had been the one to get sick, why she had
not noticed the infection sooner. She had come to believe that there was something fundamentally
wrong with her, that she was a weak person for still being so afraid when Adam was healthy and
running around the soccer field. She was certain that nothing good could ever happen to her again,
that the world had revealed itself as a place where catastrophe could strike at any moment, and that
she was too broken to cope with it.

The consequences of her avoidance had crept up on her slowly. She had stopped going to family
gatherings because her sister’s house was near the hospital where Adam had been treated. She had
stopped dating, too, because the thought of explaining her life to someone new felt impossible. Her
closest friend at the pharmacy had asked her several times if she was okay, and Leila had brushed
her off with a smile that felt like a lie. She had come to therapy not because she believed it would
help, but because her sister had said, with tears in her eyes, that she missed the person Leila used
to be. What Leila hoped for, more than anything, was to stop being afraid of a danger that had
already passed. But what she feared most was that the fear itself was proof that something inside
her was broken beyond repair.
