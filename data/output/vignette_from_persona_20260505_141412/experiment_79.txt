For David Nguyen, a 47-year-old park ranger of Vietnamese descent, the trauma did not arrive with a
bang or a crash. It came as a whisper that grew into a scream over the course of a single night. His
son, then twelve, had been playing in the yard when a sudden fever sent him to the emergency room.
What began as a suspected flu spiraled into septic shock within hours. David stood in the
fluorescent glare of the pediatric ICU, watching machines breathe for his child, the antiseptic
smell of alcohol wipes and sterilized instruments filling his nostrils with every breath. The
doctors spoke in measured tones, but David heard only the word “possible” attached to “organ
failure” and “mortality.” For three days, he did not leave the hospital. He did not sleep. He only
watched, and waited, and prayed to ancestors he had not spoken to in years.

His son survived. But David did not come home the same man.

In the weeks that followed, the world became a landscape of hidden dangers. The smell of rubbing
alcohol—from a hand sanitizer dispenser at the grocery store, from a cleaning cart in a
hallway—would stop him cold. His chest would tighten, his mouth go dry, and he would feel a wave of
dizziness so profound that he feared he might collapse. That dizziness, that lightheaded spinning,
became a terror of its own. He began to associate the feeling of being unsteady with the
helplessness of those hospital hours. He could not simply feel faint; he felt as though the ground
was dissolving beneath him, as if the universe was about to take something else from him.

The alcohol smell was the worst. He had never been a heavy drinker, but after his son’s recovery, a
neighbor brought over a bottle of whiskey as a gesture of relief. David took one sip and gagged. The
smell—ethanol and grain—had become fused in his memory with the antiseptic wipes used to clean his
son’s IV ports. Now, any trace of alcohol, even on a friend’s breath at a barbecue, sent him into a
spiral of hypervigilance. He would excuse himself, find a corner, and try to breathe.

His job as a park ranger, once a source of pride and peace, became a minefield. The ranger station
had a first-aid kit with alcohol wipes. He stopped entering that room. He asked a colleague to
retrieve gear for him, offering vague excuses about a bad back. The dizziness would strike without
warning—during a quiet patrol, while he was alone on a trail—and he would have to sit down on the
forest floor, gripping the earth, certain he was about to pass out. He never did, but the certainty
that he might grew stronger each time.

David began to dwell. Not on the illness itself, but on the people he blamed for it. He became
consumed with thoughts about the first doctor who had dismissed his son’s symptoms as a virus. He
would replay that conversation in his mind, crafting arguments, imagining confrontations. He spent
hours ruminating on how to get even with the hospital administration, the insurance adjuster, the
nurse who had been slow to respond. He knew, rationally, that no one had acted with malice. But the
anger felt more manageable than the fear. It gave him something to hold onto. He would mutter to
himself while driving, rehearsing lawsuits, imagining the doctor’s face when he was finally exposed.
His wife, a high school teacher, began to notice that he was no longer present at dinner. He was
somewhere else, arguing with ghosts.

He avoided any conversation about his son’s illness. When friends asked how the boy was
doing—healthy now, thankfully—David would change the subject or leave the room. He could not bear to
hear the story told by someone else, because their version would be too simple, too safe. They would
say “he’s fine now,” but David knew that fine was a fragile word. Being reminded of the event by
others felt like a betrayal. They did not understand that the threat had never really ended. It had
only gone dormant.

Over the months, his world shrank. He stopped attending his son’s soccer games because the crowd and
the noise made him dizzy. He stopped visiting his own parents, who lived near a hospital. His
marriage grew quiet. His wife stopped asking what he was thinking, because the answer was always the
same: a replay of a conversation he could not win, a smell he could not escape, a fear he could not
name.

What finally brought David to seek help was a moment on the job. He was leading a school group on a
nature walk when a child tripped and scraped her knee. A teacher pulled out a first-aid kit. The
smell of antiseptic hit David like a wave. He turned, walked into the woods, and vomited behind a
tree. The children did not see. But David saw himself clearly for the first time in two years. He
was a man who could no longer do the thing he loved, who could not be present for his own son’s

name. He wanted to stop. He wanted to walk back out of the woods and not be afraid of the smell of
