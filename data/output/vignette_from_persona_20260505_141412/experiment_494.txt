Ray, a thirty-nine-year-old construction worker, had been divorced for three years by the time he
walked into the clinic, though he would have struggled to say whether the divorce was a cause or a
symptom of what had happened to him. What he could say, with a certainty that felt like a physical
weight in his chest, was that his life had split cleanly into before and after the day on the job
site. He had been working a high-rise frame in downtown Phoenix, standing on a steel beam six
stories up, when the man two beams over—a younger guy named Derek who had just started on the
crew—lost his footing. Ray saw it happen in a sequence of still images that would never leave him:
the way Derek’s hard hat tipped back, the way his arms went wide like he was trying to catch the
air, the way his boot slipped off the wet steel. Ray heard the impact, but more than that, he
remembered the smell—the hot metallic tang of fresh rebar and diesel exhaust mixed with the sudden,
sharp sweetness of blood. The taste of it, too, came back to him sometimes, like copper and old
pennies coating his tongue, even when he hadn’t eaten anything.

In the months afterward, Ray had tried to keep working. He needed the money, and he needed to show
himself he wasn’t afraid. But the site itself became a minefield of triggers. Every time he stepped
onto a beam above the third floor, his chest would tighten and his vision would narrow until he felt
like he was watching himself from somewhere outside his own body. He learned to do that on purpose.
When the images came—always the images, Derek’s arms, Derek’s boot, the awful stillness—Ray would
focus on a point in the distance and deliberately let his mind go blank, as if he could step out of
his own head and leave the pictures behind. It worked for a few minutes at a time, but it left him
feeling hollow and disconnected, as though he were walking through his own life from a great
distance.

The anger was the hardest part. Ray had never been an angry man, but now he felt a constant low-
grade fury simmering beneath his skin, and it terrified him. He had come to believe that if he ever
let it out, even a little, he would destroy something—maybe himself, maybe someone else. So he
clamped down on it, refused to acknowledge it, refused to let his voice rise or his fists clench. He
told himself that anger was what got people killed on a job site. But the effort of holding it in
was exhausting, and the exhaustion made him feel weak, and the weakness confirmed something he had
begun to suspect: that he was inadequate, fundamentally not enough to handle what life threw at him.
The world, he now understood, was not a place where hard work or caution could protect you. It was a
place where bad things happened for no reason, and he had no power to stop them.

The gambling started about six months after Derek died. Ray found a small card room near his
apartment, and on nights when the images were too loud or the blankness wouldn’t come, he would go
there and sit at a blackjack table for hours. The noise, the lights, the constant small decisions—it
gave his mind something to do besides replay the fall. He didn’t think of it as a problem. He
thought of it as a way to survive the evenings. But the money he lost began to add up, and he found
himself borrowing from the crew’s gas fund, then from a friend, then from a loan shark who charged
interest by the week. The debt sat on him like another weight, but it also gave him something
concrete to worry about, which was a relief compared to the abstract terror of the fall.

By the time he came to therapy, Ray had been off the job for two months. He had stopped going to the
site altogether after a foreman asked him to climb a ladder and Ray had frozen at the third rung,
his hands shaking, the taste of copper flooding his mouth. He was living on savings and the small
checks from his divorce settlement, and he knew he couldn’t keep it up. What finally pushed him to
call was a moment in the card room: he had lost seven hundred dollars in one night, and as he drove
home, he realized he had been trying to blank out so hard that he had missed his exit by ten miles.
He had no idea how he had gotten there. That scared him more than the debt. He came to the clinic
because he was afraid of what he might do next, but also because somewhere underneath the fear, he
was hoping that someone could show him a way to live in his own body again without having to leave
it on purpose.
