Kofi is a 23-year-old urban planner who came to therapy at the urging of his supervisor, after
several months of declining performance and increasing withdrawal from colleagues. He sat silently
for the first ten minutes of the session, his eyes fixed on the floor, before finally beginning to
speak in fragments—short, halting sentences that seemed to cost him enormous effort. The event that
had fractured his life had happened nearly eighteen months earlier, on a warm evening in late
spring, when he was still a graduate student.

He had been walking home from a late study session through a neighborhood that was usually quiet. A
group of young men had been drinking on a stoop, and as he passed, one of them shouted something
Kofi could not quite make out. He kept walking. Then came the sound of a bottle shattering against
the pavement just behind him. He turned, and saw one of the men—face twisted in anger—pick up
another bottle and hurl it directly at him. It struck his shoulder, and before he could run, the man
was on him, fists connecting with his ribs and jaw. Kofi remembered only fragments: the smell of
alcohol on the man's breath, the crack of his own head against the concrete, the sight of a police
badge appearing above him, the voice of an officer saying, "You're okay, you're okay now." But he
was not okay. The officers took a report, but no arrest was ever made. The case was closed within
weeks, with a note that the suspect could not be identified. Kofi had given a description, had
pointed to the stoop, had provided the names of two people who had witnessed the attack from a
window. None of it had mattered.

Since then, Kofi's world had narrowed. He had come to believe, with a certainty that felt like bone
knowledge, that institutions did not protect people like him. The police had been the ones who were
supposed to help, and they had done nothing. Now, when he heard anyone express anger—even calmly,
even in a meeting where a colleague disagreed with a zoning proposal—his chest tightened and his
vision tunneled. He could not trust that the person would not escalate, that the disagreement would
not turn into something worse. He had started avoiding meetings altogether, sending emails instead,
and when conflict was unavoidable, he agreed to anything to end the conversation. His colleagues had
begun to notice; one had asked if he was "okay," and he had changed the subject.

The memory of the assault did not unfold like a story. It came to him in still images—the bottle
mid-air, the man's hand reaching for another, the concrete rushing up to meet his face. Each picture
was vivid and frozen, and when one surfaced, he felt the same terror and helplessness he had felt in
that moment. He could not describe it to anyone; the words simply would not come. He would go
silent, his throat closing, and sometimes he would have to leave his desk and stand in the bathroom
until the wave passed.

At night, he gambled online, spending hours on poker sites and sports bets. It was the only time he
felt anything other than dread—the rush of a win, the focus required to calculate odds, the way the
numbers filled his mind and pushed out the images. He told himself it was just a way to unwind, but
he knew, somewhere underneath, that he was using it to escape. The losses were mounting, and he had
started borrowing money from a friend.

Everyday sounds now made him flinch. A door slamming in the office, a coffee cup set down too hard,
the sudden movement of a coworker reaching for a file—each one triggered a full-body startle that
left him embarrassed and exhausted. His supervisor had told him, "You're safe here, Kofi, no one is
going to hurt you," and he had nodded, but he did not believe it. He could not believe it. He was
certain that he could not handle even the smallest upset, that any unexpected event could unravel
him completely. He felt helpless, as though bad things were simply drawn to him and he had no power
to stop them.

What finally brought him to therapy was not the fear or the memories or the gambling. It was the
moment last week when his supervisor pulled him aside and said, "Kofi, I'm worried about you. You're
not yourself. I need you to talk to someone, or I can't keep you on this project." He had agreed
because he had no other option. But as he sat in the chair, he was terrified that therapy would be
another institution that promised to help and then failed. He wanted to believe that this time would
be different, but he did not yet know how.
