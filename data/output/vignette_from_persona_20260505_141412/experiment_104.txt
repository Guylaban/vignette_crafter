Leilani was fifty-three years old and had owned a small gift shop in Honolulu for nearly two
decades, a place of shell leis, postcards, and hand-carved tiki figurines that tourists browsed
through on their way to the beach. She had been widowed for fourteen months. Her husband, Mako, a
commercial fisherman, had died in a sudden storm off the coast of Kauai—a squall that had swallowed
his boat whole, leaving no body, only a memorial service three weeks later that she had barely been
able to walk through. The Coast Guard had called it a freak accident, a wave no one could have
predicted. Leilani had not seen it coming. She had kissed him goodbye at four in the morning,
smelled the faint salt and beer on his breath from the night before, and told him she loved him.
That was the last smell she had of him that was still hers.

The trigger that undid her most often was alcohol. A customer would walk into the shop reeking of
rum or whiskey, and Leilani’s chest would seize. The smell hit her nostrils and suddenly she was
back in the kitchen the morning after he died, the half-empty bottle of Kona Longboard left in the
fridge, the way it had made her gag when she poured it out. She had come to believe that the smell
itself was dangerous, that it meant something terrible was about to happen. She had started spraying
lavender air freshener at the front door, but it never quite masked the scent. When a funeral
procession passed by her shop—black cars, slow and solemn—she would duck into the stockroom, heart
pounding, certain that if she looked, she would see her own grief mirrored in a stranger’s face, and
that would break something inside her. She avoided cemeteries entirely. She had not visited Mako’s
memorial plaque at the harbor since the unveiling.

Her memory of the event was not a story she could tell. It came in fragments, like a film that
played over and over with no beginning or end. She would be ringing up a purchase, and suddenly she
was standing in the living room, the phone ringing, the voice of the Coast Guard officer saying
words she could not string together. The memory forced its way into her mind when she was driving,
when she was trying to sleep, when she was laughing at a customer’s joke. She had no coherent
timeline. She could not say what happened between the phone call and the memorial, only that she had
been there, and then she was not. What frightened her most was the flashbacks—moments when she would
feel the cold of the ocean on her skin, or hear the wind howling, and she would be back on that
dock, waiting for a boat that was never coming. She would gasp, drop whatever she was holding, and
for a few seconds she was not in her shop at all. She was drowning.

Leilani had come to believe that her life had been destroyed by the trauma. She could not see a
future. The shop, which had once felt like her sanctuary, now felt like a cage. She had stopped
ordering new inventory; the shelves were thinning, and she did not care. She had lost herself. She
used to be someone who laughed easily, who hosted family dinners, who had plans. Now she woke up
each morning with a weight on her chest, certain that nothing would ever matter again. She could not
relax even when she was home alone with the doors locked. Her body stayed tight, waiting for the
next alarm to sound. She often reacted as if she were in danger when nothing was actually wrong—a
car backfiring outside would send her to the floor, heart hammering. She knew, intellectually, that
she was safe. But she did not feel it.

The consequences had become concrete. Her daughter, who lived on the mainland, had stopped calling
as often, because every conversation ended with Leilani crying or snapping. The shop’s revenue had
dropped by half. A neighbor had offered to help her clean out Mako’s fishing gear, and Leilani had
refused so sharply that the neighbor now crossed the street to avoid her. She was alone in a way she
had never been before.

What finally brought her to seek help was a morning in late September. She had opened the shop, and
a customer had walked in—an elderly woman, weeping, buying a sympathy card for a friend who had lost
her husband. Leilani had rung up the purchase, hands shaking, and then she had locked the door, sat
down on the floor behind the counter, and sobbed for twenty minutes. She realized she could not keep
living this way. She was afraid of what she might become if she did not find a way out. She was
afraid she might close the shop forever, or worse, that she might stop caring enough to close it.
She wanted to remember Mako without being destroyed by the remembering. She wanted to know who she
was now, if she could still be someone. She wanted her life back, even if it was a different life
than the one she had planned.
