Marcus is a forty-two-year-old ship captain who has spent his entire adult life on the water,
commanding crews through storms and tight schedules with a steady hand. He came to therapy at the
insistence of his wife, who told him she could not watch him disappear any further. He sat in the
chair with his arms crossed, scanning the room before he sat down, then chose the seat with his back
to the wall. He did not call it anything. He simply said he felt better that way.

The event that changed everything happened eighteen months ago, on a cargo vessel out of a Gulf
port. Marcus had been piloting the ship through a narrow channel when a deckhand, during a routine
line-handling evolution, hurled a heavy mooring line across the deck in frustration. The line struck
a piece of loose equipment, which skidded and struck a pressurized cylinder. The cylinder ruptured
with a sound Marcus later described as the world splitting open—a bang so loud and percussive that
he felt it in his teeth. The explosion killed two men instantly and severely burned a third. Marcus
was thrown against the console and suffered a concussion, but he was the one who had to give orders
for the emergency response, who had to radio for help, who had to stand on the bridge while the
bodies were recovered. He still remembers the sight of the uniforms—Coast Guard and port
authority—swarming the deck afterward, their crisp blue fabric soaked with seawater and blood.

In the months that followed, Marcus did not talk about it. He told himself he was fine, that he had
handled worse, that a captain does not fall apart. But his body began to make decisions his mind
would not acknowledge. He started dropping responsibilities. He took a sudden leave of absence from
his command, citing exhaustion, and then quietly resigned from the shipping company altogether. He
told his wife he needed a break, but the truth was that he could not bring himself to step onto a
vessel again. The thought of being on the water, of hearing a line slap the deck or a hatch slam
shut, made his chest tighten until he could not breathe. He had come to believe that if he let
himself think about that day—really think about it, replay the bang and the sight of the uniforms
and the sound of the cylinder—he would not be able to handle it. He would lose control entirely. So
he kept the memory locked away, and he filled the space around it with other things.

He started gambling. At first it was just online poker late at night, a way to distract himself when
the images crept in. Then it became a daily ritual, then an expensive one. He lost thousands of
dollars, but the risk, the focus, the rush of the next hand—it kept the explosion at bay. He told
himself it was just a hobby, but his wife found the credit card statements and the arguments began.
He did not tell her about the other changes. He had become vigilant in a way that exhausted him.
Every time he walked into a store or a restaurant, he scanned for exits, for people who looked
threatening, for anyone wearing a uniform. He avoided unfamiliar places entirely because he could
not assess the danger in advance. He could not trust that a stranger would not throw something, that
a loud noise would not come from somewhere he could not see. He had stopped going to his son’s
baseball games because the crowd was too unpredictable, too many people moving too fast. He had come
to believe that he could not rely on other people—not his crew, not the port authorities, not the
company that had paid off the families of the dead men without ever holding anyone accountable. The
deckhand who threw the line was never charged. The investigation was quietly closed. Marcus was
certain that if the world could let that happen, then no one was looking out for him, and he had to
be the one watching.

Sleep became a battleground. He would lie awake for hours, his ears straining for any sound—a car
backfiring, a door slamming, his wife rolling over in bed. He kept his eyes open in the dark,
listening, because he was sure that if he let himself fall asleep, he would not be alert enough to
survive whatever came next. He had not had a full night’s rest in over a year.

What finally brought him to therapy was not the insomnia or the gambling or the isolation. It was
the morning his wife told him she was thinking of leaving. She said she did not know who he was
anymore. Marcus sat in the therapist’s office and said, very quietly, that he was afraid she was
right. He did not know if he could get back to who he used to be. But he was even more afraid that
if he started talking about what happened, he would never stop falling.
