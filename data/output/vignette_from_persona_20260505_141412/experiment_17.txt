Rajesh stood at the window of his small apartment, watching the rain fall on the parking lot below.
He was eighty years old, a widower for six years now, and a retired Coast Guard veteran who had
spent the better part of his life on the water. He had joined the service at nineteen, fresh off a
boat from Mumbai, and had risen through the ranks with a steady hand and a quiet sense of duty. But
that duty had broken him one gray afternoon off the coast of Alaska, when a routine rescue operation
had gone catastrophically wrong.

It had been a storm, the kind that turned the sea into a churning wall of white and black. A fishing
vessel had sent a distress call, and Rajesh had been second-in-command on the cutter that responded.
The seas were too high for a direct approach, so they launched a smaller inflatable boat to pull the
fishermen off the deck. Rajesh was operating the winch, lowering the basket down through the spray.
He saw a young man, maybe twenty-five, waving from the rail. The boat lurched. The cable snapped.
The basket swung hard and struck the man across the skull, sending him into the water. They
recovered his body twenty minutes later, but he was already gone. Rajesh had killed a man with his
own hands, even if those hands had only been operating a machine. The Coast Guard ruled it an
accident. Rajesh never believed them.

What he remembered now, at eighty, came in pieces that did not fit together. He could see the young
man’s face, but only in flashes—the open mouth, the wet hair, the eyes that looked surprised. He
could hear the sound of the cable snapping, a sharp metallic crack, but he could not place it in
sequence. He could feel the cold spray on his cheek, the vibration of the winch through his gloves,
the sickening jolt when the basket hit. But when he tried to deliberately recall the moment—when his
therapist had asked him, years later, to walk through it—the memory would dissolve into static,
leaving only a hollow dread in his chest. At other times, the memory would return on its own,
unbidden, and it would feel completely real: the salt in his nostrils, the scream of the wind, the
weight of the cable in his hands, the sight of the basket swinging. He would be back on that deck,
and he would be forty years younger, and he would be killing a man again.

After the accident, Rajesh had told himself it did not really happen. He had spent weeks replaying
the event in his head, then forcing himself to stop. He would think of the weather report, the
maintenance logs, the paperwork—anything to keep the image from forming fully. He had learned to
force himself to think only positive thoughts: the lives he had saved, the years of service, the
medals. But underneath the reframing, a deeper belief had taken root: there was something wrong with
him as a person. The event had happened to him because of the sort of man he was. He had been
careless, or arrogant, or cursed. He had been the kind of man who killed people, even when he was
trying to save them.

That belief followed him into retirement. He had stopped going to unfamiliar places because he could
not assess the danger. He could not walk into a new store or a park he had never visited without
feeling a creeping sense that something terrible was about to happen, that he would cause it again.
He had begun to take too many risks in other parts of his life—driving too fast, walking through bad
neighborhoods at night—as if daring the universe to punish him properly. And he always chose where
to sit based on how safe he felt: back to the wall, exit visible, nothing behind him. His daughter
had noticed. She would ask why he always sat in the same corner of the restaurant, and he would say
it was the light.

The world had become a place of constant threat. He had to be on guard all the time. A siren in the
distance—an ambulance, a police car—would freeze him mid-step, his heart hammering, his hands
tingling, because the sound was the same pitch as the cutter’s warning horn that day. Being in
nature, especially near any body of water, would trigger a flood of sensory fragments: the salt, the
cold, the lurch of the deck. He could not walk along the river near his apartment without feeling
the floor drop out from under him. And when his daughter talked about the future—her plans to
retire, her son’s college applications—Rajesh would feel a cold dread settle in his stomach. The
future was a place where he did not belong. The future was where he would have to live with what he
had done.

He had come to the clinic because his daughter had finally insisted. She had found him one evening,
sitting in the dark, staring at the wall. He had not told her what he was thinking. What frightened
him most was not the memory itself, but the certainty that he could never be forgiven—not by the
dead man, not by his family, not by himself. He was hoping, though he could not say it out loud,
that someone might tell him he was wrong.
