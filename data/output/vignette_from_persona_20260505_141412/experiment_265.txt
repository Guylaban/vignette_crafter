engineer, had always prided himself on his ability to anticipate problems. His mind worked in
systems, tracing every variable, every point of failure before it could occur. That was why, when
the wildfire swept through his neighborhood three years ago, the memory had lodged itself in him not
as a single event but as a permanent, repeating loop of what he should have done differently. He had
smelled the smoke first, a sharp, acrid sweetness that seemed to seep through the cracks in the
windows, and he had watched the orange glow crest the ridge with a sense of disbelief that hardened
into horror. He and his wife had evacuated with only minutes to spare, but in the months and years
that followed, Carlos could not stop returning to that moment. He would lie awake replaying the
sequence: the way he had hesitated, the time he had wasted checking the gutters, the fact that he
had not packed the photo albums. He told himself that if he had just been faster, smarter, more
vigilant, the terror would not have taken root so deeply. He ruminated endlessly on how the event
could have been prevented—if only he had cleared the brush earlier, if only he had installed a
better sprinkler system, if only he had been a different kind of man.

The world after the fire became a landscape of hidden dangers. Carlos found himself scanning every
room he entered for signs of smoke, his nostrils flaring involuntarily at the faintest whiff of a

heart hammering against his ribs, his palms slick with sweat, his breath turning shallow and ragged.
He became acutely aware of his own body’s betrayals: the way his hands trembled when he saw a flame
on a stove, the way his legs felt weak and unsteady when the weather turned hot and dry, the wind
picking up in that particular way that reminded him of that afternoon. He had come to believe that
his physical reactions were a sign that something was fundamentally wrong with him, that his body
was telling him the danger was still present, even when his rational mind knew otherwise. He was
constantly on guard, watching for the first flicker of orange, listening for the crackle of burning
wood. The startle response that seized him when a car backfired or a door slammed left him feeling
humiliated and exposed, as if everyone could see how broken his internal alarm system had become.

his house disappear behind a wall of flame. He began to take his lunch in his car, eating quickly
with the windows up, just to avoid the possibility of that smell. He tried to push the memories out

did, and the shame of it only deepened his conviction that he was failing at the one thing he had
always done well.

distance in her eyes, the way she no longer asked him why he was standing at the window, staring at
the sky. He knew he was exhausting her, but he could not stop. What frightened him most was the

protect. It was that fear, finally, that brought him to a therapist’s office, not because he
believed he could change, but because he was desperate for his wife to see him try.
