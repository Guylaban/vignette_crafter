Rajesh is a 44-year-old chef who came to therapy at the urging of his sous-chef, who had grown
concerned about his increasingly erratic behavior in the kitchen. Rajesh had been a respected head
chef for over a decade, known for his calm precision under pressure, but in recent months he had
become someone his staff barely recognized. He had come to the United States as a refugee nearly
twenty years ago, fleeing a civil war that had destroyed his village and taken the lives of his
parents and younger sister. What he carried with him was not a story he could tell in words—when he
tried, the details dissolved into images and sensations that felt disconnected from language, as
though the event belonged to someone else entirely. Sometimes he could recall the day in fragments:
the sound of mortar fire, the dust rising in clouds, the weight of his sister’s hand slipping from
his as he ran. But when he tried to describe it to a doctor or a friend, the words felt hollow, like
reading a script about a stranger’s life. And at other times, when the memory surfaced unbidden, he
was flooded with a grief so overwhelming that he would have to stop whatever he was doing and brace
himself against a counter until it passed.

In the kitchen, Rajesh had always thrived on controlled chaos—the heat, the noise, the urgency of
service. But now that same environment had become a minefield. The clatter of a metal tray hitting
the floor could send him into a silent freeze. A delivery driver’s loud laugh from the alley made
him drop a knife. What frightened him most was the way his own body responded before his mind could
catch up: his heart would race, his palms would sweat, and he would find himself scanning the room
for exits, counting the steps to the back door, the fire escape, the alley. He had begun to insist
on prepping in a corner of the kitchen where he could see both doors, and he would become irritable
if a line cook stood too close to the only exit. His concentration, once his greatest asset, had
fractured; he would read a ticket three times and still forget the order, or find himself staring at
a cutting board for minutes at a time, unable to remember what he was doing.

Outside of work, Rajesh’s world had shrunk. He had stopped going to the farmer’s market, which he
had once loved, because the open plaza left him feeling exposed and vulnerable, as though he might
be seen from too many angles. He avoided driving past the VA hospital because the sight of a
uniformed officer or a parked military vehicle made his throat tighten. He had learned to change his
route to the grocery store to avoid a corner where a car had rear-ended another vehicle the week
before—not because the accident was serious, but because the sight of the dented bumper and the
bystanders’ worried faces had triggered a flash of the dust and the screaming that he could not put
into words. When a customer at the restaurant mentioned a news story about a car crash, Rajesh would
excuse himself to the walk-in cooler and stand there, breathing slowly, until the urge to flee
subsided.

The guilt was a constant companion. He had come to believe that he should have done more—should have
pulled his sister faster, should have stayed behind, should have somehow stopped what was
unstoppable. He could not forgive himself for being alive when his parents and sister were not. And
he was certain that the person he had been before—the easygoing, focused chef who laughed with his
staff and experimented with new recipes—was gone forever. He felt permanently broken, as though the
trauma had rewritten his core. When he felt threatened, which was often, he found himself becoming
overly compliant, agreeing with whatever a customer or manager said even when he disagreed, nodding
along, offering apologies for things that were not his fault. It was a reflex he despised but could
not control.

What finally brought Rajesh to seek help was a single moment during dinner service. A paramedic had
walked into the restaurant to pick up a takeout order, and Rajesh, catching a glimpse of the uniform
from the pass, had frozen mid-plate. He could not speak. He could not move. His sous-chef had to
physically guide him to the office, where he sat shaking for twenty minutes. That night, Rajesh
called the number his employee had given him weeks ago. He was terrified that he was losing his
mind, but more than that, he was terrified of losing the only world he had left—the kitchen, his
staff, the small life he had rebuilt from rubble. He did not know if he could be fixed, but he knew
he could not go on as he was.
