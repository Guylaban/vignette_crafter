Jason is twenty-one years old, married, and currently unemployed. He sits forward in the chair, his
hands clasped tightly between his knees, and speaks in short, halting bursts. He is a tall, pale
young man with close-cropped light brown hair, and he looks older than his age—not in his features,
but in the hollowed, watchful quality of his eyes. He has come to therapy because his wife, Brianna,
told him she could not keep living like this, and he could not argue with her.

The event that shattered everything happened eight months ago, in the parking lot of a gas station
off Interstate 80. Jason was nineteen then, working nights at a warehouse, and his younger brother,
Caleb, was seventeen. They had stopped for gas on the way home from a fishing trip. Jason was at the
pump. Caleb was standing by the passenger door, stretching. A man walked up fast from behind the
convenience store, and Jason saw him only in his peripheral vision—a sudden movement, a shape
closing in. He turned. The man had a knife. He did not say a word. He stabbed Caleb twice in the
chest and once in the stomach, then ran. Jason remembers the sound—a wet, percussive grunt from his
brother—and the smell. There was a dumpster nearby, and the hot summer air was thick with the stench
of rotting garbage and gasoline. That smell is still with him. Sometimes, when he is lying in bed at
night, it fills his nose without warning, and he is back in that parking lot, frozen, watching his
brother fall. Caleb survived, after three surgeries and a month in the ICU, but Jason has not
survived the same way.

What Jason cannot do is put the event into words. When he tries to tell the story, even to Brianna,
the sequence dissolves. He sees it in fragments—still images, like photographs laid out on a table.
His brother’s hand reaching for the door handle. The blade. The blood on the asphalt. The man’s
forearm, which is the body part he cannot stop seeing: a particular tattoo, a coiled snake, that he
can summon in perfect visual detail even now. He cannot narrate the event because it does not live
in language for him. It lives in pictures and smells and the sudden, overwhelming feeling that it is
happening again—that he is standing at the pump, that the man is coming, that he will fail to move.

He has come to believe that his body has turned against him. His heart races when he walks into a
quiet room. His palms sweat when a stranger walks too close. He tells the therapist that his body
keeps reacting as if he is still in danger, even though he knows, logically, that he is safe. But
knowing does not stop the reaction. He has also become convinced that he can no longer rely on
himself. Before the attack, Jason was the steady one—the older brother, the one who worked double
shifts, the one who fixed things. Now he cannot trust his own instincts. He froze at the gas
station. He did not tackle the man, did not shield his brother. He stood there. And so he has come
to believe that he is no longer capable of doing the things that defined him: protecting the people
he loves, keeping his head in a crisis, being someone worth counting on.

The avoidance began quietly and then consumed everything. He stopped watching television entirely,
because any show with violence or suspense might show a knife, or a sudden attack, and he would be
flooded with images he could not control. He stopped reading the news. He stopped scrolling social
media. When his mother called to talk about Caleb’s recovery, he found himself unable to listen—her
comments about the family, about how brave Caleb was being, about how they had all been through so
much, felt like pressure on a bruise. He started leaving the room when she talked. More than
anything, he cannot be alone. He cannot sit in a room by himself. The silence presses in, and the
photographs in his mind start to move, and he feels the attack happening again in his chest. So he
follows Brianna from room to room. He sleeps with the light on. He wakes at 2 a.m. and cannot fall
back asleep, his mind replaying the smell of garbage and gasoline. Brianna works as a receptionist,
and when she leaves for her shift, Jason drives to the public library and sits in the busiest
corner, just to be near other people. He has stopped applying for jobs. He cannot imagine standing
in a warehouse again, hearing footsteps behind him.

Two weeks ago, he hurt himself for the first time. He was home alone for forty minutes—Brianna had a
dentist appointment—and the panic built until he could not breathe. He punched the wall, then took a
shard of glass from a broken picture frame and cut his forearm. He says it released the pressure. He
says it was the only thing that made the feeling stop. He told Brianna it was an accident, but she
did not believe him.

Now he is here, in a therapist’s office, because he is terrified that he will lose his wife, and he
is even more terrified that he will lose himself completely. He does not know how to stop the cycle.
He does not know how to be alone. He does not know how to be the person he was before. But he came,
and he is sitting in the chair, and that is the first thing he has done in months that feels like a
choice.
