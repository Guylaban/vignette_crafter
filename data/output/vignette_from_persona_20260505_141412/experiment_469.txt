Sister Carmen had been a nun for eighteen years when the bomb went off. She was forty-four now,
three years removed from that morning in the plaza, and she still could not say the word *terrorism*
aloud. She had been walking toward the cathedral for early Mass, her sandals slapping the warm
cobblestones, when the world became a single, deafening pressure. She remembered none of it in
order. When she tried to deliberately reconstruct what happened—the seconds before, the sound, the
way she hit the ground—her mind went blank, a white wall she could not climb. But the memory did not
wait for her permission. It arrived without warning, as it had last night, tearing her from sleep
with her heart hammering so hard she thought it would break her ribs.

She woke gasping, the dream already fading into a residue of dread, but the feeling stayed: the
smoke, the screaming, the wet warmth spreading across her face. Her hands flew to her cheeks,
checking for blood. There was none. There never was. But the terror was as real as it had been when
she had opened her eyes in the rubble and seen her own hand, red and slick, and for one horrible
moment believed it belonged to someone else.

The physical pain had started about a year after the attack. A deep, grinding ache in her lower back
that no doctor could explain. X-rays were clean. MRIs were clean. But the pain was real—sharp enough
to make her catch her breath when she bent to pray—and it had become a trigger in itself. Every time
her back seized, she was back in the plaza. Every time she felt a twinge, she braced for the world
to explode again.

Sister Carmen had stopped going to the grocery store. She had stopped attending the diocesan
retreats she once loved. Crowds were out of the question. She had tried, once, to go to the
Christmas market with the other sisters, but the press of bodies had sent her heart into a gallop,
and she had ducked into a chapel and sat trembling in the back pew until the panic passed. Now she
only felt safe in three places: her cell, the convent chapel, and the small garden behind the
kitchen. Anywhere else, she scanned for exits. Before she could sit down in a room, she had to know
where the doors were, where the windows were, whether she could get out. She had come to believe
that safety was an illusion she could no longer afford.

She had also stopped letting anyone touch her. Not in anger—she was never angry—but because
emotional closeness had become unbearable. When another sister placed a hand on her shoulder in
comfort, Carmen flinched. When the Mother Superior tried to hug her after a difficult anniversary,
Carmen went rigid and pulled away. She knew it hurt them. She saw the confusion in their eyes. But
she could not explain that intimacy felt like vulnerability, and vulnerability felt like being
caught in the open when the bomb went off.

What consumed her most, however, was the replaying. She went over and over the event in her mind,
trying to make sense of it. Why had she been walking so slowly that morning? What if she had stopped
to tie her shoe? What if she had turned left instead of right? She could not stop the questions.
They looped endlessly, a rosary of guilt she could not lay down. She was certain, on some level,
that if she could just find the right answer—the right piece of the puzzle—she would finally feel
safe again. But she never did.

The shame was the worst part. The guilt. The anger at God. She had devoted her life to faith, and
now she could not pray without feeling the hot, sticky terror of the plaza. She had come to believe
that she was broken in a way that prayer could not fix. That God had turned His face away.

What finally brought her to seek help was not the nightmares, or the pain, or the isolation. It was
the moment she realized she could no longer enter the chapel without checking the exits first. She
stood at the back of the sanctuary, her eyes sweeping the walls, cataloging every door, every
window, every possible route of escape. She could not find the altar. She could not find God.

She was terrified that she was losing her vocation. But what she hoped for, in the quietest part of
her heart, was that she might one day sit in a room and not need to know the way out.
