Ravi is a 29-year-old painter who came to therapy at his wife’s urging, though he would not say he
came willingly. He sat forward in the chair, hands clasped, and spoke in a low, careful voice, as
though he were still deciding whether the room was safe.

The event that changed everything happened eight months ago, on a Tuesday evening in late October.
Ravi had just finished a residential interior job in a neighborhood he did not know well. He was
walking back to his van, carrying a five-gallon bucket of primer in one hand and his phone in the
other, when a man stepped out from between two parked cars. Ravi remembered the hood of the
sweatshirt, the hand in the pocket, the voice saying *give me your phone and your wallet*. He
remembered handing over the phone. He remembered the man grabbing the bucket by its handle and
yanking it out of his grip. What he did not remember—what he could not reach no matter how hard he
tried—was how he ended up on the ground. There was a gap in the memory, a dark space where something
should have been. He knew his knee was scraped and his palm was bleeding, but he had no image of
falling. When he tried to deliberately recall that part, his mind went blank and his chest
tightened, and he was left with only the feeling of guilt: *I should have fought back. I should have
seen him coming. I should have taken a different street.*

In the weeks that followed, Ravi found himself avoiding certain intersections and blocks without
fully deciding to. He took longer routes to job sites, routes that added twenty minutes to his
commute, because the shorter way passed too close to where it happened. He stopped working in that
neighborhood altogether, turning down a repeat client without explanation. His wife noticed the lost
income before he mentioned it. She asked why he was so quiet, why he flinched when she came up
behind him. He told her he was tired.

The nightmares started around the third week. They were not replays of the mugging. They were
fragments—a dark shape, a voice, the sensation of falling—and he would wake with his heart pounding
and a vague, suffocating dread that followed him into the morning. The dream residue clung to him
for hours. He would stand at his easel, brush in hand, and feel the tension in his shoulders and
neck, a constant readiness that had no object. He scanned every doorway, every alley, every person
who walked too close. His body stayed on alert even at home, even in his own studio, and he found
himself snapping at his wife for small things—leaving the back door unlocked, moving his tools
without telling him. He knew he was irritable. He could hear his own voice, sharp and unfair, but he
could not stop it.

The memory came to him without warning. A flash of the sweatshirt hood, the glint of a streetlight
on the van window, the weight of the bucket being pulled from his hand. It arrived suddenly, like a
door slamming open, and left him shaken and silent. He had come to believe that people could not be
trusted—not strangers, not clients, not the man on the street who smiled too broadly. He felt
isolated from everyone, even his wife, because she had not been there and could not understand what
it was like to feel so exposed. He was certain that he could not rely on anyone else, that he had to
handle everything himself, and that needing help was a weakness that would only let him down.

What finally brought Ravi to therapy was a job he could not turn down—a large commercial contract
that required him to work in a downtown area with heavy foot traffic. He lasted two hours. He stood
on the scaffolding, looking down at the moving crowd, and felt his chest lock. He climbed down,
called his wife, and told her he could not do it anymore. He is afraid that his life will keep
shrinking, that the streets he can walk and the jobs he can take will keep disappearing. He is
hoping, though he would not say it aloud, that someone can help him find a way back to the person
who used to pick up a brush without first checking the door.
