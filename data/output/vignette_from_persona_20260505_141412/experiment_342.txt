Leila arrived for her first session with the clipped, efficient manner of someone who had learned to
keep the world at arm’s length. At thirty, she had been a bartender for nearly a decade—a job she
had chosen, she explained, because the dim, controlled lighting and the solid wooden barrier of the
bar itself made her feel less exposed. She had been divorced for two years, and the dissolution of
her marriage had been, in her own words, "quiet and necessary," though she offered no further
detail. What had finally driven her to seek help was a pattern of dread that had become
unmanageable: she could no longer walk through a grocery store without her heart hammering, and the
sight of a man raising his arm to wave at someone across the street would send her into a cold,
silent panic.

The event that had shaped her lived inside her like a splinter she could not extract, though she
struggled to describe it in words. She knew what had happened—she had been seven years old, sitting
on the stairs of her family home, watching her father’s hand connect with her mother’s face in the
kitchen. She had seen her mother stumble backward, had heard the crack of bone against bone, had
felt the air leave her own lungs as if she had been the one struck. But when she tried to tell this
story to a therapist, or even to her ex-husband, the words would vanish. She would open her mouth
and find only silence, her throat tight, her chest heaving with an emotion that felt too large for
language. "I know what happened," she said once, frustrated, "but I can't find the words to describe
it." The memory itself was not a narrative; it was a sensory flood—the smell of cooking oil, the
sound of a chair scraping tile, the sudden, overwhelming heat of shame and terror that would force
her to stop whatever she was doing and brace herself against a wall until it passed.

In the years since, Leila had come to believe that she could not rely on herself. This conviction
was not abstract; it governed her smallest decisions. She would stand in front of her apartment door
for ten minutes, checking and rechecking the deadbolt, because she could not trust that her own
hands had done the job correctly. She would mentally rehearse worst-case scenarios before every
shift—a customer becoming aggressive, a fire breaking out, a sudden movement from a drunk patron—not
because she expected these things to happen, but because rehearsing them was the only way she knew
to feel even marginally prepared. If she did not rehearse, she was certain she would freeze, and
freezing meant failing, and failing meant confirming what she already suspected: that she could not
count on herself to do the right thing.

Her sense of safety had shrunk to a handful of locations: her apartment, the bar where she worked,
and the short, predictable route between them. Open spaces—parking lots, parks, the wide aisles of a
big-box store—left her feeling raw and visible, as though she were a target on a stage. She had
learned to scan constantly, reading the bodies of strangers for threat. A sudden movement, even a
benign one—a man reaching for his wallet, a child darting across a room—would snap her attention
into hyperfocus, her muscles tensing, her mind racing with the question of whether she needed to
run. She had come to see people as inherently untrustworthy, not because she judged them harshly,
but because her own history had taught her that the people who were supposed to be safe were the
ones who caused the most damage.

The consequences of these patterns had accumulated quietly. Her divorce, she now suspected, had not
been about her ex-husband's failings but about her own inability to let him close. She had monitored
his moods, his movements, his tone of voice, always waiting for the moment when the mask would slip.
She had never told him why. She had never told anyone. The effort of controlling her emotions—of
keeping her face neutral, her voice steady, her reactions invisible—had exhausted her for so long
that she had stopped recognizing it as effort. It was simply how she lived.

What brought her to the clinic now was not insight but exhaustion. A customer at the bar had raised
his hand to order a drink, and she had flinched so visibly that the man had asked if she was okay.
She had lied and said she was fine, but the lie had felt heavier than usual. She was afraid of what
would happen if she did not find a way to put the event into words—afraid that the memory would keep
returning, overwhelming her, until one day she would not be able to stop. She wanted to feel safe in
more than two rooms. She wanted to trust herself. She was not sure she could, but she had shown up
anyway, and for now, that was enough.
