the quiet, steady heart of her community’s temple education program. She taught children the old
stories, the chants, the rituals of respect. She had lived through the killing fields of Cambodia as
a young woman, and she had built a life in America that she believed had finally buried that past.
But the past, she had learned, does not stay buried. It rises through the soil.

The event that had fractured her life was not a single moment but a season of terror. In 1975, when
the Khmer Rouge took Phnom Penh, Sophea was forced from her home and marched into the countryside.

die of starvation, where she was beaten for praying, where she was made to dig graves for strangers
and friends alike. The memory that now consumed her most was not the largest atrocity but a
specific, small horror. One evening, she was ordered to burn a pile of clothing taken from executed
prisoners. As the flames rose, a soldier shoved her forward without warning—his hand flat against
her back, hard enough to make her stumble. She fell into the edge of the fire. The smell of her own
burning sleeve, the heat on her arm, the shouts, the laughter of the guards—these fragments had
become the architecture of her waking life.

Now, eighty years old and safe in a tidy suburban home, Sophea could not walk into her own backyard
without her heart seizing. The smell of damp earth, of turned soil, of the garden her husband had
planted—it was the same smell as the camp. She had stopped going outside entirely. When her grandson
ran to hug her from behind, she screamed. She had come to believe, with a certainty that shamed her,
that her body was no longer her own. She could not trust it to stay calm, to stay still, to not

current husband of forty years.

than the present. When she closed her eyes, the fire was brighter than the lamp on her nightstand.
The smell was stronger than the jasmine incense she burned each morning.

making new friends. After a woman at the temple invited her to a gardening club, Sophea had politely
declined, then avoided the temple for two months. She could not explain why. She only knew that the
