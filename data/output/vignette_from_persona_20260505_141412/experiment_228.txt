Thuan Nguyen is a 75-year-old widower who works as a cybersecurity analyst, a role he has held for
over three decades. He came to therapy at the urging of his adult daughter, who had grown concerned
about his increasing isolation and the way he would become unreachable—staring blankly, his hands
trembling—whenever she mentioned her own pregnancy. Thuan’s trauma began five years ago, when his
youngest son, then thirty-two, was diagnosed with a rare and aggressive form of leukemia. The
diagnosis came suddenly, after a routine physical revealed alarming blood work. What followed was a
year of hospitalizations, chemotherapy rounds, and two bone marrow transplants. Thuan spent every
night in a vinyl recliner beside his son’s bed, listening to the rhythmic beep of the IV pump and
the hiss of the oxygen concentrator. The smell of antiseptic—a sharp, chemical scent that seemed to
coat everything—became the permanent background of his life. He watched his son’s body shrink,
watched the color drain from his face, watched the doctors come and go with their clipboards and
their careful, neutral expressions. His son survived, eventually achieving remission, but something
in Thuan had not survived the year intact.

In the months after his son came home, Thuan noticed that the smell of rubbing alcohol or hand
sanitizer could stop him cold. He would be walking through the grocery store, reach for a cart, and
suddenly the antiseptic odor would hit him—and he would be back in the hospital room, watching the
bag of platelets drip, hearing the soft alarm of a pump that needed attention. His chest would
tighten, his palms would sweat, and he would feel a surge of pure, animal fear, as though his son’s
life was ending in that exact moment, all over again. He learned to avoid hospitals, declining his
own annual physicals and ignoring the dental cleanings he had once kept like clockwork. When his
daughter announced her pregnancy, he felt a jolt of dread so physical that he had to excuse himself
from the dinner table. Babies, pregnancy, childbirth—all of it now felt like a doorway to the same
corridor of waiting and watching. He could not explain why. When she tried to talk to him about her
due date or her nausea, he would lose the thread mid-sentence, the words dissolving into images of
his son’s pale hand against the white hospital sheets. The memory felt less like a recollection and
more like a recurring event—a film that played behind his eyes without his permission, always the
same scenes, always the same helplessness.

At work, Thuan had always been known for his methodical calm, the way he could trace a breach
through layers of code without flinching. But now, the same analytical mind that had served him so
well became a trap. He found himself replaying the diagnosis conversation in his head—the doctor’s
careful phrasing, the way his son had looked at the ceiling—and each time, the replay brought the
same raw horror, as if he were hearing it for the first time. He began to avoid meetings where
medical leave or health insurance was discussed. He stopped going to the break room, where someone
might be eating hospital-scented crackers or using a hand sanitizer dispenser. His colleagues
noticed he had grown short-tempered, that he left the office the moment the clock struck five, that
he no longer joined them for after-work drinks. He had come to believe that if he let his guard
down, if he stopped monitoring the world for reminders, the fear would overwhelm him completely. He
was certain that his body was betraying him, that the physical reactions—the racing heart, the
nausea, the feeling of being smothered—were signs that he was about to lose control, maybe collapse,
maybe die, just as he had feared his son would die.

What finally brought Thuan to seek help was a moment of public shame. During a routine cybersecurity
audit at a hospital—a contract he could not refuse—he walked past an open supply closet and caught a
wave of antiseptic smell. He froze in the hallway, his vision tunneling, his hands shaking, unable
to speak or move. A young nurse touched his arm and asked if he was okay, and he could not answer.
He could only stand there, lost in a memory that felt more real than the corridor around him. His
daughter drove him to the appointment the next week. Thuan is not sure what he hopes for. He knows
he cannot keep avoiding doctors, cannot keep dodging his daughter’s pregnancy, cannot keep leaving
meetings early. But what frightens him most is the possibility that talking about it will only make
the film play louder, that the memory will finally swallow him whole.
