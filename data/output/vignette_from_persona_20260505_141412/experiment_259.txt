Dr. Elena Va’a was seventy-seven years old, a retired surgeon who had spent four decades in the
operating rooms of a major Honolulu hospital. She was also, until eighteen months ago, a woman who
had never been a patient. The traumatic event was not a single moment but a siege: a diagnosis of
pancreatic adenocarcinoma, followed by a Whipple procedure, followed by a septic shock that nearly
killed her on the tenth postoperative day. What she remembered of that period was a series of
violent, disconnected fragments. She could see the ceiling tiles of the ICU with perfect, horrifying
clarity—every fleck, every water stain—but she could not remember her daughter’s face at the
bedside. She knew her daughter had been there; the nurses had told her. But the memory was simply
gone, a black hole in the timeline. Other moments were hyper-lucid: the cold of the defibrillator
pads on her chest, the sound of a central line being inserted, the smell of betadine. Between these
islands of clarity lay vast stretches of blankness, and the gaps terrified her more than the images
she did have. She would sit in her armchair and try to force the pieces together, like a puzzle with
half the pieces missing, and each failed attempt left her feeling that her own mind had become a
foreign, broken instrument.

In the months after her discharge, Elena found herself thinking obsessively about why this had
happened to her. She had been a surgeon. She had saved lives. She had followed every rule of health
and discipline. The question circled without answer, and the lack of answer felt like a judgment.
She began to force herself to think only positive thoughts—gratitude lists, affirmations, mantras
about resilience—but the positivity felt hollow, a thin skin stretched over a chasm. What frightened
her most was the person she saw in the mirror. The woman who had once made life-and-death decisions
in seconds now jumped at the sound of a siren. The woman who had trusted her own hands implicitly
now could not bear the sight of a stethoscope. She had lost her faith in the medical system that had
saved her, in the colleagues she had once called family, in any higher order that would allow a
surgeon to become a patient. She felt, in a way she could not articulate, that the real Elena had
died on that ICU bed and someone else had woken up.

The avoidance was systematic and, from the outside, almost invisible. She stopped driving past the
hospital. She changed her primary care physician to a clinic forty minutes away that had no
affiliation with her former workplace. She cancelled all her subscriptions to medical journals and
deleted her hospital alumni association emails unread. When a friend mentioned needing a routine
colonoscopy, Elena changed the subject so abruptly that the friend asked if she was feeling unwell.
The consequence was gradual but profound: she had always been the person her family and friends
turned to for medical advice, the steady voice in a health crisis. Now she could not even look at a
thermometer without her chest tightening. Her daughter stopped calling for advice. Her bridge group
learned not to discuss their own appointments in front of her. Elena had become, in her own home, a
woman who could not be relied upon for the very thing she had once done best.

What finally brought her to seek help was a physical examination. Her new physician, a young
internist who did not know her history, asked her to undress for a routine skin check. Elena froze.
She could not remove her blouse. Her hands would not obey. She sat on the edge of the examination
table, staring at the paper roll, and felt the ceiling tiles from the ICU swim into her vision. She
left the appointment without the exam and drove home shaking. That night, she called a psychologist
who specialized in older adults and left a message. She was not sure what she hoped for—perhaps
simply that someone might help her understand why the person she had become was so unrecognisable,
and whether there was any way back to the woman she used to be.
