The night it happened, Samir had been driving home from a late shift at the social media agency, his
mind still half on the campaign metrics he’d been reviewing. A routine traffic stop on the
interstate—blinkers, license and registration, the standard script. What he remembered most vividly
now, months later, was the taste. The metallic tang of his own blood, sharp and coppery, flooding
his mouth after the officer’s knee pinned his face to the asphalt. He could summon that taste at
will, and often did without meaning to, the sensation arriving unbidden while he was chopping
vegetables or sipping his morning coffee, pulling him back into that moment of helpless grinding
pressure. What he could not do was let the memory play through to its end. He always pushed it away
before the full sequence arrived—before the handcuffs tightened, before the humiliating questions
about where he was going, before the realization that his wrists were bound and his body meant
nothing to the men standing over him. The rage that flooded him on recall was so intense that his
hands would shake, his jaw clench, and he would have to leave the room, whatever room he was in.

Samir had come to believe that he had acted in ways that violated who he fundamentally was. He had
not fought back. He had not demanded a lawyer. He had lain still and compliant, whispering “yes sir”
while a boot pressed into his spine. In the weeks afterward, this thought circled him like a
vulture: he was not the man he had thought he was. And if he could be wrong about that, he could be
wrong about everything else. People were not what they seemed—not the officers who smiled before
they struck, not the colleagues who clapped him on the shoulder, not the neighbors who waved from
their driveways. The world, he now understood with a certainty that felt like wisdom, was a
dangerous place, and he was the only one who knew it.

At work, his desk faced a wall of windows overlooking the street, and he had taken to angling his
chair so that his back was to the glass. He could not concentrate otherwise; his eyes would drift to
every passing car, every figure pausing on the sidewalk. He had become a student of escape routes.
Entering a restaurant with his wife, Layla, he would scan for the kitchen exit, the emergency
stairs, the fire door behind the hostess stand. He could not relax anywhere, not even in his own
living room, where he sometimes found himself sitting rigidly on the edge of the couch, listening
for footsteps on the porch. He had stopped watching television altogether. Any film involving
police, any news report about an arrest, any documentary about the justice system—he turned it off
or left the room. The anger was too immediate, too consuming.

The consequences had accumulated quietly. Layla had started to complain that he was never present,
that he flinched when she touched his shoulder from behind, that he had stopped laughing at her
jokes. He had missed two deadlines at work because he had spent the morning checking the parking lot
for unmarked cars before he could bring himself to leave the building. What frightened Samir most
was not the memory itself but the feeling that he was living in a state of constant readiness,
waiting for the next moment when the world would reveal its true nature. He had come to therapy
because Layla had said, gently, that she could not live this way anymore, and because he was
exhausted by the vigilance. He wanted to stop tasting blood in his mouth. He wanted to sit through
an entire dinner without mapping the exits. He was not sure if he believed recovery was possible,
but he was certain that he could not continue as he was.
