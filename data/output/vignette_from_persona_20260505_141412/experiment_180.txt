Michael is a 33-year-old graphic designer who lives alone in a modest apartment in a midsized city.
He is divorced, and the separation occurred about a year after the event that brought him here. He
came to therapy at the urging of his ex-wife, who told him she could not watch him destroy himself
anymore, and because his boss had recently warned him that his performance was slipping. Michael’s
PCL-5 score was 74, indicating very severe posttraumatic symptoms. He does not argue with the
number.

The event that changed everything happened three years ago, when Michael was working late at a small
design studio in a neighborhood that had seen a rise in break-ins. One night, just after midnight,
he was walking to his car in the dark parking lot behind the building. He heard footsteps, then a
voice shouting. Before he could react, a police officer had him pinned face-down on the asphalt, a
knee in his back and a gun pressed against his temple. The officer had mistaken him for a suspect in
a recent armed robbery. Michael was handcuffed, searched, and yelled at for several minutes before
the officer realized the mistake. He was released with a curt apology and told to “get home safe.”
Michael drove home in a daze, his hands shaking on the wheel. He did not sleep that night, and he
has not slept through the night since.

In the weeks that followed, Michael began to notice that certain cars made his stomach drop. A dark
sedan, especially a model similar to the unmarked police car that had been idling in the lot that
night, would make him scan the driver’s face, his pulse quickening. He started taking longer routes
to work to avoid the parking lot where it happened, and eventually he stopped going into the office
altogether, working exclusively from home. Crowded places became unbearable. The press of bodies,
the inability to see who was behind him, the sudden loud noises of a city street—all of it felt like
a setup for another ambush. He stopped going to grocery stores, then to coffee shops, then to the
gym. His social life evaporated. His marriage, already strained by his constant irritability and his
refusal to explain why he flinched every time she touched him from behind, collapsed.

The most immediate strategy Michael developed was drinking. He started with a beer or two to fall
asleep, but soon it was a bottle of wine, sometimes more, just to quiet the hypervigilance that kept
him scanning the dark corners of his own apartment. He also began covering his face with his hands
whenever he walked past a police car or saw a uniform in a store. He knew it looked strange, but he
could not stop himself. The sight of a badge or a holster sent a bolt of terror through him, and his
hands would fly up as if to block the image. He also avoided any news or television shows that
featured police, and he deleted all social media apps after a friend posted a video of a traffic
stop. The world had become a minefield of reminders.

What frightened Michael most was the dark. Nighttime, when the sun went down, he felt a dread that
was almost physical. He kept every light in his apartment on, from the kitchen to the bathroom, and
he slept with the television on. Waking up in the middle of the night, even to use the bathroom, was
a crisis. He would lie still, heart hammering, certain that someone was in the room. He had come to
believe that he was not safe anywhere, that the world was fundamentally dangerous, and that another
attack could happen at any moment, without warning. He was certain that he had done something wrong
that night, even though he knew logically he had not. The officer’s face, the weight of the knee,
the cold metal against his temple—these images played in his mind every day, unbidden, as vivid as
the moment they happened.

Michael finally sought help because he could no longer work. His concentration was gone; he stared
at his computer screen for hours, unable to design. He had missed three deadlines in a row. His
boss, a patient man, told him he needed to take a leave of absence or risk termination. Michael
agreed to come to therapy because he was terrified of losing his job, the last stable thing in his
life. But underneath that, he admitted, he was also terrified that nothing would help. He had spent
three years trying to manage on his own, and he was exhausted. What he hoped for, he said quietly,
was to be able to walk to his car at night without feeling like he was going to die. He did not know
if that was possible, but he was willing to try.
