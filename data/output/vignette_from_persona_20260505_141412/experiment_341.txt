At fifty-one, David had spent twenty-seven years in education, the last nine as principal of a
suburban middle school. He was known for his steady presence, his ability to walk into a chaotic
hallway and quiet it with a look. Colleagues called him unflappable. Then, eighteen months ago,
during a routine physical, his doctor found something on an ultrasound. The something turned out to
be a tumor wrapped around his left kidney. Surgery was scheduled quickly, the tumor was removed, and
follow-up scans showed no spread. Medically, he was told he was in the clear. But David had not felt
clear for a single day since.

The event itself, when he tried to deliberately recall it, felt oddly inaccessible. He could
describe the facts—the date of the surgery, the name of the hospital, the surgeon’s face—but the
actual texture of those hours was gone, as though a drawer had been pulled shut. Yet, without any
warning, the full emotional weight of that time would crash over him. He might be standing in the
school parking lot, or sitting at a red light, and suddenly his chest would tighten, his throat
would close, and he would feel himself back in that pre-operative bay, the fluorescent lights
humming, the cold gel on his abdomen. There was no image that accompanied these waves of fear—just
the raw feeling, as though his body knew something his mind refused to hold. And sometimes, when the
feeling came, he would notice his arms pressed rigidly at his sides, his legs straight and still,
exactly as they had been on the gurney during the CT scan, when he had been told not to move. His
body reenacted the position without his permission.

The smell of antiseptic became a landmine. A single whiff of hand sanitizer in the school nurse’s
office could send him into a spiral. He had stopped walking past the local hospital on his way home,
taking a three-mile detour through residential streets instead. When his annual physical came due,
he canceled it twice, then simply stopped answering the phone calls from his doctor’s office. He
knew this was dangerous—he was a widower, his wife having died of ovarian cancer six years earlier,
and he had two adult children who worried about him constantly—but the thought of lying on another
examination table, of hearing that paper crinkle beneath him, was unbearable. It was not merely
unpleasant; it felt like a threat to his survival.

To manage the mounting dread, David had begun rehearsing conversations before any interaction.
Before calling his daughter, he would script the exchange in his head: what she would ask, what he
would say, how he would deflect her concerns. Before faculty meetings, he would practice his opening
remarks five or six times, mouthing the words in his empty office. This bought him a fragile sense
of control, but it also exhausted him. And despite the rehearsals, he found himself pulling away. He
stopped answering texts from his oldest friend. He declined dinner invitations. He let the weekly
poker game lapse. Isolation felt safer than the risk of being asked how he was doing, because he no
longer knew how to answer that question truthfully.

The person he had become was unrecognizable to him. David looked in the mirror and saw a man who
flinched at a hospital smell, who could not trust his own body to stay healthy, who could not rely
on himself to endure a routine checkup. He had always been the one people leaned on. Now he felt
like a structure with a cracked foundation. The body that had once carried him through marathons and
parent-teacher conferences now felt like a traitor, capable of harboring a hidden growth, of
betraying him without warning. He had come to believe that he could not count on himself to handle
even ordinary life, because ordinary life was what had preceded the discovery of the tumor.

What finally brought him to seek help was a single moment in the school hallway. A student had
spilled a bottle of rubbing alcohol, and the sharp, sweet smell hit David from twenty feet away. He
had turned, walked into his office, closed the door, and sat in the dark for forty minutes, shaking,
unable to speak to anyone. His assistant principal knocked twice. He did not answer. That night, he
called a therapist listed on his insurance website. He was terrified that if he did not find a way
through this, he would lose his job, his relationships, and whatever was left of himself. He was
also terrified that therapy would force him to remember what he had worked so hard to forget. But he
was more afraid of staying the way he was.
