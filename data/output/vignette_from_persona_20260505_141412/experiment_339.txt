Mark is a 55-year-old counselor who has spent two decades helping others navigate their worst
moments, yet he finds himself unable to navigate his own. He lives alone in a quiet apartment
complex, and for the past eight months, since the fire at the community center where he was leading
a support group, his life has contracted into a narrow, watchful existence. He came to therapy not
because he wanted to, but because his supervisor at the county mental health agency noticed he had
begun canceling his evening groups and avoiding the break room where someone once microwaved popcorn
too long.

The fire started during a routine Tuesday evening session. A client, a young man who had been
struggling with rage after a workplace accident, had become agitated during a check-in. Mark had
seen escalation before and was reaching for the de-escalation script when the client suddenly swept
a stack of papers and a coffee mug off the table. The ceramic mug shattered against the wall, and in
the same instant, a faulty space heater in the corner sparked. The smell of burning dust and plastic
filled the room before the smoke alarm even sounded. Mark remembers the client’s face, the way the
man’s anger collapsed into terror as the smoke thickened. He remembers the smell—acrid, chemical,
alive—and he remembers thinking, *I have to get everyone out*. He did. No one was hurt. But in the
weeks that followed, Mark began to realize that he had not left that room.

The smell is what haunts him most. It returns without warning—the sharp, burnt smell of smoke and
plastic—and when it does, his body reacts before his mind can catch up. His heart pounds, his palms
sweat, and he feels a rush of adrenaline that leaves him trembling and disoriented. He has come to
believe that his body has turned against him, that it keeps reacting as if he is still in danger
even when he is standing in his own kitchen or walking through a grocery store. What frightens him
most is that sometimes the emotion arrives with no image at all. He will be sitting at his desk,
reviewing case notes, and suddenly he is flooded with the same terror he felt that night, but there
is no picture in his mind, no memory of the smoke or the client’s face—just the raw, unbearable
feeling. He has started to feel that the memory itself does not belong to him. When he tries to
recall the fire, it feels like a story he was told about someone else, not something he lived
through. And yet his body remembers what his mind cannot claim.

Mark has become vigilant in a way that exhausts him. He scans every room he enters for loose
objects, for things that could be thrown, for anything that might spark or smolder. He is certain
that if he lets his guard down, something terrible will happen again. He monitors his own thoughts
constantly, checking himself for signs that he is losing control, because he is afraid that if he
allows himself to think about the event, he will not be able to handle it. He has stopped seeing his
friends. They call, they leave messages, but he cannot bring himself to respond. He tells himself he
is just busy, but the truth is that he is afraid of what might come up if he has to talk about his
life. He is afraid that if someone asks him how he is, he will crack open.

At work, he has become a ghost. He still sees his individual clients, but he has stopped running the
evening support groups. He avoids the staff lounge, where the faint smell of burnt coffee triggers a
cold sweat. He eats lunch in his car. His supervisor has asked if he is okay, and Mark has said yes,
because he does not know how to say that he is terrified of his own mind. What finally brought him
to seek help was a moment last week when a client, in a moment of frustration, slammed a notebook on
the desk. Mark flinched so violently that he knocked over his chair. He excused himself to the
bathroom and stood there, gripping the sink, trying to breathe. He realized that he could not keep
pretending that he was fine. He is afraid that if he starts talking about the fire, he will never
stop, that the feelings will drown him. But he is also afraid that if he does not, he will lose the
only thing he has left—his ability to do the work that has given his life meaning.
