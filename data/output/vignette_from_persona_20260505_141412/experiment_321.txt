Dr. Priya Mehta is an 80-year-old divorced South Asian woman who works part-time as a dentist in a
small private practice in a mid-sized American city. She came to therapy at the urging of her adult
daughter, who had grown concerned about how withdrawn and vigilant her mother had become over the
past several years. What Priya initially described as "just getting older and more careful" began to
unravel during the first session, when she quietly recounted the morning of September 11, 2001.

Priya had been working at a dental office near the World Trade Center that day. She remembered the
sound of the first plane — a low, shuddering roar that she initially mistook for construction noise.
Then came the sirens. She described them as "endless," a wailing that seemed to come from every
direction at once, layering over each other until the air itself felt like a scream. When the second
plane hit, she and her colleagues evacuated. The smoke was thick and acrid, stinging her eyes and
coating her throat. She could taste it for days afterward. In the chaos, she became separated from
her staff and found herself pushed along by a crowd she could not see the front of, her body pressed
against strangers, unable to stop or turn. For what felt like hours, she was completely powerless —
someone else was deciding where she would go, someone else controlled whether she lived or died. She
survived physically unharmed, but something in her never fully left that street.

In the years that followed, Priya noticed that sirens — even distant ones — made her chest tighten
and her hands shake. She began to avoid driving during certain hours, worried she might be caught
near an emergency vehicle. She also realized that the smell of smoke, even from a neighbor’s
barbecue, could send her heart racing and make her feel dizzy, as though the air itself had turned
poisonous. She started keeping her windows closed in the summer, and she stopped visiting friends
who used fireplaces. More subtly, she became hyperaware of situations where someone else held
authority over her. At work, she found herself resenting the new office manager, a younger woman who
scheduled her shifts. Priya knew this was irrational — the manager was perfectly pleasant — but the
feeling of someone else deciding her movements made her stomach churn. She began to insist on
controlling her own schedule, which created tension with the staff.

Her avoidance deepened over time. She refused to go anywhere near lower Manhattan, even for dental
conferences. She would take long detours to avoid certain intersections. At home, she always sat
facing the door in restaurants, and in any public space, she scanned the room repeatedly, noting
every exit. She would not sit with her back to a door, and if a room had only one exit, she felt
trapped and would leave early. Her daughter noticed that Priya no longer attended family gatherings
in unfamiliar venues, and she had stopped going to the theater or movies altogether. The cost of
this vigilance was exhaustion. Priya slept poorly, her neck and shoulders perpetually tense from the
constant scanning.

What finally brought Priya to seek help was not the anxiety itself, but the loneliness. She had
retired from full-time practice two years ago, thinking it would bring relief, but instead she found
herself spending most days in her apartment, venturing out only for groceries and the occasional
dental shift she could not cancel. She had stopped calling old friends, because every invitation
meant navigating a new location, and the effort felt insurmountable. Her daughter had said, "Mom,
you used to love going out. Now you just sit here." That sentence had hit Priya harder than she
expected. She realized she did not know how to get back to the person she had been before the sirens
and the smoke and the feeling of being helpless in a crowd. She was afraid that she was too old to
change, but more afraid that she would spend the rest of her life in a shrinking world, waiting for
a sound that might never come.
