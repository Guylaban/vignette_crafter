For as long as Elena could remember, she had been the one people leaned on. At seventy-four, she
still worked three days a week as a pharmacist in a small neighborhood pharmacy, her hands steady as
she counted pills, her voice calm as she explained side effects to anxious patients. But that
version of herself had begun to feel like a stranger, a photograph of someone she no longer
recognized. The change started two years ago, when her husband of fifty years—a man she had trusted
with her life—first raised his hand to her during an argument about their daughter. It had not been
a single event but a series of them, escalating over months, until the night he shoved her against
the kitchen counter so hard that her ribs cracked against the edge. She remembered that night not as
a story with a beginning, middle, and end, but as a handful of snapshots: the sound of the door
slamming as he stormed out, the cold tile against her cheek, the way her body had frozen in place as
if she were already a corpse. When the memories came now, they did not arrive as a narrative. They
came as those same still images, each one a photograph she could not look away from, and with them
came the feeling that her body was back in that exact position—stiff, pinned, unable to move. She
would be standing at the pharmacy counter, handing a customer a receipt, and suddenly her shoulders
would lock, her breath would stop, and she would feel the cold floor beneath her cheek again. The
shame that followed was worse than the fear. She had been a pharmacist for forty years, a mother, a
grandmother, a woman who had built a life. How could she have let this happen? How could she still
love the man who had done this? She had come to believe that she was weak, that she had failed some
fundamental test of personhood, and that the person she used to be—capable, steady, reliable—was
gone forever.

Elena had always been alert, but now her alertness had become its own kind of prison. She listened
constantly. She listened for the sound of the front door opening, for the creak of a floorboard, for
any noise that might mean someone was entering her home unannounced. Even when her husband was at
work, even when she knew she was alone, her body remained tense, coiled, ready. She could not relax
in her own living room. She could not sit down to watch television without her eyes drifting to the
door, her ears straining for the click of the lock. At night, she slept on the edge of the bed, one
foot on the floor, as if she might need to run. The hypervigilance had bled into her work. She began
to avoid her own body—she stopped looking in mirrors, stopped noticing how her clothes fit, stopped
paying attention to the ache in her ribs because acknowledging her body meant acknowledging what had
happened to it. She also stopped allowing herself to feel. When a patient thanked her warmly, when a
coworker touched her arm in kindness, she felt a wave of panic. Emotional closeness had become a
threat. She had learned that people you loved were the ones who could hurt you, and so she began to
pull away. She stopped attending family dinners. She stopped calling her daughter. She told herself
it was because she was busy, but the truth was that any comment about family—even a cheerful one
from a customer about their own children—sent a jolt through her chest. She avoided people
altogether when she could. She took the longer route home to avoid neighbors who might want to chat.
She ate dinner alone in the kitchen while her husband ate in the living room, the silence between
them thick and unbroken.

What finally brought Elena to seek help was not the fear or the shame, but the loneliness. She had
spent her whole life surrounded by people, and now she had almost no one. She had lost her faith—not
just in her husband, but in people, in the idea that the world was safe or fair. She had lost her
faith in herself. She came to the clinic because her daughter had begged her, because she could no
longer bear the weight of her own silence, and because somewhere beneath the shame and the
hypervigilance and the frozen snapshots of that terrible night, she still hoped that the woman she
used to be might still exist. But what frightened her most was the possibility that she was
right—that she was too weak to recover, that the capable Elena was gone for good, and that all that
remained was a body waiting for the next door to slam.
