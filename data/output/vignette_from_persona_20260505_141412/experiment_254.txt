Samuel is a seventy-eight-year-old married man who works as a librarian, a position he has held for
over four decades. He was born in West Africa and immigrated to the United States in his twenties,
building a quiet, orderly life around books, routine, and family. That order was shattered on a
Tuesday afternoon three years ago, when a lone gunman entered the public library where Samuel
worked. The man was shouting political slogans, waving a handgun, and firing indiscriminately into
the reading room. Samuel, who was at the reference desk, watched as two patrons were shot before his
eyes. He remembers every detail with brutal clarity: the crack of the gunshots, the smell of
cordite, the way a woman’s glasses flew off her face as she fell. He can still hear the screaming.
The gunman was eventually subdued by police, but Samuel spent twenty-three minutes hiding behind a
collapsed bookshelf, certain he would die.

In the months that followed, Samuel found that the memory of that afternoon did not fade. Instead,
it played over and over in his mind, like a film he could not stop. He would be shelving books or
helping a patron find a title, and suddenly the full scene would return without warning—the
shouting, the shots, the terror—as vivid as if it were happening again. He began to notice that
certain things could bring it back: the anniversary of the event, which he now dreads each year; the
sound of raised voices in the library, which he now interprets as the beginning of violence; and
conversations about politics or public safety, which he avoids entirely, even with his wife. What
frightens him most is not the memory itself, but the feeling that he is still in that moment, that
the danger has not passed.

Samuel has come to believe that the world is fundamentally unsafe, and that people are capable of
terrible things at any time. He watches the behavior of others with constant vigilance. When a
patron speaks too loudly, or when a child runs down an aisle, he feels a surge of adrenaline. He is
certain that any misbehavior—a raised voice, a sudden movement—could be the start of another attack.
He has started avoiding crowds altogether. He no longer goes to the supermarket or to public events.
He has stopped attending his weekly book club, because the lively debates now feel threatening
rather than enjoyable. He has also begun to avoid situations that might make him feel happy or
hopeful. He does not want to let his guard down. He is convinced that any moment of peace is a trap,
a prelude to disaster.

At home, Samuel has developed a habit of drifting off into a world of his own. He sits in his
armchair for hours, staring at nothing, replaying the event in his mind or imagining alternate
endings. His wife, Grace, has noticed that he no longer laughs at her jokes, no longer plans for the
future. She has tried to draw him out, but Samuel finds that even talking about the weather feels
dangerous. He has become convinced that certain conversation topics—anything related to violence,
politics, or even the news—will trigger a flashback, so he avoids them entirely. The consequence has
been a profound withdrawal. He and Grace now eat dinner in silence. He has stopped calling his adult
children. His world has shrunk to the narrow corridor between his armchair and the bathroom.

What finally brought Samuel to seek help was a panic attack on the anniversary of the shooting. He
was in the library, his workplace, when a patron dropped a heavy book on the floor. The sound was
loud, sudden, and Samuel found himself on the floor, shaking, gasping for air, convinced the gunman
had returned. His colleague called an ambulance. Now, sitting in the clinic, Samuel is afraid of two
things: that he will never feel safe again, and that seeking help will somehow make him more
vulnerable. He hopes, quietly, to one day walk into the library without flinching. He hopes to laugh
with his wife again. But he is not sure that is possible.
