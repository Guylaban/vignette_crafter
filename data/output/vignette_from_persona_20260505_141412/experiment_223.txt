his hands rested on his knees as if bracing for impact. At sixty-eight, he had spent decades as a
policy analyst in Washington, D.C., parsing data and forecasting outcomes, but nothing in his

heart attack had nearly killed him. He remembered the fluorescent lights of the office flickering
above him as colleagues shouted, the paramedics’ hands pressing rhythmically on his sternum, the
cold oxygen mask fogging his vision. What he remembered most vividly, however, was the sensation of
his own heart—his own reliable, unremarkable heart—becoming a foreign agent, a traitor that had
turned against him without warning. That was the moment that had never ended.

In the months following his discharge, Kwame tried to return to his routines. He went back to the
office, sat at the same desk, and opened the same spreadsheets. But something fundamental had
shifted. The memory of the attack did not feel like a memory at all. When it surfaced—often without
any obvious trigger—it arrived with the full sensory force of the original event: the pressure in
his chest, the flickering lights, the urgent voices. It was as if time had collapsed and he was once
again on that cold floor. At other times, he could recall the event with clinical detachment,
narrating the sequence of symptoms and interventions as though describing someone else’s medical
chart, and this numbness frightened him more than the terror did. He had come to believe that he
would never feel normal emotions again—that the heart attack had stolen not only his health but his
capacity for genuine feeling. When his wife of forty-two years, Ama, died of cancer eighteen months
after his own illness, he attended the funeral in a state of hollow composure, unable to weep,
certain that the tears belonged to a man he could no longer be.

Kwame’s body had become a source of constant menace. He felt perpetually on edge, his muscles
coiled, his breath shallow, as though danger lurked just beyond his peripheral vision. Even inside
his own home—the same house where he had raised his children and hosted holiday dinners—he could not
relax. He found himself scanning the living room for exits, checking his pulse multiple times a day,
interpreting every skipped heartbeat or muscle twitch as a prelude to collapse. His body, he was
certain, could no longer be trusted. It kept reacting as if the attack were still in progress,
flooding him with adrenaline in response to nothing at all. He had begun to avoid physical exertion
entirely, refusing to climb the stairs to his second-floor study because the slight increase in his
heart rate felt indistinguishable from catastrophe.

The consequences of his avoidance had become concrete and undeniable. As a policy analyst, Kwame had
been known for his sharp focus and his ability to synthesize complex data under tight deadlines. Now
he struggled to hold a single train of thought for more than a few minutes. During meetings, his
mind would drift to the sensation of his own heartbeat, and he would lose the thread of the
discussion. His supervisor had gently suggested he take a leave of absence, and Kwame had accepted
with a shame that sat in his chest like a second heart attack waiting to happen. He spent most days
now in his armchair, watching the news with the volume low, his hand pressed against his sternum as
if to reassure himself that the organ beneath was still beating.

What had finally driven him to seek help was not the occupational decline or the social isolation,

could no longer distinguish between a genuine medical threat and the mere thought of one. He was
hoping, though he barely dared to articulate it, that a therapist might help him separate the
two—might teach him to trust his body again, or at least to stop fearing it every moment of every
day.
