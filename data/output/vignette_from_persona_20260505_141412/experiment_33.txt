In March of last year, Specialist Mariana Rojas was stationed at a forward operating base in the
Mississippi floodplain when a catastrophic storm surge overwhelmed the perimeter. She had been on
overnight guard duty in a reinforced concrete shelter, but the water rose so fast that the door
became impossible to open against the pressure. For nearly four hours she stood waist-deep in muddy,
freezing water, listening to the metal walls groan and buckle, certain she would drown before anyone
could reach her. When the rescue team finally cut through the roof, she had to be pulled out by her
vest straps, shivering and silent, unable to speak for the rest of the day.

In the weeks that followed, Mariana returned to duty, but something fundamental had shifted. She
found herself unable to tolerate the sound of rain on the barracks roof. The first time a heavy
shower began during morning formation, she felt her chest seize and her vision narrow, and she had
to excuse herself to the latrine to breathe. She began checking the weather forecast obsessively,
sometimes ten or twelve times a day, and any prediction of storms sent her into a quiet, controlled
panic. By summer, she could no longer sit with her back to a door. She had started scanning every
room she entered for exits, for high ground, for anything that could become a trap. At the dining
facility, she would circle the entire space before choosing a seat, noting the fire escape and the
reinforced windows, and she would still feel unsafe enough to eat quickly and leave.

Her husband, Carlos, noticed first that she no longer wanted to drive through the city after dark.
Then he noticed that she flinched at the sound of a truck backfiring, or a car door slamming, or a
dropped tray in the commissary. Mariana began to interpret these reactions as evidence of a deep
personal failure. She told herself that other soldiers had been through worse and were fine, that
her own jumpiness meant she was weak, that she was a lousy coper who couldn't handle what the job
required. She started to feel less like a person and more like a piece of equipment that had been
damaged beyond repair. When her unit held a mandatory resilience training, she sat through it with a
blank face, certain that nothing could change what she had become. She began to believe that bad
things were simply going to happen to her, that there was no point in planning or hoping because she
had no real control over anything.

Her avoidance grew more elaborate. She stopped going to the base gym because it had no windows. She
requested a desk assignment in a windowless administrative office, telling her sergeant it was for a
back problem. She began taking a circuitous route to work that avoided a particular bridge over a
river, even though it added twenty minutes to her commute. When Carlos suggested they take a weekend
trip to the coast, she told him she couldn't leave the area because of training requirements. In
truth, she was terrified of being anywhere unfamiliar without a clear escape plan, and the thought
of being near a large body of water made her feel like she was already drowning.

The police presence at a traffic stop two months ago became a new trigger. She was driving home when
she saw a patrol car with its lights on, and her mind immediately went to the rescue team cutting
through the roof, the flashing emergency lights reflecting off the floodwater, the feeling of being
trapped and helpless. She pulled over herself, shaking, and had to call Carlos to come get her. Now
she avoids the main road where the police station is located, and she feels a spike of fear every
time she sees a uniformed officer, even though she knows rationally that they are there to help.

What finally brought Mariana to the clinic was a recent thunderstorm that rolled through at three in
the morning. She woke up disoriented, convinced the water was rising again, and she spent the rest
of the night sitting on the bathroom counter with her knees pulled to her chest, unable to wake
Carlos because she was ashamed of how irrational it all sounded. The next morning, she called the
behavioral health office and made an appointment. She is not sure what she expects—she does not
believe she can be fixed, and she is afraid that talking about it will only prove how broken she
really is. But she is also tired of living this way, of checking exits and watching the sky and
feeling like a stranger in her own life. She wants to stop being afraid of the rain, but she does
not know if that is possible.
