Rukmini was thirty-two years old, a chaplain in a large urban hospital, and she had been divorced
for three years when she finally walked into a therapist’s office. She had come to believe that she
was broken in a way that could not be fixed, and that the only reason she was seeking help was
because her hands had begun to shake so badly during a patient’s family consultation that the
attending nurse had asked if she was ill. Rukmini had said she was fine, but she was not fine. She
had not been fine for a long time.

The event that had fractured her life happened five years earlier, during a humanitarian mission in
a conflict zone. She had been part of a small interfaith team providing spiritual care to displaced
civilians. One evening, armed men in military uniforms had stormed the compound. They had separated
the team members, and Rukmini had been dragged into a windowless storage room. For three days, she
was held there, bound at the wrists and ankles with thick plastic ties, her body pinned to a
concrete floor. The men wore uniforms she recognised—she had seen them on checkpoints for weeks—and
they spoke a language she understood only in fragments. What frightened her most was not the blows,
though there were blows, but the sound of their boots on the concrete, the scrape of a chair being
dragged, the jingle of keys. She had been certain she would die in that room, and that no one would
ever find her body.

When she was released, she did not speak of it. She flew home, resumed her chaplaincy, and told
herself the nightmare was over. But the nightmare had followed her. She found she could not describe
what had happened. When colleagues asked, gently, what she had experienced overseas, the words would
not come. She knew exactly what had occurred—the sequence of events was burned into her mind—but
when she tried to explain, her throat closed, and the sentences tangled and died. She would lose the
thread, stumble, and eventually fall silent. The memory existed in her body, not in language: the
feeling of plastic cutting into her wrists, the pressure of a knee on her back, the smell of sweat
and diesel. She could not put it into words, and this failure made her feel as though she was lying,
or as though she had imagined the whole thing.

Her work as a chaplain became a minefield. The hospital had a security desk at the main entrance,
and the guards wore uniforms. Rukmini began to take a service elevator that opened directly into the
chapel wing, avoiding the lobby entirely. She could not explain why without sounding irrational, so
she told herself it was faster. Open spaces—the hospital atrium, the parking lot, the long corridor
to the cafeteria—made her feel exposed, as though she might be seen and targeted. She started eating
lunch in her office, door locked. She stopped attending staff meetings in the conference room, which
had large windows. These avoidances cost her: she missed important briefings, lost touch with
colleagues, and was perceived as aloof. The hospital chaplaincy director mentioned that she seemed
withdrawn. Rukmini agreed, and offered no explanation.

The social contacts she had maintained before the event—friends from graduate school, members of her
interfaith network—became unbearable. She stopped returning calls. She told herself they would not
understand, and what frightened her more was the possibility that they might ask questions she could
not answer. She had come to believe that if anyone knew what had happened to her, they would see her
as weak, or damaged, or somehow complicit. So she withdrew entirely, and the silence grew around her
like a wall.

At night, the sounds came back. The jingle of keys. The scrape of a chair. She would wake with her
heart pounding, her wrists crossed in front of her as though still bound. During the day, she began
to hurt herself physically to stop the thoughts. A sharp pinch on the inside of her arm would
interrupt the spiral; a thumbnail pressed into her palm would bring her back to the present. She
knew it was wrong, but it was the only thing that worked. She had not told anyone.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment in the hospital chapel. A patient’s family
member, a young woman, had begun to cry, and Rukmini had reached out to hold her hand. The woman had
gripped her back tightly, and for a split second, Rukmini had felt herself pinned to that concrete
floor again, unable to move, unable to breathe. She had pulled her hand away too quickly, and the
woman had looked at her with confusion. That night, Rukmini sat alone in her apartment and admitted
to herself that she could not go on like this. She did not know if therapy could help—she was not
sure she could even find the words—but she was terrified that if she did not try, she would lose the
one thing she still had: her ability to be present for others. She wanted that back. She was not
sure she deserved it, but she wanted it.
