Elena came to therapy at twenty-one, a year after she had graduated from art school and begun
working as a junior animator for a small studio. She sat forward on the edge of the chair, her hands
clasped tightly in her lap, and spoke in a low, rapid voice as though she needed to get the story
out before she lost her nerve.

The event that had shaped her, she said, was not one single moment but a decade of them. From the
time she was seven until she was seventeen, her father had thrown things at her mother. Elena had
watched from doorways, from behind the sofa, from the top of the stairs. She remembered the sound of
a ceramic vase shattering against the kitchen wall, the smell of the dust from the drywall it had
punched through, the way her mother would stand very still afterward, not crying, not moving, as
though staying frozen might make her invisible. No one had ever been held accountable. Her father
still lived in the same city. Her mother had never pressed charges. Elena had learned early that the
world did not correct itself, and that bad things simply happened and kept happening.

Now, as an adult, she found herself living inside the echo of those years. Her memories of the worst
incidents did not feel like memories at all. They felt more real than the present moment. When she
thought about her father’s face contorted in rage, she could smell the faint metallic tang of blood
from the time her mother’s lip had split, or the dusty smell of the living room rug where she had
crouched, pressing her hands over her ears. She would try to push the memory away before it fully
arrived—she had developed a skill for slamming a mental door on an image before it could play
out—but she was not always fast enough. When she failed, the memory consumed her, and she would find
herself trembling in her chair at work, the animation software still open on her screen, her
colleagues unaware.

Her triggers were everywhere. At the studio, a coworker tossed a stress ball across the room to
another desk, and Elena’s entire body locked. Her heart slammed against her ribs, and she had to
excuse herself to the bathroom, where she stood with her hands braced on the sink, telling herself
she was safe, she was an adult, no one was throwing anything at her. She had become hypervigilant to
movement in her peripheral vision; any sudden motion—a hand raised to wave, a chair pushed back too
quickly—made her flinch as though she were about to be struck. Her startle response was so
pronounced that she had snapped at a colleague who had simply walked up behind her desk. She had
been irritable for months, short-tempered with everyone, and she knew it was costing her
friendships, but she could not seem to stop the angry words that came out of her mouth before she
could think.

To manage the chaos she felt inside, Elena had begun to control the one thing she could: food. She
restricted her eating with rigid precision, counting calories, skipping meals, telling herself that
if she could govern her own body with enough discipline, she would not be helpless. But the belief
that she could not cope with even the slightest upset persisted. A small frustration—a file that
would not render, a bus that was late—sent her into a spiral of despair. She was certain that she
could not handle anything, that she was fundamentally incapable of managing her own life.

She had also stopped making plans. When friends asked if she wanted to grab dinner next week or see
a movie in two months, she felt a cold dread. Making a plan felt like tempting fate, like assuming
the future existed when she knew, deep down, that it did not. She avoided anything that reminded her
of her father—certain songs, certain brands of beer, the particular shade of gray of a sedan like
his—and she had deleted all the old family photos from her phone, but she could not delete the
images from her mind.

What finally brought Elena to therapy was a morning at work when a coworker, trying to be friendly,
had shown her a photo from a recent family gathering. The flash of a smiling group, the warmth of a
living room, had triggered a memory so vivid that Elena had stood up, walked out of the building,
and sat on a bench in the parking lot, shaking, for twenty minutes. She knew she could not keep
living this way. She was afraid that she would never feel safe, that the past would always be more
real than the present. But she was also, for the first time, hoping that someone might show her how
to stop running from a memory that would not stay locked away.
