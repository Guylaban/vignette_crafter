Marcus was twenty-nine years old, a jeweler by trade, and he had come to understand that the steady,
careful work of setting stones and repairing delicate chains was the only thing that still felt
safe. He had not always been a jeweler. Three years earlier, he had been a relief worker in a
conflict zone, part of a small team that delivered medical supplies and food to villages caught
between shifting front lines. The work had been dangerous but necessary, and for the first few
months, he had believed that the danger was something he could manage, something with rules. He had
been wrong. What happened on the final day was not a single explosion or a firefight. It was a crowd
of displaced families, desperate and terrified, pressing against the gate of the compound where his
team was sheltering. A child was crying, a high, piercing sound that cut through the shouting.
Marcus had tried to help, to open the gate just enough to pass out a crate of supplies, but the
crowd surged. He was pulled from the vehicle, knocked to the ground, and for a long, suffocating
moment, he was pinned beneath bodies, unable to breathe, his heart hammering so hard he thought it
would burst. A hand clamped over his mouth, someone’s knee drove into his ribs, and he was certain
he was going to die. He did not die. Someone dragged him back inside. The child kept crying. He
never forgot that sound.

In the months that followed, Marcus returned home and tried to resume his life. He took the job at
the jewelry shop because it was quiet, because it required fine motor control and deep
concentration, and because no one there knew what he had seen. But the quiet did not last. He began
to notice that his own body had become a source of alarm. When his heart raced, even from something
as simple as climbing stairs or drinking coffee, he felt a surge of panic so intense that he had to
sit down, certain that the pounding in his chest was a warning of something catastrophic. He could
not be touched unexpectedly. A colleague clapping him on the shoulder, a customer reaching past him
to point at a display case, even a friend’s hand on his arm in greeting—each time, he flinched as if
struck, his muscles locking, his breath catching. And children crying. That was the worst. A toddler
fussing in a stroller outside the shop window could send him into a state of hypervigilance that
lasted for hours, his jaw tight, his eyes scanning the street for threats that no one else seemed to
see.

He developed a set of habits that he told himself were just common sense. He sat with his back to
the wall in every restaurant, every waiting room, every bench in the park. He could not eat lunch in
the shop’s break room because the door was behind him. He constantly scanned his environment for
potential weapons—a pair of scissors on a counter, a heavy glass paperweight, a sharpened pencil—and
noted their locations as if cataloging ammunition. When others told him he was safe, he found it
impossible to believe them. The words felt hollow, like a script someone had handed him to read,
while his own mind insisted that safety was a luxury he no longer had access to. He tried to manage
the fear with thoughts he had learned in a self-help book: he reminded himself that it could have
been worse, that he had survived, that others had not. He forced himself to think only positive
thoughts, repeating affirmations until his temples ached. But the fear did not leave. It only
waited.

The memories came without warning. A particular smell in the air, the angle of late afternoon
sunlight, the sound of a distant siren—something in his surroundings could instantly bring back the
full experience, and for a few seconds, he was back on the ground, the weight pressing down, the
child crying. Sometimes the emotion came without any image at all. He would be sitting at his
workbench, a loupe in his eye, and suddenly he would be flooded with terror, his hands shaking, his
throat tight, and he could not connect the feeling to anything in the present. The memories
themselves felt more real than ordinary memories. When he thought about his last vacation, the
images were flat and distant, like photographs in an old album. But the memory of that day was
vivid, immediate, and alive. It did not feel like the past. It felt like the present, waiting to
happen again.

His social life had shrunk to almost nothing. He stopped answering calls from old friends because he
could not explain why he had to check every exit before sitting down, why he could not tolerate a
hand on his shoulder, why the sound of a child playing in the next room made him want to run. He had
not been on a date in two years. The jewelry shop had become his entire world, a small, controlled
space where he could manage the variables. But even there, the vigilance was exhausting. He came
home each night with sore shoulders and a dull headache, his jaw clenched from hours of scanning, of
checking, of waiting.

What finally brought him to seek help was a morning in the shop when his heart began to race for no
reason at all. He was alone, the store was silent, and there was no threat. But his body did not
believe that. He stood with his back to the wall, his hands trembling, and for the first time, he
understood that he could not out-think this. He did not know if talking could help. He was afraid
that it would only make the memories more real. But he was also afraid of living the rest of his
life this way, afraid that the only thing he could trust was the feeling of being unsafe. He wanted
to believe that there was another way to be in the world, even if he could not yet imagine what it
looked like.
