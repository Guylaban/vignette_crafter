Liam owned a small hardware store on a quiet street in a Midwestern town, a business he had built
with his younger brother, Derek, over twenty years. They had been partners in everything, their
families intertwined. Two years ago, Derek was killed in a car accident. A drunk driver ran a red
light. Liam arrived at the scene minutes after it happened, drawn by the commotion, only to see his
brother’s truck crumpled against a telephone pole. He had stood on the sidewalk, frozen, as
paramedics worked. He had seen Derek’s hand, still and pale, hanging out of the shattered window.
That image had not faded. It was as clear and detailed now as it had been in that moment, a film
that played over and over in his mind, often without warning. The memory did not feel like a memory.
It felt like it was happening again, right there in the aisle of his store, or while he was trying
to fall asleep beside his wife, Marie.

Liam had come to believe that the world was full of hidden traps. A customer mentioning that their
mother had passed away would make his chest tighten, his throat close. He would grip the edge of the
counter, his knuckles white, waiting for the feeling to pass. He had stopped watching the news
entirely, because any story about a family losing someone felt like a direct attack. He could not go
to funerals. He had not attended Derek’s. The thought of standing near a grave made him feel
physically ill, as if the ground itself might pull him under. He had begun to avoid the cemetery
entirely, taking a three-mile detour on his way home from work because the sight of the iron gates
made his heart hammer in his ears.

The store had become a minefield. Every morning, as he unlocked the door, he braced himself. A
sudden movement from a customer reaching for a tool would make him flinch, his body jerking as if he
were about to be struck. Ordinary sounds—a door slamming in the back room, a metal shelf being
restocked—would make him jump so violently that his employees had started to notice. He had taken to
smoking again, a habit he had quit a decade ago, standing alone behind the store in the alley. It
was the only time he felt he could breathe. He had also stopped thinking about the event
deliberately, pushing it away whenever it surfaced, but the harder he tried to suppress it, the more
the film played, relentless and vivid.

The consequences had accumulated quietly. Marie had asked him, gently, why he no longer wanted to
visit her parents, who lived near a cemetery. He had made excuses—the store was busy, he was tired.
He had stopped attending his son’s school events because one of the other fathers, a grief
counselor, had once given a talk about loss. Liam had not told Marie the real reason. He was certain
that if he admitted how afraid he was, how the grief felt like a physical threat, he would somehow
make it worse. He had come to believe that he could not handle even a glimpse of that pain again.

What finally brought him to seek help was a morning in the store, two weeks ago. A woman came in,
her eyes red, and asked for a lock for a garden gate. She mentioned, offhand, that she was securing
it for her husband’s memorial garden. Liam had felt the memory surge, the film starting, and he had
excused himself to the back room, where he stood shaking, a cigarette unlit in his hand. He had
realized, with a cold clarity, that he was no longer living. He was just reacting. He wanted to stop
being afraid of the world. He wanted to be able to think of his brother without feeling like he was
going to die. But what frightened him most was the possibility that talking about it would make the
memory feel even more present, more real—that the film would never stop playing.
