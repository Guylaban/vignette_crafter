Maya grew up in a small apartment in San Antonio, the oldest of three, learning early how to fix
things. By twenty-seven, she had been working as an HVAC technician for five years, comfortable in
crawl spaces and mechanical rooms—places where the work was straightforward, the instructions clear,
and the people she dealt with were usually men who kept conversation minimal. She had always
preferred that. What happened two years ago made that preference feel less like a choice and more
like a necessity.

She had been dating a woman named Elena for about four months when Elena’s ex-boyfriend, a man Maya
had never met, found out where she lived. He broke in late one night while Maya was asleep. He had a
knife and a length of electrical cord. For reasons Maya still could not make sense of, he did not
kill her. Instead, he kept her in her own apartment for nearly six hours, tying her wrists and
ankles to the frame of her bed, covering her mouth with duct tape. He told her she had ruined
something for him, that Elena had left him because of Maya, and that he was going to make sure Maya
understood what it felt like to have no choices. He cut her arm twice with the knife, shallow lines
that healed into thin white scars, but the worst part was the waiting—the hours of not knowing when
he would stop talking, when he would leave, whether he would come back. She remembered the sound of
his footsteps pacing the hallway, the way the tape pulled at the skin around her mouth, the
certainty that no one was coming. When he finally left at dawn, she lay there for another hour
before she could move her hands enough to free herself.

She did not call the police. She told herself it was over, that she could just move on. She packed a
bag and stayed with her brother for two weeks, then returned to her apartment and scrubbed the
bedroom floor until her knees ached. She thought if she cleaned hard enough, the memory would fade.
It did not. Instead, the memory began to feel strange to her, like something that had happened to a
different person. She could see the scene in her mind—the cord around her wrists, the knife blade
catching the light from the window—but it did not feel like her own life. That detachment might have
been a relief, except that when the memory did hit her, it hit with a force that stopped her cold.
She would be in the middle of replacing a compressor unit behind a restaurant, and suddenly her
hands would go numb and she would have to sit down on the concrete floor, overwhelmed by a wave of
shame so sharp it felt physical. She was ashamed of what? She could not name it. She had done
nothing wrong. But the shame sat in her chest anyway, heavy and familiar.

Over the months that followed, Maya began to notice changes in how she moved through the world. She
started checking her apartment door three times before bed, then four, then five. She checked the
windows even though she lived on the third floor. She woke at two or three in the morning, heart
pounding, and lay still in the dark, listening, before she could convince herself she was alone. She
jumped at the sound of a dropped tool in a warehouse. She started to believe that she could never
really know who might hurt her—that anyone could be the one, and she would not see it coming. She
also began to doubt herself in small, grinding ways. When a customer asked her a question about a
thermostat installation, she second-guessed her answer for hours. She started to feel as though she
could not trust her own judgment, that maybe she had missed something obvious about Elena, about the
ex-boyfriend, about herself. She had come to believe that she was permanently damaged, that the
person she had been before—confident, direct, unafraid of dark basements and locked crawl spaces—was
gone, and that the woman who replaced her was brittle and watchful.

Her work became a minefield. The most difficult situations were the ones where she had to be alone
with a female customer. She could not explain why, but being in a house with only a woman present
made her feel trapped. Her throat would tighten. She would finish the job as fast as possible and
leave without making eye contact. She also began to dread seeing visible injuries on others—a
bandaged hand, a bruise on someone’s arm, a healing cut. Those sights sent her straight back to the
feeling of the knife on her skin, and she would have to look away. She started avoiding jobs that
required her to work in tight spaces with no clear exit. She told her supervisor she had developed
claustrophobia. She began to turn down assignments that felt too enclosed, which meant fewer hours,
less money, and a growing reputation as unreliable.

What finally brought Maya to seek help was not the fear or the shame or the lost income. It was a
moment at a grocery store, three weeks ago, when a woman ahead of her in line had a small bandage on
her forearm. Maya stared at it for too long, then dropped her basket and walked out. She stood in
the parking lot, hands shaking, and realized she could not keep living this way—constantly scanning,
constantly braced, constantly afraid of what her own mind would do next. She wanted to stop feeling
like the memory owned her. She wanted to trust herself again. She was terrified that she could not.
