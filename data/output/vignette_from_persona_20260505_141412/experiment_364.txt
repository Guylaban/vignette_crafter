Captain David Tavita still remembers the weight of the helicopter door sliding shut. It was 0430
hours, the same time of day that now, thirty-five years later, makes his chest tighten before he
even opens his eyes. He was a young Marine pilot then, ferrying a squad through the dense jungle
canopy of an island chain he had never heard of before deployment. What he remembers most clearly is
not the explosion itself—the RPG that punched through the floor panel—but the texture of the
hydraulic fluid that sprayed across his hands afterward. Thick, warm, and syrupy, it coated his
flight gloves and soaked into the fabric. He can still feel it. That specific viscosity, that smell
of burnt metal and hot oil, returns to him without warning in the most ordinary moments. A spilled
soda on the counter. The grease on a restaurant napkin. His wife’s hand lotion if it is too heavy.
Suddenly he is back in the cockpit, the helicopter shuddering, the crew chief screaming, and the
terror floods through him as fresh as if it were happening now.

David is sixty-three years old, a married man who has flown commercial airliners for three decades.
He has logged more than twenty thousand hours in the air without incident. But the sound of a
helicopter—even one on the news, even one miles away—can derail an entire day. He has learned to
keep his office door closed at the hangar. He avoids the tarmac when the medevac choppers are
running drills. His colleagues think he is particular about noise. The truth is that he is certain
the sound will pull him under, that the memory will seize him by the throat and he will lose control
in front of everyone.

The memory itself feels strange to him, like a story that happened to someone else. He can narrate
the facts: the extraction gone wrong, the two Marines who did not make it, the medal he never wore.
But when he tries to place himself inside that cockpit, something in his mind resists. It is as
though the man who lived through that night is a stranger. And yet, when the trigger comes without
warning—when a sudden movement from a passenger boarding the plane makes him flinch, or when he
walks into a briefing room and finds himself counting the exits before he can stop himself—the
terror is undeniably his own. It belongs to him in a way the memory does not. That contradiction
hollows him out.

He has come to believe that there is something fundamentally wrong with him. Other pilots from his
unit went on with their lives. They have barbecues and grandchildren and hobbies. David used to be a
happy man. He remembers laughing easily, joking with the crew, looking forward to layovers in
foreign cities. Now he is miserable in a way that feels permanent. He does not trust the world. He
scans every restaurant, every waiting room, every hotel lobby for the nearest way out. Night is
worse. He has not slept through the night in years. He lies awake listening for sounds that are not
there, his body braced, his mind running through emergency procedures for threats that do not exist.

His wife has stopped asking why he paces the bedroom at 0430. She knows better than to touch him
when he is standing at the window. The marriage has narrowed into a careful choreography of unspoken
rules. He cannot explain to her why a certain restaurant booth is unbearable, why he needs to sit
facing the door, why he sometimes leaves the table mid-meal because a texture on the napkin has sent
him reeling. He has stopped trying. What would he say? That the world is dangerous and he is the
only one who sees it clearly? That he is broken in a way that cannot be fixed?

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment in the cockpit last month. A passenger made a
sudden lurching movement during turbulence, and David’s hands went white on the yoke. His first
officer asked if he was all right. David said yes. But he was not all right. He was back in the
jungle, and the terror was absolute, and for a few seconds he could not remember which war he was
in. That night he told his wife he would make an appointment. He does not know if anything can
change. He is afraid that the memory will always feel like someone else’s, and that the terror will
always feel like his own. But he is more afraid of what will happen if he does nothing.
