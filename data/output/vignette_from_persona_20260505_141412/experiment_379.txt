Michael is a 49-year-old architect living in a midwestern city with his wife of twenty-two years. He
came to therapy at his wife’s urging, after months of what she described as him “living like a
prisoner in his own head.” He did not disagree. What had happened, he explained slowly, was that he
had witnessed a man die. It was late afternoon on a Tuesday, and Michael had stopped at a downtown
convenience store for coffee. As he walked toward the entrance, a man in a hooded jacket emerged
from the side alley, and another man followed. There was a brief, incomprehensible exchange, and
then the second man raised a gun and shot the first in the chest. Michael saw the impact, heard the
sound, smelled something metallic and sharp that he would later come to associate with blood and
cordite. The shooter fled. Michael stood frozen, coffee cup still in his hand, as the man crumpled
to the pavement. He did not know how long he stood there. He remembered fragments—the way the man’s
body hit the ground, the pooling red, the high-pitched ringing in his own ears—but he could not put
the pieces in order. When he tried to tell the police what he had seen, the words would not come. He
knew what had happened; he had watched it. But when he opened his mouth, the sequence dissolved, and
he could only repeat, “I saw it, I saw it,” as if that were explanation enough.

In the weeks that followed, Michael found that the memory would ambush him without warning. He would
be sitting at his drafting table, calculating load-bearing beams, and suddenly he was back on that
sidewalk, the smell flooding his nostrils, his heart slamming against his ribs, the event happening
all over again. His hands would tremble; his stomach would clench with nausea. He learned to avoid
certain streets, certain times of day, anything that might remind him of the smell or the sound. But
the nausea came anyway, triggered by nothing he could name, and he began to feel as though his own
body had become a trap.

At work, his colleagues noticed a change. Michael had always been meticulous, but now he was visibly
uneasy. He insisted on sitting with his back to the wall in every meeting, positioning his chair so
he could see the entire room. When he entered a new building—a client’s office, a conference
center—he would scan for exits before he could focus on the ceiling heights or the window placement
his job required. He became jumpy; the clatter of a dropped blueprint could send him lurching
upright, his breath shallow. He knew this was irrational, but he could not stop. He was certain that
something terrible could happen again at any moment, and that he would be helpless to stop it.

The helplessness had taken root in him like a splinter. He began to believe that there was something
about him—some weakness, some failure of character—that had made him witness what he had witnessed.
He replayed the event in fragments, searching for the moment he could have intervened, could have
shouted a warning, could have done anything. He found nothing, and the nothing became a verdict: he
was the kind of person bad things happened to, and he could not stop them. He did not tell his wife
this. He did not tell anyone. He felt isolated, set apart from the ordinary lives of the people
around him, as though he now carried a knowledge that made him unfit for normal conversation. He
stopped accepting invitations to dinner. He stopped going to the gym. He came home, sat with his
back to the wall in his own living room, and waited for the night to pass.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment at a client presentation. He had walked into the
conference room, seen the open floor plan with no clear sightline to the door, and felt such a surge
of panic that he had excused himself to the restroom and vomited. He stood over the sink, sweating,
and realized he could no longer do his job. That night, his wife told him she was afraid for him. He
did not argue. He is not sure what he hopes for, exactly. He knows he cannot undo what he saw, and
he does not believe he can ever feel safe again. But he is tired of living with his back to the
wall, and he is terrified that if he does not find a way to speak about what happened, he will lose
the words for everything else.
