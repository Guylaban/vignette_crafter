Amir is a 54-year-old community organizer, married, and a father of three grown children, who came
to therapy at the urging of his wife after months of escalating tension at home. For the past year,
he has been living with a kind of dread that he cannot shake, a sense that the ground beneath him is
unstable. He is a tall, broad-shouldered man who speaks with a careful, measured tone, but his hands
tremble slightly as he describes what happened two years ago.

The event itself was a betrayal so complete that Amir still struggles to hold it in his mind as a
single story. He had been in a romantic relationship with a man named Khalid, a fellow activist he
had known for years and trusted implicitly. One evening, after a community meeting, they drove to a
secluded spot near the river to talk. What began as an argument over a political disagreement turned
quickly into something Amir had never imagined possible. Khalid, a man Amir had loved and confided
in, struck him repeatedly in the face and ribs, then pinned him down and choked him until Amir saw
stars. The assault lasted perhaps ten minutes, but in Amir’s memory, it exists only in fragments:
the sound of his own ribs cracking, the feel of gravel digging into his back, the sight of Khalid’s
face twisted into a mask of rage, and then—nothing. He remembers coming to on the ground, alone in
the dark, with the distant sound of a helicopter overhead, its rotors thumping in the silence.

Amir never reported the assault. He was too ashamed, too certain that people would blame him for
being naive. In the weeks that followed, he found himself unable to go near the riverbank where it
happened. He started taking long detours on his commute, adding twenty minutes to his drive, because
even the street that led in that direction made his chest tighten. At home, he began closing the
blinds at dusk. He had never been afraid of the dark before, but now he could not bear a room
without light. He told himself it was just a phase, that he was being sensible. But the memories
kept coming without warning. He would be standing at the kitchen sink, washing dishes, and suddenly
he would hear Khalid’s voice in his head—not words, just the sound of his breathing, heavy and fast.
Or he would be driving and the thrum of a helicopter overhead would send a jolt through him so sharp
that he would have to pull over.

Amir began to change the way he dressed. He had always been a man who took pride in his appearance,
but now he bought loose, dark jackets and hooded sweatshirts, even in summer. He wore them zipped
up, the hood often half-up, because he had come to believe that if people could not see him clearly,
they would not notice him, and if they did not notice him, they could not hurt him. His wife
noticed, of course. She asked him why he was dressing like he was hiding, and he told her he was
just cold. She did not press, but she saw the way he scanned every room they entered, his eyes
moving from face to face, cataloging threats. At community meetings—the very work that had once
given him purpose—Amir now sat with his back to the wall, watching the door. He had started to
believe that what happened to him was not an accident of circumstance but a revelation of something
fundamental about himself. He was the kind of person people could betray. He was the kind of person
who deserved to be hurt. His old belief that the world was basically fair, that people were
basically good, had been shattered, and he could not find the pieces.

The consequences have been severe. Six months ago, Amir stepped down from leading a major coalition
campaign because he could no longer tolerate the unpredictability of public spaces. His colleagues
were confused; they thought he had burned out. He did not correct them. At home, he has become
irritable and withdrawn. His wife has told him, gently, that she feels like she is living with a
stranger. What finally brought Amir to therapy was a moment last week when his youngest daughter,
visiting for dinner, reached out to touch his arm and he flinched so violently that he knocked over
his glass. He saw the hurt in her face, and he knew he could not keep pretending that everything was
fine. He is terrified that the person he used to be is gone forever, but he is even more afraid of
what he might become if he does not find a way to put the pieces back together.
