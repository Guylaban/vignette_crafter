Omar is a 40-year-old software engineer who lives with his wife and two children in a suburban
development outside of Houston. He came to therapy at his wife’s insistence, six months after
Hurricane Harvey flooded his neighborhood and nearly killed his family. Omar had been working from
home that afternoon when the bayou behind his house began to rise. By the time he realized the water
was not stopping, it was already seeping under the front door. He grabbed his children, ages five
and seven, and his wife, and they climbed into the attic as the water rose to chest height in the
living room. In the attic, the sound of the water rushing against the walls was deafening, a low
roar punctuated by the crash of furniture slamming into walls below them. His youngest daughter was
screaming, and Omar remembers only fragments: the darkness, the smell of wet insulation, his wife’s
voice saying “we’re going to be okay” over and over, and then the sound of the Coast Guard
helicopter blades, which seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere at once. He cannot put the
sequence in order. The memories come back as a single, overwhelming moment: the roar, the smell, the
terror, all at once, as if he is back in that attic. Sometimes, when he is trying to fall asleep, he
hears the water again, clear as day.

In the months since, Omar has come to believe that he should have prevented the situation entirely.
He tells himself that somebody else—somebody more prepared, more competent—would not have gotten his
family into that kind of danger. He had lived in that house for twelve years; he knew the bayou
flooded. Why had he not left earlier? Why had he not moved them to a hotel when the first warnings
came? He used to be a happy person, joking with his coworkers, coaching his son’s soccer team. Now
he feels hollow, convinced that nothing good can happen to him anymore. He has stopped answering
calls from friends. He stopped coaching. When his wife suggests they take the kids to the lake for
the weekend, he feels a wave of dread and says no, without explanation.

The world has become a landscape of potential threats. When someone at the grocery store steps into
his peripheral vision unexpectedly, Omar’s entire body lurches, his heart hammering, before he can
even register what happened. If his wife touches his shoulder from behind while he is working at his
computer, he flinches away, sometimes shouting before he can stop himself. He has started scanning
every room he enters for exits. At his office, he sits with his back to the wall so he can see the
door and the stairwell. He cannot explain why this feels necessary—he is not in a war zone, he is in
a cubicle—but the feeling is absolute: if he does not know the way out, he is trapped.

Omar has also developed a deep aversion to medical settings. When his daughter needed a routine
checkup, he told his wife he had a deadline and could not come. The truth was that the sight of the
ambulance bay at the clinic, the smell of antiseptic, the sound of a gurney being wheeled down a
hall—all of it brought him back to the attic, to the moment he thought they would not make it. He
forces himself to think only positive thoughts when these feelings arise, repeating “we are safe, we
are fine” under his breath, but the relief never lasts. He also finds himself, late at night,
replaying the storm in his mind, but not the helpless parts. Instead, he imagines himself doing
something violent to the storm itself, or to the water, or to the man on the news who said the
levees would hold. He ruminates about getting even, though he knows there is no one to get even
with.

What finally brought Omar to seek help was a moment at his son’s school. A fire drill sounded, and
Omar, who had come to pick up his son early, dropped to his knees in the hallway, shaking, unable to
breathe. A teacher helped him to a chair, and he heard himself say, “I thought it was the water.”
That night, his wife told him she was afraid for him, and for their children, and he saw the look on
her face—the same look she had in the attic. He agreed to come to therapy because he does not want
to lose his family a second time, even though he is not sure what good talking can do. What
frightens him most is the thought that this is just who he is now, that the happy person he used to
be is gone for good.
