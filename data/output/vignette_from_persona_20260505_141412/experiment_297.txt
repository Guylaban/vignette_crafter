For the twelve years she had worked as a refugee aid worker, Maly had prided herself on her ability
to stay calm under pressure. She had navigated checkpoints, negotiated with armed guards, and sat
with families who had lost everything. She had never expected that the violence would find her not
in a conflict zone, but on a quiet Tuesday afternoon in her own city, at a routine community
outreach event she had organized for newly arrived families. When a police unit arrived in response
to a noise complaint from a neighboring business, everything happened too fast. Maly had stepped
forward to explain the situation, identification in hand, but within seconds she was on the ground,
her wrists pinned behind her back, the weight of a knee pressing into her spine. She heard someone
screaming—it took her a moment to realize it was her own voice. The charge was later dropped, but
the moment itself never left her.

That was eighteen months ago. Now, Maly cannot walk past a police car without her chest tightening
and her breath turning shallow. She has stopped driving altogether on days when she knows there will
be a checkpoint near her office. But the triggers have spread far beyond authority presence. She
lives near a bike path, and the sudden whoosh of a cyclist passing behind her—the silent approach,
the unexpected proximity—sends her heart slamming against her ribs. She has come to believe that her
nervous system is broken, that she is permanently on the wrong frequency for the world. Crowded
spaces have become unbearable. Last month, she had to leave a grocery store halfway through her
shopping, abandoning her cart in the aisle, because the press of bodies and the low roar of voices
felt exactly like the moment before the officers had grabbed her arms.

Maly tries not to think about what happened. She has become expert at steering her mind away from
the memory, but the effort exhausts her. When the memory does break through—usually at night, or in
a quiet moment when her guard is down—it arrives with the same raw terror she felt on the ground,
and something else she did not expect: a grief so vast it feels like a physical weight in her chest.
She has to stop whatever she is doing, sometimes mid-sentence, and wait for the wave to pass. What
she feels is not just fear. It is the loss of something she cannot name, a sense that the world she
believed in no longer exists.

She has also begun to do things that worry her husband. Small things at first—driving too fast on
empty roads, picking arguments with strangers online, placing bets on sports games she does not care
about just for the jolt of adrenaline. The gambling started six months ago, a way to occupy her mind
with something other than the memory. It gives her a few minutes of something that feels almost like
excitement, but it leaves her hollow afterward, and she has lost more money than she wants to admit.
At home, she is irritable. She snaps at her husband for small things—leaving a light on, chewing too
loudly—and then lies awake for hours, staring at the ceiling, unable to quiet her mind.

What frightens Maly most is that she does not recognize herself. She used to be the person who
organized community dinners, who volunteered for extra shifts, who believed that her work mattered.
Now she turns down invitations to gatherings she would have once looked forward to. She has stopped
accepting offers to celebrate colleagues' birthdays or attend holiday parties. She has come to
believe that feeling happy or hopeful is dangerous, that letting her guard down will only invite
another blow. She finds herself thinking, over and over, that somebody else would not have gotten
into this situation. Somebody smarter, more careful, more worthy. The injustice of what happened—the
fact that no one was held accountable, that the report she filed went nowhere—sits inside her like a
stone she cannot pass.

She came to therapy because her husband told her, gently, that he was afraid for her. She came
because she lost her temper at work and screamed at a volunteer who had only been trying to help.
She came because she is exhausted, and because some small part of her still hopes that the person
she used to be is not gone forever. But what she is most afraid of, as she sits in the waiting room,
is that talking about it will make the memory stronger, that she will have to feel that terror and
that grief all over again, and that this time she will not be able to get back up.
