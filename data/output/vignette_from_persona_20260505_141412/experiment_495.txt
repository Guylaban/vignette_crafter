Anita was forty-three years old, a plumber, and she had spent the last fifteen years working for a
small commercial company in the suburbs of Chicago. She was South Asian, married, and had built a
reputation as someone who could handle any job, no matter how messy. The traumatic event had
happened eighteen months ago, on a Tuesday afternoon in the basement of a newly renovated office
building. A subcontractor had used the wrong solvent to clean a pipe joint, and when Anita began
cutting into the line with her torch, the chemical residue vaporized. She did not see the flash so
much as feel the air turn solid in her lungs. The smoke that followed was not the gray haze of a
controlled burn; it was a dense, sweet, acrid cloud that clung to the inside of her nostrils and
throat. She dropped her tools, stumbled up the stairs, and spent the next forty minutes in the
parking lot, coughing until her ribs ached, convinced that her lungs were sealing shut from the
inside. A coworker called an ambulance, but Anita refused to get in. She told herself she was fine,
that the paramedics were overreacting, that if she just sat still and breathed shallowly the panic
would pass.

What stayed with her, long after the coughing stopped, was the smell. It was not a single scent but
a compound thing—burnt plastic, scorched metal, something organic and wrong. In the weeks that
followed, Anita found herself catching that same odor everywhere. A neighbor’s barbecue, a passing
car’s exhaust, the faint electrical burn from a hairdryer. Each time, her chest would tighten and
her palms would go slick before her mind had even registered what she was smelling. She began to
avoid anything that might produce those cues. She stopped driving past the office building. She
asked her husband to take over the grilling. She threw out a pair of boots that had absorbed the
smell during the incident, even though they were barely worn. The avoidance spread quietly. She
started leaving the house through the back door so she would not have to pass the kitchen if toast
was burning. She took a different route to work that added fifteen minutes to her commute, just to
stay away from a stretch of road where a restaurant’s grease trap sometimes smoked.

site before she could focus on the task. She checked the labels on every canister, sniffed the air
before she lit her torch, and positioned herself so that she always had a clear path to an exit. Her
coworkers noticed her hesitation but did not understand it. She told them she was being careful,
that the incident had made her more safety-conscious. Privately, she was terrified that she would
miss something, that one wrong breath would send her back to that parking lot, gasping and alone.

still in the dark, listening for the hiss of a gas line or the crackle of a flame. She would get up

settle again. Her husband asked her what was wrong, and she said she could not sleep, that her mind
was busy. She did not tell him that she was afraid to close her eyes.

The physical reactivity was the hardest to ignore. A whiff of smoke from a distant wildfire, even on

avoided the hardware store aisle where propane tanks were stored. She began to refuse jobs that
involved soldering near plastic pipes, even when the work was straightforward and well-paying. Her
income started to dip, and she made excuses to her boss about wanting to take on smaller residential
jobs. She told herself she was just being practical, that she did not need the stress.

What finally brought Anita to seek help was a night in late autumn. A neighbor’s leaf pile caught
fire, and the wind carried the smoke through her open bedroom window. Anita woke already running,
her body halfway to the front door before her mind understood there was no danger. She stood on the
lawn in her nightclothes, shaking, while her husband called after her from the porch. The next
morning, she made an appointment. She was not sure what she hoped for—only that she could not keep
waking up convinced she was dying, and that she could not keep shrinking her life to avoid the smell
of something that had already happened.
