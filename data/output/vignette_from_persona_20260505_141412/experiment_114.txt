only he could feel. At nineteen, he was the youngest detective in his precinct, a fact that had once
filled him with pride. Now it felt like a target. Six months earlier, he had been taken. The details
came out in fragments over several meetings, each word pulled from him like a splinter. He had
responded to a routine call about a domestic disturbance in a basement apartment on the south side.
When he entered, the door swung shut behind him and locked. Three men were waiting. They kept him
for eleven hours, bound to a chair with zip ties, while they demanded information about an ongoing
narcotics investigation. He gave them nothing, but they did not believe him. They took turns. The
things he heard during those hours—the wet sound of a belt being pulled through its loops, the low
hum of a space heater that clicked on and off, the way one of them whistled tunelessly between
questions—those sounds had lodged themselves inside his skull. He could not find the words to
describe what happened, even now. When he tried, his throat closed. He knew the sequence of events,
could recite the facts in a deposition, but the shape of the experience itself remained formless,
unspeakable. What came instead were the sounds. At night, lying next to his girlfriend, he would
hear the belt again, or the click of the heater, and his body would go rigid. Sometimes the terror
arrived without any image at all—just a wave of heat and dread that rose from nowhere, leaving him
shaking and nauseous on the bathroom floor.

The world outside had become a threat. Moses stopped going out after dark. He told his girlfriend it
was because he was tired from work, but the truth was simpler and more absolute: night was when they
had taken him, and night felt like a room with a closing door. He worked double shifts to avoid the
sunset, then came home and sat in the dark apartment with the television off, listening. He had come
to believe that if he let his guard down—if he let himself feel safe—something would find him.
Physical contact had become unbearable. When his girlfriend reached for his hand, he flinched. When
a colleague clapped him on the shoulder, he felt a spike of adrenaline so sharp it left him dizzy.
He had always been a tactile person; now he kept his arms crossed, his body angled away from
everyone. She had started sleeping on the couch, not because he asked her to, but because she could
not stand the way he stiffened when she rolled toward him in bed. He saw the hurt in her face and
told himself he would fix it tomorrow. Tomorrow never came.

The suppression was a full-time job. He tried not to think about the basement. He tried not to think
about the zip ties or the whistling or the way the space heater had smelled of burning dust. He
pushed the memories down so hard that they migrated into his body instead. His jaw ached from
clenching. His hands trembled during briefings. He became known at the precinct as the detective who
never stayed for the debrief, who left the moment the lights came on in the parking lot. His captain
had pulled him aside twice and asked if he was alright. Moses said he was fine. He had become an
expert at saying he was fine.

What finally brought him in was the night he came home to find his girlfriend packing a bag. She was
crying. She said she loved him but she could not live in a house where she was treated like a
threat. He watched her leave and felt nothing at first—just the familiar numbness—and then, hours
later, the wave hit him without any image at all. He was on the floor, gasping, and he had no idea
why. The next morning he called the department's employee assistance line. He told the intake worker
that he needed to talk to someone. He did not say what about. He still could not find the words. But
he was afraid that if he kept going like this—alone in his apartment, jumping at every sound,
pushing away the person who loved him—he would lose everything he had left. He was not sure he
deserved to keep it. But he was here. That was the first thing.
