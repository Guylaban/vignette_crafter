At forty-nine, Marco had spent two decades building a name for himself in New York’s fashion
industry, known for his meticulous attention to detail and his ability to envision a collection
months, sometimes years, in advance. That capacity for forward thinking had collapsed entirely in
the fourteen months since his wife, Elena, had been struck by a delivery truck while crossing
Seventh Avenue. He had not been there. He had been in a fitting room, approving a hemline, when the
call came. By the time he reached the hospital, she was already in surgery. The neurosurgeon’s words
— “she may not wake up” — had landed somewhere in his chest and never left. She had woken,
eventually, but the recovery had been slow, and Marco had stopped making plans of any kind. When a
buyer asked about his fall line, he said he was “taking a season off.” When his studio manager
mentioned a trunk show in March, Marco felt his throat close. He could not bring himself to agree to
anything that sat beyond the immediate hour. The future had become a place where terrible things
happened without warning, and he refused to walk into it willingly.

The memory of that day arrived in fragments, and the fragments were unbearable. He could see the
pattern of the hospital floor tiles with excruciating clarity — little speckled squares of beige and
brown — but the face of the nurse who had led him to the waiting room was a complete blank. He
remembered the smell of antiseptic and the sound of a vending machine humming, but he could not
recall whether he had sat down or remained standing. These gaps terrified him. He would be standing
in his kitchen, reaching for a coffee cup, and suddenly he was back in that hallway, his knees weak,
his stomach dropping, and the emotion would hit him so hard that he had to grip the counter and
breathe slowly until it passed. What came with the memory, every time, was guilt. He had been
approving a hemline. He had been thinking about fabric swatches while a truck was bearing down on
his wife. The guilt was not a thought he could argue with; it was a physical sensation, a weight in
his ribs, and it made him want to disappear.

He tried not to think about any of it. He kept his mind occupied constantly. When he was not at the
hospital sitting beside Elena’s bed, he was in his home studio, sorting buttons by size,
reorganizing his thread cabinet, sketching designs he knew he would never produce. He listened to
podcasts while he worked — true crime, history, anything with a narrative that demanded his
attention. Silence was dangerous. In silence, the images came. At night, sleep was nearly
impossible. He lay beside Elena, who was home now but still fragile, and listened to her breathing.
Every time she shifted, his heart hammered. He was certain, in those dark hours, that something was
about to go wrong again. He had not told her this. She thought he was just restless.

The triggers were everywhere, and they were physical. A delivery truck rumbling past his window made
his hands shake. The squeal of brakes outside a coffee shop sent a bolt of heat through his chest,
and he would have to set down his cup before he dropped it. He had stopped walking anywhere near
Seventh Avenue. He took the long way to the subway, adding twenty minutes to his commute, and he had
begun to avoid the neighborhood where the accident happened entirely. His assistant had noticed. She
had asked, gently, if he wanted her to handle the meetings with the fabric suppliers who were based
in that part of town. He had said yes, and he had felt a wave of relief so deep it shamed him.

What finally brought him to seek help was a moment of quiet. Elena had asked him, over dinner, if he
wanted to visit her sister in California next summer. She had said it casually, as if the future
were still a place one could discuss. Marco had opened his mouth to answer and found that he could
not. The word “yes” would not come. The word “no” felt like a betrayal. He had sat there, silent,
until Elena put down her fork and looked at him with an expression he recognized as worry. He was
afraid, he realized, of everything. He was afraid of planning, afraid of sleeping, afraid of the
gaps in his own memory, and most of all, he was afraid of the guilt that sat inside him like a
second skeleton. He did not know if therapy could help, but he knew he could not go on like this — a
man who had once designed entire seasons, now unable to say yes to a summer trip.
