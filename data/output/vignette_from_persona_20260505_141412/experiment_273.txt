The stalking began with small things that Amir could almost explain away. A bicycle that appeared
too often on his route home from the firehouse. The same sedan parked a few houses down from his
own, engine idling, then gone by the time he looked again. He was a firefighter—he was trained to
assess threats, to stay calm under pressure. For weeks he told himself he was being paranoid. But
then the notes started appearing under his windshield wiper, and the calls came at odd hours, and
the bicycle began to show up outside the station itself, always just far enough away that he could
never get a good look at the rider. The night it finally broke—the night he came home to find the
back door unlocked and a single photograph of his wife on the kitchen counter, a photograph he had
never seen before—he called the police, filed a report, and spent the next three months sleeping
with a crowbar under his pillow. The stalker was never caught. The case went cold. And Amir, who had
spent his entire adult life running into burning buildings, found himself unable to walk to his own
mailbox without his heart slamming against his ribs.

That was fourteen months ago. Now, sitting in a therapist’s office for the first time, Amir cannot
bring himself to say the word “stalking” out loud. He knows what happened—he can see the photograph,
the curve of the kitchen counter, the exact shade of dusk coming through the window—but when he
tries to describe it, the words jam somewhere in his throat. He gestures, shakes his head, and
finally says, “I can’t. I just can’t.” It is not that he has forgotten. It is that remembering feels
like being dropped into the middle of that night all over again, and the emotion that floods
him—terror, rage, a kind of sick helplessness—is so overwhelming that he has to stop talking, close
his eyes, and breathe until his hands stop shaking. The memories do not come when he expects them. A
car door slamming in the parking lot of the grocery store can drop him straight back into that
kitchen. The squeak of bicycle brakes on the street outside the firehouse can make him freeze mid-
sentence, his coffee cup halfway to his lips, his entire body locked in a moment that is over but
somehow never ends.

Amir has changed, and he knows it. He used to be the guy who cracked jokes in the engine bay, the
one who could walk into any burning apartment and immediately map the layout, the exits, the points
of collapse. Now he does the same thing in every room he enters, but not for the reasons he used to.
He scans for exits because he is certain that someone is watching him, that someone might come
through a door he has not accounted for. He watches strangers in restaurants, in the firehouse
lobby, at his son’s school pickup, cataloguing their hands, their eyes, the angle of their
bodies—looking for the tell that means they intend to harm him. Loud, unexpected sounds make him
flinch so hard that his colleagues have started to notice. He has told them he is just tired. He is
not tired. He is terrified that he is losing his mind. The way his body reacts before his brain can
catch up, the way he cannot stop checking the back seat of his car even when he knows no one is
there—these things feel to Amir like evidence that something inside him has broken permanently. He
used to believe he was strong. He ran into buildings when everyone else ran out. Now he believes
that the stalking did not just scare him; it revealed that he was never strong in the first place. A
strong person would have moved on by now. A strong person would not be jumping at bicycle bells and
scanning parking lots like a hunted animal.

The avoidance has reshaped his life in ways he did not anticipate. He stopped riding his bicycle—the
one he used to take to work on sunny days—because the sight of another bike on the road made his
stomach drop. He takes longer, more circuitous routes to the firehouse now, avoiding the blocks
where he used to see the sedan. He has stopped going to the grocery store after dark. He has stopped
walking his son to the bus stop in the morning. His wife has started doing all of these things
alone, and she does not complain, but Amir sees the worry in her face, and he knows that she is
carrying something that should be shared. He cannot bring himself to tell her the full truth about
what he feels—that he is afraid all the time, that he thinks he might be going crazy, that he
watches her sleep some nights because he is terrified that the stalker has found them again and he
will not hear the door open. He has come to therapy because his captain pulled him aside after he
froze during a call—a routine kitchen fire, nothing dangerous—and told him that he needed to get
help or he would be put on leave. That scared Amir more than the stalking ever did. Losing his job
would mean losing the one place where he still felt like himself, even if that self is fading. He is
not sure he can get better. He is not sure he deserves to. But he showed up anyway, and that, at
least, feels like the first thing he has done right in over a year.
