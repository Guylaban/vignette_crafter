For as long as she could remember, Amara had carried the sense that she was somehow to blame for
what happened to her. She was seven years old, living with her aunt in a small house in Accra, when
her uncle—a man her mother had trusted to help raise her—began a pattern of physical abuse that
would define her childhood. The beatings came without warning, often after she had done something as
minor as speaking too loudly or failing to complete a chore quickly enough. He would call her name
in that flat, quiet voice—“Amara”—and she would freeze, knowing what was coming. The sound of his
voice, even now, thirty years later, could stop her breath. She had come to believe, over the
decades, that the abuse happened because of the sort of person she was: clumsy, careless,
fundamentally wrong. Nothing in her life felt meaningful anymore, and she was certain that there was
something deeply defective about her as a person—a flaw that no amount of hard work or success could
erase.

Amara had left Ghana at twenty-two and built a life in the United States. She married a kind,
patient man named Kwame, and together they raised two children in a modest home in Maryland. She
became a plumber, a job she was good at and proud of, though it placed her in constant proximity to
things that undid her. The work required her to enter basements, crawl spaces, and unfamiliar homes
where she might encounter a man with a certain tone of voice, or hear a child being scolded in
another room. The worst triggers were auditory: the sound of a man clearing his throat in a
particular way, the echo of a name that resembled her uncle’s, the exact pitch of a voice that cut
through the noise of a job site and sent her heart racing. She had learned to wear noise-canceling
headphones at work, but that only helped so much. When a customer called out “Amara” from across the
house, she would flinch, and then hate herself for flinching.

Over the years, Amara developed a set of habits that she knew were not normal but felt necessary.
She avoided authority figures of any kind—supervisors, inspectors, even uniformed delivery drivers.
She would ask Kwame to handle any interaction with their landlord or the school principal. She also
avoided anything that reminded her of her uncle: the smell of cheap cologne, the sight of a certain
type of watch, the sound of a man clearing his throat. These avoidances had narrowed her world
considerably. She no longer accepted jobs that required her to work in homes where a man might be
present during the repair. She told her dispatcher she had developed a dust allergy. Her income
dropped, and she began to resent herself for the lost wages, but the thought of facing a stranger
who might speak to her in that tone was unbearable.

What frightened Amara most was not the memory itself, but the way it seemed to leak into every part
of her life. She spent hours each week ruminating on what her uncle had done to her, turning the
events over in her mind like stones she could not put down. She was not angry at him in a way that
felt active; it was more like a low, constant hum of bitterness that she could not silence. She
dwelled on what he had taken from her: a sense of safety, a belief in her own worth, the ability to
hear her own name without her stomach dropping. She had stopped attending family gatherings where
she might hear a voice that reminded her of his. She had stopped visiting her mother, who still
lived in Ghana, because the thought of being in that country, in that house, was too much.

It was Kwame who finally convinced her to seek help. He had found her one evening in the kitchen,
standing frozen, a glass of water in her hand, because a neighbor’s television had played a scene
with a man whose voice sounded familiar. Amara had not been able to speak for several minutes. That
night, she agreed to call a therapist. What she hoped for, more than anything, was to hear her own
name without fear—to be called “Amara” and feel only the simple fact of being seen, not the weight
of a past she could not escape.
