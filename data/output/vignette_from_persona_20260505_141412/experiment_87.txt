had done everything by the book. She had followed protocol. She had trusted her partner to make the
right call. But in the months that followed, that single moment began to feel less like a memory and
more like a trap she kept falling back into.

It started with the sounds. A car backfiring on the street would send her heart slamming against her
ribs, and for a split second she would be back on that porch, the air thick and wrong. Then the
smells came—cooking meat, the iron tang of a rusted pipe, anything that remotely resembled that
copper-and-sweat scent—and her stomach would lurch before her mind could catch up. She began to
notice that her body was reacting before she even knew what was happening. Her hands would tremble,
her jaw would clench, and she would break out in a cold sweat, and all the while her brain was

body did not believe her. Maya came to feel that her own flesh had turned traitor, that it was stuck
in a loop of alarm that no amount of reasoning could switch off.

consciousness while she was driving or showering or trying to fall asleep—it did not feel like

shoved into her head. She would watch herself kneeling there, her hands red, her partner’s voice
shouting in the background, and she would think, *That isn’t me. That can’t be me.* But the images
were so vivid, so complete across every sense, that she could not shake the feeling that she was
living it all over again, even as she knew she wasn’t. The disconnection was terrifying—she was both
trapped inside the moment and completely alienated from it, as if the woman in the memory was a
stranger she could not stop watching.

she was certain that danger was hiding everywhere—behind every door, in every quiet street, in the
face of every civilian she stopped for a traffic violation. She began scanning constantly,

frightened her most, though, was the sense that she had been betrayed by someone she trusted. Her

nothing. But she could not stop thinking about the way her partner had looked at her afterward—not

Avoidance crept into her life gradually. She stopped volunteering for overtime. She started taking
the long way home to avoid driving past that residential block. She told her boyfriend she was too

explain that the memory did not feel like hers, or that her hands would not stop shaking when she
heard a siren. She stopped calling her friends. She stopped answering texts. The only place she
still felt any sense of control was at work, but even there, the hypervigilance was exhausting her.

wheel. Maya saw the fear in his eyes—the same fear she had seen in the man on the porch—and she
froze. She could not move. She stood there, her hand on her holster, her breath coming in short
