Before the summer he turned twenty-three, Miguel had never thought of himself as someone who could
be broken. He had grown up in a small town in Jalisco, worked cleaning houses since he was sixteen,
and moved to the United States at twenty-one with a work visa and a wife who believed they would
build something steady. By the time he was twenty-five, that wife was gone, and Miguel was living
alone in a studio apartment in Houston, waking up three or four times every night to check that the
door was locked. He would lie still in the dark, listening to the hum of the window unit, and only
fall back asleep when he had confirmed, with his own hand on the deadbolt, that no one had come in.

The thing that had happened to him had happened in a parking lot behind a strip mall, on a Tuesday
afternoon in late August. He had been walking back to his car after finishing a shift at a motel
when a sedan pulled up beside him—a dark blue Honda, common enough that he had seen three just like
it that same week. Two men got out. They were wearing uniforms, or something close to uniforms: dark
polo shirts with a logo on the chest, the kind of thing that might belong to a security company or a
towing service. They asked for his papers. He showed them his work permit. They said it was fake.
They said they were going to call immigration. And then they did not call anyone. Instead, they
drove him to a warehouse on the edge of town, and for the next six hours they asked him questions he
could not answer, and when he could not answer, they used a Taser on his ribs and a plastic bottle
filled with water and a rag over his face. He had wet himself. He had begged. He had told them
everything he knew about everyone he knew, and still they kept going. Eventually they let him go,
dumped him on a curb with his wallet empty and his phone gone, and said if he told anyone, they
would find him again.

That was three years ago. Miguel had not told anyone. He had not filed a police report. He had not
told his wife, who had left him six months later because he had become someone she did not
recognize—someone who flinched when she touched his shoulder from behind, someone who smoked
cigarette after cigarette on the balcony at night, staring at the street. He had not told his boss
at the cleaning service, though he had started requesting only morning shifts so he would never have
to walk to his car after dark. He had not told the therapist at the free clinic, because he had only
gone once and then stopped.

What brought him to the clinic now, at twenty-six, was a Tuesday much like that other Tuesday—hot,
humid, the kind of sky that pressed down on you. He had been standing at a bus stop after an
appointment, and a dark blue sedan had slowed as it passed him. The driver had been an older woman
with gray hair, probably lost, probably looking for an address. But Miguel’s chest had seized. His
vision had gone spotty. He had dropped his cigarette and walked—not run, because running would have
drawn attention—into the nearest store, a dollar store, and stood in the cleaning aisle until his
hands stopped shaking. That night he had smoked half a pack and checked the lock on his door seven
times before he could lie down.

He was certain, in a way that felt like simple fact, that the men who had taken him were still out
there, still looking for him. He did not know why they would be—he had nothing they wanted, he had
told them nothing useful—but the certainty lived in his body, not his mind. When a stranger
approached him on the sidewalk, even an old woman asking for directions, his pulse would spike
before he could think. When someone touched him unexpectedly—a hand on his arm, a brush in a
crowd—he would jerk away with a sound that embarrassed him. He had stopped going to the grocery
store. He had stopped answering calls from numbers he did not recognize. He had stopped feeling
anything close to safe, and he had also stopped feeling much of anything else. Positive emotions had
become confusing, even frightening. A moment of laughter with a coworker would leave him
disoriented, as though he had forgotten how to be in his own skin, and he would retreat to the break
room and light a cigarette.

had been standing there, holding a bottle of bleach, and he had thought: *I cannot live like this
anymore.* He did not know if he could live any other way. What he was most afraid of, as he sat in
the waiting room with his hands on his knees, was that the therapist would tell him the same thing
the men in the warehouse had told him—that he was lying, that he was making it up, that he deserved
what happened. What he was hoping for, though he could barely admit it even to himself, was that
someone might finally believe him.
