Mei Lin was sixty-one years old, a sculptor who had spent decades shaping clay into forms that felt
both ancient and alive. She had come to therapy at her husband’s quiet insistence, though she would
not have used that word — insistence. He had simply said, one evening after she had flinched at the
sound of his footsteps on the porch, “Mei, I think you need someone to talk to.” She had agreed
because it was easier than explaining, and because she was tired.

The event that had reshaped her life happened three years earlier, on a warm autumn afternoon in the
parking lot of a suburban shopping plaza. She had been walking back to her car, a small bag of
groceries in one hand, when she heard a man’s voice shouting. She turned. A young woman was backing
away from a man in a dark jacket, her hands raised. Mei Lin saw the man’s arm move, saw the glint of
metal, and then heard the sound — a sharp crack that seemed to split the air itself. The woman fell.
Mei Lin did not see where the man went. She did not remember running. She only remembered the smell
of cologne, heavy and sweet, hanging in the warm air, and the sound of her own sandals slapping the
asphalt as she fled. She had not been able to give a clear statement to the police. The words would
not come in order. She kept starting and stopping, and eventually a kind officer told her it was all
right, she could go home.

In the months that followed, Mei Lin found herself unable to work in her studio. The studio was in a
converted garage behind her house, and to reach it she had to walk across a short stretch of lawn.
At night, that walk became impossible. She would stand at the back door, her hand on the knob, and
feel a tightness in her chest that told her she could not go out there. During the day, she could
sometimes manage it, but only if she turned on every light in the house first, and only if she knew
her husband was home. She had come to believe that the world was a place where danger could appear
without warning, where a normal afternoon could turn into something that left a woman on the ground.
This belief was not a thought she examined; it was a feeling that lived in her body, a constant low
hum of readiness.

Her husband, a retired accountant, had begun to notice changes. She no longer wanted to go to the
grocery store with him. She said it was the crowds, but what she really feared were the
unpredictable moments — someone stepping too close, a sudden voice behind her. She had stopped going
to the art supply shop altogether. The sales clerk there wore a strong cologne, the same sweet,
heavy scent she had smelled that day. Just catching a whiff of it from a passerby on the street was
enough to make her heart pound and her palms sweat. She would cross the road to avoid anyone who
seemed to be wearing perfume. She knew, logically, that this was not reasonable. But knowing did not
stop the reaction.

Her memories of the event were strange to her. They did not feel like memories at all. When she
thought about that afternoon, she did not recall a sequence of events. She recalled fragments: the
glint of metal, the crack of sound, the smell. But these fragments felt more real than ordinary
memories. They would arrive unbidden, and for a moment she would be back there, her body responding
before her mind could catch up. When her husband asked her to tell him what happened, she found she
could not. She would start, and then the words would tangle, and she would stop, unable to finish.
It was as if the story did not exist in her mind as a story at all, only as a series of sharp,
disconnected pieces.

She had also begun to feel that something was wrong with her, fundamentally. Other people, she
thought, would have gotten over this by now. Other people would not still be jumping at footsteps.
She had always been a steady person, a maker of solid things. Now she felt fragile, defective. She
did not trust herself to be in public, and she did not trust the people in public either. She had
come to believe that most people were not what they seemed, that anyone could be hiding something
dangerous. This suspicion had crept into her marriage, too. She found herself watching her husband
more closely, wondering if she really knew him.

What had finally brought her to seek help was a moment in her studio, three days ago. She had forced
herself to go out there in the afternoon. She had stood before a half-finished piece, a clay figure
of a woman with her hands raised, and she had been unable to touch it. The figure looked too much
like the woman in the parking lot. Mei Lin had backed away, her heart hammering, and had not
returned since. That night, she told her husband she would see someone. She did not know if talking
could help. She was afraid it would only make the fragments more real. But she was also afraid of
staying as she was, afraid of the life that was shrinking around her, day by day.
