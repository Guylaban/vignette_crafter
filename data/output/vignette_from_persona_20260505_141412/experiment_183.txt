Deborah is a 48-year-old social ministry worker who lives alone in a midwestern city. She came to
therapy six months after a life-threatening accident that she still cannot fully describe without
her voice dropping to a whisper. She had been driving home from a community outreach event late on a
rainy November evening when a pickup truck ran a red light and T-boned her sedan at an intersection.
The impact spun her car into a concrete median. She remembers the sound of metal crumpling, the
smell of gasoline, and the terrifying realization that she could not move her legs for what felt
like an eternity. Emergency crews had to cut her out of the vehicle. She was hospitalized for three
days with a fractured pelvis and internal bruising, but physically she made a full recovery.

What stayed with Deborah, though, was not the pain of the injury but the feeling of absolute
helplessness in those minutes after the crash. She had been pinned, unable to do anything, waiting
for help that might not come. In the months since, she has come to believe that what happened to her
was deeply unfair—and that nothing has been done about it. The other driver was never charged, and
Deborah cannot shake the conviction that the world simply lets bad things happen without
consequence. She finds herself thinking, “I can’t stop bad things from happening to me,” and this
thought settles into her chest like a cold weight whenever she tries to go about her day.

The most disorienting change, however, is that Deborah no longer trusts her own body. Before the
accident, she had been an active woman who walked to work and volunteered on weekends. Now, any
sensation of physical strain—her heart beating faster after climbing stairs, her muscles aching
after carrying groceries—sends a jolt of alarm through her. She has come to believe that her body is
unreliable, that it might fail her at any moment. She avoids situations that put her body under
stress, which means she no longer exercises, she takes the elevator even for one flight, and she has
stopped helping with the heavy lifting at the church food pantry where she works.

Her work as a social ministry worker has become a minefield of triggers. She coordinates outreach
programs, which used to involve driving to different neighborhoods to meet with families. Now she
cannot drive at all. She cannot even be a passenger in a vehicle without her hands going clammy and
her breath shortening. The sight of a car that has been in an accident—even a photograph in a news
article—forces her to look away, her stomach clenching. She has arranged for colleagues to cover her
driving duties, which has strained her relationships at work. They are understanding, but she can
feel their patience thinning.

At home, Deborah has developed a pattern that puzzles even her. She avoids relaxing. Sitting on the
couch with a cup of tea, letting her mind wander—these things feel dangerous, as though lowering her
guard invites disaster. She keeps herself busy with small tasks until she is exhausted enough to
sleep. When she does enter a room, she scans for exits immediately. At the grocery store, at church,
at the doctor’s office, she notes every door, every hallway. Even when a friend or coworker tells
her she is safe, she finds it impossible to believe them. Ordinary sounds—a car horn outside, a door
slamming, a dropped pan in the kitchen—make her jump as though she has been touched with
electricity.

What finally brought Deborah to therapy was a panic episode at the food pantry last week. A
volunteer dropped a metal tray, and Deborah screamed and backed into a corner, unable to breathe.
She was mortified. She realized she cannot keep living this way, but she is also terrified that
therapy will force her to relive the crash, or that she will never feel truly safe again. She hopes,
quietly, that someone can help her trust her own body once more.
