When the bomb went off at the outdoor market in downtown Oakland, Srey was buying lemongrass. She
remembers the exact shade of green in the bundle she was holding, the way the rubber band cut into
her thumb, and then the sound—not a bang so much as a pressure that folded the air inward. She was
thrown sideways into a metal cart stacked with mangoes. Her left eardrum perforated immediately, and
for the first few seconds she existed in a muffled, ringing world where the dust looked like fog and
the screaming came from very far away. The smell that stayed with her, the one she cannot shake, was
not smoke or blood but the sharp, sweet reek of alcohol-based hand sanitizer that had exploded from
a vendor’s stall. That smell, even now, eight years later, can drop her back into the concrete and
the ringing before she has time to brace herself.

Srey is fifty-seven, a married interior designer who works from a home studio in a quiet suburb. She
has built a career on creating calm, orderly spaces, and she used to pride herself on her ability to
walk into a client’s chaotic renovation and see exactly what needed to happen. That confidence has
eroded slowly, then all at once. She finds herself unable to tolerate even small disruptions now—a
misplaced paint swatch, a contractor who is ten minutes late—and she has come to believe that she
simply cannot cope with the slightest upset. The world, she is certain, is a dangerous place, and
she has to be on guard all the time. She watches people closely, scanning their hands, their
expressions, the way they move through a room. When a client reaches out to touch her arm to make a
point, she flinches so hard she knocks over her coffee. She has started doing the initial
consultations by video call, and she avoids showing clients the finished spaces in person. The
consequence is that she has lost three major contracts in the last year. Her husband, who is patient
and bewildered, has taken over the grocery shopping because she cannot walk into a store without the
alcohol smell from the cleaning aisle triggering a visceral, full-body re-experiencing of the
market. She feels, sometimes, as though she is back there, as though the present is a thin film over
something much more real.

To manage the unbearable pressure that builds in her chest, Srey has developed strategies that she
knows are not healthy but that feel necessary. She hurts herself physically—digging her nails into
her forearms, pressing the heel of her hand into her thigh—because the sharp, clean pain cuts
through the fog and gives her a few minutes of quiet. She runs, sometimes for two hours at a time,
until her knees ache and her lungs burn, because exhaustion is the only thing that reliably dims the
hypervigilance. And she eats, more than she means to, late at night when the memories press in:
crackers, bread, anything that requires chewing and swallowing and occupies her hands and mouth so
she does not have to think. The weight gain has been gradual, but it is another thing she monitors,
another reason to feel that she is losing control.

What finally brought Srey to seek help was a moment that terrified her more than the bomb itself.
Last month, her daughter sent her a photo from a family vacation years before the attack—Srey at a
picnic, laughing, her hair loose. Srey looked at the photo and felt nothing. The woman in the
picture was a stranger. She could not connect to that version of herself, could not remember what it
felt like to be unguarded. That night, she sat on the floor of her studio and hurt herself more
seriously than she ever had, and in the morning she called a therapist. She is afraid of what she
might do if she keeps going this way. She is also afraid that talking about the event will make it
worse, that the memories will become even more real. But she is more afraid of the alternative: a
life in which she is a stranger to everyone she loves, watching the world from behind glass, waiting
for the next explosion.
