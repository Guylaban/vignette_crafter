Mai Nguyen was fifty-two years old, a librarian at a public branch in a small coastal town in
Oregon, and she had been married to her husband, Tom, for twenty-eight years. She came to therapy at
the urging of her supervisor, who had noticed that Mai had stopped attending the monthly staff
meetings held in the basement conference room and had begun taking her lunch breaks alone in the
locked reference office. Mai had not told anyone why, and she was not sure she could.

The event that had reshaped her life occurred fourteen months earlier, during the winter storm that
flooded the lower half of her town. Mai had been working late, alone in the library after closing,
when the river broke its banks. The power went out at 7:13 p.m. She remembered the exact time
because the wall clock had stopped. In the sudden silence—the humming fluorescent lights, the
heating system, the computer fans, all gone—she had stood frozen in the dark. Water began seeping
under the emergency exit door. She had to wade through rising, ice-cold water in complete blackness,
feeling her way along shelves, certain she would not make it. She found the back staircase and
climbed to the second floor, where she waited for six hours until a rescue boat arrived. During
those hours, alone in the dark, she had been certain she would die. She had thought about her
daughter, about Tom, about the books she would never shelve again.

In the months that followed, Mai found that silence had become unbearable. Not loud noises—she could
tolerate those—but sudden, complete quiet. When the refrigerator compressor stopped at night, her
heart would slam against her ribs. When a patron left the reading room and the door clicked shut
behind them, leaving her alone with no footsteps, no voices, no rustling pages, she would feel the
memory surge up without warning: the dark, the cold water, the certainty of death. The memory did
not come as a story she told herself. It came as a full-body return—the smell of wet carpet and
river silt, the sensation of her skirt soaking against her thighs, the absolute blackness. She could
not control when it arrived. It forced its way in, and when it did, she felt not fear but
overwhelming grief. She would remember the faces of her family, the things she had not said, the
life she had not finished living, and she would cry silently at her desk.

She began to avoid circumstances that might bring the memory back. She stopped going anywhere dark.
She left work before sunset in the winter months. She rearranged her home so that she could sleep
with a lamp on and a white noise machine running, because waking in the dark was the worst trigger
of all. She stopped exercising, because the feeling of breathlessness during a run—the sensation of
not getting enough air—reminded her of the panic in the stairwell. She stopped attending any
gathering where people who had been in the flood might be present, because their voices, their
casual mentions of that night, could send her into a dissociative state where she deliberately tried
to leave her body, to blank out, to become nothing until the moment passed.

At work, Mai had developed a set of habits she did not fully acknowledge as compulsive. She always
sat with her back to a wall, facing the door. She chose her seat in the staff room based on how safe
she felt—never near a window, never with an unmonitored entrance behind her. She scanned every room
she entered for exits, for places to hide, for signs of danger. She was hypervigilant in a way that
exhausted her. She could not relax. She could not read a book for pleasure anymore, because she kept
looking up to check the windows, the doorways, the faces of people who might or might not have been
there that night.

The consequence was that Mai had become isolated. She had stopped seeing her close friends from the
library, because they wanted to talk about the flood, about how lucky they all were, about the
recovery. She could not bear it. She had stopped going to the weekly book club she had hosted for
fifteen years. Tom had begun to worry, then to feel hurt, then to express frustration. He did not
understand why she could not just talk about it. Mai did not understand either. She only knew that
something inside her had broken, and that she was living in a state of constant watchfulness,
waiting for the next moment when silence or darkness would pull her back into that stairwell.

What finally brought her to seek help was a panic attack in the library stacks two weeks ago. A
child had pulled a book from a shelf, and the thud of its spine hitting the carpet had been followed
by a sudden hush—the kind of silence that falls when a group of people all stop talking at once. Mai
had frozen. The memory had come without warning. She had stood gripping a shelf, unable to move,
unable to breathe, until a coworker touched her arm and she flinched away. Her supervisor saw. Her
supervisor asked. And Mai, ashamed and frightened, finally said yes, she needed help.

She came to therapy hoping, she said, to stop being afraid all the time. But what she feared most
was not the fear itself. It was the possibility that she would never feel safe again—that the world
had become a place where silence was a threat, where darkness was a trap, where her own mind could
betray her at any moment and send her back to that night, drowning in grief.
