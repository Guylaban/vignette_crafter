Maya is a 35-year-old married woman who works as a counselor at a community mental health clinic.
She came to therapy at the urging of her husband, who had grown increasingly worried about the
changes he saw in her over the past eight months. Maya described the traumatic event in fragments,
her voice faltering, her eyes fixed on the floor. She had been driving home from work on a rainy
evening when a teenage boy on a bicycle darted out from between two parked cars. She swerved, but
not in time. The impact was a sound she could not describe, only feel—a thud that seemed to happen
inside her own chest. She stopped the car, ran to him, and held his hand while someone called an
ambulance. He survived, but with a traumatic brain injury that left him permanently disabled. What
Maya could not piece together, even now, was the exact sequence: the moment she saw him, the moment
she hit the brakes, the moment of contact. Her memory came in disconnected pieces—the wet asphalt,
the handlebar twisting, the boy’s mother screaming later at the hospital. She could not tell a clear
story of what happened, even to herself. When she tried, she felt an overwhelming grief that rose up
like a wave, and she would stop.

In the weeks that followed, Maya began to avoid anything that reminded her of that evening. She
stopped making eye contact with people, especially in the waiting room of her own clinic, because
she had come to believe that if she looked at someone too long, they would see what she had done.
She was certain that her face betrayed her—that clients, colleagues, even strangers in the grocery
store could sense the guilt she carried. She also avoided all medical cues: she no longer drove past
the hospital where the boy had been treated, she took a different route to work to avoid the
intersection, and she had not been for a physical examination in over a year, even though she had
chronic headaches she worried about. At home, she tried hard not to think about the accident. She
would turn on the television the moment she walked through the door, or start a conversation with
her husband about something trivial, anything to crowd out the images. But at night, when the house
was quiet, her mind would not cooperate. She had trouble falling asleep, and when she did, she often
woke in the early hours with her heart pounding, a vague sense of dread pressing down on her chest.

Maya’s work as a counselor became a painful paradox. She sat with clients who talked about their own
traumas, and she found herself flooded with strong negative feelings—shame that she was sitting in
judgment of others when she had caused harm, guilt that she was being paid to help people when she
could not help the boy she had hurt. She became hypervigilant during sessions, scanning her clients’
faces for any sign that they could tell she was a fraud. She began to dread going to her office, and
she started cancelling sessions, then taking sick days. Her supervisor noticed and asked if
everything was all right. Maya lied and said she had a stomach virus. The lie itself felt like
further proof of her moral failure.

The most pervasive change was in how Maya saw her life. Nothing felt meaningful anymore. She had
once believed that her work mattered, that she was helping people heal. Now she thought: I destroyed
a family. I ruined a boy’s future. I should have protected him—I was the adult, I was behind the
wheel—and I failed. The self-blame was a constant companion, a loop that ran beneath every thought.
She had come to believe that the event happened because of the way she acted: she should have been
going slower, she should have seen him sooner, she should have been paying more attention. The fact
that the police report found her not at fault did not matter. She knew the truth.

What finally brought Maya to seek help was a moment at the dinner table. Her husband asked her a
simple question about her day, and she burst into tears. She could not explain why. She was
terrified that he was starting to see her the way she saw herself—as someone dangerous, someone who
had failed the most basic human duty of not harming another person. She came to therapy hoping, she
said, to stop feeling like she was drowning. But underneath that, what she was most afraid of was
that no amount of talking could undo what she had done, and that she would carry this grief and this
guilt for the rest of her life.
