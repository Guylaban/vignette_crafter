Frank is a 77-year-old delivery driver, a job he has held for over three decades and which once felt
as natural as breathing. The assault that upended his life happened eight months ago, on a Tuesday
afternoon in late November. He had pulled his truck into the loading bay of a large hardware store,
a routine stop he had made hundreds of times. As he stepped out of the cab, a man he had never seen
before came at him from behind the dumpster, demanding the keys to the truck. Frank, startled but
not yet afraid, had tried to calmly explain that he couldn’t do that. The man didn’t argue. He
simply struck Frank across the face with a heavy metal pipe. The pain was immediate and sickening, a
bright white light behind his eyes. He remembers the wet, sharp crack of his own cheekbone breaking,
and the feeling of his knees hitting the cold asphalt. The man took the keys and drove the truck
twenty feet before abandoning it, but for Frank, the world had already ended. He was left on the
ground, bleeding, unable to move, waiting for someone to find him.

In the weeks that followed, Frank tried to convince himself it was just a bad moment, a story to
tell. He refused to take more than a few days off, insisting he was fine. But the world outside his
front door had become a place of hidden threats. Every blind corner on his delivery route, every
person standing too still near a loading dock, every unexpected sound from behind a parked car—his
body would seize up, his heart hammering against his ribs. He started taking longer routes to avoid
the hardware store, and then to avoid any street that looked like the one he was on. He told his
wife, Margaret, that he was just being careful, that at his age he had to watch his step. But what
he could not tell her was that he had begun to believe something far more frightening: that he could
no longer trust his own judgment. He had always prided himself on reading people, on knowing when a
situation was safe. But he had walked right into that loading bay. He had not seen the threat
coming. Now, every decision felt suspect. He would stand in the grocery aisle for five minutes,
paralyzed, because he could not be sure which route to the register was the safe one. He had come to
believe that if he let his guard down for even a second, he would be caught off guard again, and
this time, he might not get up.

To manage the constant, low-grade dread, Frank worked harder. He volunteered for extra shifts,
taking any run that would keep him behind the wheel for ten or twelve hours. The motion of the
truck, the focus required to navigate traffic, the sheer exhaustion—these things pushed the memory
of the pipe and the asphalt to the edges of his mind. He was grateful for the fatigue, because it
meant that when he finally fell into bed, he was too tired to dream. But the strategy had a cost. He
was snapping at Margaret, withdrawing from their weekend card games with the neighbors, and eating
mechanically, almost unconsciously, at the end of every shift. He would come home, sit in the dark
kitchen, and eat an entire loaf of bread or a bag of cookies, not out of hunger, but as a way to
fill the hollow, trembling feeling inside him. He knew it was unhealthy, but it was easier than
sitting with the thought that had begun to haunt him: that his understanding of the world—a world
where hard work and decency kept you safe—had been completely destroyed.

He tried not to think about the assault. He would actively steer his mind away whenever the image of
the pipe swinging toward his face surfaced, because he was certain that if he let himself really
think about it, he would not be able to handle it. The memory would swallow him whole. He would be
back on that cold ground, helpless, and he was terrified that he might never find his way out again.

hollow-eyed, and she had simply said, “Frank, you’re not here anymore. You left the day that man hit

the growing certainty that the man he used to be—the confident, decisive driver who knew the city
like the back of his hand—was gone for good, and he did not know how to find his way back.
