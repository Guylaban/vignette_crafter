Marcus is a thirty-four-year-old school counselor, married for six years, who lives with his wife in
a small suburban town. He came to therapy at his wife’s urging, though he is not sure what he
expects to gain. What he is sure of is that something inside him has been broken since the accident,
and he no longer trusts himself to do anything right.

The event itself happened fourteen months ago. Marcus had been driving home from a late parent-
teacher conference on a rainy November evening. He was tired, distracted, replaying a difficult
conversation in his head. At an intersection he had driven through a thousand times, his light
turned green, and he proceeded forward. A motorcycle ran the red light from the side street. Marcus
hit the brakes, but it was too late. The rider, a twenty-two-year-old man named Derek, was thrown
thirty feet. Marcus remembers the sound of impact, the smell of gasoline and wet asphalt, and the
sight of Derek’s helmeted head lying at an unnatural angle on the pavement. He got out, called 911,
stayed until the ambulance came. Derek survived, but with a traumatic brain injury and permanent
paralysis from the waist down. The police investigation cleared Marcus of any fault—the other driver
had been speeding and ran a red light—but that finding means nothing to him. He knows what he did.
He was not paying full attention. He could have stopped sooner. The event happened because of the
way he acted, and he cannot forgive himself for it.

In the months that followed, Marcus’s life began to collapse inward. He stopped driving through that
intersection, then stopped driving altogether if he could avoid it. His wife drives him to work now,
and he takes the bus when she cannot. He used to enjoy their weekend trips to the nearby mountains,
but now the idea of being behind a wheel fills him with dread. At work, he finds himself unable to
concentrate during staff meetings, his mind racing with images of the crash. He has become skilled
at keeping his mind occupied all the time—counting ceiling tiles during conversations, reciting the
names of all his students alphabetically, doing multiplication tables in his head. If his thoughts
drift for even a moment, the memory comes back completely real across all his senses at once: the
screech of tires, the smell of gasoline, the wet chill of rain on his face, the sight of Derek’s
body on the ground. And when it comes, it does not feel like a memory of something that happened. It
feels like it is happening right now, in this room, and he is back on that road again, helpless.

He cannot talk about it. When his wife asks how he is doing, he opens his mouth and finds that he
knows exactly what happened, but he is unable to find words to describe it. The sentences will not
form. The sounds will not come. So he shakes his head and says he is fine, and she knows he is not.

He has come to avoid any thought of the event. If a news story about a motorcycle accident comes on
the radio, he changes the station. If a student mentions a car crash in passing, he finds an excuse
to leave the room. He has also begun avoiding the police. There is a police substation two blocks
from his school, and he now takes a different route to the main office so he does not have to walk
past it. He is not sure what he is afraid of—he was never charged—but the sight of a squad car makes
his heart pound and his palms sweat.

Sleep has become a battle. He lies awake for hours because he feels he needs to stay alert, as
though something terrible will happen if he lets his guard down. When he does sleep, he wakes from
nightmares drenched in sweat, the smell of gasoline still in his nostrils. He has strong negative
feelings almost constantly—guilt that sits like a stone in his chest, shame that burns in his face,
and a low hum of fear that never quite goes away. When he is reminded of the crash, his body reacts
before his mind does: his hands shake, his stomach lurches, and he feels like he might pass out.

He has come to believe that his life has been destroyed by the trauma. He used to think of himself
as a good person, a helper, someone who made a difference in kids’ lives. Now he is certain that he
is dangerous, that he cannot trust himself to do the right thing. He second-guesses every decision
he makes at work. He wonders if he should even be counseling students when he is the one who ruined
another person’s life. His wife tells him he is being too hard on himself, but he does not believe
her. She does not know what he knows.

What finally brought Marcus to therapy was a moment last week when a seventh-grader came to his
office in tears after a fight with her mother. Marcus sat across from her, and for a full minute he
could not think of a single thing to say. His mind went blank. He felt the familiar surge of panic,
the certainty that he was about to do something wrong, that he would fail this girl the way he had
failed Derek. He excused himself, told the front desk she needed a different counselor, and locked
himself in the bathroom until his hands stopped shaking. That evening, his wife told him she was
scared for him. She said she missed him. She said he needed help.

Marcus is here now, sitting in a therapist’s office, because he does not know what else to do. He is
afraid of what he might become if this continues. He is afraid he will lose his job, his marriage,
his sense of who he is. But more than anything, he is afraid that he is right—that he really cannot
be trusted, that he is a danger to everyone around him, and that no amount of talking will change
that.
