F-16’s oxygen system malfunctioned at thirty thousand feet. Within seconds, Carl experienced a
sudden, overwhelming dizziness and lightheadedness—a sensation he had never known in thirty years of
flying. The world tilted, his vision narrowed, and he felt physically unwell in a way that terrified
him more than any enemy fire ever had. He managed to wrestle the aircraft down, but the moment he
unbuckled, his legs gave out. The medics found him on the tarmac, disoriented and vomiting,
surrounded by the echoes of an explosion that had never happened—only the sound of his own
aircraft’s emergency systems, which to this day he heard as a deafening bang in his memory.

In the months that followed, Carl’s world contracted. He had always believed that skill and
discipline could control any situation. That belief was destroyed. The world, he now felt, was a
fundamentally dangerous place where a man could be undone by a simple mechanical failure, by his own
body betraying him. He felt dead inside, emotionally numb to his wife’s attempts to comfort him, to
his children’s calls, to the sunsets he used to love watching from his back porch. He gave up his
hobbies—woodworking, running, fly-fishing—because nothing brought him pleasure anymore. He stopped
eating regularly, restricting his food intake to a narrow set of bland, predictable meals. It was
the only thing he could control. When his wife suggested a weekend getaway, he snapped at her, then
withdrew. He had come to believe that any change in routine was a risk, that any unfamiliar
sensation could trigger that dizzying spiral again.

every corner. An unexpected movement nearby—a colleague stepping into his peripheral vision, a bird

scanning every room for exits, every face for signs of danger. The most crippling avoidance,
however, was medical. He refused physical examinations, avoided hospitals and ambulances with a
dread that bordered on phobic. When his wife developed a persistent cough, he insisted she see a

doctor’s office would recreate the sensations of that day—the dizziness, the feeling of being
unwell, the loss of control.

started at the sound of a door slamming, tensed at the rumble of a fuel truck. He requested desk

need for control. What finally brought him to seek help was not his own suffering, but a moment with
