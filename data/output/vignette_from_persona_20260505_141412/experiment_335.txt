Adebayo was twenty years old, a pharmacist at a downtown clinic, and she had been in the United
States for just over three years. She came to therapy reluctantly, referred by her supervisor after
she had frozen mid-shift, staring at a sink full of water, unable to move or speak for nearly two
minutes. Her coworkers thought she was having a seizure. Adebayo knew she was back in the room.

The room was a concrete cell in the basement of a government building in her home country. She had
been detained for her family’s political affiliations—her older brother had been a student
organizer, and when he fled, they took her instead. For three days, men she could not identify held
her under. They forced her head into a barrel of water, over and over, until her lungs burned and
her legs kicked uselessly against the floor. She remembered the sound of water sloshing, the cold
shock against her scalp, and the way her body seized when she could not breathe. She remembered the
metal taste of fear. She did not remember how it ended. The last guard left, and she was simply
there, on the wet concrete, alone. Other prisoners in nearby cells had not been so lucky. She knew
that because she heard them. She heard them stop making noise. She told herself she should have been
the one who stopped.

Now, when a memory came, it was not a story she could tell. It was a position. Her back would arch,
her hands would clench, and her neck would strain as if someone were pressing her face downward. She
would feel the cold floor against her cheek, the weight of a hand on the back of her head, and she
could not speak. Words did not belong to that memory. The memory belonged to her body, and her body
did not have a language.

She had learned to avoid anything that might bring on those moments. She would not wash dishes at
home because the sound of running water made her chest tighten. She stopped showering at night,
because the dark and the steam together felt too close. She avoided the breakroom at work if anyone
was arguing, because raised voices made her stomach drop and her eyes dart for an exit. She had come
to believe that if she let herself think about what happened, even for a second, she would shatter
completely—that the memory would swallow her and she would not come back. So she pushed it away,
kept her mind busy, stayed in motion. But the pushing took a toll. She could not fall asleep at
night without her back against the wall, facing the door, listening. She had to know every way out
of every room before she could sit down. She scanned the faces of customers, checking for anyone who
looked like the men from the basement. She had started arriving late to work because she could not
bring herself to walk through the parking lot without checking every car, every shadow.

Her boyfriend, a kind man who knew only fragments of her story, had begun to pull away. He did not
understand why she flinched when he touched her neck, or why she insisted on sleeping with the light
on. She told him she was fine. She told herself she deserved the sleeplessness. She told herself she
deserved the fear. Some nights, lying rigid beside him, she whispered that she should have been the
one who did not make it out. The guilt was not a thought she could argue with—it was a fact, as
solid as the floor beneath her.

What finally brought her to the clinic was not the guilt, or the scanning, or the way her body
reenacted the drowning when she least expected it. It was the moment at the sink, when her
supervisor had to shake her shoulder to bring her back, and she saw real fear in her coworker’s
eyes. Adebayo realized she could no longer hide what was happening inside her. She was afraid of
being seen, but she was more afraid of what might happen if she kept pretending nothing was wrong.
She wanted to stop feeling like she was still in that room. She wanted to sleep without watching the
door. She wanted to believe, for the first time in three years, that she was allowed to be alive.
