cut through the windshield at just the wrong angle. She was sixty-three then, still working steadily
as a model, driving home from a shoot in the city. The intersection was familiar—she’d taken it
hundreds of times. But that day, a man on a bicycle appeared from her blind spot as she turned
right. She didn’t see him until she felt the thud, the terrible give of metal against flesh. She
stopped the car immediately, got out, and found him lying on the pavement, his leg bent at an angle
that made her stomach turn. She called 911, knelt beside him, and held his hand until the ambulance
came. But in the weeks that followed, that single image—his face twisted in pain, his leg, the
blood—began to replay in her mind, not as a memory she could set aside, but as a loop she could not
stop.

Margaret is sixty-six now, widowed for eight years, and still modeling part-time, mostly for high-
end catalogues and the occasional editorial. She has always taken pride in her composure, her
ability to hold a pose for hours, to project calm and confidence. But since the accident, that
composure has eroded from the inside. She cannot relax, even when she knows she is in a safe place.
At home, in her quiet apartment, she finds herself sitting on the edge of the sofa, her shoulders
hunched, her hands gripping her knees. Her body feels constantly tense, as if it is waiting for
something terrible to happen. She has trouble concentrating during fittings and shoots; she forgets
instructions, loses her place in the sequence of poses, and has to ask photographers to repeat
themselves. They have been patient, but she sees the flicker of irritation in their eyes, and it
deepens her sense of humiliation.

What frightens Margaret most is not the memory of the accident itself, but what she has come to
believe about herself because of it. She is certain that somebody else—someone more alert, more
capable—would not have gotten into this situation. She replays the turn again and again, imagining
how another driver would have checked the blind spot twice, would have slowed down, would have seen
the cyclist in time. She feels permanently humiliated by what happened, as though the accident has
stained her character, made her visibly incompetent to anyone who knows. And beneath that is a
sharper, more private pain: she will never forgive herself for not doing more to stop what happened.

sits in her chest like a stone. She should have done something—screamed, swerved, braked harder—and
she did not.

The avoidance crept in slowly, over weeks and months. She stopped driving through that intersection,
then stopped driving on that side of town entirely. She began to take back roads, then to ask
friends for rides, then to cancel appointments altogether if she could not arrange transportation.
Her agent noticed. She lost two catalogue jobs because she could not get to the studio on time. She
told herself she was being careful, but she knew, deep down, that she was shrinking her life. Even
walking near traffic became difficult; she would flinch at the sound of a bicycle bell, her heart
hammering, her body bracing for a crash that had already happened.

What finally brought Margaret to seek help was a shoot last month. The photographer asked her to
stand near an open window for natural light. She could not do it. She felt her throat close, her
palms sweat, and she had to excuse herself to the bathroom, where she stood trembling for ten
minutes. She could not explain why. She could not tell him that the window reminded her of the

she will never feel like herself again, that the accident has rewritten who she is. But she is also
hoping—cautiously, quietly—that someone can help her find a way to live with what she has done.
