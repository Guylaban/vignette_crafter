The first time Richard tried to tell himself the story of what had happened, he could not find the
beginning. He was sixty-eight years old, a clinical psychologist who had spent decades helping other
people make sense of their worst moments, and he could not make sense of his own. What he had
instead were photographs—sharp, still images that arrived without warning. The glint of a
chandelier. The pattern of a rug. The particular shade of red on a sweater. He could see these
things with perfect clarity, as though they had been taken with a camera and tucked away in a
drawer, but when he tried to arrange them into a sequence, into something that had happened to him,
the pictures refused to connect. There was no story. There was only the photograph, and then the
feeling.

The feeling came without the image, too. Sometimes he would be standing at the kitchen sink, washing
a coffee cup, and suddenly his chest would tighten and his hands would shake and he would be flooded
with a terror so complete that he had to grip the counter to keep from falling. No picture. No
memory. Just the raw, physical certainty that he was in danger, that something terrible was
happening right now, even though the kitchen was quiet and the sun was coming through the window and
his wife was in the next room reading.

The danger had come from someone he loved. That was the part that had shattered everything. Richard
had been married for thirty-two years to a woman he trusted completely, and for the last five of
those years, she had hurt him. Not every day. Not in ways that left marks that other people could
see. But in ways that left him lying on the floor of the bedroom, staring at the chandelier, trying
to understand how he had gotten there. She had hit him. She had shoved him. She had screamed at him
until his ears rang. And he had stayed, because he was a psychologist, because he believed he could
help her, because he believed that if he just tried harder, if he was more patient, more
understanding, more adequate, she would stop. She did not stop. He left after five years, and he had
not seen her since, but she was still inside him.

Now he lived alone in a small apartment, though he still saw his patients at a clinic across town.
Every morning he stood in front of his closet and considered the clothes. He had stopped wearing
anything that resembled what she used to wear—no dark reds, no particular shade of blue, no soft
grey sweaters. He did not know why this mattered, but it did. If he put on a shirt that reminded
him, even vaguely, of something she had owned, he would feel a crawling unease that lasted all day.
So he wore only beige and white and navy, and he checked himself in the mirror before he left the
house, and he made sure.

The bicycle was a problem. He had ridden a bike for exercise his whole adult life, but now he could
not look at one without his stomach dropping. He did not remember why. There was no photograph of a
bicycle. But the association was absolute: bicycle meant danger. He walked everywhere now, even when
it took twice as long.

The women were a problem, too. He still saw female patients—he had to, it was his profession—but he
scheduled them only in the mornings, when the clinic was quiet, and he always left the door slightly
ajar. He did not know what he was afraid they would do. He knew, intellectually, that they were not
her. But when he was alone in a room with a woman, his body forgot what his mind knew. His shoulders
would rise toward his ears. His breathing would shorten. He would watch her hands, her posture, the
expression on her face, searching for the signs that she was about to turn. He watched everyone,
actually. He watched people on the street, in the grocery store, in the waiting room. He watched for
the small changes in posture, the tightening around the mouth, that meant they were about to hurt
him. He had become an expert at reading threat in other people’s bodies, and he was certain that if
he stopped watching, even for a moment, someone would catch him off guard.

At night it was worse. He woke at two, three, four in the morning, his heart already pounding, and
he lay in the dark and listened. He listened for footsteps, for the creak of a floorboard, for the
sound of someone breathing who should not be there. He checked the locks. He checked the windows. He
knew it was irrational. She lived in another state. She did not know where he lived. But the night
felt different, felt dangerous, and he could not convince his body otherwise.

He had stopped feeling angry a long time ago. He had decided, somewhere in the middle of those five
years, that anger was not allowed. Anger was what she had, anger was what destroyed things, and he
would not be like her. So he suppressed it. He pushed it down so thoroughly that he sometimes forgot
he had any emotions at all. When a patient asked him how he was doing, he said fine. When his sister
called and said he sounded distant, he said he was just tired. He tried to control everything he
felt, because he believed that if he let even one feeling escape, he would not be able to stop the
rest.

He had come to therapy because his supervisor at the clinic had noticed he was canceling
appointments. He had come because he was sixty-eight years old and he could not tell the story of
what had happened to him, even to himself. He sat in the chair across from his new therapist—a man,
he had made sure of that—and he said, quietly, that he thought his beliefs about the world had been
destroyed. He said he did not trust anyone, not really. He said he felt inadequate, like he had
failed, like he should have been able to fix her or leave sooner or be stronger. He said he wanted
to stop waking up afraid. He did not say that he was terrified of what would happen if he let
himself remember the whole story. But he thought it, sitting there in the quiet room, and he knew
that the therapist could see it in his face.
