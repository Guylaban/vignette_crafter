The event that undid Marcus was not a bomb. After twenty-two years dismantling improvised explosive
devices in conflict zones, he had developed a quiet, almost surgical relationship with controlled
danger. He knew the weight of a trigger mechanism, the precise pressure required to disarm. What he
could not prepare for was a wall of water. During a routine deployment following a Category 5
hurricane, Marcus was leading a team to clear debris from a flooded residential area when the levee
upstream gave way without warning. The surge came as a roar, a sound that was not a blast but
carried the same obliterating force. He was swept against a collapsed garage, pinned by the current
as a car door slammed into his ribs. He watched a mother and her two children being pulled from
their roof, and he could not reach them. He could not move. The water was brown and loud and he
screamed for help that did not come for nearly forty minutes. The family did not survive. Marcus
did, but something in him had been torn loose.

In the months that followed, he returned to his job, but the world had changed. A colleague shouting
across the base to get his attention would send him into a cold sweat, his heart hammering before
his mind could register that there was no threat. He began to notice that his body reacted before he
did—a sudden loud noise, a truck backfiring, a door slamming—and he would find himself on the
ground, hands over his head, breathing in ragged gasps. He had flashbacks that came without warning:
the brown water, the screaming, the weight of the current. During one such episode in the mess hall,
he shoved a table aside before he realized where he was. The rage that surfaced with these memories
was volcanic. He would feel a burning, wordless fury at the water, at the engineers who had built
the levee, at himself. But when he tried to recall the full sequence of events, the memory seemed to
stop short, like a film reel that snapped mid-scene. He knew he had been underwater at one point,
but the seconds between being submerged and being pulled out were a blank space he could not enter.
He pushed the memory away before he could complete it, and the incompleteness gnawed at him.

To manage the constant sense of being on alert, Marcus developed a set of habits that he did not
recognize as strategies. He began to avoid anything that could be stressful. He stopped answering
phone calls from his unit. He declined invitations to barbecues, to birthday parties, to his wife’s
work events. If a situation felt unpredictable—a new restaurant, a change in schedule, a crowded
grocery store—he simply did not go. He kept his mind occupied all the time. At home, he would have
the television on while scrolling through his phone while listening to a podcast, because silence
was where the water waited. He began to eat excessively, often late at night after his wife had gone
to bed, consuming entire pizzas or bags of chips without tasting them, just to have something to do
with his hands and his mouth. His weight climbed by thirty pounds. His wife, Amara, began to sleep
in the guest room because his thrashing and shouting woke her. He told her he was fine. He told
himself the same thing.

What finally brought Marcus to the clinic was a moment at work. He was inspecting a suspicious
package in a suburban backyard when a child in the neighboring house slammed a screen door. The
sound was sharp, sudden. Marcus dropped his tools, spun around, and drew his sidearm before he had
consciously registered the noise. His team looked at him with something between concern and pity.
His supervisor sent him home and told him to take leave. That night, sitting alone in his living
room with three different screens running, Marcus realized he could not remember the last time he
had felt safe. He was terrified of his own reflexes. He was afraid that the next time his body
reacted before his mind, someone would get hurt. He wanted to stop being afraid of the world. He
wanted to sit in a quiet room and not hear the water.
