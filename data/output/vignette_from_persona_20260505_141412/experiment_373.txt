Rajesh still remembers the silence. Not the kind that settles over the water at dawn, which he had
known for forty years—that was a working silence, a patient one. The silence he remembers is the one
that came after the metal stopped screaming. He was fifty-five then, three years ago, hauling nets
off the coast of Cape May in his own boat, the *Lakshmi*, which he had named for his wife. A
recreational cabin cruiser had come out of the fog, its driver distracted by his phone, and had
T-boned Rajesh’s hull at a speed that should have killed him. He felt the impact in his spine first,
then heard the groan of fiberglass giving way, and then—for what felt like an eternity—there was
only the sound of water lapping against the wreckage and his own breath, ragged and too fast. He was
thrown clear, but his left leg was pinned beneath a tangle of railing and net. He remembers the cold
saltwater rising past his chest, past his chin, and the thought that was not a thought but a
certainty: *This is where I die. My wife is already gone, and now I will join her.* The Coast Guard
pulled him out with minutes to spare.

Now, at fifty-eight, Rajesh lives in a small apartment two blocks from the harbor, but he has not
been to the docks in eighteen months. He sold the *Lakshmi* for scrap. He tells himself it was the
right decision—the boat was damaged beyond repair—but the truth is that he cannot stand to look at
the water. The water is where the silence lives.

The worst moments come when the world goes quiet in a different way. He will be sitting in his
living room, the television off, and the refrigerator will cycle down, and suddenly the stillness
presses against his ears like a physical weight. His heart begins to pound. His palms sweat. He is
certain that something is about to happen, that the air itself is holding its breath before a blow.
He has come to believe that silence is not peace but a threat waiting to reveal itself. He keeps the
radio on at all hours now, even when he sleeps. He sleeps in the living room, on the couch, because
the bedroom feels too far from the front door. Even inside his own home, he is alert. He scans the
room constantly—the shadow behind the curtain, the crack in the closet door, the unfamiliar shape of
a coat hanging on the hook. He knows it is his own apartment, but his body does not believe it.

He cannot find the words to describe what happened. When his daughter, Priya, asks him about the
accident, he opens his mouth and nothing comes. He knows the facts—the boat, the cruiser, the
water—but the *knowing* and the *telling* are two different things. The memory does not behave like
a memory. It visits him as a present-tense event: the cold water at his chin, the pressure on his
leg, the taste of diesel and salt. He feels the same terror now that he felt then. His body responds
as though it is still in the water, even though he is dry and safe.

He has stopped going to the grocery store because he cannot manage the strangers. The checkout
clerk, the man in the aisle who asks for the salt—every interaction feels like a mask hiding
something. He has come to distrust people entirely. They seem friendly, but he is certain that they
are not what they appear. A kind word feels like a setup. A smile feels like a lie. He avoids the
fish market, where the other fishermen gather, because someone will inevitably talk about a lost
crewmate, a widow left behind, a man who went out and did not come back. The talk of loss lands in
his chest like a stone. He cannot bear it.

The person he was before the accident—the man who could fix a diesel engine with his eyes closed,
who knew the currents of the Delaware Bay the way he knew his own breathing—that man feels gone.
Rajesh looks in the mirror and does not recognize himself. He cannot trust his own body anymore. The
leg that was pinned aches in the cold, but it is not the leg he distrusts; it is the whole machine.
His heart races without reason. His hands shake. He feels betrayed by his own flesh, which once
carried him through storms and now cannot carry him to the mailbox.

He came to the clinic because Priya found him sitting on the living room floor at three in the
morning, staring at the front door, waiting for something he could not name. She said, “Dad, you
need help.” He did not argue. What he is most afraid of is that he will never feel like himself
again—that the man he was died in the water three years ago, and this stranger who jumps at silence
and trusts no one is all that is left. What he hopes for, though he cannot say it aloud, is that
somewhere inside this terrified body, the old Rajesh is still there, waiting to be found.
