Maria is a 38-year-old bartender who has been with her partner, David, for nearly six years. She
came to therapy at David’s urging, though she is not entirely sure what talking can change. What
happened is over. The problem, as she sees it, is that she cannot stop seeing it.

The event itself took place eighteen months ago, on a warm October evening. Maria had just finished
a double shift and was walking home through the alley behind the bar—a shortcut she had taken
hundreds of times. She heard a scuffle, then a man’s voice shouting, then a wet, cracking sound. She
rounded the corner and saw two men on the ground. One was on top of the other, fist raised. The man
on the bottom was David. She did not see the punch land, but she heard it. She screamed, and the
attacker ran. David’s face was already swelling, blood coming from his nose and a cut above his eye.
Maria called 911, but in the moments before help arrived, she could not make herself touch him. She
stood frozen, phone in hand, repeating his name. Later, at the hospital, the doctors said it was a
concussion, a broken nose, and three fractured ribs. He healed. But Maria did not.

Her memory of that night is a strange patchwork. Some parts are impossibly vivid: the sound of the
punch, the pattern of the gravel where David’s head hit the ground, the way his eyes fluttered
before he lost consciousness. She can replay these images in high definition, as though they are
happening in the present. Other parts are simply gone. She cannot remember dialing 911, though she
knows she did. She cannot remember what she said to the paramedic. There is a blank space where
those minutes should be, and she has learned not to push against it, because pushing only brings the
clear images back stronger.

Maria has come to believe that what happened is her fault. She was the one who chose the shortcut.
She was the one who wanted to get home faster. If she had taken the main street, David would not
have been attacked. She tells herself this so often that it has become a kind of fact, as solid as
her own name. And if she cannot trust her own judgment about something as simple as a route home,
then how can she trust anyone else? She has started to see people differently. A customer at the bar
who seems too friendly—she wonders what they are hiding. A neighbor who offers to help carry
groceries—she wonders what they want in return. She has become certain that people are not what they
seem, that everyone has a hidden agenda, and that relying on anyone is a mistake she will not make
again.

To manage this, Maria has built a careful set of rules. She avoids the news entirely. If a
television in the bar is tuned to a local station, she asks a coworker to change it. She cannot
watch stories about assaults, muggings, or random violence—they feel too close, as though the screen
might reach out and pull her back into that alley. She has also learned to control her emotions with
fierce discipline. At work, she is professional, smiling, efficient. But the moment she feels a
flicker of anger or fear, she pushes it down, locks it away. She has become an expert at appearing
fine while feeling nothing at all. And she has avoided seeking any kind of help—medical or
psychological—until now. David made this appointment for her, and she came because she was tired of
his worry, not because she believed it would work.

The consequences have been accumulating quietly. She has stopped walking anywhere alone. She drives
even the shortest distances, circling blocks for parking rather than taking a two-minute walk. At
work, she has started asking a coworker to walk her to her car after every shift, even though the
lot is well-lit and only fifty feet from the door. She knows this looks strange, but the
alternative—standing in that dark lot by herself—is unthinkable. David has noticed, and their
conversations have grown strained. He wants to go out, to see friends, to live normally. She cannot
explain why she feels safest in their apartment, with the door locked and the blinds drawn.

What finally brought Maria to this chair is not insight or readiness. It is exhaustion. She is tired
of the vigilance, tired of the images that play behind her eyes when she tries to sleep, tired of
the way her chest tightens every time she hears a sudden noise. She does not know if therapy can
help, and she is afraid that talking about it will only make the images sharper. But she is here.
And for now, that is enough.
