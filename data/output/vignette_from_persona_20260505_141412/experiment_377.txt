Michael is a 45-year-old journalist, married, and based in the Midwest. He came to therapy
reluctantly, at his wife’s urging, after months of what he called “not being himself.” The event
that changed everything happened two years ago, while he was embedded with a humanitarian convoy
near a conflict zone. He and two colleagues were taken by an armed group and held for eleven days.
For the first three days, they were blindfolded and moved between locations. On the fourth day, the
blindfolds came off, and the interrogations began. Michael was tied to a metal chair in a small room
that smelled of cheap liquor and sweat. One of his captors, a man who reeked of alcohol, would lean
in close to shout questions, his breath hot against Michael’s face. When Michael did not answer
quickly enough, the man would grip the back of his neck, hard, and press his thumb into the muscle
just below his skull. The pain was sharp and constant, a pressure that radiated into his shoulders
and made it impossible to think. He learned to brace for it, to anticipate the touch before it came.
That touch, that smell, that specific ache in his neck—they became the coordinates of his terror.

After he was released and returned home, Michael initially believed he had simply been through a bad
experience that would fade. But it did not. Within weeks, the memory of that room began to force its
way into his mind without warning. He would be sitting at the dinner table with his wife, and
suddenly the film would start playing: the chair, the smell, the hand on his neck. It came with the
same visceral terror he had felt in the moment—his chest would tighten, his palms would sweat, and
he would feel, for a few seconds, that he was back in that room. He learned to hide these episodes,
to excuse himself to the bathroom or to pretend he had forgotten something in the car.

His hypervigilance became a constant companion. He found himself scanning every room he entered,
noting exits, watching people’s hands. Unexpected or loud sounds—a car backfiring, a door slamming,
a dropped pan in the kitchen—would make him flinch and freeze, his heart hammering before his mind
could even register what had happened. He became aware that he was often reacting as if he were in
danger even when nothing was actually wrong. He would be walking to his car in a parking lot and
feel a spike of fear at the approach of a stranger, his body preparing for a confrontation that
never came. He knew, logically, that he was safe. But the feeling overrode logic.

His work as a journalist became impossible. He had always covered difficult stories, but now the
idea of being in a crowd, of interviewing someone in a close space, of smelling alcohol on a
source’s breath, was unbearable. He stopped taking assignments that required travel. He stopped
attending press conferences. He began working from home, filing stories based on phone interviews,
avoiding the newsroom entirely. His editor, a longtime colleague, expressed concern, but Michael
could not explain why he could no longer do his job. The word “trauma” felt too dramatic, too self-
pitying. He told himself he just needed more time.

At home, his avoidance became more subtle but no less costly. He started sitting with his back to
the wall in restaurants, and then he stopped going to restaurants altogether. He found reasons to
decline invitations from friends. He stopped letting his wife touch his neck during sex, and then
stopped wanting to be touched at all. He could not explain why the sensation of a hand on his
shoulder, even hers, made his stomach turn. He could not tell her that when she reached for him in
bed, his mind flashed to that chair, to that grip. She grew hurt and confused. He grew isolated and
ashamed.

What finally brought Michael to therapy was a moment of collapse. He was walking through his own
kitchen, and his wife, trying to comfort him after a bad night, placed her hand on the back of his
neck. He spun around, shouting, before he even knew what he was doing. She stepped back, her face
white. He saw her fear, and he saw that she was afraid of him. That night, he sat alone in the dark
and admitted to himself that something was broken that he could not fix alone.

He is afraid, now, of what he might do if he keeps living this way. He is afraid that the man who
smelled of alcohol has not stopped holding him, even from across the ocean. He is hoping, though he
cannot quite say it out loud, that there is a way to stop the film from playing.
