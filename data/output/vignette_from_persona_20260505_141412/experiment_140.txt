At sixty-seven, Marcus Tuala had spent forty years transforming other people’s homes into
sanctuaries—spaces of light, proportion, and calm. He was known for his patience, his meticulous
eye, and the way he could make a room breathe. Now, he could barely step into his own office. The
trauma that had undone him had happened three years earlier, during a business trip to the Middle
East, when he was taken captive by armed men during a hotel siege. For eleven hours, he was held in
a windowless storage room with seven other hostages, blindfolded, his hands bound. The men shouted
at them in Arabic—commands, threats, he never knew which—and he had been certain he was going to
die. He had been certain, too, that he would never see his wife again. She had died two years before
that, and he had sometimes wondered if the terror of the captivity had been sharpened by the
knowledge that there was no one waiting for him on the other side.

Since returning home, Marcus had found that the world had become a series of traps. The trigger
could be almost anything: a raised voice in a restaurant, a door that clicked shut too firmly, the
sound of a language he did not understand. But the worst was the shouting. He would be walking down
the street in Honolulu and hear a man call out in Arabic on his phone, and suddenly Marcus would be
back in that storage room, his heart slamming against his ribs, his palms slick, his mind gone
blank. His body reacted before he had even registered what he had heard—a lurch, a freeze, a
desperate scan for an exit. He had come to believe that he was no longer safe anywhere. He could not
bear to have his back to an open space. In restaurants, he insisted on a booth against the wall, and
even then he found himself turning his head constantly, checking the door, checking the faces. He
had begun to rehearse conversations before any interaction—calling a client, ordering coffee,
greeting a neighbor—running through possible lines in his head because he was terrified that if he
was caught off guard, he would lose his words, and then he would look weak, and then something bad
would happen.

The avoidance had crept in slowly, then swallowed him whole. He stopped going to his design studio
altogether. At first, he told himself it was just a break—too many memories of the trip, too much
noise. But weeks became months, and he found he could not bring himself to walk through the door.
The building had a small elevator, and the thought of stepping into that enclosed box, the doors
sliding shut, the air growing still—it was impossible. He began working from home, taking only
clients who agreed to meet at his kitchen table. His business shrank. His income dropped. He told
himself it was temporary, but he knew, in the hollow hours of the night, that he was hiding.

The memories themselves had become strange. Sometimes he could recall the event in perfect
detail—the smell of sweat and dust, the pressure of the zip-ties—but he felt nothing. It was as if
it had happened to someone else, a man he watched from a great distance, numb and untroubled. And
then, without warning, the sound would come back: a shout, a curse, a phrase he did not understand
but could still hear as clearly as the day it was screamed. When he tried to tell his sister what
had happened, he would lose the thread halfway through, stumbling over words, unable to finish the
sentence, and he would stop trying.

What finally brought Marcus to seek help was not the fear, but the loneliness. He had been widowed
for five years, and for the first time, he felt entirely cut off from the world. He could not work.
He could not visit friends. He could not sit in a room without checking the walls. He came to a
therapist because he was afraid that if he did not, he would spend the rest of his life in his
kitchen, back to the wall, rehearsing the same conversations with no one.
