Layla is a 36-year-old construction worker, married for twelve years, who came to therapy at her
husband’s urging after months of what she described as “falling apart.” She was reluctant, certain
that talking would only confirm what she already feared: that something inside her had broken beyond
repair. The traumatic event that set everything in motion was a sudden, life-threatening illness.
Two years ago, Layla was hospitalized with a pulmonary embolism that nearly killed her. She had felt
a sharp pain in her chest while on a job site, dismissed it as muscle strain, and kept working until
she collapsed. She woke up in the ICU, intubated, with no memory of the preceding hours. What she
did remember, with terrible clarity, was the sensation of not being able to breathe—a feeling that
now returned without warning, often in the middle of the night.

In the months following her discharge, Layla threw herself back into work with a ferocity that
surprised even her colleagues. She took on double shifts, volunteered for the most physically
demanding jobs, and stayed late to organize equipment. She had come to believe that if she stopped
moving, if she allowed herself to rest, her body would betray her again. Overworking became her
primary way of keeping her mind occupied, but it came at a cost. At home, she found herself unable
to engage with her husband or their small social circle. She would sit through dinners in silence,
her thoughts elsewhere, and then eat mechanically afterward—sometimes an entire box of pastries or a
bag of chips—not because she was hungry, but because the act of chewing and swallowing gave her a
few minutes of numbness. She had learned to numb her feelings deliberately, pushing them down until
she felt hollow, because the alternative—the raw, formless terror—seemed unbearable.

But the numbness never lasted. Layla’s body remained on constant alert. She startled at the sound of
a dropped tool, at her husband’s hand on her shoulder, at the hum of a ventilation fan that reminded
her of the hospital. Her sleep was fragmented; she would lie awake for hours, listening to her own
heartbeat, certain that each irregular thump could be the start of another collapse. When she did
fall asleep, she woke drenched in sweat, her chest tight, her mind already scanning for danger. She
had begun to check her pulse multiple times a day, especially on the job site, where the dust and
noise and physical strain felt like a direct threat. She was certain that if she stopped monitoring
her body, she would miss the warning signs and die.

What frightened Layla most, however, was not the physical symptoms but the growing conviction that
her mind was unraveling. She told me that she felt like she was going crazy, that her reactions
since the embolism—the hypervigilance, the flashbacks, the sudden tears—were proof that she had lost
control of herself. She had become convinced that she had no future, that the life she had built
with her husband was already over, that she was merely waiting for the next disaster. She could not
stop bad things from happening; she had learned that lesson in the ICU. When her husband tried to
reassure her, she felt only a hollow despair. What was the point of planning for a future that would
never arrive?

At the construction site, Layla now avoided the area where she had collapsed, taking long detours
even if it meant extra time. She had stopped using the break room because the sound of the coffee
machine reminded her of the hospital’s beeping monitors. Her colleagues noticed her jumpiness, her
withdrawal, and had begun to ask if she was okay. She would shrug them off, but the questions only
deepened her shame. She was certain that if they knew how terrified she was, they would think she
was weak, unfit for the job.

She finally agreed to come to therapy after a night when she woke up convinced she was having
another embolism, called 911, and spent four hours in the ER only to be told she was fine. Her
husband sat beside her in the waiting room, silent and exhausted. Layla looked at him and saw, for
the first time, the toll her fear was taking. She did not know if therapy could help. She was afraid
that speaking about the event would make it worse, that she would confirm once and for all that she
was going crazy. But she was also afraid of staying as she was—afraid of herself, afraid of her own
body, afraid of a future that felt like it had already ended.
