Marco came to therapy at the urging of his older sister, who had watched him retreat from the world
over the past eighteen months. He was twenty-four, a financial analyst at a mid-sized firm in
Dallas, and he lived alone in a one-bedroom apartment he barely left except for work. On paper, he
had done everything right—graduated, landed a solid job, kept his head down—but inside, he felt like
a dead man walking. The number he had circled on the PTSD checklist was seventy, and even that felt
like an undercount of the weight he carried.

The event that had broken him happened when he was twenty-two, during his second deployment as a
Marine rifleman in Helmand Province. What Marco remembered came in fragments, but the fragments were
always the same: the dust, the heat, the radio crackling in a language that was not English, and
then the IED. He could see the Humvee ahead of him lift off the ground and come down twisted. He
could see his team leader, a man named Reyes who had taught him how to tie his boots on their first
day, thrown sideways against the armored door. But he could not put the pieces in order. He knew the
blast came first, then the screaming, then the blood, but the sequence shifted every time he tried
to replay it. Sometimes he saw Reyes’s face before the explosion. Sometimes after. Sometimes not at
all, just the empty space where Reyes had been standing.

What Marco remembered most vividly was the moment he realized he was alive and Reyes was not. He had
crawled over to him, and there was nothing to be done. He remembered the feeling of Reyes’s dog tags
in his hand, how they were still warm. He remembered thinking, *I should have been the one.* That
thought had never left him.

Since returning home, Marco had become a person he did not recognize. The most immediate change was
his avoidance of anything that reminded him of Reyes. He had deleted every photo from his phone,
unfriended every mutual contact on social media, and stopped answering calls from anyone who had
served with him. He could not bear to hear Reyes’s name spoken aloud. When his sister mentioned him
once, Marco left the dinner table and did not speak to her for a week. He had come to believe that
if he let himself feel the grief, it would swallow him whole, and so he built his life around not
feeling it.

His job as a financial analyst required him to sit in a cubicle under fluorescent lights, and that
was tolerable because the space was enclosed and predictable. But the walk from the parking garage
to the office building was a nightmare. The lot was open to the sky, with no walls, no cover,
nothing between him and the vastness above. Every morning, Marco felt his chest tighten as he
crossed that stretch of asphalt. He walked fast, head down, counting the seconds until he reached
the revolving doors. He had come to believe that in an open space, he could be seen, targeted, hit
from any direction. It made no sense in Dallas, but the feeling was absolute.

He had also stopped talking about the deployment entirely. When a colleague asked him once if he had
served, Marco said no. The lie came easily, but it left a sour taste. He had learned that the moment
he told someone the truth, he would see the pity or the discomfort in their eyes, and he could not
bear to be looked at that way. He was certain that he was permanently different now, that he
belonged to a world civilians could never understand, and that he would always be an outsider in his
own life. The isolation was total.

What frightened Marco most was not the memories themselves, but what they meant about him. He had
believed, before the deployment, that he was a good person. But he had acted in ways that went
against that belief—he had left Reyes, in the end. He had not died in his place. He had dragged
himself to cover while Reyes lay still. The thought that he was a coward, that his survival was a
mistake, had become a fixed point in his mind. He had no future, because he did not deserve one.

He came to therapy because his sister had found him sitting in the dark one night, staring at the
wall, and he had not been able to explain why. He came because he was tired of the fragments, tired
of the open sky, tired of the lie. But what he wanted, more than anything, was to stop feeling like
a ghost in his own body. He was afraid that he could not.
