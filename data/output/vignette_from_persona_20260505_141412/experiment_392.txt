Rajan had worked the night shift at the warehouse for nearly two decades, a schedule that had once
felt as natural as breathing. Now, at sixty-four, the approach of dusk sent a cold dread through his
chest that no amount of logic could dispel. He would stand at the window of his small apartment,
watching the orange light fade, and feel his throat tighten. The same time of day. That was when it
had happened.

Twelve years ago, during a business trip to a rural region for a supplier audit, Rajan had been
taken by a group of men who had mistaken him for a rival. He had been held for six days in a dark,
windowless shed. The men had spoken little, but they had brought a snake into the room on the second
day—a thick, slow-moving python they let coil near his leg while they laughed. He had not moved. He
had not screamed. He had simply frozen, certain that if he flinched, they would let it strike. On
the sixth night, around the same hour the sun was now setting, he had managed to slip through a
loose board and run until his lungs burned. He had never told the full story to anyone, not even his
wife. He had told the police enough, and then he had tried to forget.

But the forgetting never held. At first, it was only the occasional nightmare, a jolt awake in the
dark. Over the years, it had grown into a wall of vigilance that separated him from the world. He
had stopped sleeping in the same bed as his wife, Priya, because lying in the dark made his skin
crawl. He would doze in the living room armchair with the television on, waking at every creak and
groan of the building. Going to sleep had become an ordeal he dreaded more than the exhaustion that
followed. He had come to believe that if he let his guard down—if he let himself be truly
unconscious—something terrible would find him. He was certain that he could not stop bad things from
happening, that his own helplessness was a law of the universe.

At work, he had transferred to the day shift, but the damage was done. He could not walk through the
warehouse aisles without scanning every shadow, every gap between pallets. He constantly checked his
body for signs of injury—a twinge in his knee, a scratch on his arm—as if his own flesh might betray
him with a wound he had not noticed. He frequently looked behind him, sometimes stopping mid-stride
to turn his head, checking if someone was following. His coworkers had noticed. They joked that he
was jumpy. Rajan had stopped eating lunch in the break room because someone had left a nature
magazine open to a page about pythons, and the sight of the photograph had sent him to the bathroom
to vomit.

He had begun to ask Priya the same question every night, sometimes three or four times: "Am I okay?
Tell me I am okay." She would reassure him, her voice patient, but he could see the worry in her
eyes. He knew he was wearing her down. He knew he was becoming someone she had to manage rather than
love. That knowledge sat inside him like a stone. He had started to think, more and more often, that
he was not sure his life was worth living anymore. He did not want to die. He simply could not
imagine how to go on living like this—always watching, always waiting, always afraid of his own
mind.

What finally brought him to the clinic was a night last month when he had woken in the armchair,
drenched in sweat, convinced he could hear the rustle of scales on the floorboards. He had screamed.
Priya had run in, and he had pushed her away, shouting at her to stay back. He had seen the fear on
her face, and then the hurt. The next morning, she had made an appointment for him. He had not
resisted. He was terrified of what he might find in therapy—of what he might have to remember—but he
was more terrified of losing her. He was certain that if he thought about the event, he would not be
able to handle it. But he had come anyway, because the alternative felt like drowning.
