Kai is a 27-year-old non-binary special education teacher who lives with their partner in a mid-
sized city. They came to therapy at the urging of their partner, who had grown worried about the way
Kai’s life had narrowed over the past year. What Kai described, in fragments and false starts, was a
story that began about fourteen months ago, when a former student’s older brother started appearing
outside the school where Kai worked.

The stalking began subtly. At first, Kai thought it was coincidence—seeing the same car parked near
the grocery store, noticing the same figure at the end of the block during their evening walk. But
over three weeks, the pattern became unmistakable. The man would wait outside the school at
dismissal, standing just far enough away that Kai could not be certain he was there for them. He
left notes on Kai’s windshield, cryptic and friendly at first, then increasingly personal. He knew
things: where Kai had lunch, which coffee shop they visited on Saturdays, the route they took to
their partner’s parents’ house. The worst moment came on a Thursday evening in late October. Kai was
walking to their car after a parent-teacher conference when they heard footsteps behind them—steady,
deliberate, matching their pace. They turned. No one was there. But when they reached their car, a
photograph of Kai had been tucked under the windshield wiper, taken from across the street, showing
them standing in their own kitchen.

The police were involved, a restraining order was filed, and the man was arrested for violating it
twice before he finally stopped. But for Kai, the event never really ended. It lived inside them as
a series of disconnected, vivid shards: the sound of gravel crunching underfoot, the cold weight of
the photograph in their hand, the face of a stranger they could never quite see clearly. Some
moments were hyper-detailed—the exact shade of blue of the sky that evening, the smell of damp
leaves—while other parts, like how they got from the parking lot to the front door of their
apartment, were simply gone. Kai described the memory as a puzzle with most of the pieces missing,
and the ones that remained did not fit together in any order that made sense. This fragmentation
frightened them more than almost anything else, because it made them feel as though they could not
trust their own mind to tell them what had really happened.

In the months that followed, Kai’s life became a constant, exhausting process of scanning. They
checked the parking lot before leaving the building, circling the car twice, looking in the back
seat. They scanned every face in every room, every reflection in every window, every set of
footsteps that fell into rhythm with their own. The hypervigilance was not something they chose; it
was automatic, a reflex that had become as natural as breathing. Sudden movements by students in the
hallway—a child darting out from behind a door, a hand reaching toward them from the side—sent Kai’s
heart racing and their vision narrowing. They had learned to keep their back to the wall in the
classroom, to stand where they could see both doors at once. Their partner noticed that Kai no
longer sat on the couch facing away from the window. They no longer wore headphones on their
commute, because they needed to hear what was behind them.

Kai began to avoid relaxation entirely. Evenings at home, once a time to decompress, now felt
dangerous. If they let their guard down, they reasoned, they would not hear the warning signs. So
they stayed busy—grading papers, cleaning, reorganizing the same cabinet three times—anything to
stay alert. They also spent hours ruminating, replaying the early weeks of the stalking in their
mind, trying to find the moment they could have stopped it. If they had parked in a different spot.
If they had told their supervisor sooner. If they had not walked alone that Thursday evening. The
thoughts looped endlessly, offering no answers, only the quiet certainty that they should have
known, should have seen it coming.

The consequences accumulated quietly. Kai’s teaching evaluations, once excellent, slipped as
colleagues noted they seemed “distracted” and “on edge.” Kai had stopped attending the weekly team
meetings where teachers shared strategies, because the conference room had a glass wall and anyone
could see in. Their relationship frayed at the edges; their partner felt shut out, unable to help,
while Kai felt misunderstood and exhausted by the effort of explaining something they themselves
could not fully articulate.

What finally brought Kai to therapy was a morning in late November when they could not bring
themselves to walk into the school building. They stood in the parking lot, engine running, watching
the door, until their partner called to check in. Kai could not explain why they could not go in.
They only knew that the footsteps were still there, even when no one was behind them. They were
hoping, more than anything, to stop feeling like they were still being followed.
