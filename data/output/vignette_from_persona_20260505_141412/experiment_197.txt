Carlos, a sixty-five-year-old special education teacher, had been a medic in the Army during the
Gulf War. The event that now governed his life had not been a firefight, but a heat-stroke
evacuation gone wrong inside a sweltering aid station. He had been helping to stabilize a soldier
with a fever of 105 when the man suddenly seized and stopped breathing. As Carlos worked the bag-
valve mask, he tasted something metallic and sweet—the soldier’s blood from a bitten tongue, mixed
with the chemical tang of the ammonia inhalants he was snapping under the man’s nose. Then the
dizziness hit him, a spinning, tilting vertigo that made the canvas walls of the tent buckle. He
remembered thinking, *I’m going down too*, and the terror was not for the soldier but for
himself—the shame of being a medic who could not hold steady. The soldier died. Carlos was evacuated
for heat exhaustion, but the taste and the dizziness never really left him.

In the decades since, Carlos had built a careful life around those two sensations. He worked as a
special education teacher in a public middle school, a job that required patience, structure, and
proximity to children who sometimes made sudden, loud sounds. He had learned to manage by staying
near the classroom door. Every morning, before the first student arrived, he would walk the
perimeter of the room, checking that the door was unobstructed, that he could step into the hallway
in three seconds. He kept his desk at the back corner, farthest from the windows, so that no one
could approach him from behind. He had come to believe that if he could not see an exit, the
dizziness would return and he would fall, and that this time he would not get back up.

The taste appeared without warning. Sometimes it happened when a student unwrapped a piece of gum,
or when the cafeteria served something with a metallic aftertaste. He would excuse himself, walk
quickly to the staff bathroom, and rinse his mouth with water until the flavor faded. His colleagues
had noticed. The principal had asked, gently, if he was feeling unwell. He had laughed it
off—allergies, he said—but he knew they were watching him differently.

His hypervigilance had become a constant companion. In the hallway between classes, he scanned the
faces of students, the angles of open locker doors, the shadows behind water fountains. He
identified escape routes in every room he entered: the library, the auditorium, the faculty lounge.
He could not sit in a restaurant booth; he needed a table near the door, his back to the wall. When
a fire alarm drill sounded, he felt his heart hammer and his vision narrow, and he had to grip the
doorframe to keep from bolting. He had learned to avoid situations that made him feel strong
emotions—anger, excitement, even joy—because he was certain that any emotional spike would bring
back the dizziness and the taste, and he would lose control.

He had been divorced for fifteen years. His ex-wife had said he was never present, that he was
always looking over her shoulder, always restless. She had been right. He had not dated since. He
lived alone in a small apartment with a clear line of sight to the front door. He did not own a
television because the sudden sounds of action movies or news reports triggered the alertness that
left him exhausted by noon.

What finally brought Carlos to seek help was a morning in late October. A student with autism,
overwhelmed by a math worksheet, had begun to scream—a high, sustained shriek that filled the
classroom. Carlos had frozen, his mouth filling with that old metallic sweetness, his vision
swimming. He had stumbled toward the door, left the room, and stood in the hallway shaking. Another
teacher had to take over. He drove home that afternoon and called the clinic. He was most afraid
that this was not something therapy could fix, that the taste and the dizziness were now part of who
he was. But he was also hoping—quietly, without letting himself believe it—that he might one day sit
through a full meal with a friend, or walk through a crowded hallway without checking every door.
