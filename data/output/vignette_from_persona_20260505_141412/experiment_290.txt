Leilani is a 46-year-old forensic analyst, a woman whose professional life had always been built on
order, pattern recognition, and the quiet certainty of evidence. She came to therapy at the urging
of her husband, who had grown increasingly alarmed by the changes he saw in her over the past eight
months. She sat in the chair across from me with her hands folded tightly in her lap, her gaze fixed
on a spot on the floor. She spoke in a flat, measured tone, as if she were delivering a lab report,
but her voice dropped to a whisper when she reached the part she had been dreading to say aloud.

The traumatic event occurred on a Tuesday afternoon in early spring. Leilani’s younger brother, Koa,
had been a commercial diver, a man who spent his life working in the deep, dark waters off the coast
of Alaska. He had called her the night before, laughing about a seal that had stolen his glove, and
she had laughed too, telling him to be careful. The next day, a supervisor called her at her desk.
There had been an accident. A sudden equipment failure during a deep-water repair. Koa was gone
before anyone could reach him. Leilani did not see his body. She did not hear his voice again. What
she did hear, and what she could not stop hearing, was the sound of the supervisor’s voice on the
phone—the way his words had stopped and started, the hollow silence between each sentence. That
voice lived inside her now. It would return without warning, usually at night when the house was
quiet, and she would hear it again, word for word, as if the call were happening in real time. When
the memory came, it was not a story with a beginning and an end. It was a snapshot: the grey
afternoon light through her office window, the phone pressed to her ear, the supervisor’s voice
saying *there’s no easy way to say this*. That single image, frozen and unbearable, would flood her
with a wave of emotion so overwhelming that she had to stop whatever she was doing—sometimes mid-
sentence, sometimes mid-step—and press her palms against her eyes until it passed.

In the weeks that followed, Leilani’s world contracted. She had always taken pride in her work, in
the meticulous analysis of digital evidence that helped build cases, but now the thought of sitting
at her desk made her chest tighten. She began to avoid it. She would arrive late, leave early, or
call in sick on days when she knew a case file would require sustained concentration. Her supervisor
noticed. A colleague had to take over a major project. Leilani told herself it was just a temporary
thing, that she needed a break, but she knew, deep down, that she was afraid. Afraid that the phone
would ring again. Afraid that the snapshot would return while she was trying to focus. She stopped
eating properly. She would go the entire day on black coffee, telling herself she wasn’t hungry, and
then at night, when the house was finally still, she would stay awake until two or three in the
morning, scrolling through news articles about diving accidents, reading comments from strangers,
searching for something she could not name. Her husband would find her in the living room, the
laptop glowing in the dark, and she would close it quickly, saying she couldn’t sleep.

What frightened Leilani most was not the memory itself, but what it had done to her sense of the
world. She had lost her faith. Not in God—she had never been particularly religious—but in the basic
assumption that life made sense. She had believed, without ever saying it aloud, that if you worked
hard and loved your people and stayed careful, you would be safe. Koa’s death had shattered that
belief. Now she saw the world as a place where the worst thing could happen at any moment, for no
reason, and there was nothing you could do to stop it. Nothing in her life felt meaningful anymore.
She had stopped looking forward to things. She went through the motions at work, at home, in
conversations with her husband, but she felt hollow, as if the person she used to be had been
erased. She said to me, in a voice that was barely audible, “My life has been destroyed by this. I
don’t know what’s left.”

She came to therapy because her husband had told her, gently but firmly, that he could not watch her
disappear any longer. She was not sure she wanted to get better. She was not sure she deserved to.
But she was tired—so tired of the snapshots and the voice and the long, sleepless hours—and she was
afraid that if she did not try something, she would lose what little she had left. What she hoped
for, she said, was not to forget Koa, but to stop feeling like his death had taken her with him.
