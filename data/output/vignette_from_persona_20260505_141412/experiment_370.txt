Connor was nineteen, a counselor at a residential treatment center for adolescents, and he had been
with his girlfriend, Maya, for just over a year when his life was interrupted by a fever that would
not break. What started as a persistent flu-like illness escalated over three days into septic
shock, a life-threatening cascade that landed him in the intensive care unit for a week. He
remembered the moment the emergency room doctor inserted the first IV—a thick-gauge needle sliding
into the crook of his arm—and the way his mother’s face had crumpled when the crash cart was wheeled
in. He remembered the antiseptic smell that clung to every breath, the beeping of monitors that
seemed to count down rather than count up, and the terrifying sensation of his own body turning
against him: his heart racing uncontrollably, his skin mottling, his limbs trembling as if they
belonged to someone else. The medical team had saved his life, but Connor had come to believe that
his body was no longer his ally. It had betrayed him once, and he was certain it would do so again.

In the months following his discharge, Connor returned to work, but the treatment center became a
minefield. The building smelled faintly of cleaning solution, a scent that triggered a visceral
lurch in his stomach and a sudden, cold sweat. He began to avoid the infirmary entirely, taking
circuitous routes through the hallways to bypass it. When a client mentioned a routine blood draw,
Connor felt his own pulse spike, his chest tightening as if the needle were approaching his own arm.
He started staying late at the office, not out of dedication, but because he could not bring himself
to go home and lie still in the dark. Sleep had become a battleground: every time he began to drift
off, a sudden movement from Maya beside him—a shift, a sigh—would jolt him upright, his heart
hammering, his hands shaking, convinced for a split second that he was back in the ICU, that a nurse
was about to insert another line. He would stay awake until two or three in the morning, scrolling
through his phone, delaying the inevitable moment when he would have to surrender to a body he no
longer trusted.

His relationship with Maya began to fray. She would reach for him in bed, and he would flinch,
startling at her touch. He could see the hurt in her eyes, but he could not explain that his body
was reacting to a threat that had already passed, that it treated every unexpected movement as a
potential assault. He had come to believe that nothing in his life felt meaningful anymore. The work
he had once loved—helping teenagers navigate their own traumas—now felt hollow. He found himself
avoiding any discussion of therapy content with his colleagues, steering conversations away from
clinical topics, because talking about fear or helplessness brought him too close to his own. He
stopped attending supervision meetings, claiming scheduling conflicts, because the mere mention of a
patient’s medical history sent his heart racing.

What finally brought Connor to seek help was a single incident during a routine physical exam
required for his job. The doctor had barely touched his arm with a stethoscope, and Connor had
jerked away, his breath coming in gasps, his vision tunneling. He had excused himself to the
bathroom and stood gripping the sink, staring at his own pale face in the mirror, and felt a
profound, sickening certainty: his body was not just unreliable—it was his enemy. It kept reacting
as if he were still in that hospital bed, still fighting for his life, even though he was safe. He
could not keep living this way, jumping at shadows, avoiding his own girlfriend’s touch, dreading

lose his job, his relationship, and any sense of a future. He hoped, vaguely, that talking about it
might help him reclaim a life that felt like his own again.
