Valeria was twenty-one when the bomb went off in the food court. She had been on her lunch break,
standing in line at the taco stand, when the pressure wave threw her against a concrete pillar. She
remembers the sound—not as an explosion, but as a physical force that erased everything else—and
then the dust, so thick she could taste metal. What she cannot remember, no matter how hard she
tries, is the next several minutes. She knows from news reports that people were running, that first
responders arrived, that she was found sitting on the floor with her hands over her ears. But that
part of her own life is a blank, a torn-out page. When she tries to describe what happened to
anyone, the words simply will not come; she knows the facts, can see the images in her mind, but her
throat closes and her mouth goes dry as though the event itself has stolen her language.

In the weeks afterward, Valeria returned to her job at the warehouse. She had always been a quiet,
focused worker, but now her focus took on a different quality. She began to notice things she had
never noticed before: the way a coworker would lift a box too quickly, the sudden pivot of a
forklift, the sharp clang of metal shelving being loaded. Each sudden movement sent a jolt through
her chest, a spike of alarm that left her hands trembling. She started scanning the warehouse floor
constantly, her eyes moving from one corner to the next, checking for anything out of place—a pallet
left in an aisle, a loose strap, a person approaching from behind. She had come to believe that the
world was a fundamentally dangerous place, that danger could arrive without warning, and that the
only way to survive was to be ready at all times. This vigilance exhausted her, but she could not
stop.

She also found herself avoiding eye contact. At first it was subtle—she would look at her
supervisor’s shoulder instead of his face, at the label on a box instead of a coworker’s eyes. But
over time it became automatic. Looking someone in the eyes felt like exposure, like letting them see
that she was fragile, that she was waiting for something terrible to happen. She was certain that if
people saw her fear, they would use it against her, or worse, that they would confirm what she
already suspected: that she could no longer trust herself. Her own body had betrayed her during the
attack—it had frozen, it had failed to run, it had sat uselessly on a dirty floor while others fled.
She now believed that her body was unreliable, that she could not count on it to protect her, and
that she had to monitor it constantly for signs of weakness.

At night, the vigilance sharpened. Valeria found it harder to feel safe after dark. She checked the
locks on her apartment door three times before bed. She kept a light on in the hallway. She lay
awake listening to the building’s sounds—the groan of pipes, the footsteps of a neighbor—each noise
a potential threat. Sometimes, when the anxiety became unbearable, she deliberately tried to leave
her own body, to blank out, to float away from the fear. It was the only way she knew to escape when
there was nowhere to run. But the dissociation left her feeling hollow and disconnected, as though
she were watching her own life from a great distance.

When she did remember the event—and she could not always control when it came—what surfaced most was
not fear but rage. A hot, wordless fury at the people who had done this, at the randomness of it, at
the fact that her life had been split into before and after. The rage frightened her because it felt
as uncontrollable as the explosion itself. She had no outlet for it, no way to speak it, because she
could not find the words.

What finally brought Valeria to seek help was not the anger or the scanning or the sleepless nights.
It was the eye contact. A supervisor had pulled her aside and asked why she would not look at
anyone. He said it seemed like she was hiding something, or that she did not care. Valeria realized
that her survival strategy—her constant scanning, her refusal to meet eyes—was being read as
hostility or indifference. She was afraid of losing her job. But more than that, she was afraid that
she was becoming someone she did not recognize: a person who could not trust, could not speak, could
not rest. She wanted to feel safe again, or at least to stop feeling like a target every moment of
every day. She was not sure if that was possible. But she was willing to try.
