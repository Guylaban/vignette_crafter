Margaret was seventy-three years old, a painter who had lived alone in her small house on the
outskirts of a Midwestern town since her husband Thomas died six years earlier. The house was still
full of his presence—his worn leather armchair in the corner of the living room, the half-finished
model ship on the shelf in the study—but she had learned to live around the silence. What she had
not learned to live around was the memory of the night, eighteen months ago now, when a gas main
ruptured two blocks from her home and the explosion tore through the neighborhood. She had been
standing at her kitchen sink, washing a brush under the tap, when the windows blew inward and the
floor lurched beneath her feet. The sound was not a sound she could describe to anyone; it was more
like the world itself had split open. She had fallen sideways, her shoulder striking the counter,
and for a long moment she lay there, pinned by the dark and the ringing in her ears, certain that
the house was about to collapse on top of her. She had not been physically injured beyond a deep
bruise that faded over weeks, but something else had not faded.

What stayed with her, in the months that followed, was not the noise or the shaking but the
stillness that came after, when she had crawled to the front door and opened it and seen the orange
glow at the end of the street and smelled the alcohol-thick odor of gas and burning. That smell was
everywhere now, it seemed. She caught it on the breath of a man at the grocery store, on the coat of
a neighbor who had been having a beer in his garage, and each time her chest would tighten and her
throat would close and she would have to leave wherever she was, quickly, without explanation. She
had stopped going to the grocery store altogether, in fact. Her daughter brought her supplies once a
week and left them on the porch. Margaret did not like to be touched anymore, not even a hand on her
shoulder, because when someone touched her she felt her body lock into the same position it had been
in that night—twisted, braced, waiting for the next blow. She had told her daughter that she was
fine, that she was just getting older and more set in her ways, but she knew it was not true.

The memories came like photographs, sharp and still and perfectly detailed. She could see the cracks
spreading across her kitchen ceiling, the way the coffee cup had tipped and spilled its brown arc
across the white counter, the exact angle of the knife block as it slid and clattered to the floor.
She could see these images at any moment, without warning, and when they came she felt her body
arrange itself into the same posture she had held on the kitchen floor: one arm pinned beneath her,
her head turned to the side, her knees drawn up. She would sit like that on her couch, frozen, until
the image faded, and then she would feel a wave of guilt so heavy it pressed her down into the
cushions. She had not done anything wrong that night—she knew that rationally—but the guilt was not
rational. It was a feeling that she should have done something, that she should have known, that she
should have been able to protect the house that Thomas had built for her, that she had failed him
somehow by surviving it.

She had stopped watching the news entirely. Any report of a fire, an explosion, a disaster in
another city sent her heart racing and her mind skittering back to that night. She kept the
television off and the radio off and she did not read the newspaper that her daughter left on the
porch. Instead, she filled her days with work. She painted—landscapes mostly, soft and blurred and
safe—and when she was not painting she cleaned, or she sorted through old boxes in the basement, or
she walked in circles around her small backyard. She kept her mind occupied all the time,
deliberately, because the moment she let it rest the photographs would slide back in. She tried not
to think about the explosion at all, but the effort of not thinking exhausted her. At night, she lay
awake listening to the creaks and settling of the house, certain that each sound was a footstep, a
hand on the doorknob, someone entering. She did not sleep more than a few hours at a time, and when
she woke she was irritable, snapping at her daughter on the phone, angry at the mailman for leaving
the gate open, angry at herself for being angry.

She had come to believe, slowly and quietly, that she would never feel normal again. The emotions
she used to have—the quiet pleasure of a morning coffee, the satisfaction of finishing a painting,
the warmth of remembering Thomas’s laugh—had gone somewhere she could not reach. She was not sure
her life was worth living anymore, and that thought frightened her, but it also felt honest. What
finally brought her to seek help was not the fear or the guilt or the sleeplessness, but the moment

she had been working on sat unfinished on their easels, and when she looked at them she felt
nothing. She called her daughter and said, in a voice that barely carried, that she thought she
needed to talk to someone. She did not know if talking could help. She was afraid it could not. But
she was more afraid of what it meant that she no longer wanted to paint.
