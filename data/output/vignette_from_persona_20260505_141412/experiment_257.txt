Martha is a sixty-eight-year-old married woman who came to therapy at her daughter’s urging, though
she herself is not certain what talking can do now. The event that reshaped her life happened nearly
forty years ago, during her first marriage, when she was a young woman living in a small apartment
with a man who was charming in public and unpredictable behind closed doors. The physical abuse was
not constant, but it was severe when it came, and it always followed the same pattern: a period of
tension, then an explosion over something small—a meal not ready on time, a question he considered
disrespectful—and then his hands around her throat, or the back of his hand across her face, or her
body shoved against the kitchen counter. She left him finally, but the leaving did not end what he
had done.

What Martha describes now is a memory that does not behave. It arrives without warning, triggered by
something as ordinary as the smell of damp soil when she walks past a garden center or steps onto
the grass after rain. That smell—earthy, slightly metallic—was the smell of the fire escape landing
where she once curled up after a beating, waiting for the pain in her ribs to subside before she
could stand. She will be in the grocery store parking lot, and a whiff of turned earth will bring
the whole scene back: the cold metal against her back, the taste of blood, the sound of his voice
from inside the apartment. The memory forces its way in before she can stop it, and she finds
herself pushing it away before it fully arrives, cutting it off mid-scene, leaving herself with a
jolt of adrenaline and no clear memory of what she just blocked. She has learned to keep her mind
occupied at all times—counting steps, reciting song lyrics, mentally organizing her daughter’s
schedule—so that there is no empty space for the memory to slip into.

Martha’s husband now is a gentle man, and they have been married for thirty years. She loves him.
But she cannot sleep beside him. She has not shared a bed with anyone since the abuse, because
falling asleep requires her to lower her guard, and she cannot do that with another person in the
room. She sleeps on a small couch in the living room, fully dressed, with the television on low.
Even alone, sleep is difficult: she lies awake for hours, listening to the house settle, alert to
any creak or shift. She has come to believe that safety is something that must be actively
maintained, and that the moment she stops paying attention, something will find her. She feels on
guard inside her own home, even in the daytime, even with her husband in the next room. There are
only two places where the vigilance eases slightly: her daughter’s kitchen, where she drinks tea at
a specific chair with her back to the wall, and the small bench at the end of her own garden, where
she can see all approaches. Everywhere else feels exposed.

To manage this constant sense of threat, Martha has developed a way of moving through the world that
is almost invisible to others. She agrees with whatever anyone wants, because disagreeing feels too
dangerous—she has come to believe that conflict, even mild disagreement, could escalate
unpredictably, though she knows rationally that her husband would never hurt her. She says yes to
invitations she does not want, eats food she does not like, stays late at gatherings she wishes she
could leave, because the alternative—asserting a preference—makes her chest tighten with a fear she
cannot explain. She also keeps busy constantly. Her days are filled with small tasks: folding
laundry, wiping counters, organizing drawers, sweeping floors that are already clean. She cannot sit
still for more than a few minutes without the restlessness building, without the sense that she is
about to be caught off guard. The busyness has consequences: she is exhausted by mid-afternoon, and
her husband has gently suggested she might rest, but she cannot explain to him that rest feels like
vulnerability.

What finally brought Martha to seek help was not her own distress but a practical problem. Her
daughter, who lives two hours away, is expecting a baby and has asked Martha to stay for a week
after the birth. Martha wants to go. She loves her daughter and wants to be present. But the thought
of sleeping in an unfamiliar house, in a guest room alone, has filled her with a dread she cannot
talk about. She is afraid of the night there—the strange sounds, the unknown layout, the feeling of
being unmoored from her small circle of safe places. She is also afraid of what will happen if she
says no: her daughter will be hurt, and Martha will have to explain something she has never spoken
aloud. She came to therapy hoping, she says, to find a way to get through that week. She is not sure
she believes it is possible, but she is willing to try.
