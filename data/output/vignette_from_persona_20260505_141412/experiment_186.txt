Kofi was twenty-three years old and working as a freelance journalist in a mid-sized American city
when he was pulled over during a routine traffic stop that became anything but routine. He had been
driving home late from covering a city council meeting, his laptop bag on the passenger seat, a
half-finished article about housing policy still open on his phone. The officer approached his
window and asked for his license and registration. Kofi reached for the glove compartment, and in
that moment—he still could not fully explain why—the officer drew his weapon and ordered him out of
the car at gunpoint. Kofi was forced to the ground, his hands cuffed behind his back, his face
pressed into the cold asphalt while the officer shouted questions he could not answer coherently.
The whole incident lasted perhaps twelve minutes. He was released with a warning. But those twelve
minutes had rewritten everything.

What stayed with Kofi most vividly was not the visual image of the gun, though that was burned into
his mind. It was the sound. The officer’s boots on the pavement as he approached. The sharp metallic
click of the handcuffs. And later, when he tried to describe it to his girlfriend, he found he could
not get the words out. He would start the story, reach the part where the officer’s voice changed,
and then the thread would vanish. His mouth would go dry. He would lose the sentence entirely and
have to stop. The memory itself, however, never lost its vividness. It felt more real than anything
that had happened since. Sometimes, without warning, he would be standing at the kitchen counter
making coffee and he would hear the officer’s footsteps again, clear as day, coming up behind him
from the hallway. He would spin around, heart hammering, only to find an empty corridor.

In the months that followed, Kofi’s life contracted. He had always been someone who moved through
the world with ease—a journalist who loved the unpredictability of the streets, the hum of a new
neighborhood, the challenge of finding a story in an unfamiliar place. Now, he found himself
avoiding unfamiliar neighborhoods entirely. He could not assess the danger in a place he did not
already know, and that uncertainty felt intolerable. He began checking behind him constantly,
especially when walking to his car or waiting for the subway. He started wearing hooded sweatshirts
and dark, shapeless coats—clothing that let him blend in, that made him harder to notice. He told
himself it was practical. But he knew, deep down, that he was trying to become invisible.

His work suffered. He stopped taking assignments that required him to go to parts of the city he had
not scouted in advance. He turned down a story about police accountability—the irony was not lost on
him—because the thought of sitting in a precinct and interviewing officers made his chest tighten
until he could barely breathe. He had difficulty concentrating during interviews now; his mind would
drift to the sound of footsteps in the hallway outside the room, and he would miss what the source
was saying. His editor noticed. His girlfriend noticed. He noticed most of all.

He had come to believe, quietly and without ever saying it aloud, that his life had been destroyed
by those twelve minutes. He used to be a happy person. He remembered that version of himself the way
you remember a photograph of someone you used to know. That person was gone. He was no longer
capable of doing the things that had defined him—the curiosity, the courage, the willingness to go
anywhere for a story. He avoided anything that might make him feel, whether positive or negative,
because feelings felt dangerous now. He even avoided songs that reminded him of a friend he had lost
years ago, not because the grief was fresh, but because any emotional intensity felt like a door he
did not want to open.

What finally brought Kofi to seek help was a moment that frightened him more than the traffic stop
itself. He was at a stoplight, his girlfriend in the passenger seat, when a police cruiser pulled up
alongside them. The officer glanced over. Kofi’s hands went numb on the wheel. He heard boots on
pavement, a voice shouting, the click of metal. He pulled over and began to cry, and he could not
explain to his girlfriend why. He could not explain to himself. He came to therapy because he was
afraid of what he might do next time—not to someone else, but to himself, to the life he was still
trying to hold together. He wanted to know if the person he used to be was still in there somewhere,
or if that person had been erased for good.
