Ramesh sat across from me with the stillness of a man who had learned to be invisible. At seventy-
five, his hands bore the permanent grey of clay worked into the creases, but they rested motionless
on his knees, palms up, as though waiting for something to be placed into them. He had come to the
clinic at his wife’s insistence, though he could not say what he hoped for. What he could say,
haltingly, was that for the past several months he had felt as though he no longer existed as the
person he had been. He was a potter—had been a potter since he was twelve years old in Sri Lanka,
apprenticed to his uncle in a village north of Jaffna. He had built a life in the United States over
thirty years, had raised children here, had taught ceramics at a community center. But now, he said,
he could not bring himself to touch the wheel. The clay sat in bags in his garage, hardening. When
his wife asked why he no longer worked, he told her he did not have the strength. What he meant was
that he no longer knew who would sit down at that wheel.

The event that had undone him happened in 1983, during the Black July pogroms. Ramesh had been a
young man then, living with his parents and younger sister in a mixed neighborhood. He remembered
the sound first—the crackle of burning shops, the shouting that moved like a wave through the
streets. He remembered the police truck that pulled up, and the officer who got out and looked at
them not with anger but with something worse, a kind of recognition. Ramesh had been told to lie
face down on the ground, and he had done so, and he had listened to his sister being pulled from the
house. He had not moved. He had not moved because he believed that if he stayed still, if he made
himself small and obedient, they would leave and everything would be fine. His sister had been
seventeen. He never saw her again. For forty years he had told himself this story in a way that made
it bearable: he had survived, he had built a new life, the past was the past. But in the last year,
something had broken. He had attended a funeral for an old friend, and standing at the graveside,
the smell of wet earth had hit him with a force that buckled his knees. The memory came not as a
story but as a physical assault—the grit of dirt against his cheek, the weight of a boot on his
spine, the sound of his sister’s voice calling his name. He could not speak of it afterward. When
his wife asked what was wrong, he could only shake his head. The words would not come. The memory
existed in his body, in his senses, but it had no language.

Since that funeral, Ramesh had become a man who watched. He scanned every room he entered, every
street he walked, every parking lot. He checked the faces of passing drivers, the posture of
strangers, the direction of their hands. He was certain that danger could arrive without warning,
and that only constant vigilance would keep it at bay. He had stopped driving altogether because the
sound of a car backfiring, or even a loud engine, sent his heart into his throat and left him
shaking in his seat. He avoided the neighborhood where the Sri Lankan temple was located because he
had seen a police car there one afternoon and had turned around and walked home without knowing why,
only knowing that he could not go closer. He had also begun to avoid anything that might make him
feel hope. When his grandson invited him to a school art show, Ramesh declined. When his wife
suggested they take a weekend trip to the mountains, he said he was too tired. He had come to
believe that any moment of happiness was a trap, a vulnerability that would be punished. Better to
stay flat, to feel nothing, to keep the world at arm’s length. The only thing he allowed himself was
the scanning—the endless, exhausting search for the threat that was surely coming.

His wife had noticed the changes. He no longer laughed at her jokes. He flinched when she touched
his shoulder from behind. He had started sleeping in the guest room because he could not bear the

know. But I have to watch.” It was this that finally brought them here. Ramesh did not believe that
talking would help. He was certain that he had already failed the only test that had ever mattered,

earth. He did not know if such a thing was possible, but he had come anyway, and that, for a man who
had spent forty years learning not to hope, was perhaps the bravest thing he had done in decades.
