temper. What she hadn’t known, not until she was seven years old, was what it looked like when that
temper turned physical. She remembered the night precisely — the way the ceiling fan clicked
rhythmically overhead, the smell of fried fish still clinging to the kitchen curtains, the sound of
her mother’s body hitting the linoleum floor. Her father had been shouting about something Lena no
longer recalled, and then suddenly her mother was on the ground, and Lena was standing in the
doorway, frozen, watching. She had not screamed or run for help. She had simply stood there, a small
girl in a cotton nightgown, and watched. And in the years that followed, a quiet certainty had
settled into her bones: that something about her, something she could not name, had made it happen.
That she had been betrayed not only by her father, who was supposed to protect them, but also by her
mother, who had never spoken of it afterward, who had cleaned the blood from her own lip and acted
as though nothing had occurred.

lighting technician she had met during a rehearsal five years ago. She danced professionally for a
contemporary company, and on good days the studio felt like the only place where her body belonged
to her. But on bad days, the studio became a trap. The mirrors that lined three walls meant she was
always being watched — by herself, by the choreographer, by the other dancers. The sensation of eyes
on her skin made her shoulders climb toward her ears, made her movements small and tight. She had
come to believe that people were not what they seemed, that a colleague who smiled at her during
warm-up might say something cutting behind her back, that her husband, who had never raised his
voice, might one day turn into someone she did not recognize. She did not tell him this. She could
not find the words.

across the studio, and Lena’s chest would seize — the name itself, the sound of it, the shape of the
syllables. She had no Maria in her life now, but the name belonged to her mother. Hearing it was
like being seven again, standing in the doorway. Other times, she would be stretching on the floor
after a difficult run of a piece, and her heart would be pounding from exertion, and suddenly the
pounding was not exertion anymore — it was fear, pure and animal, and she would have to press her
palms flat against the wooden floor to remind herself she was not falling. Sometimes the memories
came without any warning at all. She could be buying groceries, reaching for a can of coconut milk,

feel nothing. Absolutely nothing. As if the memory belonged to a stranger, a different little girl

present, but not really there. She watched him from a distance, wondering when he would reveal

was broken beyond repair. But she was also, somewhere underneath that fear, hoping that a stranger
might finally tell her the truth about what had happened to that little girl in the doorway.
