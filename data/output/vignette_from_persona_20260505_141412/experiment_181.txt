paramedic on scene after a multi-car collision on the interstate, and he had recognized the car. It

wreckage, working with a calm he no longer recognizes in himself, applying pressure to the wounds
that would not stop bleeding. He remembers the antiseptic smell of the hospital bay where he finally
handed him off to the trauma team, the sharp chemical scent that now seems to follow him everywhere.

brother-in-law would survive. What Amir cannot remember, no matter how hard he tries, are the
minutes between recognizing the car and getting him onto the stretcher. There are gaps in his memory
that feel like missing pieces of himself, and when the memory does come back—which it does, without

he is still there on the asphalt with blood on his gloves. The terror that grips him in those
moments is the same terror he felt then, undiminished by time.

In the weeks and months that followed, Amir became convinced that he had permanently changed for the
worse. He could feel it in his bones. Before the accident, he had trusted his instincts, his hands,
his ability to stay calm under pressure. Now he cannot rely on himself. He replays the scene
obsessively, searching for something he could have done differently, and the conclusion is always
the same: he should have protected his brother-in-law, and he failed. This belief has settled into
him like a weight. He tries hard not to think about it, pushing the memories away when they surface,
but suppression only seems to make them return more forcefully. So he has adopted another strategy:
he throws himself into work. He volunteers for extra shifts, takes the most difficult calls, fills

child’s arm, a wound being bandaged—send him reeling. He sees the faces of victims he has treated,
unbidden, at the worst possible moments. And the smell of the hospital, the antiseptic that hangs in
every corridor, has become a permanent part of his sensory landscape, a reminder he cannot escape.
