radiologist’s quiet, rehearsed tone, in the pause before he said “malignancy,” in the way the paper
gown crinkled against her skin as she sat frozen on the examination table. Margaret was sixty-one
then, a project manager who had spent thirty years orchestrating timelines, budgets, and cross-
functional teams. She had never been someone who fell apart. But the diagnosis—stage II breast
cancer—did not simply threaten her life. It dismantled the architecture of trust she had placed in
her own body. The chemotherapy cycles that followed were not just treatments; they were a series of
invasions. Needles found her veins with mechanical precision, and each time a nurse swabbed her arm
with alcohol, Margaret would close her eyes and feel herself back on that table, the radiologist’s
words still echoing, her body suddenly a foreign, treacherous thing.

Two years later, Margaret’s hair has grown back, her scans are clear, and her oncologist has used
the word “remission.” But she does not feel healed. She feels occupied. At work, she still manages
complex deliverables, but her concentration has frayed like old fabric. During status meetings, her
mind will snag on a colleague’s casual mention of “surgery dates” or “IV drips” for a project
timeline, and suddenly she is no longer in the conference room—she is in the infusion suite, the
cold drip entering her arm, the chemical taste flooding her mouth. The memory does not feel like a
memory. It feels like a full-sensory reenactment: the antiseptic smell, the fluorescent hum, the
precise angle of her arm on the armrest. She has learned to steer conversations away from medicine,
from hospitals, from anyone’s health updates. But steering requires vigilance, and vigilance
exhausts her.

At home, her husband, David, has learned not to touch her unexpectedly. Even a gentle hand on her
shoulder while she is washing dishes makes her flinch, her heart hammering as though she has been
attacked. She knows this reaction puzzles him, and she has stopped trying to explain it. What would
she say? That when his hand lands on her back, she feels the pressure of the biopsy needle again?
That her body remembers positions she cannot put into words? She has tried, once or twice, to
describe what it was like—the terror of waiting, the shame of losing her breast, the way her body
felt like a broken machine that had betrayed her. But the words come out flat, incomplete. The event
itself lives in her muscles and her skin, not in language.

Margaret has come to believe that her life has been destroyed. Not ended, but hollowed out. The
woman she was before—the one who trusted her own stamina, who felt at home in her own skin—is gone.
She feels ashamed of what her body became: scarred, weak, unreliable. She no longer trusts it to
carry her through a workday without a sudden wave of dizziness or a flash of intrusive heat. She
avoids annual physicals. She has rescheduled her mammogram three times. The thought of lying on
another table, of a machine pressing against her chest, sends her into a cold sweat that lasts for
hours.

What finally brought Margaret to seek help was not a crisis, but a quiet collapse. Last week, during
a routine project review, a junior analyst mentioned the word “biopsy” in reference to a tissue
sample for a materials test. Margaret’s vision tunneled. She excused herself, walked to the
bathroom, and stood gripping the sink, her body locked in the exact position she had held during her

returned to the meeting, she smiled and said she had felt faint. But that night, she told David she

end. She is afraid of what she might lose if she stays stuck—her marriage, her career, her capacity
for joy. But she is also afraid of what it might cost to truly feel what happened. She hopes,
hesitantly, that there is a way to reclaim her body from the memory that has claimed it.
