Mai had never thought of herself as someone who could be frightened. At seventy-six, she had spent
more than fifty years working as a mechanic, her hands stained with grease, her back bent over
engines that others could not fix. She had raised three children in a small house outside Portland,
had buried her mother, had watched her husband retire and then un-retire because he could not sit
still. Fear was not something she had made room for. But that was before the man in the white sedan
began parking outside her shop.

It started with small things. A coffee cup left on the hood of her car. A note tucked under her
windshield wiper that said only her name. Then the calls at night, breathing and nothing else. Mai
told herself it was nothing, that the world was full of troubled people, that she was not the kind
of woman who needed to be afraid. But her body did not listen. She began to notice smells that
should not have been there—damp soil, wet grass, the particular heaviness of air after rain—and each
time her stomach turned cold. These smells followed her: in the alley behind the shop, in her own
garden when she tried to water the tomatoes, even once in her kitchen when her husband came in from
mowing the lawn. She could not explain why they frightened her. She only knew that they did.

The memory of the worst night came to her in fragments that refused to form a whole story. She
remembered the photograph perfectly—a single image, frozen and clear, as if someone had pressed
pause on a movie. It was the man’s face, inches from her car window, his expression flat and
unreadable, the streetlight catching the sweat on his forehead. She could see that photograph in her
mind at any moment, sharp and complete. But when she tried to tell her daughter what had happened
next, the words collapsed. She would start, and then the thread would vanish, and she would find
herself staring at the wall, unable to finish a single sentence. She had learned to stop trying. She
pushed the memory away before it could fully arrive, like slamming a door on someone’s fingers.

Over the months that followed, Mai’s world shrank. She stopped walking to the corner store. She
stopped sitting in her backyard. She became watchful in a way that exhausted her—scanning every
parking lot, every rearview mirror, every shadow that moved at the edge of her vision. She looked
behind her constantly, a quick turn of the head that her husband called a nervous habit but that she
knew was a necessity. Even inside her own home, she could not let her guard down. She locked doors
she had never locked before. She checked the locks twice. She sat with her back to the wall during
dinner, though she did not tell her husband why.

The worst part, the part she could not admit to anyone, was that happiness had become dangerous.
When she felt relaxed—when a customer smiled at her work, when her granddaughter laughed on the
phone—a spike of fear would pierce the moment. She had come to believe that feeling good was a trap,
that the moment she let her guard down, something terrible would find her again. So she stayed
tense. It felt safer.

Her work suffered. She had always been fast, but now she double-checked every bolt, every seal, and
still she found herself losing focus when a customer walked in unexpectedly. Her boss said nothing,
but she saw him watching her. She had always been the one everyone relied on. Now she felt like a
woman holding a cup of water that was about to spill.

What finally brought her to a clinic was a single afternoon in late October. She had been standing
under a customer’s car, tightening an oil pan, when she smelled soil—fresh, damp, unmistakable. Her
hands went numb. She dropped the wrench. She lay on the concrete floor, staring up at the
undercarriage, unable to move, until her coworker found her and helped her sit up. That night, her
husband held her hand and told her she needed to talk to someone. She did not argue. She was tired
of being afraid of the smell of earth. She was tired of being afraid of her own relief. She wanted
to tell the story from beginning to end, without losing the thread, without slamming the door. She
wanted to finish a sentence about that night and let it be over.
