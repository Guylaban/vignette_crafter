The trauma that still governs Thien’s life happened twenty-two years ago, during a deployment in a
dense, humid region he has never named aloud to anyone except his wife, and even then only in
fragments. He was a radio operator, and his unit was ambushed while moving through a narrow valley
at dusk. What he remembers most vividly is not the gunfire itself but the smell—the thick, sweet rot
of wet earth mixed with diesel fuel and something metallic, the smell of the river that ran
alongside the road and the smoke from a burning supply truck. That smell comes back to him without
warning. It arrives in his kitchen when his wife fries shallots in oil, or in the parking lot of his
office building after a rain shower, and when it does, he hears the sounds again: the flat crack of
small-arms fire, the urgent shouting, the high-pitched whine of a motorcycle engine that belonged to
one of the scouts who was hit. That particular sound—a two-stroke engine revving—has become a kind
of auditory trap. A motorcycle passing his house on the main road will stop him mid-sentence. He
will freeze, and for a few seconds he is not in his living room but back in that valley, watching
the scout fall.

Thien cannot put the event into words. When his therapist asks him to describe what happened, he
shakes his head. The memory is not a story he can tell; it is a film that plays over and over,
always the same scenes, always without language. He sees the mud on his hands, the way the light
shifted through the canopy, the scout’s helmet rolling sideways. But he cannot narrate it. The
images and sounds and smells arrive whole, and they arrive without his permission.

For years, Thien managed. He worked, he married, he raised two children. But after a company-wide
restructuring two years ago, his avoidance tightened like a vise. He stopped watching television
altogether—not just news or war films, but any programme that might contain a sudden loud noise or a
scene of violence. He stopped reading novels. He stopped going out. He used to run three times a
week, a loop through the park near his home, but one morning a motorcycle backfired on the street
beside him and he spent the next hour on a bench, shaking, unable to stand. He has not exercised
since. Running, which once cleared his mind, now feels like exposure.

At home, Thien is never fully off guard. He sits with his back to the wall. He checks the locks
three times before bed. He has come to believe that strangers are a danger he cannot assess, so he
avoids unfamiliar places entirely. He drives the same route to work every day. He eats lunch at his
desk. When a new hire in his team—a young man with a loud voice and an unpredictable laugh—walked up
to his cubicle to introduce himself, Thien felt his chest tighten and his vision narrow. He knew,
intellectually, that the man was harmless. But his body did not believe it. He excused himself to
the bathroom and stood in a stall for ten minutes, breathing slowly, waiting for the feeling to
pass.

The most difficult trigger, and the one he finds most shameful, is being naked. He cannot explain
why. There is no logical connection between the ambush and his own bare skin. But undressing for a
shower, or standing in the bedroom with his clothes off, makes him feel exposed in a way that is
intolerable. He has begun to shower with the bathroom door locked and the light off, as if darkness
might protect him. His wife has noticed. She has asked him, gently, what is wrong. He has not told
her.

What finally brought Thien to seek help was a panic attack in the grocery store. A shopper walked
past him carrying a bag of charcoal, and the smell—smoke and lighter fluid and something
organic—sent him reeling. He dropped his basket. He left the cart in the aisle and walked out into
the parking lot, and he stood there, in the rain, unable to go back inside. He drove home and sat in
the car in the driveway for forty minutes before he could open the door.

Now he is sitting in a clinician’s office, a man who has built his life around control and
predictability, and he is afraid. He is afraid that talking about the ambush will make the film play
louder. He is afraid that he will never be able to run again, or watch a movie with his wife, or
walk into a store without smelling smoke. But he is also, beneath the fear, hoping for something he
cannot quite name—a kind of quiet, perhaps, or a door that he can open without bracing himself for
what is on the other side.
