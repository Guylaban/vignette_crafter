Rekha came to the clinic at the urging of her daughter, who had grown worried by the changes she saw
in her mother over the past two years. At sixty-six, Rekha had been a midwife for over four decades,
a career she had built with steady hands and a calm voice, delivering hundreds of babies in a busy
urban hospital. She still worked three shifts a week, but the work no longer felt like a calling. It
felt like the only place she could breathe.

The traumatic event had occurred when she was a young woman in her early twenties, living in a
region of South Asia that was then under military occupation. She had been walking home from a
friend’s house when a military checkpoint stopped her. Two soldiers, both men, had pulled her into a
side alley. They did not rape her, but they held her against a wall, tore open her blouse, and
pressed the cold metal of a rifle barrel against her throat while they laughed and asked her
questions she could not answer. One of them had kept his eyes fixed on her, watching her face, her
chest, her trembling hands. After what felt like an hour but was probably minutes, they let her go.
She had never told anyone the full story. She had packed it away, married, moved to America, and
raised her children. For decades, it stayed buried.

Then, two years ago, her husband retired and began spending more time at home. Around the same time,
a new security guard began working the evening shift at the hospital entrance—a tall man in a crisp
uniform who stood near the sliding doors and watched people as they came and went. Rekha found
herself avoiding that entrance. She started parking farther away and entering through the emergency
bay, even though it meant walking through a darker corridor. She told herself it was nothing. But
soon, she could not be in the staff break room if a male colleague was the only other person there.
She would make an excuse and leave, grab her coffee and retreat to the supply closet. She began to
schedule her shifts so that she was always working alongside at least one other woman. She never
said it aloud, but she had come to believe that being alone with a man, any man, was dangerous. Her
husband, a gentle man who had never raised his voice to her, began to notice that she flinched when
he entered a room from behind. He started announcing himself before walking in.

Her response to the growing fear was not to withdraw from work but to throw herself into it with a
ferocity that exhausted her colleagues. She took extra shifts, volunteered for the most complicated
deliveries, stayed late to restock supplies. The hospital had become her sanctuary because it was
full of people, noise, and purpose. In the delivery room, she could focus entirely on the mother and
the baby, on the rhythm of breath and contraction, and for those hours, the watchful feeling
receded. But the moment she stepped into the hallway, it returned. She was hypervigilant in a way
that felt automatic—scanning every face, every uniform, every sudden movement. A male doctor
reaching quickly for a chart made her heart pound. A security guard walking toward her made her
change direction. She could not relax, even in her own home. She sat with her back to the wall at
the dinner table. She checked the locks three times before bed.

The consequence of this constant vigilance was a slow erosion of her marriage. Her husband, confused
and hurt, began to feel that she was avoiding him. She was not, exactly—she loved him—but she could
not bear the feeling of his eyes on her when she was undressing, or his hand on her shoulder from
behind. She had started sleeping in the guest room, telling him she was too tired. She knew this was
hurting him, but what frightened her most was the thought that if she let her guard down, something
terrible would happen. She was certain that the world was full of dangerous people, and that she had
to be ready.

What finally brought her to seek help was a moment in the delivery room. A young father, a man in an
army uniform visiting his wife, had stepped toward her to thank her. His sudden movement, the sight
of the uniform, the way he leaned in—she had frozen, dropped a surgical clamp, and backed away. The
room had gone quiet. Her colleague had covered for her, but Rekha knew she could not keep running.
She came to the clinic hoping to find a way to stop being afraid, though she was not sure if that
was possible. She was most afraid that if she talked about what happened in that alley, she would
fall apart completely. But she was also, quietly, hoping that she might one day sit in a room with
her husband again and not feel the need to watch the door.
