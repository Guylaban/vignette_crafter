When the bombing started, Samir had been a relief worker for nearly two decades. He was sixty-four
now, a divorced man living alone in a quiet suburb, but the man he had been then—the one who
coordinated supply chains through active war zones, who pulled children from rubble while the air
burned—felt like a stranger. That stranger had been in a field hospital on the outskirts of a city
whose name he no longer said aloud. A shell had landed two hundred meters away. The blast had thrown
him against a wall, and for a long moment there was only a high ringing and the smell of burnt
wiring, flesh, and diesel. He had crawled through smoke that tasted of metal, his hands finding
pieces of people that did not fit together. He remembered the terror not as a story he could tell,
but as something that happened to his body: the way his heart slammed against his ribs, the way his
vision narrowed to a tunnel of gray. Even now, twenty years later, when the memory returned it came
not as a coherent narrative but as disconnected fragments—the sound of a child's shoe hitting
concrete, the heat on his face, a single image of a severed hand still wearing a wedding ring. Each
fragment carried the same raw terror he had felt in that moment, and when it hit him he would have
to stop whatever he was doing, overwhelmed by a wave of emotion that left him breathless and
shaking.

Samir had learned to keep this hidden. As a politician—a city councilman in a Midwestern district
with a growing Middle Eastern population—he had built a career on composure. He spoke at town halls,
shook hands at community events, and sat through long budget meetings. But the world had become a
landscape of hidden threats. Silence unnerved him most: a pause in conversation, the hush of a
hallway between meetings, the quiet of his own apartment at night. He was certain that unusual
silence meant something dangerous was about to happen. He checked his doors and windows repeatedly
before bed, sometimes three or four times, and he scanned every room he entered for exits, for
shadows, for anyone who seemed out of place. He could not help it; his eyes moved constantly,
reading faces, reading hands, reading the space around him for signs of ambush.

His work had become his shield. He threw himself into tasks—drafting policy proposals, returning
calls, attending evening events—because keeping his mind occupied meant less room for the fragments
to surface. He tried hard to control his emotions, especially in public. When a constituent brought
up military funding at a meeting, he felt his chest tighten and his mouth go dry, but he nodded and
changed the subject. When someone shook his hand too firmly, he felt a flash of the old panic and
pulled away. He avoided talking to strangers whenever possible, delegating small talk to his aide.
And burnt smells—a neighbor's barbecue, a car backfiring, the toaster left on too long—sent him into
a cold sweat that he masked with a cough and a quick exit.

The consequences had accumulated quietly. His marriage had ended years ago, in part because his wife
could not understand why he would not go to restaurants with open kitchens or why he flinched when
she touched him from behind. His relationships with his adult children were strained; they saw him
as distant, work-obsessed, unreachable. He had stopped attending mosque because the crowded room and
the sudden silences during prayer felt unbearable. His colleagues respected him but found him
guarded, hard to read.

What finally brought Samir to seek help was a city council hearing on a new housing development near
an old industrial site. A member of the public had stood up and described the demolition process,
using the word "blast." Samir had felt the floor drop out from under him. He had excused himself,
walked to the bathroom, and stood with his hands on the sink, shaking, the smell of burnt dust from
the construction site drifting through a window. He had looked at his face in the mirror—sixty-four
years old, tired, alone—and had understood that he could not keep managing this by himself. He was
afraid of what might happen if he let the memories come fully, afraid that he would fall apart and
never piece himself back together. But he was also afraid of spending the rest of his life checking
locks and flinching at silence. He wanted, more than anything, to sleep through the night without
waking to the smell of smoke.
