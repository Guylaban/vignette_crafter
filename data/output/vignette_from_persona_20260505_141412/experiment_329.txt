stretches between phone notifications—until silence became something that pressed against her chest
like a weight. When the house fell completely still, she would freeze at her desk, her hands
hovering over the keyboard, certain that something terrible was about to happen. It was a feeling
that had no logic, but it lived in her body anyway.

The terrible thing had already happened, fifteen years ago. Her son, then twelve, had been diagnosed
with a rare and aggressive form of leukemia. She remembered the smell of the hospital corridor on

days afterward. She remembered the silence in the car on the way home, her husband gripping the
steering wheel, neither of them speaking, and how that silence had felt like a held breath before a
fall. Her son had survived. He was twenty-seven now, healthy, living in Chicago. Ming knew she

she had told herself it was just roughhousing, why she had waited another week before taking him to

of the way she acted—or failed to act—and she carried that belief the way other people carried a
scar.
