Frank is a sixty-year-old warehouse worker who lives with his partner in a mid-sized Midwestern
town. He came to therapy at his partner’s urging, though he was not sure what talking could do now.
What he carried with him was a single night in Vietnam, fifty years ago, that had never stopped
happening. He had been a young infantryman, barely nineteen, when his unit was ambushed in a rice
paddy at dusk. He remembered the wet heat, the shouting, the way the mud sucked at his boots. He
remembered a friend—a boy from Ohio named Danny—taking a round to the chest right next to him. Frank
had tried to drag him to cover, but Danny was already gone. He remembered the medevac coming in, the
rotors whipping the smoke, and how he had been the one lifted onto the bird while three others were
left behind. He had not chosen to be first. He had not even understood what was happening until he
was already in the air, watching the paddy shrink beneath him. But that did not matter. What
mattered was that he got out and they did not.

For decades, Frank had managed. He worked, he drank, he kept his head down. But in the last few
years, the memories had become harder to push away. When they came, they came with the same terror
he had felt at nineteen—his chest would seize, his palms would sweat, and he would be back in that
paddy, the smell of mud and diesel in his nostrils. And alongside the terror came a guilt so heavy
it felt physical. He would see Danny’s face, hear his own voice screaming for help, and he would
think: *Why him and not me?* He had no answer. He had never found one. And that absence of an answer
had become a kind of answer in itself—that he was not meant to have survived, that his life since
had been borrowed time, that the man who came home was not really the same person. He did not know
himself anymore. The world, which had once seemed ordinary, now felt like a place where danger could
spring from anywhere, at any moment, without warning. He found himself scanning parking lots,
watching doorways, noticing the way people moved. He was always ready for something to go wrong.

Frank worked the night shift at a regional distribution center, unloading pallets and stacking
boxes. The work was physical and mindless, which suited him. But the warehouse itself had become a
problem. It was a low, concrete building with fluorescent lights that buzzed and flickered, and the
loading bay doors opened onto a dark yard that reminded him, in some way he could not articulate, of
the edge of that rice paddy. He had started taking routes through the building that kept him away
from the bay doors. He avoided the break room because someone had left a jacket on a hook that
looked too much like an old fatigue shirt. He had stopped wearing olive green or khaki entirely,
even though those colors had been staples of his wardrobe for years. He wore only black or navy now.
At home, he kept the television on constantly—sports, news, anything—because silence made his mind
drift toward the event, and he had learned that if he let himself think about it, he would be stuck
there for hours, unable to find the words to describe what he saw or felt. He would open his mouth
and nothing would come out. So he did not try.

The consequences of these strategies had been slow but real. His partner had noticed that he no
longer went to the grocery store that sat near the old VFW hall, even though it was the closest one.
He claimed it was out of the way, but she knew better. He had stopped attending family barbecues at
a cousin’s house that was located on a street that reminded him, distantly, of the road leading to
the deployment base. His social world had shrunk to work and home, and even home had become tense.
His partner had tried to get him to talk, but he could not. The words were not there. And so she had
finally made the appointment for him, and he had agreed because he was tired—tired of the scanning,
the avoiding, the guilt that sat on his chest like a weight he could not set down.

What Frank hoped for, when he walked into the therapist’s office, was unclear to him. He did not
expect to feel better. He did not expect to forgive himself. But he was afraid that if he did not
try something, he would lose the one person who still looked at him like he was worth saving. And
that, more than anything else, was what finally brought him through the door.
