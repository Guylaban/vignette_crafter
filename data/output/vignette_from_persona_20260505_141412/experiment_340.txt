six months ago. She was the lead paramedic on a code-three response to a multi-vehicle collision on
the interstate. The extrication was routine until it wasn’t. A sedan had wedged under the rear of a
semi, and the driver, a man in his late fifties, was trapped. Jenna worked the hydraulic cutter
herself, positioning the spreader arms against the crumpled door pillar. She felt the metal give,
heard the groan, and then the shift—the car dropped six inches onto its suspension, and the steering
column, which had been pinned against the man’s chest, drove upward into his throat. The sound he
made was not a scream. It was a wet, percussive gasp that stopped almost immediately. He died before
they could get him out. The official report listed cause of death as traumatic asphyxiation due to
steering column intrusion. Jenna knows the truth: she moved the car. She cut the wrong piece. She
killed him.

In the months since, Jenna’s mind has stopped working the way it used to. She does not remember the
whole scene—she remembers five or six moments, each one a still image that drops into her
consciousness without warning. The dashboard clock reading 3:47. The man’s right hand, still
gripping the steering wheel, the knuckles white. The sound of the spreader arms biting into the
metal. And then, always, the gasp. When these snapshots come, she feels the same terror she felt in
that moment, a full-body electrical jolt that leaves her shaking and breathless. She has learned to
expect them, but she cannot prepare for them. They arrive in the middle of a conversation, while she
is driving, while she is trying to fall asleep.

She has come to believe that she is dangerous. That she cannot trust her own hands. That if she
makes a single mistake again, someone else will die. This belief has changed how she moves through

front, because from the back she cannot accidentally give a bad direction. She checks her equipment
three times before every call. She has started finding it harder to feel safe at night than during
the day—the darkness reminds her of the interstate at 3:47 a.m., and she is certain that the worst
things happen when no one can see them coming. She is hyperalert on every call, scanning the scene
for anything that could go wrong, but the vigilance has backfired: she cannot concentrate on what is
actually in front of her. Last week, she missed a patient’s low oxygen saturation for nearly a full
minute because she was fixated on the angle of the stretcher relative to the curb.

To manage the terror and the images, Jenna has developed habits that make sense to her but have
begun to shrink her life. She drinks two glasses of wine every night to take the edge off the
hyperarousal before bed. On her days off, she sometimes drinks earlier in the afternoon, because the
snapshots come harder when she is still. She fills every quiet moment with distraction—podcasts,
television shows she does not care about, scrolling her phone until her eyes burn. What she will not
do is talk about it. When her husband, Mark, asks how her shift was, she says “fine” and changes the
subject. When her partner on the truck tried to debrief the call the next day, Jenna cut him off and

she already suspects: that she is incompetent, that she killed a man, that she should never have
been a paramedic. Silence feels like the only way to keep the story contained.

The consequences have been slow and cumulative. Mark has stopped asking. They eat dinner in front of
the television now, and he falls asleep on the couch while she sits awake, watching the clock,
waiting for the images. She has stopped meeting her friends for drinks after shift. She has stopped
going to the gym. She has stopped calling her mother on Sundays. Her life has become a series of
small rooms—the ambulance, the living room, the bedroom—and she moves between them without speaking.

What finally brought her to the clinic was a call last week. A teenage boy had crashed his dirt bike

got back in the truck, she started shaking so hard she could not write her report. She sat in the
parking lot of the hospital for forty minutes, staring at her hands, convinced that if she had made

help. She is terrified that she will be pulled from active duty. She is terrified that she will be
