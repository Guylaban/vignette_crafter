outside a mid-sized Midwestern city. He came to therapy at his wife’s urging, after months of

happened eighteen months ago, on a cool autumn evening when Ravi was driving home from a late client
meeting. He had stopped at a gas station just off the highway, a place he had visited a dozen times
before, to fill his tank and grab coffee. As he walked toward the convenience store entrance, he
noticed two police officers speaking with a man near the pumps. Ravi did not think much of it; he
simply wanted to get his coffee and leave. But as he passed them, one of the officers turned, looked
at him, and without a word, grabbed his arm and slammed him against the side of the patrol car. The
officer shouted something Ravi could not process. The other officer was already patting him down,
pulling his wallet from his pocket, demanding to know whose car he was driving. Ravi tried to
explain—he had the registration, the insurance, everything was in order—but the words came out
tangled. The first officer kept his forearm pressed across Ravi’s throat, and Ravi could smell the
man’s breath, stale coffee and something metallic, and the burnt smell of the patrol car’s engine
idling nearby. They held him there for what felt like an eternity before a third officer arrived,
reviewed the license plate, and told them they had the wrong person. The officers let him go. No
apology. No explanation. Just a curt “You can go now.” Ravi got back in his car, hands shaking so
badly he could barely turn the key, and drove home in silence. He never called the station to
complain. He never told anyone the full story. He just stopped going to that gas station.

In the weeks that followed, Ravi found himself unable to shake the memory. It would surface without
warning—the pressure of the officer’s arm, the sound of his own voice cracking as he tried to speak,
and always that burnt smell, as if the engine were still idling right beside him. When he tried to
tell his wife what happened, the words would not come. He knew exactly what he wanted to say—he
could see the scene in perfect detail—but his mouth would not cooperate. He would start a sentence,
lose the thread, and trail off. After a few attempts, he stopped trying. The guilt was worse than
the fear. He kept replaying the moment and asking himself why he had not fought back, why he had not
demanded an apology, why he had just stood there like a child. He had come to believe that he should
have done something differently, that if he had been more assertive or more articulate, the whole
thing would never have happened. That belief settled into something deeper: a certainty that people
could not be trusted, that the world was a dangerous place, and that he had been betrayed by someone
he had no reason to suspect. The officers were supposed to protect him. Instead, they had treated
him like a criminal. He began to see danger everywhere.

elevator if he saw a guard in the lobby.
