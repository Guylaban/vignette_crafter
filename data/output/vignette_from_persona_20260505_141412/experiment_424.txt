by force of will. He was fifty-four years old, a married father of three grown children, and the
owner of a small but respected construction company in El Paso. He had come to therapy at his wife’s
insistence, after a series of escalating arguments that had left her in tears and him pacing the
kitchen at three in the morning, a loaded pistol tucked into the waistband of his sweatpants. He
knew she was afraid of him now, and that knowledge sat in his chest like a stone.

The thing that had broken him happened twenty-two years ago, in a dusty village in northern Bosnia.
Carlos had been a relief worker then, part of a small team delivering food and medical supplies to
communities that the larger convoys could not reach. He had been in the country for eight months,
long enough to grow accustomed to the rumble of distant artillery, the way children flinched at the
bang of a door, the smell of woodsmoke and cordite. He was not a soldier. He carried no weapon. He
believed, with the earnest certainty of a man in his early thirties, that his humanitarian badge
would protect him.

On the afternoon of the incident, his team had stopped at a checkpoint outside a town called Maglaj.
The soldiers were young, maybe nineteen or twenty, their faces as hard as the metal of their rifles.
The lead officer had waved Carlos’s vehicle forward, then changed his mind, shouting something in
Serbian that Carlos’s interpreter translated as “Get out, get out now.” Carlos stepped onto the
muddy road, hands raised, and the officer walked directly up to him, pressed the muzzle of an AK-47
against his sternum, and held it there for what felt like a full minute. The barrel was cold. Carlos
could smell the man’s breath—coffee and cigarettes. The officer laughed, a short, barking sound, and
then waved him back into the truck. They drove away without another word. Carlos finished his
deployment, completed his reports, and returned to the United States. He did not speak of it.

It was not until years later, when he was home, that the memory started to take on a life of its
own. It did not come back as a story he could tell. It came back as a sensation—the pressure of cold
steel against his breastbone, the taste of bile in his throat, the certain knowledge that he was
about to die. A firecracker on the Fourth of July could drop him to his knees. A car backfiring in
the parking lot of his own hardware store would leave him shaking for an hour. He learned to scan
every room he entered for exits, for the shape of a man’s hand near his belt, for the weight of a
bulge under a jacket. He could not stand to have anyone stand directly in front of him, especially
not in a doorway.

The worst part, the part he could not explain to his wife, was what happened when something good
occurred. A birthday party for his youngest daughter. A promotion at work. The moment his wife
leaned in to kiss him after a quiet evening. In those seconds, when his body should have felt light,
he felt instead a surge of raw, inexplicable dread. His mind would offer him no image, no
thought—just a wave of wrongness, as if joy itself were a trap. He had come to believe, without ever
saying it aloud, that if he let himself feel happy, something terrible would happen. He would be
punished for the lapse in vigilance. And so he had learned to dampen the good moments before they
could crest, to pull back, to find a reason to leave the room.

That vigilance had curdled into something darker over the past five years. Carlos had started
carrying a weapon everywhere—a compact Glock 19 that he kept in a holster under his jacket or, at
home, in a drawer beside his bed. He knew it was illegal in some of the places he took it. He did
not care. The weight of it against his hip was the only thing that quieted the spinning in his head.
He had also begun to argue. It started small—a sharp word to a subcontractor who left a mess, a curt
reply to his wife about dinner. But it had escalated. He found himself picking fights on purpose,
saying things he knew would sting, then watching the other person’s face harden. In those moments,
the dread receded. The anger was simpler. It gave him something to focus on besides the cold metal
of a memory.

His business had suffered. He had lost two reliable foremen because he could not stop berating them.
His wife had stopped inviting him to family gatherings. His eldest son, a quiet young man who worked
in IT, had told him he was “scary to be around.” Carlos had heard that word—scary—and it had lodged
in him like a fragment of shrapnel that no doctor could find.

He came to therapy because his wife had given him an ultimatum, but also because, late at night,
when the house was silent and the gun was in his hand and he could not remember why he had picked it
up, he was terrified of what he might do. He did not want to hurt anyone. He wanted to stop being
afraid of the cold, the dark, the good, the bad. He wanted to stop going over and over the question
that had no answer: why had that soldier chosen to let him live, and what did it mean that Carlos
had done nothing to deserve it?
