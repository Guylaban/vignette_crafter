Amir, a 38-year-old hairdresser who had built a loyal clientele over fifteen years at the same salon
in Dearborn, Michigan, could trace the exact moment his life split in two. It was a Tuesday
afternoon, three years ago, when a routine physical for a minor shoulder strain turned catastrophic.
The doctor, a man Amir had seen twice before, had pulled up a CT scan on the monitor and said, in a
voice so flat it seemed rehearsed, that there was a mass on his pancreas. The word "malignant" came
next. Amir remembered the fluorescent hum of the lights, the antiseptic sting in the air, and the
way his own breath suddenly smelled wrong to him—metallic, sour, like something inside him was
already rotting. He remembered the exact pressure of the cold stethoscope disk against his sternum
as the doctor listened to his heart, and how that pressure, that listening, had felt like a verdict
being delivered to every cell in his body.

What followed was eight months of surgeries and chemotherapy, and Amir survived. The cancer went
into remission. But his body had not forgiven him. Now, years later, the smell of antiseptic wipes
in the salon—the kind he used to clean his clippers without a second thought—would hit him like a
wave, and suddenly he could taste that copper-sour smell from the exam room again, his mouth filling
with a phantom metallic saliva. When he woke from nightmares, which he did most nights now, he would
lie perfectly still in the dark, his hand pressed to his abdomen, certain he could feel the exact
ache of the surgical incision, the ghost of the drain tube pulling at his skin. The worst moments
were the ones he could not explain to anyone: he would be standing over a client, chatting about the
weather, and a wave of pure terror would crash through him—cold, electric, wordless—with no image
attached, no memory, just the full, suffocating feeling of being told he was going to die. He had
come to believe that these episodes proved something terrible about himself. His body kept reacting
as if the cancer were still there, as if danger were still present, and there was no logical reason
for it. That, to Amir, meant he was going crazy. His own nervous system had become a traitor, and he
could not trust a single sensation it sent him.

He had started declining medical care. A routine blood test last spring sent him into a spiral so
severe he had to close the salon for two days. He had sat in the waiting room, his skin prickling
with heat, his heart slamming against his ribs, and when the phlebotomist touched his arm he had
flinched so violently he knocked over a tray of vials. He had not gone back for the results. The
thought of another examination, another cold probe, another doctor’s pause before speaking, was not
just frightening—it was unendurable. At work, he had become brittle. The salon was full of mirrors,
and sometimes, catching his own reflection in a certain light, he would see the gaunt, bald man from
the hospital photos and his hands would start to shake. He had learned to angle his chair away from
the glass. He had also learned to avoid the salon’s back room, where a framed photo from his salon’s
ten-year anniversary party still hung—a picture of himself, healthy and smiling, that now felt like
a photograph of a dead man.

He was single, and he had stopped dating entirely. The thought of someone touching his torso, of a
lover’s hand brushing the scar tissue that ran from his sternum to his navel, made his throat close.
He was certain no one could understand what it felt like to live in a body that had turned into an
enemy, a body that could not be trusted not to betray him again. What brought Amir to therapy,
finally, was not the terror itself but the exhaustion. He had woken up one morning after a
nightmare, his sheets soaked with sweat, his hand already pressing into his abdomen, and he had
realized he could not remember what it felt like to wake up without that gesture. He was tired of
being afraid of his own pulse. He was tired of the smell of rubbing alcohol ruining his days. He did
not know if talking could fix a body that had taught him it could kill him, but he was desperate
enough to hope that it might.
