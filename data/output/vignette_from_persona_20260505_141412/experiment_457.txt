Andrea, a forty-one-year-old firefighter, had spent nearly two decades running toward the worst
moments of other people’s lives. She had pulled victims from burning buildings, stabilized trauma
patients in the field, and never once questioned whether her own body would hold. That changed in
the winter of her fortieth year, when a persistent cough she had dismissed as a chest cold turned
into a sepsis diagnosis that landed her in the intensive care unit for eleven days. What she
remembered most vividly, even now, was not the pain or the fear of dying—it was the feeling of being
strapped to a hospital bed with an IV line in her arm, unable to move, unable to speak, while a
ventilator hissed beside her. She could still feel the cold plastic of the oxygen tubing against her
face, the antiseptic smell that seemed to coat the inside of her nostrils, and the way her body had
gone rigid with terror as a nurse approached with a needle. That moment, the sheer helplessness of
it, had lodged itself somewhere deeper than language.

Now, more than a year later, Andrea could not put what had happened into words. When her husband
asked her to talk about the experience, she found herself staring at the wall, her throat tight, the
sentences dissolving before they formed. The memory did not come to her as a story she could tell.
It came as a physical event—her back would arch, her hands would clench, and she would feel herself
pinned to the mattress again, the ventilator hiss filling her ears. The terror that had gripped her
in that hospital room returned undiminished, as if no time had passed at all. She had come to
believe that her body had betrayed her once and would do so again, and that belief had turned her
everyday life into a minefield.

At work, the station house had become a place of constant vigilance. Andrea found herself scanning
every room she entered for signs of danger—not the dangers of a fire scene, but the subtler threats
of illness and injury. A crewmate coughing made her heart pound. The smell of hand sanitizer in the
ambulance bay sent a wave of nausea through her. She began to avoid the medical equipment she had
once used without a second thought; the sight of an IV drip in the back of the rig made her chest
tighten, and she would look away, forcing herself to think of something else—the truck's engine, the
weather, anything but the plastic tubing and the memory it carried. Her body stayed tense, a low hum
of alertness that never switched off, and she had taken to declining assignments that involved
hospital transfers or medical calls. Her captain had noticed, and there had been a
conversation—supportive, but pointed—about her declining availability for certain shifts. Andrea had
told him she was fine, that she just needed a break from the medical side of the job, but she knew
she was not fine.

At home, the avoidance had spread. She had stopped watching medical dramas on television, had pulled
her car to the other side of the parking lot when she saw a hospital entrance, and had begun to
steer conversations away from anything that might stir strong feelings. When her teenage daughter
cried over a breakup, Andrea felt her own throat close and excused herself to the kitchen, where she
stood gripping the counter until her knuckles went white. She was certain that if she let herself
feel the full weight of any strong emotion, she would unravel completely. Her husband had started to
ask, gently, whether she was okay, and Andrea had learned to say "I'm tired" in a way that ended the
conversation.

room with a stethoscope, Andrea felt her body seize—her arms locked at her sides, her breathing
turned shallow, and she was back in that hospital bed, the same terror flooding through her. She had
bolted from the office before the doctor could touch her, and sat in her car shaking for twenty
minutes. That night, she told her husband she needed to talk to someone. She was afraid of what she
might find—afraid that talking about it would make the terror worse, that it would confirm she was
broken. But she was more afraid of what would happen if she kept running.
