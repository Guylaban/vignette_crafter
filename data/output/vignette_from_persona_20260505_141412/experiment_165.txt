Malynda is a nineteen-year-old hairdresser who lives with her boyfriend in a small apartment above a
nail salon in the city’s southeast Asian district. She came to therapy because she had started
shaking during a haircut. A client had sneezed suddenly, and the sound—a sharp, percussive
burst—sent Malynda’s scissors clattering to the floor. She had apologised so many times, so quickly,
that the client had asked if she was okay. Malynda had said yes, but she wasn’t. She had spent the
rest of the day scanning the salon for exits, her chest tight, her hands trembling every time a car
passed too loudly on the street outside.

The event that had fractured her world happened eleven months ago. Malynda had been driving home
from a late shift, her boyfriend asleep in the passenger seat. It was raining. A man had stepped off
the curb without looking—later she would learn he was intoxicated—and she had hit him. She
remembered the thud, the way the windshield spiderwebbed, the screaming of her own voice before she
even knew she was making sound. The man survived, but with catastrophic injuries: a traumatic brain
injury, a shattered pelvis. He would never walk the same way again. Malynda had not been charged;
the police report called it an unavoidable accident. But that word—accident—had become a lie she
could not swallow. She knew, with a certainty that hollowed her out, that she should have seen him
sooner. She should have braked faster. She should have protected him, and she had failed.

In the months that followed, Malynda’s life contracted. She stopped driving entirely. Her boyfriend
drove her to work, and she sat rigid in the passenger seat, her hands pressed flat against her
thighs, her eyes fixed on the road as if she might will every pedestrian into safety. If she saw a
person near a curb, her stomach dropped. She would close her eyes, but that was worse: the image
would rise, unbidden, of a body hitting metal, of a face she had never clearly seen but could now
picture in perfect, terrible detail. Her memories of the event were almost entirely
visual—snapshots, really, that arrived without warning: the wet gleam of the road, the shape of a
hand against the windshield, the red that spread. Sometimes, when she tried to tell her boyfriend
what she remembered, she felt nothing. Her voice would go flat, and she would describe the accident
as if it had happened to someone else, a stranger in a story. And then, minutes later, she would be
crying, overwhelmed by a grief that felt bottomless—grief for the man she had hurt, for the life he
would never have, for the person she used to be.

She had come to believe that her own body was untrustworthy. Her hands, which had always been
steady, now shook without warning. Her heart would pound at a sudden noise—a car horn, a dropped
tray, a door slamming—and she would feel the crash all over again, her breath short, her skin hot.
She could not relax, even at home, even in her own bed. She lay awake at night scanning the dark for
threats she could not name. When she and her boyfriend argued, or even when he seemed mildly
frustrated, she became excessively compliant—agreeing too quickly, apologising for things that were
not her fault, smoothing over any edge that might cut. She had not always been like this. She had
been the one who teased him, who argued back, who laughed too loud. Now she was afraid that if she
was not perfect, if she was not good enough, something terrible would happen again. She could not
rely on other people, she told herself, because she could not rely on herself.

The anniversary of the accident was approaching. She had already told her boyfriend she wanted to do
nothing that day—no acknowledgment, no mention, no marking of it at all. She hoped that if she
ignored the date hard enough, it might pass without her falling apart. But she knew, somewhere
beneath the surface of that hope, that she was already falling apart. She had come to therapy
because she was afraid of what she might do to herself, or to her relationship, or to her job. She
was afraid that the grief would never stop. And she was afraid, most of all, that she was right:
that she had failed, and that failing was who she was now.
