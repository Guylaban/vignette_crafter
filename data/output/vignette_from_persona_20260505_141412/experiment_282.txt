George is a seventy-year-old salesperson who has been married for forty-three years, and when he
first sat down in the consulting room, he did so carefully, as though the chair might not hold him.
He is a large man, broad-shouldered and silver-haired, but he held himself tightly, his hands
clasped in his lap, his gaze fixed on a spot on the carpet. He had come because his wife, Margaret,
had finally insisted. She had watched him shrink for six months, she said, and she could not watch
it anymore.

The event that had undone him happened on a Tuesday evening in late November. George had stopped at
a convenience store on his way home from a sales call, a place he had visited a hundred times
before. As he walked back to his car, a man came around the side of the building, fast and silent.
George never saw the punch coming. The first blow caught him on the temple, and the second, a fist
driven into his ribs, sent him to the pavement. He remembered the smell of alcohol on the man’s
breath, sour and sharp, as the man knelt over him, demanding his wallet. George gave it to him. He
did not fight. He lay still and let it happen, and when it was over, the man ran off into the dark.
George drove himself home with a cracked rib and a bruise blooming across the side of his face. He
told Margaret it was a fall.

In the weeks that followed, George began to change. The sales route he had driven for fifteen years
suddenly felt dangerous. He started taking longer roads to avoid the block where the store stood,
and then he stopped driving that part of town altogether. He told his manager he was cutting back
his territory, and his manager, puzzled, agreed. George did not explain why. He did not tell anyone
that the thought of seeing that corner again made his chest go tight and his hands go cold. He
simply stopped going.

The harder he tried not to think about the assault, the more the thoughts came. They arrived without
warning—the smell of alcohol on a stranger’s breath in a grocery aisle, the flash of a man’s
silhouette in a parking lot, the sensation of cold pavement against his cheek. Each time, George
would push the memory down, clench his jaw, and force himself to think about something else. But the
pushing only made the thoughts louder, and he began to feel as though he was fighting a war inside
his own head.

What frightened him most was not the memory itself, but what the memory had done to him. He no
longer recognised the man he saw in the mirror. That man had been easygoing, confident, a
storyteller who could charm a customer into a sale. The man he had become was jumpy and silent,
suspicious of strangers, reluctant to leave the house after dark. He had come to believe that there
was something fundamentally wrong with him, some hidden defect that had been exposed by the attack.
He told himself that the event had happened because of the way he acted—because he had not been
paying attention, because he had walked too slowly, because he had not fought back. He should have
fought back. A man his age, a man who had raised two sons, should have done something. The thought
that he had simply lain there, passive and afraid, filled him with a shame that felt physical, like
a weight in his chest.

He had learned to numb his feelings as a matter of survival. When Margaret reached for his hand, he
felt nothing. When his youngest son called to check in, George answered in monosyllables and ended
the call quickly. He had stopped going to Sunday dinner at his daughter’s house because the drive
took him past the convenience store. He had stopped meeting his old sales colleagues for coffee
because one of them might ask why he seemed different. The numbing was a relief at first, a kind of
quiet, but it had become a hollow that grew wider each week. Margaret had started crying in the
kitchen, quietly, thinking he could not hear.

What finally brought George to seek help was a moment in the produce section of a grocery store. A
man walked past him, unshaven, wearing a dark jacket, and George froze. The man was not his
attacker. George knew that. But the smell of beer on the man’s breath hit him like a blow, and for a
long second he could not breathe, could not move, could not remember where he was. A woman asked if
he was all right, and he had nodded and walked out, leaving his cart behind.

He told Margaret that night, haltingly, piece by piece. She held his hand and did not let go. And
now he is here, in this room, hoping that someone can help him find his way back to the person he
used to be—but terrified, in the quiet of his own mind, that that person is already gone.
