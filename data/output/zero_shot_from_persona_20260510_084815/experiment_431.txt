# Eleanor

Eleanor is a 77-year-old woman who spent the better part of five decades studying wetland ecosystems
across North America. She has always been most comfortable in the field — wading through marshes,
cataloguing species, writing reports that translated the quiet language of the natural world into
something policymakers might understand. She never married, a choice she made deliberately and
without regret, and she built a life that felt full and purposeful on her own terms.

Fourteen months ago, Eleanor was raped by a man who broke into her home during the night. She had
returned late from a conference where she had presented research she considered among the most
important of her career — a decades-long study on the degradation of coastal wetlands. She remembers
the particular pleasure of that evening, the warmth of colleagues congratulating her in the hotel
lobby, the quiet satisfaction of the drive home. Then the door, the darkness, and what happened
after. She does not speak about the assault itself in much detail. What she returns to, again and
again, is the moment she realized she could not make him stop. After seventy-seven years of
competence — of solving problems, of navigating difficult terrain both literal and institutional —
she could not make it stop.

In the weeks that followed, Eleanor did not tell anyone. She cleaned her house. She filed a police
report in a flat, procedural voice, the way she might dictate field notes. She told herself she had
survived difficult things before. She had worked in environments hostile to women for most of her
professional life and had learned to keep moving.

But she stopped sleeping. When she does sleep, she wakes in the early hours with her heart
hammering, certain someone is in the room. She has installed three separate locks on her front door
and a security system she checks compulsively before bed, sometimes four or five times. She keeps
the lights on throughout the house at night, which means she rarely sleeps more than two or three
hours at a stretch.

She has not returned to fieldwork. This is perhaps the loss she finds hardest to articulate. The
marshes where she has spent her life require solitude, require moving through isolated landscapes
alone, and she cannot do it. She drove to a research site she has visited for thirty years and sat
in her car for forty minutes before driving home. She told a graduate student she was mentoring that
she was "taking a step back from active fieldwork," and the professional neutrality of that phrase
seemed to cost her something.

In ordinary moments — at the grocery store, in a waiting room — she finds herself scanning exits and
calculating distances. She startles at sounds she would never have noticed before. A door closing in
another room. A car slowing on the street outside. Her concentration, which was always one of her

grant deadlines, which she describes with a kind of bewildered shame, as though she has become a
stranger to herself.

What troubles Eleanor most, she says, is not the fear. It is the way the assault has revised her
memory of herself. She had always believed, in some fundamental way, that she was a woman who could

and how long she has been holding it alone.
