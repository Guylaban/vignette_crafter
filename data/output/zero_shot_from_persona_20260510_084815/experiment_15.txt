# Vignette: Karim, Age 41

Karim came to the United States from Lebanon twelve years ago, carrying with him the particular
exhaustion of someone who had spent a lifetime learning to read danger in the air. He had grown up
during a period of relative stability, but violence was always something that existed close to the
surface of daily life — a fact he had made his peace with, or so he believed. He settled in a mid-
sized American city, found work at a fast food restaurant, and built a modest, quiet life. He was
good at the job. He liked the predictability of it — the same fryers, the same orders, the same
faces coming through the drive-through window each morning.

Two years ago, that predictability shattered.

Karim was working the lunch rush when a man entered the restaurant and shot another customer in the
dining area. The victim — a young man Karim recognized as a regular, someone who always ordered the
same combo meal and left a small tip — died before the ambulance arrived. Karim was standing less
than ten feet away. He watched the man fall. He watched the blood spread across the tile floor he
mopped every closing shift. He heard sounds he has never been able to unhear.

In the days that followed, Karim told himself he was fine. He returned to work within a week because
he needed the income and because staying home felt unbearable in its own way — the apartment too
quiet, the silence too full. But the restaurant had changed for him in ways he could not explain to
his coworkers. He began scanning the dining area compulsively every few minutes, tracking the door
each time it opened. His manager noticed he had started flinching at the sound of trays being
dropped, at car backfires in the parking lot, at anything loud and sudden. Karim noticed it too, but
felt powerless to stop it.

Sleep became something that happened to other people. When he does manage to fall asleep, he often
wakes at two or three in the morning, heart pounding, certain he has just heard a gunshot. The
dreams are vivid and unrelenting — sometimes a faithful replay of what happened, sometimes distorted
versions where the victim is someone he loves, or where he himself is the one who falls. He lies
awake afterward with a dread he cannot locate or name, staring at the ceiling until his alarm goes
off.

During the day, Karim moves through the world feeling as though he is watching himself from a slight
distance. He describes it as being wrapped in glass — present, but separated from everything around
him. He has stopped calling his sister in Beirut as often as he used to. He has no interest in the
small pleasures that once sustained him: coffee with a coworker after a shift, football on
television, the occasional hookah lounge where he used to feel briefly at ease. He tells himself he
is simply tired.

What troubles him most, though he rarely says this directly, is the guilt. He was not hurt. He did
not know the young man well. He has no right, he believes, to be undone by something that happened
to someone else. This belief sits in him like a stone, making it difficult to reach out, difficult
to admit that he is struggling. He is a man who crossed an ocean, who rebuilt a life from very
little. He is not supposed to be brought low by something he only witnessed.

And yet, standing at the drive-through window each afternoon, watching cars pull up and strangers
reach for their orders, Karim finds himself calculating exits, watching hands, bracing for something
he knows, rationally, is unlikely to come. The knowledge offers him no relief. He simply waits, and
works, and carries it.
