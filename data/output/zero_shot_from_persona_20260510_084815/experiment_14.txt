# Clinical Vignette: Elena R.

Elena is a 54-year-old Latin American woman who has spent the better part of her adult life in
service as a combat engineer. She is married, and by most outward measures, she carries herself with
the kind of steadiness that decades of military discipline tend to produce — precise in her speech,
deliberate in her movements, rarely the first to show what is happening underneath.

The incident that reorganized her inner life occurred during a deployment in the early 2000s, when
Elena's unit was tasked with clearing a route suspected of IED placement. She had done this work
hundreds of times. She knew the ground, trusted her training, trusted her team. On this particular
morning, the detonation came without warning. The soldier directly ahead of her — a 22-year-old from
Texas named Marcus whom she had mentored for eight months — was killed instantly. Elena was thrown
by the blast and regained consciousness to find herself partially buried, her ears ringing, her
hands already moving toward Marcus before her mind had fully caught up to what had happened. She
held him. She has never fully let go of that moment.

In the years immediately following her return, Elena told herself she was managing. She stayed
active in her role, took on leadership responsibilities, and kept her grief tightly organized inside
a professional identity that had always rewarded composure. Her husband noticed she had become
quieter, more watchful, but she attributed this to maturity, to the natural gravity that comes with
having seen serious things. She did not disagree with him out loud, but she knew the truth was
something different.

Now, at 54, Elena finds that the distance she once maintained from that morning is collapsing. She
wakes most nights between two and four in the morning, not always from a nightmare but from a state
of hyperarousal she cannot immediately locate in time or place — heart hammering, body already
tensed as though the ground beneath her might give way. When she does dream, the dreams are not
dramatic recreations. They are sensory and fragmentary: the particular quality of the light that
morning, the weight of Marcus's body, the smell of disturbed earth. She wakes from these feeling not
frightened exactly, but hollowed out, as though something essential has been quietly removed.

Her days are structured around avoidance she doesn't always recognize as such. She no longer watches
news coverage involving military operations. She has stopped attending the unit reunions she once
valued. Driving on certain types of roads — particularly rural routes with uneven shoulders —
requires a deliberate internal negotiation she finds exhausting. Loud, sudden sounds in public
spaces cause a physical startle response that embarrasses her, followed immediately by a flush of
anger she struggles to contain. She has begun declining social invitations because the effort of
managing herself in unpredictable environments feels like more than she can afford.

With her husband, Elena is present but partial. She loves him and knows that he loves her, but she
finds herself unable to explain what is happening in a way that doesn't sound, to her own ears, like
weakness or complaint. She was an engineer. She solved problems with precision and intelligence.
This does not behave like a problem she can solve, and that fact alone carries its own particular
grief.

What she returns to most often, in the quiet hours when sleep won't come, is not the explosion
itself but the eight seconds or so that followed it — the moment before she fully understood what
had happened, when the world was still rearranging itself and Marcus was still, in some technical
sense, there. She has begun to understand that she has been living inside those eight seconds for
more than twenty years, waiting for them to resolve into something she can bear.
