**Vignette: Marcus**

Marcus is nineteen years old and works the morning shift at a burger chain about two miles from the
apartment he shares with his older cousin. He is quiet when he first arrives for sessions, pulling
at the strings of his hoodie and glancing toward the window more than he looks at the therapist. He
seems older than his age in some ways — careful, measured — and much younger in others.

Eighteen months ago, Marcus was living with his mother and younger sister in a small house in a
river town in the American Midwest when a flash flood tore through their neighborhood in the early
hours of a Tuesday morning. He remembers waking to his mother screaming his name. The water was
already at his knees by the time he reached the hallway. He grabbed his sister, who was seven at the
time, and the three of them climbed onto the roof through the attic hatch. They stayed there for
four hours in the dark, the water rising to the eaves below them, his sister shaking against his
chest, his mother praying quietly beside him. He did not pray. He watched the black water and
listened to what he describes as the sound of the neighborhood disappearing — fences giving way,
cars shifting, things he couldn't name moving through the dark.

They survived. Their house did not. They lost almost everything, and within a few weeks Marcus's
mother moved with his sister to stay with family in another state. Marcus, who had just turned
eighteen and felt he shouldn't uproot his mother's plans, stayed behind and moved in with his
cousin. He got the fast food job quickly, needing income, and tells himself he is fine because he
goes to work and pays his share of the rent.

But mornings are hard. He wakes between three and four a.m. most nights, heart already pounding,
convinced the floor is wet. He lies still in the dark and listens for water. He knows it isn't
rational, but knowing doesn't stop it. By the time he gets up for his shift, he is already
exhausted, running on two or three hours of sleep and a large coffee he drinks in the parking lot
before clocking in.

At work, the sound of the floor drain gurgling in the kitchen sends a jolt through him that he can't
explain to his coworkers. He has learned to position himself away from it. On rainy days, he checks
his phone compulsively for weather alerts, even when the sky is only overcast. He has called in sick
on days when the forecast showed heavy rain, not because he planned to but because he simply could
not make himself leave the apartment. His manager has noticed. Marcus tells him he's been having
headaches.

He doesn't talk about the flood with his coworkers or his cousin. When his cousin asks how he's
sleeping, Marcus says fine. He has texted his mother every day since the flood — sometimes three or
four times — needing to know she and his sister are okay, though he struggles to explain why the
need feels so urgent even on ordinary days.

What troubles Marcus most, he says, is that he doesn't feel like himself anymore. He used to play
pickup basketball on weekends and had a group of friends from high school he'd see regularly. Now he
declines invitations, not because he doesn't want to go but because being around groups of people in
spaces he can't control makes him edgy and worn out in a way he can't articulate. He sits in his
room instead, watching videos on his phone until his eyes give out.

He describes the night of the flood matter-of-factly, with few visible emotions, until he mentions
holding his sister on the roof. Then he stops talking and looks at the window for a long moment. He
says he keeps thinking about what would have happened if he hadn't woken up. He says he doesn't know
why he can't just move on.
