**Vignette: Priya**

Priya is a 44-year-old South Asian woman who works the morning shift at a large home goods store,
where she has spent the better part of twelve years folding linens, restocking shelves, and chatting
with regular customers who have come to know her by name. She is, by most accounts, warm and
dependable — the kind of person her manager calls "the backbone of the floor." But for the past two
years, Priya has been living inside a version of herself that she barely recognizes.

Two years ago, Priya was walking through the parking lot of a shopping center near her home when she
witnessed a man strike a young woman repeatedly — his girlfriend, it later emerged — before dragging
her toward a car. Priya froze. She screamed. She called out to others nearby. By the time she found
her phone and dialed emergency services, the car was gone. The young woman was found hours later,
badly injured but alive. Priya gave a statement to police and drove herself home. She did not sleep
that night, or the night after.

In the weeks that followed, Priya told herself she was fine. She went back to work. She cooked
dinner for her husband, Rajan, and attended her daughter's school events. But something had shifted
in a way she could not name. She began replaying the scene in her mind with a precision that felt
involuntary — the sound of the impact, the woman's face turning toward her, the particular gray of
the afternoon sky. These images arrived without warning: while she was pricing merchandise, while
she was standing at the stove, while she was lying next to her husband in the dark. They did not
feel like memories. They felt like it was happening again.

Priya stopped parking in open lots. She rerouted her commute to avoid the shopping center entirely,
adding fifteen minutes to a drive she had made effortlessly for years. At work, she gravitates
toward the back stockroom when she can, away from the large plate-glass windows that face the
parking area. When a customer raises their voice — even in ordinary frustration over a return — her
body responds before her mind can catch up: her heart pounds, her hands go cold, and she has to
excuse herself to the bathroom to breathe.

She has not told Rajan the full extent of what is happening. In her family, and in the community she
grew up in, there is an unspoken understanding that distress is managed privately, that one does not
burden others with what cannot be changed. She has said she is "a little tired lately," and he has
accepted this, though she notices him watching her sometimes with a concern he does not voice. She
feels the distance between them and does not know how to close it.

Sleep has become something she dreads. Most nights she lies awake for hours, hypervigilant to sounds
in the house — a car door outside, the settling of the walls — certain at some animal level that
something terrible is approaching. When she does sleep, she wakes from nightmares in which she is
watching and unable to move, her voice gone, her feet rooted to pavement. She wakes feeling not
rested but exhausted in a way that sleep cannot fix.

What troubles Priya most, when she allows herself to think about it, is the guilt. She knows,
rationally, that she could not have physically intervened. But there is a part of her that returns
obsessively to the moment she froze — replaying it, revising it, imagining a version of herself who
acted faster, more decisively, who somehow changed what happened. She carries this version of events
like a stone in her chest. She has begun to feel that she is not a good person, that something
essential in her failed when it mattered most. This belief has quietly hollowed out the pleasure she
once took in small things — a customer's gratitude, an evening meal, the particular light of early
morning before anyone else in the house is awake.
