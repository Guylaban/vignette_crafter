**Vignette: Priya**

Priya is a 32-year-old South Asian woman who works as a school counselor at an elementary school in
a mid-sized American city. She is married to a man she describes as patient and devoted, though she
acknowledges, with some guilt, that she has not let him fully in — not since she came back.

Six years ago, Priya was visiting extended family in her parents' home country when the region
descended into sudden, sustained violence. What began as a political crisis escalated within days
into open conflict. She was there for what was supposed to be a three-week visit. She stayed,
trapped, for nearly three months. She witnessed shelling in the neighborhood where her aunt lived.
She saw a man pulled from a car two streets over. She spent nights in a basement with her cousins,
listening to sounds she still cannot name out loud, telling herself that morning would bring quiet
and often being wrong.

She made it home. She told people she was fine — grateful, lucky, relieved. And for a time, she
believed that was true.

The trouble came quietly at first. She stopped sleeping well, waking at 2 or 3 in the morning with
her heart hammering, certain something was wrong inside the house. She began checking the locks —
front door, back door, the windows in the bedroom — sometimes two or three times before she could
lie back down. Her husband learned not to comment on it.

At work, Priya is warm and capable. Her students love her. She has a gift for making children feel
safe, which she thinks about sometimes with a complicated feeling she cannot quite untangle. She is
good at sitting with other people's fear. Her own is another matter.

The hardest days are the unpredictable ones. A car backfiring in the school parking lot once sent
her into the supply closet, where she stood with her back against the shelving, breathing through
her mouth, waiting for something she could not name to pass. She told a colleague she had a
migraine. Certain sounds — low-flying aircraft, fireworks, even a particular quality of silence
before a thunderstorm — pull her back in ways she finds humiliating and exhausting. She is a
counselor. She knows what is happening to her. That knowledge does not stop it.

She finds herself screening the news obsessively, then going days without looking at it at all. When
conflict abroad appears on the television, she leaves the room. She has asked her husband to watch
his news programs with headphones. He does, without complaint, and she hates that she needed to ask.

Priya has noticed she is less present with her students than she used to be. She catches herself
going through the motions during sessions — nodding, reflecting, asking the right questions — while
something in her remains sealed off, watching from a distance. She worries about this more than
almost anything else. Helping children feel heard is the whole point of what she does, and she is
afraid she is becoming someone who only performs that.

She does not talk about what happened in the basement, or about the man pulled from the car, or
about the particular way the light looked on the third morning when they did not know if they would
be evacuated. She has told her husband the broad outline. She has told her mother almost nothing.
There is a version of the story she tells, and then there is the actual story, and she keeps those
two things very carefully apart.

She came to therapy, she says, because she does not want to keep doing that. She is tired of being
vigilant. She is tired of the locks and the headphones and the supply closet. She wants, she says
quietly, to stop bracing for something that is already over — and to figure out why some part of her
cannot seem to believe that it is.
