# Marcus

Marcus is a 63-year-old Māori construction worker who has spent most of his adult life building
things with his hands — foundations, frameworks, structures meant to last. There is something
quietly ironic in this, he admits, because for nearly eleven years the foundation of his own home
was rotting from the inside.

His wife, Diane, was the kind of woman who could fill a room with warmth when she wanted to. She was
also the kind of woman who, when the drinking started, could empty that same room of every safe
feeling he had ever known. The physical violence began gradually — a shove during an argument, a
thrown plate that caught his shoulder — and then accelerated into something he still struggles to
name without lowering his voice. She broke two of his ribs during what would turn out to be the
second-to-last year of their marriage. He told the foreman on his site that he'd taken a fall from
scaffolding. Nobody questioned it. Men on construction sites fall.

Diane died of liver failure eight years ago. Marcus had not left her by then. He has spent
considerable time trying to understand why, and the closest he can get is that leaving felt more
dangerous than staying, and that somewhere along the way he had stopped believing he deserved
better. When she died, he did not feel relief the way he had imagined he might. He felt something
closer to freefall.

In the years since, Marcus has continued working — he is good at his job, respected on site, the
kind of man younger workers go to when they need something done properly. But work has become one of
the few places where he feels anything resembling competence. The noise and physicality of a
construction site keep him anchored in the present in a way that the silence of his house does not.
He lives alone now, and evenings are the worst of it. He sits with the television on but rarely
watches it. What he is actually doing is listening — for footsteps, for a particular shift in the
air, for the sound that used to precede the worst of it. His body has not yet learned that the
threat is gone.

He sleeps poorly. When he does sleep, he often wakes from dreams that place him back in the kitchen
of their old house, or in the hallway, or in the specific geometry of trying to make himself small
in a space that offered nowhere to hide. He wakes with his heart hammering and sometimes does not
know, for a few disorienting seconds, where or when he is. He has taken to sleeping with the light
on.

Marcus does not talk about any of this with his workmates. The idea of it — of being known as a man
who was beaten by his wife — produces a shame so acute it functions almost like a physical
sensation, a tightening across his chest. He is aware, in some part of himself, that this shame is
not entirely rational. He cannot talk himself out of it. He has become increasingly withdrawn from
his adult children, cancelling plans, offering vague excuses, finding the effort of being present in
a family setting overwhelming in ways he cannot explain to them.

where he was working, and Marcus found himself walking away quickly, hands shaking, needing twenty
