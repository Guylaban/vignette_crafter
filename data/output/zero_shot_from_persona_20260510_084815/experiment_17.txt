# The Weight of the Water

Rajinder is eighty years old, a retired Coast Guard officer who spent more than three decades
patrolling the waters off the western coast. His hands still carry the look of a working man —
broad, weathered, capable. He sits in the chair across from his therapist with the careful posture
of someone who was trained never to show weakness, though lately that posture costs him more than it
used to.

It happened forty-one years ago, but for Rajinder, it might as well have been last Tuesday.

He was the commanding officer on a rescue operation during a particularly brutal monsoon season. A
fishing vessel had capsized, and his crew was working in near-zero visibility, the sea churning and
black. In the chaos of the rescue, Rajinder made the call to move the cutter forward to reach a
cluster of survivors. He did not see the young deckhand — a boy of nineteen named Pradeep, who had
gone over the rail to throw a line — until it was too late. The propeller caught him. Pradeep
survived, but he lost his right arm and the use of his left hand. Rajinder pulled him from the water
himself. He remembers the color of the water around the boy. He will always remember it.

In the immediate aftermath, the service conducted its inquiry and cleared Rajinder of wrongdoing.
Visibility had been impossible. He had followed protocol. His superiors shook his hand. He returned
to duty within two weeks because that was what you did. He had a wife, Meena, two young sons, a
career. He did not speak of it again for decades.

But the silence was not the same as healing.

For years, Rajinder managed by staying busy — longer shifts, volunteer work, then after retirement,
tending his garden with an almost militant dedication. Meena knew something lived in him that he
would not name, but she also knew not to push. When she died four years ago, the structure that had
held everything in place began to give way.

Now Rajinder sleeps poorly, waking most nights between two and three in the morning with his heart
hammering, convinced he can hear the engine of the cutter. He does not always know, in those first
disoriented seconds, that he is in his bedroom in Chennai and not on that deck. He has stopped
watching television programs that involve the ocean, water, or emergency rescue — a category that
has grown surprisingly large. He changed the channel during a documentary about the Coast Guard and
did not turn the television back on for a week.

He avoids his former colleagues almost entirely now. When one of them telephoned last year to invite
him to a unit reunion, Rajinder told his son he wasn't feeling well. He was not lying, exactly. The
thought of standing in a room full of men who knew what happened — even men who had absolved him —
made it difficult to breathe.

What troubles Rajinder most, and what he speaks of with a quiet, exhausted precision, is the belief
that the inquiry was wrong. Not procedurally — he understands what the report said. But he was the
commanding officer. He gave the order. Pradeep lost his arms because of a decision Rajinder made,
and no document with an official seal has ever changed that arithmetic. He has tried, on several
occasions, to locate Pradeep. He has not succeeded, and he is not entirely sure what he would say if
he did.

He tells his therapist that he is not afraid of dying. He is eighty years old and has made his peace
with that. What he cannot make peace with is the idea that he will die without Pradeep ever knowing
that there has not been a single day — not one, in forty-one years — when Rajinder has not thought
about him.

He folds his weathered hands in his lap and looks out the window. The sky above the city is the
pale, washed-out blue of a morning after heavy rain.
