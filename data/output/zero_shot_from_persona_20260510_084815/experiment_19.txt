# The Weight of What the Body Remembers

Priya is a 45-year-old South Asian woman who has worked as a plumber for nearly two decades. She is
the kind of person who has always prided herself on being capable — someone who can diagnose a
problem, get under a sink, and fix what is broken. She has built her small business with her hands,
and for a long time, her body felt like something she could rely on absolutely.

Three years ago, that changed.

It began with fatigue she attributed to overwork, then a persistent pain in her abdomen she assumed
was stress. By the time she went to the emergency room, her doctors discovered an aggressive form of
ovarian cancer that had already progressed significantly. What followed were eight months of
surgery, chemotherapy, and hospitalizations — a period Priya describes as feeling like she was
"trapped inside a body that was trying to kill me." She remembers lying in the hospital during her
third round of chemotherapy, genuinely uncertain whether she would survive the week. A nurse told
her, gently, to prepare her family for the possibility that she might not make it home. Priya called
her mother that night and said goodbye.

She did survive. Her cancer is currently in remission. But the woman who came home from the hospital
is not quite the same woman who walked into the emergency room.

Priya returned to work about fourteen months ago, and on the surface, she has rebuilt much of her
life. She takes on jobs, manages her schedule, and shows up. But inside, something remains
fractured. Several nights a week she wakes between two and four in the morning, heart pounding,
certain that something is catastrophically wrong. The dreams are vivid — she is back in the hospital
bed, watching an IV drip, unable to move or speak while doctors talk around her as though she is
already gone. She lies awake for hours afterward, running her hands over her abdomen, checking for
pain.

During the day, her body keeps its own kind of vigil. Any physical sensation — a cramp, a headache,
an unfamiliar ache from a long day of work — sends her into a spiral of dread that can last for
hours. She has gone to her GP four times in the past six months for symptoms that turned out to be
benign. She knows, intellectually, that this is happening. She cannot stop it. When she is crawling
through a tight crawlspace or cutting pipe, her mind will suddenly return to the hospital without
warning — the smell of the ward, the cold weight of the IV needle, the sound of the monitor beside
her bed. She has to stop what she is doing and breathe until the present comes back.

Priya has become quieter socially. She was once close to a group of friends from her community, but
she finds gatherings exhausting in a way she struggles to articulate. People ask how she is doing
and she says fine, because the truth — that she is terrified every single day that the cancer will
return, that she no longer trusts her own body, that she sometimes feels she is living on borrowed
time that could be revoked without notice — feels too large and too frightening to put into a
conversation over dinner.

She is single, and she lives alone, which means that most nights the fear has no company. She keeps
the television on when she sleeps, not for entertainment but for noise — proof that the world is
still moving, that she is still in it.

What Priya finds most difficult to explain is this: she survived. She knows she is supposed to feel
grateful. And she does, in moments. But survival did not return her to who she was before. Some part
of her remains in that hospital room, listening to her own heartbeat and waiting to find out whether
it will stop.
