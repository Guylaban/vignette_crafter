**The Weight of Raised Hands**

Maria is a 38-year-old Colombian-American woman who has spent the better part of her adult life
fighting for other people's safety. As a community organizer in a mid-sized city, she coordinates
tenant rights campaigns, leads neighborhood safety coalitions, and shows up at city council meetings
with a clipboard and a voice that doesn't waver. People who know her from that world often describe
her as fearless. She is not sure they would say the same if they could see her at home.

She grew up in a household where fear arrived without warning. Her mother, overwhelmed by poverty,
immigration stress, and her own unresolved history, was unpredictable in ways that kept Maria
perpetually on guard. The beatings were not constant, but they were severe enough and erratic enough
that Maria learned early to read the room — the particular quality of silence before the storm, the
way her mother's jaw tightened, the sound of footsteps in the hallway at night. She never knew which
version of her mother would walk through the door. She learned to be very still and very small.

By adolescence she had buried most of it. She was the first in her family to attend college, then
graduate school, then to build a career defined by advocacy and collective power. There was
something clarifying about channeling vigilance into purpose — watching out for a whole community
felt more manageable than watching out for herself.

The unburying began three years ago, when her partner, Tomás, raised his hand during an argument —
not to strike her, he was reaching for something on the shelf behind her — and Maria's body
responded before her mind could intervene. She dropped to the floor, arms over her head, shaking.
The shame of that moment still visits her regularly. She knows Tomás would never hurt her. She also
knows that knowing has not been enough.

Since then, certain things have become difficult in ways she struggles to explain. She cannot
tolerate raised voices, even joyful ones at community meetings. When a colleague pounds the table to
make a point, her heart hammers and her vision narrows. She has started sitting near exits. She has
started canceling things. Last spring she drove forty minutes to a rally she had spent weeks
organizing and sat in the parking lot until it was over, unable to make herself get out of the car.

At home she is hypervigilant in ways that exhaust her and confuse Tomás. She checks the locks twice,
sometimes three times. She wakes at two or three in the morning and lies rigid, listening,
cataloguing sounds. She has not slept a full night in longer than she can remember. When she does
dream, she is often a child again in that apartment, waiting.

She carries a persistent low-grade shame that she describes as feeling like a bruise that never
fully heals. She has begun to believe, on some cellular level, that she is fundamentally damaged —
that the woman who stands at a podium and demands dignity for others is performing something that
does not actually belong to her. She finds it nearly impossible to accept comfort from Tomás,
pulling away when he reaches for her, not because she doesn't love him but because closeness has
always felt like the moment just before something is taken away.

What strikes Maria most is the disconnect. She can walk into a room full of hostile landlords and
hold her ground without blinking. She can absorb bureaucratic cruelty on behalf of strangers and
return the next day ready to fight again. But a particular angle of afternoon light coming through a
kitchen window — the same light that came through her mother's kitchen — can stop her completely.
She stands there until it passes, then goes back to whatever she was doing, folding laundry or
making coffee, trying not to think about how much of her life she has spent waiting for something
terrible to happen.
