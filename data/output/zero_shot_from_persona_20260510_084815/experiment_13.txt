**Case Vignette: Marcus, Age 32**

Marcus is a 32-year-old married man of European descent who works as a private tutor, primarily
teaching mathematics and science to secondary school students from his home office. He presents as
articulate and self-aware, though visibly tense during sessions, often positioning his chair so that
he can see the door. He speaks carefully, as though measuring each word before releasing it.

The trouble began three years ago, when a former university acquaintance — a woman Marcus had known
briefly during his postgraduate studies — began contacting him with unusual frequency. At first it
seemed innocuous: a message here, a comment on a social media post there. Marcus assumed it was
simple friendliness and responded politely. Within weeks, however, the contact escalated. She began
appearing outside his home. She sent packages to his address. She emailed his tutoring clients,
claiming to be his colleague, and later his wife. For eleven months, Marcus lived with the creeping,
suffocating sensation that nowhere was safe. He changed his phone number twice. He deactivated his
professional website. He and his wife, Elaine, installed additional locks and a security camera, and
still Marcus found himself lying awake at night listening to every sound the house made.

The stalking ended only when the woman was issued a restraining order following her arrest for
harassment. Legally, the chapter closed. Psychologically, it did not.

Marcus describes the period that followed the restraining order as one of profound disorientation.
He had spent nearly a year in a state of hypervigilance, and his nervous system, it seems, did not
receive the memo that the threat had passed. He still wakes between two and four in the morning,
heart hammering, certain that someone is outside. Some nights he gets up and checks the security
camera footage, scrolling back through hours of empty footage before he can allow himself to lie
down again. He rarely falls back to sleep.

His work has suffered in ways that quietly devastate him. Tutoring, which he once found genuinely
fulfilling, now requires him to manage a steady undercurrent of dread each time a new student
enquires about his services. He scrutinises every email from an unfamiliar address. He has declined
to take on several students because something in their initial message felt — he struggles to
articulate it — *off*. He knows, intellectually, that this caution is disproportionate. Knowing does
not seem to help.

Elaine has been patient, but Marcus can see the strain on her. He has become withdrawn in ways he
cannot fully explain to her. Intimacy feels complicated now — not because of anything between them,
but because Marcus carries a persistent sense of contamination, as though the stalking has left a
residue on his sense of self that he cannot quite wash off. He feels, at times, that he was made
into something passive and frightened, and he resents that feeling deeply.

Certain things still stop him cold. A notification sound from his phone can send a jolt of
adrenaline through him before he has even registered what it is. Walking to the corner shop, he
finds himself scanning the street in a way that feels automatic and exhausting. Last spring, he saw
a woman with a similar build to his stalker on the high street and spent the remainder of the day
unable to concentrate, replaying the sighting, reassuring himself, then doubting his reassurance.

He avoids talking about the stalking with most people, partly because he anticipates not being taken
seriously — a man, stalked — and partly because putting it into words makes it feel present again in
a way he finds difficult to manage. He came to therapy, he says, because Elaine asked him to, and
because he is tired. Tired of checking. Tired of bracing. Tired of being someone who is afraid of
his own front door.

He wants, more than anything, to feel ordinary again.
