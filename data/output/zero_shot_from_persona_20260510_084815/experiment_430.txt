# The Weight of Waiting

Rajan is a fifty-year-old South Asian man who has spent the better part of his career bringing
characters to life — giving movement and emotion to figures that exist only in imagination. He is
meticulous by nature, someone who notices the small things: the way a hand should curve, the precise
timing of a blink. It is this same attentiveness that made what happened to his son three years ago
so unbearable. He noticed everything, and he has not been able to stop noticing since.

His son, Arjun, was seven when he collapsed at school. The call came on an ordinary Tuesday
afternoon while Rajan was at his desk reviewing storyboards. Within hours, Arjun was in a pediatric
intensive care unit, diagnosed with acute bacterial meningitis. For eleven days, Rajan and his wife
Priya lived in the hospital, sleeping in chairs, watching monitors, learning to read numbers on
screens the way Rajan reads frames — obsessively, looking for any flicker of change. There were two
nights when the doctors prepared them for the possibility that Arjun would not survive. Rajan stood
in a hospital corridor on the second of those nights and felt something inside him go very quiet, as
though a part of him had already begun to grieve.

Arjun survived. He recovered fully, which everyone around Rajan treats as the end of the story. But
for Rajan, it was not the end. It was more like the moment a film reel gets stuck — the same frames
playing over and over, burning through.

He still sees the ICU when he closes his eyes. The particular green of the monitor light. The sound
of the ventilator. His son's small hand, so still, threaded with lines. These images arrive without
invitation — during morning meetings, while he is eating dinner, in the dark space between waking
and sleep. Some nights he jolts awake certain that the phone is ringing, certain it is the hospital.
He lies rigid beside Priya, heart hammering, unable to explain to her why he cannot simply be
grateful.

At work, Rajan finds himself struggling with things that once came naturally. He used to love scenes
involving children — their movement has a particular looseness he always found joyful to animate.
Now he avoids those assignments when he can, and when he cannot, he sits at his tablet feeling
something cold move through him. A child's face on screen, even a drawn one, can send him somewhere
he does not want to go. His colleagues have noticed that he has become quieter, more withdrawn. He
attributes it to getting older.

He monitors Arjun constantly. He checks on him multiple times each night, standing in the doorway of
his son's room watching the rise and fall of his chest. He has memorized the schedule of Arjun's
school day and feels a low, persistent dread whenever Arjun is out of contact for longer than
expected. Priya has gently suggested that this is too much. Rajan knows she is right, and he cannot
stop.

What troubles him most, perhaps, is the anger — sudden and disproportionate, arriving over small
things, directed at people he loves. He does not recognize himself in those moments. Afterward he
feels ashamed, and the shame sits alongside the fear and the exhaustion in a way that has made him
feel profoundly alone, even in his own home.

He carries the belief, quiet but persistent, that what happened once can happen again at any moment
— that the ordinary world is a thin surface over something terrible. He goes through the motions of
his days, animating characters who feel things he has stopped letting himself feel, waiting for a
disaster that, in some part of his mind, never quite ended.
