**Vignette: Fatima, Age 63**

Fatima grew up understanding that family is everything. Born in Lebanon and raised in a household
where her mother's voice was the center of gravity for every room she entered, she carried that
understanding across decades and an ocean, settling eventually in the United States but never quite
leaving behind the sense that her mother was the axis around which her life quietly turned. For
thirty years she built a careful, orderly life as an auditor — columns that balance, discrepancies
that resolve, the quiet satisfaction of a world made legible through numbers. She was good at her
work precisely because she trusted nothing to chance and verified everything twice.

Three years ago, her mother was struck by a car while crossing a street near her home in Beirut.
Fatima was in her office when her brother called. She remembers the exact spreadsheet she had open,
the half-eaten orange on her desk, the way the afternoon light fell across her monitor. She flew to
Lebanon within hours, but her mother had already slipped into a coma by the time she landed. Fatima
sat beside her hospital bed for eleven days, holding a hand that no longer squeezed back, watching
machines breathe for a woman who had once filled every silence with warmth and certainty. Her mother
died on a Tuesday morning without regaining consciousness. Fatima had stepped out of the room for
twenty minutes to drink a cup of coffee.

She has not forgiven herself for those twenty minutes.

Back in the United States, colleagues noticed she seemed distracted — a word that would have been
unthinkable to anyone who knew her before. She returns to the hospital room constantly in her mind,
replaying the sequence of events with the same methodical attention she brings to a financial audit,
searching for the error she must have made. She is convinced that if she had stayed, something would
have been different, though she cannot say what, exactly. The logic doesn't hold and she knows it,
but knowing changes nothing.

She sleeps poorly. Most nights she wakes between two and four in the morning with her heart
hammering, having dreamed of her mother's face — sometimes peaceful, sometimes accusing, sometimes
simply absent in a way that is worse than either. She has rearranged her apartment so that a
photograph of her mother, which used to hang in the hallway, is now turned face-down in a drawer.
She cannot explain this fully, only that seeing it unexpectedly causes something to seize in her
chest that takes a long time to release.

At work, the numbers no longer offer the comfort they once did. She checks her reports four and five
times now, not from professional diligence but from a fear she cannot quite name — a sense that if
she looks away, something will slip past her again. Her supervisor has gently noted that her
turnaround time has slowed. She has not told anyone at work what happened. In her culture, grief is
a private matter, and she is a private woman.

She has become cautious about answering her phone. When an unknown number appears, her body responds
before her mind can — a cold flooding sensation in her arms, a sudden certainty that something
catastrophic is happening to someone she loves. She has two younger brothers still living in
Lebanon. She calls them frequently, sometimes twice in a day, then feels embarrassed by her own need
and does not call again for a week.

She describes herself as someone who has always been self-sufficient, who has lived alone
contentedly for most of her adult life, who has never needed much. She says this without bitterness,
as a statement of fact. But there is something in the way she says it now — a flatness, a slight
pause before the word *contentedly* — that suggests she is no longer entirely sure it is true, or
that self-sufficiency, which once felt like strength, has begun to feel like something lonelier than
she has words for.
