At 79, Leonard was an African American man who had spent most of his adult life working in a
warehouse, where the noise, movement, and steady routines had long felt easier to manage than being
alone with his thoughts. He was divorced now and living with the quiet that came after years of
marriage, but quiet was not something he welcomed. When he first came for help, he described a
lifelong tendency to keep himself around other people, even when he did not especially want company,
because being by himself left him vulnerable to memories he could not quite grasp yet could still
feel in his body. He had built a way of getting through the day by staying busy, keeping
conversations going, and, when that was not enough, drinking or using medication or drugs to dull
the edge of what would otherwise rise up in him. He also knew, with some embarrassment, that he
sometimes picked arguments or created drama just to shift the focus away from himself and whatever
was stirring inside.

Leonard traced the beginning of all this back to childhood, to a home where domestic violence was a
recurring and frightening part of life. He remembered being a boy and hearing a door slam hard
enough to shake him, followed by shouting and the sound of adults moving with force through the
house. The details did not come to him as a neat story. Even now, when he tried to explain what had
happened, he said the memory was hard to reach deliberately, as if his mind could not bring up the
scene in a complete way. What he had instead were disconnected pieces: the crack of a door opening
or slamming, the sharp smell of alcohol on someone’s breath, the feel of standing outside or near
the house in a natural setting while something awful unfolded inside. At times the fragments did not
form pictures at all, only a full wave of fear and helplessness that arrived without warning. He
would be standing in the warehouse, or sitting at home, and suddenly feel the same terror and dread
he had felt as a child, even when he could not see the event clearly in his mind.

Certain moments in the present made those pieces more vivid. A door slamming in the warehouse, or
the heavy roll-up door closing with a sharp bang, could make him jolt and look around before he was
fully aware of why. The smell of alcohol was especially unsettling; if a coworker had been drinking
the night before, or if he passed someone carrying alcohol at a family gathering, Leonard would
tense and feel an immediate sense of unease. He also noticed that being in nature could be difficult
when it resembled the outdoor spaces around the house where he had once stood as a child, listening
from outside and waiting for the noise to stop. In parks or wooded areas, or even when the warehouse
dock opened onto an expanse of trees and open ground, he sometimes felt pulled back into that old
atmosphere of danger, as if the surroundings themselves were too similar to what he had known
before.

Over the years, Leonard learned to arrange his life around these reactions without fully naming
them. He kept himself surrounded by people because solitude gave the fragments room to surface. He
drank or used substances when the feelings became too strong or when the day had left him stripped
of his usual defenses. He stirred up conflict when he felt himself getting too inward, because
upsetting someone else seemed, for a moment, more manageable than facing the memories that hovered
just beneath awareness. These habits affected his relationships and made retirement impossible to
imagine, so he stayed with work longer than he might have otherwise, relying on the warehouse
environment to keep him occupied and near others. Even there, though, he had to navigate the
occasional slam of a door, the smell of alcohol on a colleague, or the discomfort of being sent
outdoors to check a shipment or wait near the loading area.

By the time he sought help, Leonard was worn down by how often the past could break in without a
picture, arriving instead as bodily terror and scattered scraps he could not assemble. He was not
asking to remember everything. What brought him in was the exhaustion of living around those
moments, the strain on his relationships, and the fear that the same old reactions had narrowed his
life more than he had admitted. At this stage, he hoped for some relief from the constant need to
avoid being alone with himself, and he was most afraid that if he stopped drinking, stopped filling
every silence, or stopped distracting himself with conflict, the full force of those childhood
memories would come back all at once and leave him overwhelmed.
