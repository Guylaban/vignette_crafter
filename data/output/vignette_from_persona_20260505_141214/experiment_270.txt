craft. In conversation he was reserved but attentive, and he spoke of his work with a kind of worn

tries to put it into words, the experience seems to resist language, as if the most frightening
parts were only ever registered as flashes. What remained were pieces that do not fit together
cleanly, but that return to him with an intensity that makes ordinary memories seem pale by
comparison. He described them as more real than the things happening around him now, as if the scene
were still close enough to touch.

Over time, certain places and moments began to feel loaded with that same danger. Funerals and
cemeteries were especially difficult for him, not because he was unable to understand grief, but
because the atmosphere of graves and mourning brought back the unmistakable closeness of death from
the time he fled. Even a grief-related event could leave him tense and watchful, wanting to get away
before anything in the air changed. The same was true when he heard cars approaching suddenly,
especially in a street or parking lot where the sound seemed to come up behind him. In those moments
he would stop and look around, his attention narrowing to the possible direction of threat. The
presence of police was similarly activating. A uniform at a distance was enough to pull his body
into alertness, and he became careful about where he stood, how he moved, and whether he was being
noticed. He had come to believe that he had to be especially careful because one never knew what
could happen next, and that sense of uncertainty was never far from him.

his surroundings for signs that something might go wrong, even in ordinary settings. In the workshop
where he made pottery, his gaze would drift from the wheel to the door, to the street outside, to
any approaching vehicle he could hear. In the neighborhood, a passing car could interrupt his focus
and send him checking who was near, where they were standing, and whether there was any reason to
leave. He also found himself avoiding any situation that might involve disagreement or
confrontation. If there was tension with a shop owner, a neighbor, or even a family discussion that

an argument, and he often explained this in plain terms: he could not deal with even the slightest
upset. At times this had narrowed his life more than he liked to admit. He and his wife had once
attended community gatherings more freely, but over time he began to decline invitations, especially

found that such feelings left him vulnerable to disappointment and made him uneasy about what might
follow.

clay and feel that the steadiness, concentration, and confidence he used to rely on were no longer
there in the same way. This was painful not only because of the work itself, but because it touched
his identity as a husband, craftsman, and elder in his family. His wife had noticed that he was
quieter and more guarded, and while she remained supportive, their world had gradually become
smaller and more careful.

understand why these moments still struck him with such force and why the memories came back in
pieces that felt so vivid and immediate. More than anything, he hoped for relief from the sense that
danger could appear at any moment and that he would not be able to meet it.
