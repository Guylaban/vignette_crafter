Omar was 30, a Middle Eastern American tax inspector who had once prided himself on being precise,
steady, and in control of details that other people missed. Since a life-threatening motor vehicle
collision two years earlier, he had come to see his life as divided into before and after. The crash
happened on an evening drive home after work, when another car struck his vehicle with enough force
to spin it and leave him trapped in the immediate confusion of metal, noise, and shattered glass.
What stayed with him most was not a single dramatic image but the sudden, disorienting sense that
ordinary travel had become dangerous in an instant. In the weeks that followed, he found that
certain moments would bring the scene back with startling force: passing a car that had been hit in
another accident could make him picture the wreck unfolding in front of him; the smell of alcohol
could pull him back to the sense that someone careless or impaired might be the reason a collision
happened; and seeing a car that looked similar to the one involved in the crash could make him tense
before he had even registered why. He described these experiences not as distant memories but as
brief, intrusive returns to the same sickening certainty that disaster could arrive without warning.

At first Omar tried to keep functioning by pushing the memory aside, but over time he began spending
long stretches on his phone, moving from games to internet searches and back again, not because he
enjoyed it so much as because staying absorbed in a screen gave him something to do with his
attention besides think. Even then, he often found himself drifting back to the crash image,
especially at night when he was alone. He also began restricting food, skipping meals or eating very
little, describing it as another way of coping when his mind felt too full. These habits gradually
shaped his days. He became more avoidant of driving and, when he could manage it, refused to be a
passenger in a vehicle as well. That changed how he moved through ordinary life and made even
routine errands feel complicated. As a tax inspector, he had long been used to traveling between
locations and managing his schedule independently, but now he arranged his work and personal life
around not needing a car whenever possible. The practical consequences reached beyond inconvenience:
he felt less like the capable man he had been, and more dependent on others than he could tolerate.

Omar’s sense of himself also changed in ways that he found deeply troubling. He had come to believe
he could not rely on himself, especially in situations that required quick judgment or calmness
under pressure. He said that the crash had shown him he was not as dependable as he had assumed, and
that realization lingered whenever he had to make decisions or imagine being in a situation where he
might need to act fast. He no longer felt capable of doing the things that had defined him, and this
was especially painful because his work had once been tied to competence, discipline, and a
reputation for being unshaken. Instead, he now watched himself with disappointment, comparing the
man who could once handle long days and complicated cases with the version of himself who avoided
cars and spent hours numbing out on his phone. He also worried that he would never be able to feel
normal emotions again. Rather than experiencing himself as simply anxious or shaken, he described
feeling changed in a more fundamental way, as though something essential had gone missing and could
not be recovered.

In daily life, his alertness never fully switched off. Unexpected or loud sounds made him jump, and
he found himself scanning his surroundings, especially in places where vehicles moved close by or
where he might hear sudden noise without warning. A slammed door, a sharp honk, or a bang in the
street could make him tense before he had time to think. He was constantly watchful, as if his body
were waiting for another impact. Those moments were exhausting, but they also felt necessary to him,
because being unprepared seemed even worse. Over time the strain of this vigilance, his avoidance of
driving, and the ways he had shrunk his world brought him to seek help. What finally pushed him to
ask for treatment was the realization that he was organizing more and more of his life around fear
and around the belief that the man he used to be was gone. He came hoping to understand whether he
could ever trust himself again, and fearing that if nothing changed, he would remain trapped in a
life that no longer felt like his own.
