At 77, Minh Tran still carried himself with the careful posture of a surgeon, though in recent
months he had begun to feel less steady in the operating room and more easily thrown by small
disturbances around him. Born in South East Asia and now long settled in the United States with his
wife, he remained proud of the life he had built through medicine. Yet the event he had witnessed
years earlier had come to sit uncomfortably beneath that identity. During a long shift, he had been
present when another person was badly burned. The details that stayed with him were not arranged
neatly in his mind. He could not always place the sequence in order, and when he tried to recall it,
the memory felt oddly remote, as if it belonged to someone else rather than to him. At times he had
no clear picture at all, only a sudden rush of the same emotional force he had felt then, the same
dread and helpless intensity, arriving without an image to anchor it.

Certain sensations could bring that state on immediately. A hot patch on his skin, a temperature
that felt too close to the conditions of the original event, could make him recoil before he had
time to think. The smell of something burnt was especially potent. It could appear in the kitchen,
in a hallway, in a parking lot where someone had left a cigarette smoldering, and for a moment he
would be back in the atmosphere of danger, alert in a way that felt physical rather than remembered.
He also noticed that stress in general made him vulnerable; on days when the demands of the
hospital, family responsibilities, or the ordinary pressures of aging accumulated, he felt his body
bracing itself as though another crisis were about to break open. He had come to believe that his
body kept reacting as if he were still in danger even when he was safe, and that conviction had
become hard to shake.

In the months after the event, Minh found himself trying to shut it away. He worked hard not to
think about it and learned to steer his mind elsewhere whenever the memory began to rise. He avoided
television programmes and films that showed similar injuries, and he skipped articles or news
reports that might contain the same kind of scene. Positive moments became difficult in their own
way. When his wife tried to draw him into pleasure or celebration, he sometimes pushed those
feelings away because they seemed unsafe, as if relaxation would make him careless and leave him
exposed to another shock. He did not describe this to others in dramatic terms; instead, he simply
became more reserved, less willing to linger in joy, as though keeping emotion at a distance might
keep him intact.

The sense of threat remained close to the surface. Minh was jumpy and easily startled, especially by
unexpected or loud sounds. In the hospital, a dropped metal tray, an abrupt alarm, or a raised voice
in the corridor would make him tense before he could identify what had happened. At home, a sharp
noise from the television or a pan hitting the stove could set his heart racing. Even outside the
clinic he stayed on alert when he heard sudden sounds, scanning automatically for what might be
wrong. That vigilance had become part of how he moved through the day, and he spoke of needing to be
especially careful because you never know what can happen next. Alongside that caution, there were
moments when he took too many risks or did things that could cause him harm, as if ordinary concern
for his own safety had become less convincing to him than the urge to push forward and ignore the
warning signs.

These experiences left him unsettled about himself. Minh could be exacting and outwardly composed,
but privately he had started to wonder whether there was something wrong with him as a person. He
was troubled that, despite his profession and years of training, his own body seemed unwilling to
stand down. He worried about what it meant that a smell, a sound, or a temperature on his skin could
pull him into such distress. The contrast between who he had been and how he now felt was painful to
him, and it made him question his own reliability.

What brought him to seek help was the accumulation of these moments: the startle that embarrassed
him in public, the constant readiness that exhausted him, and the sense that he could no longer
simply reason his way out of the reactions. He had begun to worry that he was living too narrowly,
avoiding reminders, holding back from pleasure, and carrying himself as if danger were always
nearby. At this stage, he wanted relief from the bodily alarm that kept returning and from the
private fear that something in him had become permanently altered.
