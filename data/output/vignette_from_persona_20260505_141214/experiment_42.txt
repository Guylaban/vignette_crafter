Malia was a 20-year-old Māori woman who had married young and was working as an interior designer
when she first came to treatment, carrying herself with the careful alertness of someone who had
learned to read a room before settling into it. A year earlier, while living with her romantic
partner, she had experienced repeated physical abuse that culminated in an assault she still
struggled to describe in a straight line. She could say that there were loud voices, sudden
movements, and the shock of realizing that the person she had trusted was the one hurting her. What
stayed most vividly with her was the sound of the blows and the way the fear seemed to fill the
whole space at once. Even now, when she tried to speak about it, she often lost the thread partway
through, staring down and restarting, as though the words could not hold together long enough to
make a complete account. She would begin with one detail, then stop, overwhelmed by a wave of guilt
that arrived as soon as she remembered it. She had come to believe, painfully and persistently, that
she should have seen it coming, should have left sooner, should have known how to prevent what
happened.

In the months that followed, Malia found herself increasingly organized around avoidance. Dark rooms
felt wrong to her now, and she would switch on every light in the house rather than stand in a dim
hallway or sit alone in the evening shadow. She avoided being alone in a room or a building, and if
her husband was out and she was at home, she would leave a television or radio on so the silence
would not gather around her. In her work as an interior designer, she noticed herself hesitating in
client homes if the lighting was low, especially in unfinished spaces where the shadows made her
feel exposed. She could still do the job, but she preferred to keep moving, keep talking, keep
herself from being caught in circumstances that reminded her of the event. Her wariness also
extended to men she did not know well. Being alone with men now felt unsafe in a way she could not
easily explain to other people; she would position herself near open doors, step back when a man
entered a room, and arrange meetings so that a colleague or family member might be present. If she
saw the offender, or even someone who resembled him in build or posture, her whole body seemed to
tighten before she had time to think. She would look down, turn away, or leave quickly, as if
distance could restore control.

That sense of danger did not stay in her thoughts alone. Malia described herself as superalert and
watchful, always scanning for what might happen next. When she walked to her car, she frequently
looked behind her to check whether someone was following her. She did the same in parking lots, in
hallways, and in the foyer of apartment buildings, her eyes moving automatically over doorways and
reflections. Her body felt constantly tense and ready to react, as though it had never learned that
the episode was over. Loud noises were particularly difficult. A slammed door, a dropped metal
object, or a sudden bang from a neighboring apartment could make her jolt sharply, shoulders braced
and breath held, as if the next strike might follow immediately. She described these moments as
happening before her mind caught up, her body reacting with a readiness that left her exhausted
afterward.

Her relationship to memory was equally distressing. Sometimes she would suddenly feel the full
emotional intensity of what happened without any clear image attached to it, a surge of dread and
shame that arrived in her body as much as in her mind. At other times she would recall fragments—the
sound, the dark, the sensation of being trapped—but not the full sequence. The missing pieces
frustrated her because she wanted to explain herself clearly, especially to her husband, yet she
often ended up feeling exposed and ashamed instead. The emotional weight of remembering was closely
bound up with guilt; the more she remembered, the more convinced she became that she had somehow
failed. She increasingly spoke about her life in global, bleak terms, saying that the trauma had
destroyed it, that she no longer belonged anywhere, and that people could not be trusted. Those
beliefs had sharpened over time, especially as she noticed how much she kept herself apart from
others and how difficult it had become to feel settled in any one place.

By the time she sought help, Malia was living with the quiet strain of these reactions every day.

avoidance of dark places and solitude, the startle at loud noises, and the fear she felt around men
were making ordinary life feel narrow and precarious. She wanted to be able to work without bracing
herself, to move through rooms without checking behind her, and to speak about what had happened
