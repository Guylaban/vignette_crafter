Yousef, a 40-year-old married Middle Eastern man, came to treatment after nearly a year of trying to
carry on as if nothing had changed. He worked as a paramedic, a job he had once experienced as
steady and familiar, but that changed after a call involving a close family member. One evening, he
was on duty when he received the kind of information every first responder dreads: a relative had
been badly injured and was being brought in. By the time he arrived, the scene was already full of
the details that now stayed with him—visible wounds, blood on skin and clothing, the sharp
antiseptic smell of the hospital corridors, and the urgency of people speaking over one another. He
found himself moving between professional response and the shock of seeing a loved one in that
condition, and what he remembers most is the sudden collapse of the distance that usually let him
function. In his telling, the event still does not feel like a finished memory; when it comes back,
it returns as if he is there again, with the same terror in his body, the same sights and sounds
crowding in at once.

Since then, Yousef has had moments when a reminder catches him without warning and the whole scene
floods back. The sight of visible injuries on other people, even minor ones, can pull him into the
same internal state, as if his mind has grabbed hold of the original incident and yanked him back
into it. At times, a hospital or antiseptic smell is enough to make his stomach tighten and his
attention narrow, because it carries him straight back to the place where he saw the injury and
heard the voices around it. He described that when the memory returns, it does not arrive as a neat
recollection but as a full sensory reliving: the smell, the light, the sight of wounds, and the
emotional shock all seeming present at once. There are also parts of that night he cannot
reconstruct no matter how hard he tries. He knows the broad outline, but there are gaps he cannot
bridge, which leaves him unsettled and frustrated when he attempts to make sense of it afterward.

Yousef has come to believe that the event changed him in a lasting way. He said plainly that he
feels permanently damaged, as if something in him did not return to baseline after that night. He
also speaks with heavy self-blame, saying he should have protected others and failed, even though he
can describe the situation as chaotic and beyond any one person’s control. He does not say this in a
dramatic way; rather, it comes out as a quiet certainty that he fell short when it mattered.
Alongside that, he has lost confidence in his own judgment and steadiness. He said he can’t rely on
himself the way he used to, and that uncertainty now sits beneath many ordinary decisions.

In the months that followed, Yousef began to avoid work in a way that troubled him because it ran
against everything he had always valued about himself. Some days he found it harder and harder to go
in, knowing the hospital environment could bring him face-to-face with injuries or the smell that
set him off. When he did force himself to show up, he often threw himself into tasks, staying busy
so there would be no room to think. He described this as a deliberate effort to keep his mind
occupied, to stop the memory from getting space. Even then, he found concentration difficult. During
routine tasks, his attention drifted or snapped away, and he had to read things more than once or
ask others to repeat themselves. At home, he kept trying hard not to think about the trauma, but the
effort itself seemed to make the memory more present, as if holding it down took constant energy.

His sense of safety also changed in smaller, everyday ways. He always chooses where to sit based on
how safe or protected he feels, scanning for the seat with a wall behind him or the clearest view of
a room. He noticed this habit first in restaurants and waiting rooms, then at family gatherings, and
later even in his own home when company was over. He also reacts with alarm when someone touches him
unexpectedly, especially if he is already tense or absorbed in thought. His wife, who has tried to
be patient, has occasionally startled him by touching his shoulder, and he recoils before he can
stop himself, then feels embarrassed by the reaction.

By the time he sought help, the strain had become hard to hide. He was working less comfortably,
avoiding parts of his job he had once handled without hesitation, and his attempts to stay occupied
were no longer giving him the same relief. What finally brought him in was the sense that he could
not keep living with the constant effort of pushing the memory away and arranging his world around
reminders of that night. He hopes treatment will help him feel like himself again, or at least help
him understand why he no longer trusts his own reactions. What he fears most is that the damage is
permanent and that he will keep failing himself and others whenever something similar happens again.
