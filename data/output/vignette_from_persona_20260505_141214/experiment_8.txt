Minh, a 35-year-old South Eastern Asian American UX designer, had always thought of himself as
disciplined and dependable, the sort of person who could manage deadlines, clients, and long
stretches at a screen without making a fuss. That sense of himself changed after a life-threatening
illness that began abruptly enough that, in the months that followed, he often replayed the first
moments as if they belonged to someone else. What he remembers most clearly is the hospital smell,
the rushing movement of ambulances and staff, and the overwhelming feeling that his body had become
unreliable without warning. He was admitted through an emergency department after he became
seriously ill, and the experience of being examined, monitored, and waiting for updates while
strangers discussed his condition left a lasting mark. Even after he recovered physically, the
memory stayed vivid in a way that felt intrusive and unwelcome to him. He found himself returning to
it in fragments, then stopping himself because thinking about it at all made him feel as if he were
back in the hospital again.

In the weeks after discharge, Minh began to organize his life around not having to encounter
anything that reminded him of that period. He avoided hospitals completely, even when passing one on
the way to work, and would cross the street or look down so he did not have to register the entrance
or the sight of an ambulance outside. He also resisted medical appointments and physical
examinations as much as he could, delaying routine care and telling himself that if he did not put
his body in that setting, he would not have to face the same fear again. At home, he shut down
conversations that drifted toward illness, hospitalization, or what had happened to him, because
thinking about the event made his chest tighten and brought back the sense that something
catastrophic was about to happen. He noticed that he was equally reluctant to do anything that made
his body feel taxed. If an activity raised his heart rate or left him short of breath, he backed
away quickly, choosing the safer option of rest over pushing himself, because he had come to
associate bodily stress with danger.

As the months passed, Minh became increasingly alert to signs that something might be wrong. At
work, where he spent long hours as a UX designer moving between design reviews, screen work, and
meetings, he found himself unsettled by delays that others barely noticed. If a teammate took longer
than expected to reply, if a server response lagged, or if a meeting started late, his mind
immediately jumped to the possibility that something bad was happening. He would wait tensely,
scanning for updates, unable to settle until he knew more. Even when his wife tried to reassure him
that everything was fine, he found it almost impossible to believe her. Reassurance did not seem to
reach the part of him that stayed on guard. His body often felt braced for impact, muscles tight and
ready to react, as if he were still waiting for the next medical emergency to announce itself.

Minh had also begun to think about himself in harsher terms than he used to. The illness had left
him feeling exposed and ashamed, and he increasingly interpreted his reactions as proof that there
was something wrong with him as a person. He described feeling weak, not only because his body had
failed him, but because he could not simply “move on” the way he believed other people might. That
sense of weakness sat alongside a kind of emotional deadness that disturbed him almost as much.
There were moments when he realized he was going through work, meals, and conversations without
really feeling present in them, as though some part of him had gone numb after the illness and never
fully returned. He worried that his wife could see this change, and although he remained engaged in
daily life, he also felt increasingly separate from it, as if he were functioning from a distance.

By the time he sought help, the pattern had become difficult to ignore. He was still working, but
the effort of avoiding reminders, monitoring his body, and staying composed was wearing him down.
The turning point came after a recent situation in which a medical-related prompt and a delayed
update at work left him tense for hours, unable to settle and increasingly convinced that trouble
was coming. He recognized that his world had grown smaller around the fear of hospitals,
examinations, and anything that might strain his body, and that his marriage was beginning to absorb
the strain of his constant vigilance and withdrawal. Minh came to therapy hoping to understand why
the illness still felt so present and why he felt both numb and on edge at the same time. More than
anything, he wanted to believe that he could be safe in his own body again, though what frightened
him most was the thought that the threat would never fully leave him.
