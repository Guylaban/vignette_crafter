Carlos was 28, a Mexican American prison guard who had always thought of himself as steady and hard
to rattle until his older brother died unexpectedly. The death had come without warning and without
time to prepare, and Carlos still spoke about it as the moment everything in his life split into
before and after. He had been especially close to his brother, the kind of sibling who knew his
routines, his jokes, and how to calm him down after a hard shift. When the call came, Carlos was not
there to help, and in the weeks afterward he began to return over and over to the same thought that
he should have protected others and failed, as if some missed action on his part had allowed the
loss to happen. He had married since then, but even at home he seemed to carry the death as if he
were still waiting for some correction that never arrived.

At first, the signs were subtle enough that he tried to push through them. Then, in ordinary
moments, he would feel abruptly detached, as though the room had become distant or slightly unreal.
He described it as a strange internal drop, like being cut off from himself for a few seconds, and
it unsettled him enough that he would sometimes stop what he was doing and cover his face with both
hands until it passed. He noticed it at work in the prison when the unit grew tense or when he had
been on his feet too long, but it also happened in quieter settings, including at home during a
conversation or while he was alone after a shift. Each episode made him more careful about what
might set it off, and he became increasingly alert for anything that might bring back the feeling.

Related news had become difficult to tolerate, especially stories about sudden deaths, accidents, or
families being told that someone they loved would not be coming home. He would start listening and
then feel a pull in his chest, as if the details were landing too close to his own life. He also
reacted strongly when people talked about losing loved ones. At work, this sometimes happened in the
most ordinary way, such as when coworkers discussed a death in the community or mentioned a family
member’s funeral; he would go quiet, leave the conversation, or find an excuse to step away. On more
than one occasion he caught himself imagining the same call that had changed his life, and he would
shut down the conversation before anyone could say more. Over time he came to avoid grief reminders
of any kind, including places, messages, and objects that made him think of his brother. He did not
keep up with memorial-related conversations and tried not to linger over anything that would bring
the loss into focus.

As the months passed, Carlos began to protect himself in ways that were noticeable to the people
around him. He would cover his face with his hands when the feeling of detachment came on, and he
used conflict as a kind of distraction, picking arguments or creating drama when he felt himself
getting too close to the trauma. This showed up at work in sharp exchanges with colleagues and at
home in arguments that seemed to come from nowhere. He recognized that the fights did not solve
anything, but in the moment they gave him something else to focus on besides the death and the
helplessness that came with it. His marriage began to absorb some of this tension, because his wife
could see that he was not fully present and that he was increasingly avoiding anything that reminded
him of someone he had lost.

Carlos had also come to believe that he used to be a happy person but now was always miserable. He
said this with a flatness that suggested it had become part of how he understood himself. Alongside
that sadness sat a bleak sense that he had no future, a conviction he stated without much
hesitation, as if he could not imagine his life opening up again. These beliefs had settled in
quietly but firmly, and by the time he sought help they colored the way he looked at his work, his
marriage, and his days off. The prison setting gave him little room to escape reminders altogether,
since loss and crisis were not distant concepts in that environment, and he found himself bracing
whenever conversations turned toward death or family emergencies.

He finally came to treatment because the pattern was wearing him down and his wife had begun urging
him to talk to someone before he pushed everything farther away. Carlos was not seeking help because
he had suddenly become hopeful; he was seeking it because he was tired of feeling detached, angry,
and trapped inside a life that no longer felt like his own. What he feared most was that the misery
and hopelessness would remain permanent, and what he hoped for, however cautiously, was some way to
think about his brother without feeling as though he had failed him all over again.
