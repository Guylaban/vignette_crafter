Mai was 57 when she came for help, a married shopowner who had spent most of her adult life keeping
a small, busy store going with her husband. For years she had been known as steady and practical,
the sort of woman who could stay on her feet all day, count stock by memory, and keep customers
moving without complaint. That changed after her child became critically ill. The illness had come
on with terrifying speed, and what stayed with Mai was not a single dramatic moment but the
relentless sequence of medical examinations, the sight of blood, the tubes, the needles, and the
drip lines that seemed to mark every hour with the same message: something was terribly wrong and
her child might not make it through. She remembered being in the hospital while others in white
coats moved around with clipped voices and efficient hands, and she felt that the people and
institutions meant to protect her family had not done enough. In the months afterward, she found
herself returning again and again to the thought that what happened was deeply unfair and that no
one had properly protected her child.

At first, Mai tried to keep the memory at a distance. She told herself she had to stay strong for
her family and for the shop, but she also began to avoid anything that stirred up the event. She
would change the television channel if a hospital scene appeared, turn away at the sight of blood on
the news or in the street, and tense whenever she saw needles, IV drips, or medical equipment in a
clinic window. Even routine medical examinations became difficult. If she had to accompany a
relative or attend a checkup herself, she would sit stiffly in the waiting area, watching every
movement around her and feeling a surge of dread as soon as she saw trays, swabs, or a nurse
preparing equipment. She began asking her husband, her children, or even the receptionist to
reassure her that she was okay, that nothing was wrong, that there was no reason to worry. The
asking never seemed to settle her for long, but she felt driven to do it anyway.

Over time, Mai also found herself trying to erase the event from her mind. She would push away any
thought of the hospital, then notice that even when she tried deliberately to remember, the memory
would not come in a clear sequence. She could say that she knew what happened, but when asked to
describe it she struggled to find words, as though the details were blocked off. At other times the
opposite happened: without any clear picture, she would be hit by the full emotional force of it all
at once, a wave of fear and grief that seemed to arrive in her body before any image did. Those
moments left her shaken and on edge for the rest of the day.

In the present, Mai described being unable to relax even in places she knew were safe. At home, in
the shop, or sitting with family after dinner, she found herself constantly scanning for signs of
danger. She watched customers’ faces, listened for unusual sounds, and kept her attention fixed on
doors, bags, and any movement that reminded her how quickly an ordinary day could turn frightening.
Blood, needles, and medical procedures remained especially potent. If a customer in the shop
mentioned a doctor visit, or if she saw someone with a bandaged arm, her body reacted immediately:
her heart would race, her chest tighten, and she would feel a strong physical jolt that made her
want to step away. These reactions embarrassed her, but she could not stop them.

The cost of all this was visible in her daily life. She had become more withdrawn and careful,
trying to avoid not only distressing reminders but anything that might bring up strong feelings of
any kind. She noticed herself flattening out emotionally, keeping conversations brief and turning
attention back to work whenever possible. Her husband had learned to answer her repeated questions
with patience, though the reassurance she sought often returned within minutes. What finally brought
Mai to treatment was the growing exhaustion of living this way and her fear that another medical
emergency in the family would leave her overwhelmed and unable to help. She hoped therapy might help
her make sense of what had happened, hold the memory without being flooded by it, and stop feeling
as though danger could appear at any moment, even in a safe room.
