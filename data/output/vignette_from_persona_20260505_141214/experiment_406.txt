Amina, a 46-year-old Middle Eastern woman who worked as a pharmacist, came to treatment after a long
period in which life had felt divided into before and after her son’s illness. When he was a child,
he became suddenly and dangerously unwell, and the episode unfolded around the places and routines
she now found hardest to tolerate: the emergency department, the sight of ambulances outside a
hospital entrance, and the clinical scrutiny of repeated physical examinations. She remembered the
sound of the ambulance doors, the bright lights, the hurried movement of staff, and the moment she
understood that this was not a routine fever or an illness that would pass with rest at home. What
frightened her most was how quickly everything became medical and urgent, with her child’s body
being examined over and over while she stood by feeling helpless and alert to every expression on
the clinicians’ faces. Since then, Amina had tried hard not to think about that period, but the
effort itself seemed to keep the memories close, as though pushing them away only left her more
aware of their presence.

In the years that followed, she found herself scanning for reminders of what had happened, and her
daily life became organized around avoiding the things that brought it back. As a pharmacist, she
had once felt steady and competent in the hospital-adjacent world of medicine, but now even ordinary
work situations could become difficult if they involved talk of admissions, emergency transport, or
pediatric medications. She preferred to stay with familiar tasks and keep conversations practical,
because hearing someone mention an ambulance bay or seeing a patient brought in for an urgent exam
could make her body tense before she had time to think. She told herself she should be able to
handle these reminders, especially since she spent her days around healthcare, yet she continued to
steer away from medical cues whenever she could. The avoidance extended beyond work: when family
members discussed hospitals or recent illnesses, she changed the subject or withdrew, and she made a
point of not revisiting the details of her son’s crisis. Even so, the questions would come back in
private. She would sit alone and wonder why the event had happened to her, turning it over
repeatedly as if there might be some explanation she had missed, some reason that could make the
suffering feel less arbitrary. More often, the searching only left her feeling stranded in the same
unanswered place.

Amina’s sense of herself had also changed in the years since the trauma. She had come to believe
that she was a weak person because she had not been able to stay calm or protect her child from what
happened. She judged herself harshly for the panic she remembered, for the feeling that everyone
else in the room seemed more capable than she was, and for how shaken she still felt by reminders
that others might dismiss as ordinary. Alongside that, she carried a more global and painful
conviction that something was wrong with her as a person. It was not just that she had had a
frightening experience; it was that the experience seemed to have revealed a flaw in her that she
could not name but could not dismiss. On difficult days she went further, telling herself that
nothing good could happen to her anymore. The future felt narrowed, as if the illness had taken away
her expectation that life could still be straightforward or fortunate. Even when things were
objectively calm, she found it hard to settle into that calm. She described being unable to relax
even when she knew she was in a safe place, as though her body had not received the memo that the
danger was over.

That sense of being on guard showed itself in small, exhausting ways. She startled at ordinary
sounds that would not have bothered most people, such as a sudden bang in the apartment building, a
sharp phone ring at work, or the abrupt opening of a door in a quiet corridor. At night, sleep was
often broken and unsatisfying. She had trouble falling asleep, and when she did drift off, she would
wake and lie awake again, listening too closely to every noise. The result was not dramatic collapse
but a steady wearing down: she moved through her days tired, braced, and carrying the memory of what
she had lived through as if it were still close enough to happen again.

What finally brought Amina in for help was the sense that she could no longer keep organizing her
life around not being reminded. She was tired of the effort of suppression, tired of the
sleeplessness, and tired of feeling that ordinary sounds or medical settings could pull her back
into that hospital room. She wanted to stop feeling defined by weakness and defectiveness, and she
hoped that treatment might help her make sense of why the event had affected her so deeply without
leaving her trapped in it. More than anything, she was afraid that nothing good was left for her,
yet she also came hoping that this belief might not be the final truth of her life.
