keep her life moving as if nothing had happened, even while feeling increasingly overwhelmed by a
story she could scarcely hold in mind. A South Eastern Asian American woman who had built her work

smell that seemed to cling to the air, the sight of needles and IV drips threading into her arm —
yet whole stretches of time were blank for her, as if they had been wiped away. She said that what
remained was so extreme that it was hard to believe it had actually happened to her. At times she
did not picture a coherent scene at all; instead, a wave of terror and helplessness would arrive in
her body with no clear image attached, leaving her shaken by emotion alone.

Mina’s illness had begun with symptoms she initially minimized, but it escalated until she needed
emergency medical care and then a hospital admission that she experienced as life-threatening. The
practical details of being monitored, punctured, and surrounded by equipment became the center of
her memory. She recalled the coldness of the room and the cold temperature of the sheets against her
skin, and those sensations now remained potent reminders. Even months later, a blast of cold air
from an office vent, a refrigerated aisle at the grocery store, or stepping outside on an
unexpectedly chilly morning could make her tense and feel abruptly pulled back toward that time. The
smell of disinfectant in a clinic, alcohol swabs, or the particular sterile odor that drifted from a
medical office would do the same. So would the sight of needles, drips, and tubing in hospitals,
ambulances, or even routine physical examinations. She said that when she saw medical equipment, her
body reacted before she had a chance to think, and she would feel a familiar surge of dread and
urgency to get away.

In the months after she returned home, Mina began organizing her days around keeping those reminders
out of reach. She stopped going to the hospital even for issues she knew should probably be checked,
and she delayed routine physical examinations because she could not tolerate the waiting rooms, the
smell, or the possibility of seeing equipment on a cart. She also tried to erase the memory of the
event by pushing away any thought connected to it, telling herself that if she could just refuse it
enough, it would disappear. Instead, she found herself constantly scanning her own thoughts for
signs that she was “going crazy,” worried that the incomplete, unreal quality of the memory meant
something was wrong with her mind. This monitoring became a private habit, carried out silently
while she was at her desk or in conversations with her husband, as if she had to keep checking
whether her thoughts were still orderly.

As a project manager, Mina depended on being present, responsive, and steady, but the practical
consequences of avoiding medical cues had begun to intrude on work and daily life. She scheduled
meetings carefully so she would not have to pass the clinic attached to her office building, and she
chose routes that kept her away from the nearby ambulance bay. She noticed herself declining
invitations that might involve a health center, and she became increasingly uneasy when colds, air
conditioning, or lingering antiseptic smells were unavoidable. Her husband had begun to notice how
often she seemed preoccupied and how quickly she would withdraw when anything reminded her of the
hospital. Mina herself spoke with a heavy conviction that her life had been destroyed by the trauma,
and that her body had been permanently damaged by what happened to her. She said this with a flat
certainty that seemed to leave little room for reassurance, as if the illness had not only
threatened her life at the time but had also altered what she could trust about her own physical
safety now.

What brought Mina to seek help was not a single crisis but the accumulation of these experiences
until she could no longer keep them hidden. She was tired of policing her own mind, tired of
arranging her work and errands around hospitals she did not want to see, and tired of being ambushed
by smell, cold, and medical equipment in ordinary places. She did not say she wanted to forget that
her illness had happened so much as to stop feeling as though it was happening again whenever a
reminder appeared. What frightened her most was that the memory remained partly blank and partly
vivid, and that her body could still react with such force to a smell, a chill, or the sight of a
needle. What she hoped for was a way to live in the present without constantly bracing for the
return of something she had survived but still experienced as devastating and unreal.
