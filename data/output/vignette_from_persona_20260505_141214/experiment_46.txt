At 63, Mark Te Rangi was still working full-time as a financial analyst, commuting each day and
spending long hours in offices and meeting rooms where he had always been known as composed and
meticulous. That image of steadiness changed after a life-threatening accident several years ago,
when a routine drive home was suddenly shattered by the sound of a car approaching too fast and the
sense, in an instant, that something was terribly wrong. He has described the event as beginning
before he could properly think: the cars around him, the noise of engines and brakes, the darkness
closing in around the roadside all seemed to hit at once, and his body reacted as if danger had
already arrived. In the aftermath, what stayed with him was not a distant recollection but something
far more immediate. When he tries to remember it, the scene does not sit safely in the past; it
comes back with a force that makes it feel as though it is happening again in the present tense,
with the same urgency and fear. He has said that sometimes the sounds from that night return in his
head exactly as he remembers them, so that a passing car or the echo of traffic can make the whole
moment flood back without warning.

Over time, Mark began to notice that certain sensations brought the accident back with particular
force. The smell associated with the event, especially similar odours that reminded him of the
roadside and the damaged car, could catch him off guard and leave him feeling suddenly back in that
moment. Dark spaces became hard for him to tolerate; entering an unlit garage, stepping into a dim
corridor at work, or walking through a parking structure after dusk could make him feel unsettled
before he had time to name why. Car sounds were especially potent. The brief rise of an engine
outside his office window, tyres passing on wet pavement, or the rumble of traffic near his home
could pull him straight back into the frightening scene with a jolt that seemed to bypass ordinary
thought. He often found that the memory arrived without any warning at all, triggered by something
small in his surroundings, and once it came, it was experienced less as recollection than as re-
entry.

Since then, Mark has lived with a persistent sense that his body is always a step ahead of his mind
in detecting danger. He notices himself tensing before he can explain why, as if his body is
preparing for something his thoughts have not yet caught up to. At night he wakes and checks whether
he is safe, sometimes lying awake in the dark listening for sounds outside the house and needing to
reassure himself that he and his wife are secure. In unfamiliar places he immediately scans for
exits as soon as he enters, noting doors, hallways, and routes out of the room before he can settle.
This has become automatic in his daily life: at work, when he walks into conference rooms or client
offices, his eyes move quickly to the nearest exit; in restaurants or at community gatherings, he
positions himself so he can see the door. He has come to believe that he has to be on guard all the
time, and that he must be especially careful because you never know what can happen next. He is not
at ease enough to let his attention rest for long, and that constant alertness has become part of
how he moves through the world.

Mark also avoids thinking about the accident because he is convinced that if he lets himself dwell
on it, he will not be able to handle what comes up. He keeps the event at a distance in conversation
and does not voluntarily revisit details, even though fragments intrude anyway. His wife has noticed
that he becomes quiet when traffic noise rises nearby or when they pass through dark areas at night,
and he often asks to drive by day rather than after sunset. The changes have reached into ordinary
routines: he is less comfortable staying late at the office, and he prefers to leave before dark
whenever possible. What troubles him now is not only the intensity of the memories, but the way they
still arrive with such immediacy that he cannot easily separate then from now. He has finally sought
help because the repeated checking at night, the constant scanning, and the sudden flood of fear in
response to smells, darkness, and passing cars have become exhausting and increasingly hard to hide.
Mark hopes to regain some sense of ease in his own home and at work, but he is most afraid that if
he loses vigilance even briefly, something terrible will happen before he is ready.
