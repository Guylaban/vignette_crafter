Yusuf was a 19-year-old Middle Eastern man who had married only recently and had begun working as an
environmental scientist, a role that took him into offices, field sites, and quiet meeting rooms
where he was expected to collaborate with people he did not yet know well. He described growing up
in a home where a caregiver’s discipline could turn physical without warning, and where arguments
were never merely words. When voices rose in the house, he learned to listen for the change in tone,
the sharpness in the room, the moment before someone moved toward him. The memories that stayed with
him were not polished or complete; what he remembered most clearly were fragments of being grabbed,
struck, and cornered while other people were speaking angrily above him. He had come to adulthood
with the sense that conflict could become dangerous very quickly, and that he had to be ready before
anything happened.

As a child and adolescent, Yusuf began to notice that crying in others unsettled him in a way he
could not easily explain. If someone in his family cried, he found himself looking at the person’s
face and body, trying to decide whether the tears meant hurt, manipulation, or the start of an
explosion. His attention would lock onto the eyes, the jaw, the shoulders, and the speed of movement
in the room. He became especially uneasy when someone touched him without warning. Even a light hand
on his shoulder could make him tense and step back before he had fully registered what was
happening. He understood this not as overreaction but as a reflex he had earned. Unprepared touch
felt like the beginning of being controlled, and he was always braced for the possibility that it
might turn rough.

In the years after those experiences, Yusuf organized his life around keeping disagreements from
escalating. He would leave conversations early if he sensed irritation, agree too quickly when
someone sounded frustrated, and avoid situations where he might need to challenge another person. He
especially disliked any setting where confrontation could not be neatly escaped. At school and later
at work, he became known as polite and reserved, but he also made careful choices that let him stay
at a distance from people. He would avoid lunch tables, linger at the edge of gatherings, and
sometimes take extra routes or unnecessary tasks so he would not have to be in crowded spaces with
others for long. Being alone in a room or building was no easier for him, however, because then he
felt exposed and unable to monitor what might be coming from behind him or from the doorway. He
often moved himself closer to exits or into spaces where he could see who was entering.

At his job, these habits showed up in ordinary but persistent ways. In meeting rooms, Yusuf always
chose his seat deliberately, looking first for a place where his back could be against a wall and
where he could see the whole room. If someone arrived late and stood behind him, he would feel his
body tighten until he could relocate. During fieldwork, when he and colleagues had to wait in
trailers or temporary offices, he became preoccupied with where he sat and how much of the room was
visible to him. He studied people’s faces and body language constantly, not because he wanted to but
because he felt he had to know who might be irritated, who might be about to speak sharply, and who
might reach toward him unexpectedly. If a coworker raised a voice in discussion, even about
something minor, he became quiet and physically alert, waiting for the atmosphere to change in the
same way it had at home years earlier.

His marriage had brought some comfort, but it had also made his reactions harder to hide. He cared
deeply for his wife, yet he sometimes pulled away when she became emotional or reached for him too
quickly. He was embarrassed by how often he found himself scanning her expression and posture
instead of listening to her words, and he disliked that he could feel safest only when he had
arranged the room to his liking. Over time, the effort of managing these habits grew tiring. At work
he was increasingly spending energy on where to sit, who was near him, and whether a conversation
might turn tense. In his personal life, he noticed how often he was steering away from people
altogether, leaving him isolated even when he was not physically alone.

He sought help after a recent stretch in which these reactions became difficult to ignore,
especially in his marriage and at work. A heated discussion with his wife left him shaken for hours,
not because anything physical happened, but because the raised voices and strong emotion brought him
back to the atmosphere of earlier years. Around the same time, a colleague placed a hand on his arm
unexpectedly in a crowded office, and Yusuf felt himself recoil so sharply that both men noticed. He
came to treatment hoping to understand why his body seemed to react as if danger were immediate, and
why he felt compelled to keep arranging himself against walls, checking faces, and keeping people at
a distance. More than anything, he was afraid that these patterns would continue to shrink his world
and interfere with the marriage and career he had worked hard to build, even as he hoped therapy
might help him feel less exposed in ordinary moments.
