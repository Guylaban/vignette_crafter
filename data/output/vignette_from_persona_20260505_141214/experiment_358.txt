Robert, a 67-year-old European American school principal, first sought help after realizing that the
driving he had once done without thought had become a source of dread, and that his nights were no
longer giving him the rest he needed. The collision that changed things occurred on an ordinary
afternoon while he was driving home from work. Another car came at him suddenly, the impact violent
enough to leave him with the stark sense that he might not survive. In the aftermath, what stayed
with him was not a clean story he could tell, but fragments that would not settle into words. He has
said that he cannot seem to put the frightening event into words, even now, and when he tries to
describe it, the account breaks apart into sensations and flashes. At times the memory returns like
a film playing over and over, and when that happens he experiences it as if it is happening right
now rather than as something safely in the past.

In the weeks and months that followed, Robert noticed that certain moments in everyday life could
bring the collision back with startling force. Shouting, especially sudden raised voices, makes him
tense and brace himself before he has even had time to think. Loud noises—something dropped hard in
a hallway, a slam from outside, a burst of sound in a busy parking lot—can send him right back to
the raw edge of that afternoon. He is also unsettled by seeing cars that have been hit by accident,
because the sight seems to condense everything he fears into one image: twisted metal, helplessness,
and the possibility that ordinary travel can become catastrophic in an instant. These cues are
particularly powerful because they resemble the sensory chaos of the crash itself, the shock of
noise, impact, and the helpless sense that danger can arrive without warning.

Robert’s understanding of himself changed after the accident as well. He has come to believe that
somebody else would not have gotten into this situation, and that he has permanently changed for the
worse. He speaks of the accident as though it marked him in a lasting way, leaving him less steady
and less able to trust his own body and judgment. He also sees the world as a dangerous place, not
in an abstract philosophical sense but as a lived expectation that danger can appear at any moment,
even during ordinary routines. This sense of danger is especially pronounced when things take longer
than expected. If his wife is delayed, if a meeting runs late, or if a student, staff member, or
delivery is late to arrive, he quickly assumes that something bad is happening. He finds himself
checking the clock, trying to make sense of the delay, and feeling his body tighten as if something
ominous must be unfolding just beyond his view.

At work, these reactions have made daily life harder. As principal, Robert still goes to the school,
but doing so has become an effort. He has begun avoiding going to work on some days, especially when
he feels poorly rested or already on edge, and he pushes himself only because he knows his
responsibilities will not disappear. The school environment itself can be difficult: loud hallway
noise, sudden shouting from students, and unexpected interruptions can all catch him off guard. He
remains outwardly composed much of the time, but he is privately working hard to keep himself
together. He has tried to force himself to think only positive thoughts, telling himself to stay
calm and not dwell on what happened, but this effort leaves him feeling no more settled. Instead, he
often feels unable to relax even when he knows he is in a safe place, sitting in his own home or in
his office with the doors closed.

At night, the strain becomes harder to manage. Robert has trouble falling asleep and also wakes
during the night, alert for sounds and thoughts he does not want. When he cannot settle, he eats
excessively to manage the distress, telling himself that at least the food gives him something
immediate and concrete to do. It offers brief relief, though not the kind that restores his sense of
calm. His wife has noticed that he seems less easygoing and more preoccupied, and Robert himself is
aware that the changes have narrowed his life. He has not come for help because he believes the
danger is gone; rather, he has come because the fear has continued to follow him into work, home,
and sleep, and he is frightened by how frequently the past now arrives in the present. What he most
wants is to sleep through the night, to drive and work without bracing for disaster, and to feel
again that an ordinary day can stay ordinary.
