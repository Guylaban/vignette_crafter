Malia was 33, a divorced network engineer who had built her life around precision, quiet systems,
and the comfort of knowing what depended on what. She had grown up in a Samoan family in the United
States, and even as an adult she stayed close to a wide circle of relatives who texted constantly,
dropped by without much warning, and expected to be part of one another’s daily lives. That
closeness had once felt ordinary to her. It changed after her son became critically ill.

The illness had started as something she thought would pass. He had seemed unwell, then worse, and
within a short time she was in and out of hospitals with him, watching adults in scrubs speak in
careful, measured tones while her child lay small and pale under fluorescent lights. What stayed
with Malia was not only the diagnosis but the severity of the examinations and procedures: the
coldness of the room, the tugging restraints of waiting, the way the staff moved quickly around her
son’s body as though every second mattered. She remembered standing beside the bed while someone
prepared another procedure, her own hands trembling so hard that she had to hide them in her
sleeves. At the time, she had focused on getting through the next hour, but afterward those same
settings—hospital corridors, forms, gloves, monitors, the sight of a child being examined—could
bring the whole experience back at once. Even now, a routine medical appointment for her son or a
visit to a clinic would make her chest tighten and her stomach drop before anything had actually
happened.

In the months that followed, Malia found herself living as if something terrible could begin again
at any moment. At home, she felt oddly on guard, scanning for sounds that did not belong. If someone
came up behind her too quickly in the kitchen or if her phone flashed with a message from a relative
about her son, her body reacted before she could think; she would jerk, gasp, or feel a hard rush
through her chest that left her shaky and faintly nauseated. She hated how visible it was, how other
people could see that she was not simply upset but physically thrown off balance by reminders of the
illness. She described it as her body “going back there” before her mind had caught up, and she was
embarrassed by how intense it felt over things other people seemed to handle easily.

Arguments became especially hard. Before her son’s illness, conflict had been something she could
usually keep practical and contained. Now any raised voice from a family member, any sharp
disagreement about schedules, medications, or who had handled what at the hospital, made her feel
cornered and raw. She started to dread conversations that might turn tense, especially with people
closest to the child, because she associated their panic, frustration, or criticism with the period
when everyone had been frightened and helpless. The same was true of known people who were tied to
that time—relatives who had been present during the crisis, or people who asked after her son and
expected updates. Their names, their visits, even a familiar ringtone could pull her back into the
role she had occupied then, the one where she had to answer questions she did not have the energy to
answer. She began shortening calls, delaying replies, and declining visits more often than she used
to, not because she stopped caring, but because contact itself felt loaded with the possibility of
being dragged into those memories.

At work, Malia kept functioning, but not comfortably. Network engineering demanded concentration,
and she could still do the job, yet she found herself more vigilant inside her own home than she
ever had been before, checking locks and listening for sudden movement nearby. She would catch
herself braced when a family member crossed a room too quickly or when her son moved unexpectedly
beside her. The startle felt disproportionate and humiliating, but she could not stop it. The more
it happened, the more she tried to predict it, staying tense even when nothing was wrong. Over time
she became more solitary at home, keeping to the edge of interactions, as if staying prepared might
spare her from being caught off guard again.

What finally brought Malia to therapy was not a single dramatic collapse but the accumulation of
these moments. She was tired of feeling as if her own house could not fully settle her, tired of
dreading arguments and appointments, and tired of being so easily thrown into that immediate
physical alarm. She had come to believe that she was not as steady as she had once been, and that
frightened her almost as much as the memories themselves. More than anything, she wanted to be able
to sit with her son at medical visits without feeling her body seize up, to answer her relatives
without bracing for a fight, and to move through her home without feeling as though danger might be
waiting in the next sudden motion.
