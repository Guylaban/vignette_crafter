Daniel, a 49-year-old European American fashion designer, came to treatment after months of trying
to keep his life moving while privately feeling as if one part of it had shattered and never
settled. Married and outwardly still functioning in his studio, he described a careful routine of
staying busy from morning until late evening, filling every stretch of the day with sketches,
fittings, emails, and fabric sourcing so there was little room for his mind to drift. He had also
stopped making any real plans for the future. When his wife would ask about a holiday, a renovation,
or even a dinner months ahead, he found himself deflecting or agreeing vaguely without committing.
He said it felt safer not to imagine a future at all, as though planning invited something
unbearable back into his life. At the same time, he made a determined effort not to think about what
had happened, pushing away memories the moment they surfaced and arranging his days so that he was
constantly occupied.

The event that changed things had involved his wife, Elena, whom he had always experienced as the
center of his daily life. Several months earlier, she had been involved in a serious accident while
crossing a street near their apartment. Daniel had not been with her, but he received the call and
rushed to the scene, where he saw the aftermath in stark, disorienting fragments: the street closed
off, flashing lights, people speaking in urgent voices, and Elena on the ground before she was taken
away. In the weeks that followed, the memory returned to him in uneven pieces. Some moments were
painfully clear, such as the sound of a responder’s voice and the sight of the pavement under the
emergency lights. Other parts were blank, including the order of events and details of what was said
to him in those first minutes. That unevenness distressed him. He found himself trying to
reconstruct the scene over and over, but each attempt left him feeling more unsettled, as if the
gaps themselves were evidence of something he could not bear to fully know.

What he remembered most vividly carried a heavy burden of guilt. He spoke of standing in the street
afterward and feeling that he should have been there, should have called her sooner, should somehow
have prevented the accident. When the memory came back, he was often overwhelmed so quickly that he
had to stop what he was doing, sit down, and breathe until the wave passed. He said the guilt was
not abstract; it felt immediate and bodily, like a crushing pressure that left him ashamed and
nauseated with himself. At times he could not keep going in conversation and would simply go quiet,
looking away until he was able to regain control.

Since the accident, Daniel’s emotional life had become dominated by strong negative feelings that
seemed to arrive without warning. He described fear when he thought about Elena leaving the
apartment, horror when he imagined the street where it happened, anger at the randomness of it, and
shame that he had become someone who could not trust his own steadiness. These feelings appeared in
settings that had once been ordinary. Walking to his studio, passing intersections, or hearing the
abrupt screech of tires could bring an immediate physical jolt. His heart would race, his chest
tighten, and he would feel a surge of tension through his body. He became highly reactive to
reminders, not only in obvious places near traffic but also in quieter moments when a siren passed
in the distance or when he saw an ambulance reflected in a shop window. At those times, he felt as
though his body reacted before he had time to think.

The changes had begun to affect his work and his marriage in concrete ways. In the design studio,
Daniel was still present but less expansive than before, keeping his head down and limiting
conversations that might drift toward plans for upcoming collections or travel associated with his
business. He noticed that future-oriented discussions, which had once energized him, now felt
threatening and vaguely intolerable, and he often redirected them back to immediate tasks. At home,
Elena noticed that he went to bed late and then struggled to fall asleep, lying awake replaying
fragments of the event or forcing himself to think about other things. Even when he did sleep, he
woke during the night and remained alert, as if part of him were waiting for something else to
happen. He admitted that this constant effort to keep his mind occupied left him exhausted, but he
feared the moment his thoughts might become still.

When Daniel finally sought help, it was after a week in which the memory returned repeatedly and
more forcefully, each time leaving him shaken and unable to settle back into his routine. He was
frightened by how quickly he could become overwhelmed and by the sense that he was living with a
sealed-off, incomplete account of what had happened to the woman he loved most. He said he wanted to
stop feeling pulled apart every time the memory surfaced, and he hoped for some way to be able to
think about the future again without feeling as if he were tempting disaster or betraying Elena’s
vulnerability.
