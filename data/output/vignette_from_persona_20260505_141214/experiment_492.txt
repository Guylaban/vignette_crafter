Noah was 18 when he first came to treatment, living in the United States and working as a project
manager while trying to keep pace with an adult life that still felt newly assembled. He had grown
up in an Oceania family, and he spoke about himself as someone who had learned early to stay alert
and not take safety for granted. The traumatic memories that brought him to therapy dated back to
childhood, to nights when he witnessed domestic violence in the home. He did not describe one single
scene in a smooth sequence; instead, when he tried to talk about it, the account came in
disconnected pieces. He remembered flashes of raised voices, the sense of being small and unable to
stop what was happening, and then abrupt gaps where whole parts of the event seemed missing. He
would stop mid-sentence and say that it felt as though he was only remembering parts of what
happened, with the rest gone or out of reach. What remained carried the same terror he had felt
then, so that even years later recalling it could pull him back into the body-level fear of being a
child in the middle of something frightening and uncontrollable.

Over time, certain situations took on a particular force because they resembled elements of those
childhood experiences. Being alone with men was one of the clearest triggers for Noah. He could sit
with a male coworker in a meeting without anything happening, yet if he found himself alone in a
room or hallway with a man, his attention narrowed sharply and he became intensely aware of exit
routes, tone of voice, and body position. He described watching other people closely for signs that
they might harm him, studying faces and movements as if he needed to identify danger before it had a
chance to unfold. At work, this meant that even ordinary interactions could become exhausting. As a
project manager, he was expected to coordinate with colleagues and move through shared spaces with
ease, but he often arranged himself so that he would not be isolated with men longer than necessary.
He also found himself avoiding other people more generally, keeping conversations brief and limiting
contact outside what he considered essential. The pattern had spread into his daily life, shrinking
how much he felt able to participate in work and relationships, even as he remained outwardly
functional.

Another powerful trigger was his bicycle. Noah explained that the bike itself was not frightening in
abstraction; what got to him was the way it could carry him into situations that felt exposed and
hard to control. Seeing a bicycle, using one, or even being near them could bring back an uneasy
bodily memory of vulnerability and sudden alertness. He did not speak of it as a symbolic trigger,
but as something that immediately put him on edge, like his body had already decided it was unsafe
before he had time to think. The smell of alcohol worked in a similarly immediate way. If he caught
alcohol on someone’s breath or noticed it in a room, he became tense and watchful, as though a
familiar danger had entered the space. He had come to avoid people, and his substance use sat
alongside that avoidance: he said he smoked, and he drank alcohol and sometimes used medication or
drugs, describing these as things he turned to in the course of trying to get through the day rather
than as a solution to anything.

In the months before he sought help, Noah’s home life had become organized around checking. He
repeatedly checked that doors and windows were locked, sometimes returning to the same lock several
times before he could settle. Ordinary sounds also set him off; he jumped or startled at noises that
would not bother most people, and this left him feeling embarrassed as well as tense. He would scan
rooms, listen for movement in the house, and wake himself up with the need to be certain everything
was secure. These habits wore on him and on his relationship, because the people around him could
see that he was on guard even when nothing obvious was happening. Still, he experienced these
behaviors as necessary precautions. He was not convinced that danger was present every moment, but
he was equally unconvinced that it was absent.

What brought Noah in now was that the pattern had become harder to hide and harder to live with. He
was tired of feeling cut off from other people, tired of the repeated checking, and tired of being
pulled back into the same fear whenever a trigger appeared. He wanted help making sense of why the
memories still felt broken and why they still arrived with such terror, and he hoped to feel less
trapped by the vigilance that had come to shape his days. At the same time, he was most afraid that
if he let his guard down, something he could not control would happen again.
