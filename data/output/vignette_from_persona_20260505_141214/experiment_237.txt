Malia had lived in the United States for most of her adult life, but in her speech there was still
the measured cadence of someone who had learned English as a second language and used it carefully.
At 61, she was known in her community as a sculptor who worked with clay and wax in a sunlit studio
at the back of the house she shared with her husband. She had come to treatment after months of
feeling increasingly on edge, especially in situations she could not predict. What she described
first was not a single dramatic incident she had experienced herself, but the night she witnessed a
young man being attacked outside a market after an argument spilled into the street. She had been
walking home with a bag of groceries when she saw the man stagger backward, heard the quick pattern
of footsteps closing in behind him, and caught the sudden wash of a sharp cologne mixed with sweat
as the assailant passed close by. The scene happened fast, but Malia said certain pieces seemed to
freeze in her mind with unusual force: the sound of shoes striking pavement, the sudden movement of
bodies in the dark, the stranger’s perfume hanging in the air after everyone scattered. Even now,
when she tried to describe it, she would stop mid-sentence, search for the next word, and then lose
the thread completely, as though the event could not be arranged into a proper beginning, middle,
and end. She could say what she saw in flashes, but not tell a clear story, even to herself. At
other times she said the memory came back “more real than real,” vivid in a way that ordinary
memories of family gatherings or years of work never did, as if she were standing again on the
sidewalk with the night pressing around her.

In the weeks that followed, Malia found that certain moments seemed to pull her straight back into
that scene. A person walking a little too closely behind her in the grocery aisle could make her
shoulders jump before she realized what had happened. Footsteps in the hall outside the studio, or
on the porch at night, were particularly hard to tolerate; she would go still and listen, her body
braced as if waiting for something to happen. She began to notice how much more difficult evenings
felt than daytime. During the day she could keep busy with the weight and texture of clay, but after
dark she had a harder time feeling safe and would check the locks more than once before settling
down. She disliked being caught off guard, whether by a visitor arriving without warning or a
neighbor appearing suddenly at the gate. Unpredictable situations left her tense and watchful, as
though she had to keep scanning for what might come next. Sometimes this showed up in her body
before she had words for it: her heart would race, her muscles would tighten, and she would have a
strong physical reaction when something reminded her of the attack, even if the reminder was brief,
like a whiff of a familiar scent from a passing stranger.

Malia had also come to hold increasingly harsh beliefs about what the event said about the world and
about herself. She had grown suspicious of people in a way that surprised even her, saying quietly
that people could not be trusted because danger could appear so suddenly and without warning. The
thought that there was “something wrong” with her came up when she noticed how easily she startled
or how long it took her to settle after a sudden sound. She was ashamed of how exposed she felt at
night and how quickly she could become alarmed by a set of footsteps behind her. At home, these
reactions had changed the rhythm of daily life. She was still working, but she had shortened the
hours she spent alone in the studio after dark, and she no longer liked to stay late finishing a
piece. Her husband had noticed that she asked more questions about who would be coming over and
when, and she had begun planning her days around what felt predictable.

By the time she sought help, Malia was worried less about the event itself than about what her life
had become around it. She wanted to be able to move through her studio, her street, and her evenings
without bracing for every sound behind her or every unfamiliar scent in the air. She was most afraid
that the world would always feel dangerous to her now, and that there was something fundamentally
flawed in her because she could not simply put the night behind her. At the same time, she hoped
that speaking about it might help her finally make sense of what had happened, even if the memory
still came to her in vivid fragments rather than a complete story.
