seek help for a trauma that had begun years earlier but still intruded on ordinary days. The event
he described was his younger son’s medical crisis, a period he still struggled to recount in a
straight line because the memories refused to settle into sequence. He could tell the broad outline:
the child had become suddenly and dangerously ill, and the family was thrust into hospitals,
ambulances, and repeated physical examinations. Beyond that, the details came back in fragments. He
remembered a corridor, harsh lights, the tight press of people moving quickly, and his own sense
that time had split apart. Even now, he said, the story felt so extreme that it was hard to believe
it had actually happened to him, as if it belonged to someone else.

When Mateo tried to describe those days, he often stalled on the order of events. He could recall
pieces with vivid force, but not arrange them properly, and that confusion was part of what made
talking about it so upsetting. He said that remembering the episode brought back the same terror he
had felt then, not a dulled or distant version of it. He could feel his body reacting as he spoke,
as though the crisis were happening again in the room with him. Certain moments were especially
potent: the wail of sirens outside the hospital, the smell of sweat or body odour in crowded waiting
areas, and crying. He did not describe crying as merely sad; it was a cue that instantly took him
back to the period when his child’s distress signaled danger and everything in him had braced for
bad news. Sirens were particularly sharp triggers because they seemed to cut through whatever he was
doing and drop him back into that earlier urgency. The smell of sweat or body odour was similarly
loaded, linked in his mind to the overheated, tense atmosphere of medical spaces where he had spent
long stretches waiting and fearing the worst.

After the illness, Mateo found himself organizing his life around the possibility of being
overwhelmed by reminders. He tried hard not to think about what had happened, and when thoughts
started intruding, he pushed them away quickly rather than let them develop. He also made a rule for
himself that he would never allow himself to feel or express anger. He described that choice matter-
of-factly, as something he had decided he had to do, though he could not say he felt free in that
state. When asked about his daily routine, he gave examples of how carefully he managed medical
cues. He avoided hospitals whenever possible, kept his distance from ambulances on the street, and
became uneasy around physical examinations. Even routine appointments were difficult to arrange, and
he tended to delay them unless absolutely necessary. In the period after the trauma, these patterns
narrowed his world in practical ways, making some everyday tasks more complicated than they needed
to be.

At work, Mateo’s data science job offered structure, but it did not fully protect him from
reminders. He noticed that ordinary sounds in the office or on his commute could make him jump in a
way that embarrassed him. A door slamming, a chair scraping, or a sudden noise from the street could
produce an immediate startle response that was out of proportion to what was happening around him.

vigilance made it hard to fall asleep at night. He would lie in bed scanning for sounds, listening

drained and self-conscious, though he continued to keep most of it to himself.

staying on guard. He had grown weary of being startled by ordinary sounds, of losing sleep because

passing outside or by the smell of sweat in a crowded place. What finally brought him in was the
accumulation of all of it: the strain of keeping the memories at bay, the difficulty of being around
medical settings, and the sense that he was still living with his body stuck in that earlier
