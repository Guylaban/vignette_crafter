unable to help enough. In her telling, one person she cared about did not make it out, and Marisol
has never been able to separate that loss from the moments when she realized she had not done what
she wished she had done. She spoke with striking bitterness about the people she believed were
responsible facing no consequences, and with equal force about her own conviction that she should
have protected others and failed, and that the event happened because of the way she acted. Even
now, those judgments sat beside one another without relief.

Over the years, the memory did not remain in the past in any steady way. Marisol described it as
forcing its way into her mind when she did not want it to, arriving without permission while she was
folding fabric, lying in bed, or standing in her kitchen. When it came, it did not feel like a
distant recollection but like the scene returning whole: the heat, the choking air, the visual chaos
of bodies and shadows, the sound of alarm and panic, and the awful sense that it was happening again
across all her senses at once. She said that when the memory hit, a smell or a sudden shift in the
room could be enough to pull her back, but the most jarring moments were when there was no warning
at all. She might be sorting trims in her studio and, in an instant, be back in that apartment
corridor, feeling the press of the air and the helplessness in her chest as if time had folded in on
itself. The memory’s force left her shaken and disoriented, and she often needed several minutes
before she could return to what she had been doing.

Certain parts of daily life now carried an uneasy charge. Marisol noticed that a specific
temperature on the skin could jolt her, especially the sensation of sudden heat or the sharp
contrast of warmth after coolness, because it could make her feel for a moment as though the fire
were close again. At work, where she handled steaming irons, pressing cloth, and heated tools as
part of her design process, she became tense in ways that made even familiar tasks feel precarious.

registered that particular heat. She also found that news reports about events similar to what
happened to her were intolerable. If a television in a café showed coverage of a building fire, or
if a headline flashed on her phone, she would turn away immediately or ask her husband to change the
channel. She avoided news in general when she could, especially if it involved disasters, because
she could not bear to feel herself being pulled back into that night.

Her efforts to keep grief reminders out of sight also narrowed her world. Anything that reminded her
of someone she had lost—a photograph, a memorial service, even a familiar song at a family
gathering—could leave her abruptly quiet and withdrawn. She began stepping back from occasions that

also found herself pushing away positive emotions because they felt unsafe, as if moments of
pleasure were invitations for something terrible to follow. She could enjoy a compliment on her work
or a warm family dinner, but then she would deliberately flatten the feeling, suspicious of any
tenderness that rose too high. Her husband noticed that she was harder to reach in those moments,
though Marisol described it less as distance than as caution, as if too much happiness might make
the eventual return of pain more unbearable.

What finally brought Marisol to seek help was the combination of these experiences remaining vivid
at her age and the growing strain of keeping them contained. She was no longer content to let the
memory take over her body in the middle of ordinary workdays, nor to continue living with the
certainty that the people who had caused the disaster had never answered for it and that she had
somehow failed everyone she should have protected. She came to treatment saying that she was tired
of being ambushed by the past and frightened by how real it still felt. More than anything, she
wanted relief from the way the scene could still claim her without warning, and she hoped to find
some way to remember what happened without being pulled all the way back into it.
