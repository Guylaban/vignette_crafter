Ethan Rourke was a 26-year-old detective who had come to treatment after months of trying to keep
his life moving in the same orderly way it always had, even as something in him remained on guard.
Before the trauma, he had built his days around work, his marriage, and the steady routines that
came with being a police detective in a North American city. The assault he described had occurred
in the apartment he shared with his romantic partner at the time, who had become physically abusive
over the course of their relationship. Ethan spoke of one particular night in vivid, almost
photographic detail: the argument, the sudden shift in tone, the smell of alcohol on the other
person’s breath, and the sickening moment when he realized the situation was no longer merely tense
but dangerous. He remembered feeling nauseous as the room spun with the force of the encounter, and
he recalled the sounds that accompanied it with such clarity that, even now, fragments of them would
sometimes force themselves into his mind uninvited. He said that when the memories came, they were
not vague impressions but clear and detailed, as though he were back in the room, noticing the same
angles of light, the same movements, the same sense of being trapped.

In the months that followed, Ethan found that the memory did not remain in the past in any orderly
way. At times it would intrude without warning, and when it did, he sometimes heard pieces of what
had been said during the frightening event echoing in his head. He described this as especially
disturbing because the memories arrived fully formed, not as something he chose to revisit, but as
something that forced its way into his mind when he wanted to be focused on work, his wife, or
simply getting through the day. As a detective, he was accustomed to taking in difficult scenes, but
he noticed that visible injuries or wounds on other people now caught him in a way they had not
before. A cut hand, a bruised cheek, or an open wound on someone he interviewed could suddenly pull
his attention and make him feel sharp, uneasy, and keyed in to what might be hidden beneath the
surface. He also became acutely aware of the smell of alcohol. What might once have been incidental
now had a powerful and immediate quality for him; if he caught it in a bar, at a social gathering,
or on someone he encountered through work, he would tense and feel himself pulling back internally.

Over time, Ethan adjusted by narrowing his world in small but consequential ways. He avoided
relaxing because it felt dangerous to let his guard down, as though the moment he settled would be
the moment something could happen again. Even sitting quietly at home with his wife could make him
uneasy if the evening felt too still. He had also come to numb his feelings rather than let them
rise fully, describing this less as a conscious choice than as a habit he had fallen into because
feeling too much made the memories feel closer. Physical contact had become another source of
discomfort. He avoided being touched by others in almost any form, including casual contact that
would previously have gone unnoticed. At work, he became more careful about stepping back from
handshakes, pats on the shoulder, or the crowded proximity that came with interviews and scene work.
The changes had not gone unnoticed by those closest to him, and although he remained functional in
many respects, his marriage had been affected by the distance he maintained around touch and
emotional closeness.

As he spoke about the trauma, Ethan’s language about other people had a hard, skeptical edge that
seemed to have settled in after the assault. He said he had lost his faith in people, in life, and
in any higher power he once believed might keep the world orderly. He felt like a permanent outsider
now, as if whatever had happened had separated him from the ease other people seemed to have with
trust. What frightened him most was the sense that people were not what they seemed. This was not
limited to strangers; it extended into his daily work, where he was expected to read people
carefully, and into his home, where he wanted to feel safe with his wife yet found himself watching
for signs that anything could turn. That suspicion sat heavily alongside his efforts to function.

By the time he sought help, Ethan was no longer only trying to outlast the memories but was
increasingly concerned by how much of his life had become organized around keeping them at a
distance. He came in at the urging of his wife, after another evening when the smell of alcohol in a
restaurant had left him visibly unsettled and withdrawn, and after he realized how often he was
bracing himself against touch, rest, and stillness. He described wanting relief from the way the
memory could seize him so completely, and he hoped treatment might help him feel less like an
outsider in his own life. At the same time, he admitted that what he feared most was learning that
he could not fully trust people again, even the ones closest to him.
