Amina was 43, married, and working in IT support when she first came to treatment, but her account
of distress began much earlier, in childhood and in the wooded place where the abuse occurred. She
had grown up in Africa in a setting where there were stretches of natural land near the home, and
she still remembered being taken outside by a caregiver and hurt there, away from other people,
where the silence and trees made it feel as though no one would hear her. What stayed with her was
not only the physical pain but the sense of being trapped in that setting, with the natural
surroundings themselves becoming part of the memory. Since then, certain places had carried that old
fear in a way she could not dismiss. If she found herself in a park, on a trail, or even in a garden
that felt too quiet and enclosed by trees, her body reacted before she had time to think. She would
feel a tightening through her shoulders and chest, as if she were bracing for something to happen,
and she would scan the area automatically, ready for danger. She could recognize that nothing in the
present was threatening, yet her body would still behave as though it were.

As an adult, Amina built a stable life in the United States, married, and took a job in IT support,
a role that required calm problem-solving, concentration, and frequent contact with colleagues. Over
the years, though, she noticed that even ordinary moments at work could be disrupted by the same old
sense of threat. When she was deep in a ticket queue or trying to follow a technical process, she
sometimes lost her place or found herself staring at the screen without taking in what she was
reading. She described it as her mind slipping away from the task while her body stayed on guard.
She was especially unsettled by small enclosed spaces. A locked room, a door that shut with a solid
click, or being inside a confined office with no easy exit could make her feel intensely aware of
the walls around her. Even if she knew she was safe in the building, she would watch the door, feel
her muscles go rigid, and wait for the wave of panic that seemed to arrive without warning. There
were times she tried to tolerate it because she did not want her coworkers to notice, but she often
left meetings early or avoided situations where she could be stuck in a room she could not quickly
escape.

Talking about the past brought its own immediate reactions. When family conversations or therapy
questions turned toward what had happened to her as a child, she could feel herself stiffen and
become guarded, as if the act of speaking made the event come back into the room. She found it
difficult to describe the abuse in detail without becoming tense and distracted, and afterward she
often needed time to settle herself before she could return to ordinary conversation. That
sensitivity made it harder to talk openly with her husband, even though he was supportive. She
worried that if she said too much, the memories would become vivid again, and she would be left
feeling exposed and out of control.

What weighed on Amina most, however, was the meaning she had come to attach to what happened to her.
She had spent years believing there was something in her that made the abuse possible, as if she had
somehow been the kind of child who attracted cruelty or was meant to endure it. Even as an adult,
she struggled with the idea that something was wrong with her at a deeper level, not just that
something terrible had been done to her. She could state this almost bluntly in session: that the
event happened because of the sort of person she was, that there was a flaw in her that explained
why she had been singled out. These thoughts sat heavily with her and shaped the way she regarded
herself in daily life. She often carried herself with a careful self-consciousness, as though she
had to monitor how she came across so that others would not see whatever defect she feared was
there.

By the time she sought help, her symptoms had become difficult to ignore. The combination of being
on edge, reacting to spaces that reminded her of confinement, and struggling to concentrate at work
had started to spill into more parts of her life. She was tired of feeling as if danger could appear
in an ordinary room or a quiet stretch of nature, and she was tired of the shame she carried about
what the childhood abuse had supposedly revealed about her. What finally brought her in was the
growing recognition that she did not want to keep living as if her body were always waiting for the
next blow. She hoped for relief from the constant tension and the false alarms, but what frightened
her most was the possibility that she would never be able to see herself as anything other than
damaged.
