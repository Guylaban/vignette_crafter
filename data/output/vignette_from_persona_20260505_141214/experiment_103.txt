Rohan was 39, a South Asian American interior designer living with his wife, and he described his
life as having divided sharply into before and after the night his younger brother died without
warning. Before that loss, he had been the sort of man who kept moving, always sketching ideas,
visiting sites, choosing materials, and thinking ahead for clients. Afterward, the ordinary
continuity of his days felt altered in ways he did not expect. He could still work, still come home,
still speak to people, but there were moments when the world would suddenly narrow around the fact
that his brother was gone, and in those moments he felt as if he were being pulled back into the
worst part of it.

He spoke about the death as something that did not feel prepared for in any human sense. There had
been no chance to say what he wanted to say, no gradual decline to brace himself against, only the
abruptness of being told and then having to stand in the reality of it. In the first weeks
afterward, funerals and any grief-related gathering felt unbearable to him. Even hearing other
people talk about losing someone could leave him tense and watchful, as if the room had changed
shape. He found himself avoiding memorials and anything that resembled one, and he also tried not to
dwell on dates connected to the loss. As the anniversary approached each year, he would notice
himself becoming more guarded, checking the calendar and then wishing he had not. He said he dreaded
those days because they seemed to carry their own force, as though the date itself could bring
everything back.

Over time, he became aware of how thoroughly he was trying to steer around reminders of death and
loss. If a colleague mentioned a relative who had died, he would change the subject or step away. If
a client meeting happened to touch on a family story or an event involving a bereavement, he would
feel a sudden urge to leave the conversation. Even in his own home, he was careful about what he
watched or listened to if it seemed likely to remind him of someone lost. He did not describe this
as a dramatic choice so much as a practical one: he had come to believe that if he gave those
reminders too much space, he might not be able to keep himself together.

Rohan also found himself mentally rehearsing worst-case scenarios, especially when something about a
day felt unsettled. He would imagine what might happen if another loved one were taken suddenly, or
if an ordinary outing turned into another irretrievable moment. He did this, he said, in the hope
that being prepared might make the future less shocking. Yet the rehearsals did not leave him calmer
for long. They seemed to keep his attention fixed on loss, and he noticed that once a scenario
started in his mind, it could be hard to put away.

At times, the past would arrive without warning. He described brief episodes in which he would
suddenly feel as if he were back in that original shock, not merely remembering it but experiencing
it with such force that his body seemed to know before his thinking did. He might be standing in a
store or at his drafting table and then, for a few seconds, feel as though something terrible were
happening again. Before he had time to reason with himself, his body would react as though danger
were present. He found these moments especially frightening because they were so immediate, so
physical, and so out of his control. Afterward, he would feel unsettled and irritated, tense with
himself and with other people around him.

Those surges of irritability had become noticeable to him and, at times, to his wife. He described
snapping more quickly than he used to, sounding harsher than he intended, and then feeling
embarrassed afterward. At work, where his role required patience, precision, and collaboration, this
was difficult to hide. He could still do the technical parts of his job, but he noticed that on days
when grief reminders were nearby or an anniversary was approaching, he was less tolerant of
interruptions and more easily angered by small frustrations. The strain of holding himself together
often showed up in the spaces between tasks, when he was alone long enough to realize how keyed up
he felt.

By the time he came for help, Rohan was tired of structuring his life around avoiding reminders and
bracing for dates he could not stop from arriving. He did not want to keep carrying the sense that
loss could come back at him in an instant, nor did he want his wife and work to keep absorbing the
aftereffects of his reactions. What brought him in now was a growing fear that he was living too
narrowly, always scanning for what might remind him, always half-preparing for the next blow. He
hoped that treatment might help him feel less hijacked by those moments and allow him to face the
anniversary, the grief talk, and the unexpected surges of memory without feeling as though he had
been thrown back into the night his brother died.
