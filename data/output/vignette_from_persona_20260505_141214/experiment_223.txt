notice vague abdominal discomfort and fatigue at 68, he initially dismissed it as something
ordinary, the kind of nuisance that could be brushed aside and worked through. He was a widower and
lived alone in a quiet house he had shared for decades with his wife, and his days were structured
around his work as a policy analyst, where he prided himself on precision, long attention spans, and
an ability to sit with complex information for hours. The life-threatening illness entered that
ordered life abruptly and without warning. What began as medical appointments quickly escalated into
a frightening period of tests, urgent discussions, and the constant sense that his body was no
longer behaving in a dependable way. He remembered the worst moments not as a single dramatic event
but as a series of terrifying instants in which he felt himself becoming less certain that he would
make it through. In retrospect, he could point to the hospital corridors, the clipped voices, the
feeling of waiting for results with his mind locked on the possibility of death, as the point at
which his body became associated with danger.

After the illness, he returned home physically altered in ways that made him feel exposed in his own
house. He described himself as being on guard even inside his own home, as if the quiet rooms might
still conceal bad news. He found that his body felt constantly tense and ready to react to danger,
his shoulders rigid at the dinner table, his chest tight when the phone rang, his muscles braced
without him deciding to do so. This state followed him into ordinary routines. At work, where he had
once been able to read policy briefs for long stretches, he now found concentration difficult; he
would start a paragraph, lose the thread, and have to go back again and again, frustrated by the way
his mind slipped away from him. He noticed the difficulty most when he was preparing reports in his
office or reviewing documents at home in the evenings, when he would realize he had been staring at
a page without taking anything in. The changes were subtle enough that colleagues might not have
noticed, but to Kwame they were evidence that something fundamental had shifted in him.

The memories of the illness came back in a way that startled him. At times he could recall what
happened, but feel completely numb, as if it had happened to a stranger. At other moments the memory
did not feel finished or safely in the past; it felt as though it were happening right then, in the
same room, with the same terror pressing in on him. When that happened, he would have to stop
whatever he was doing. A medical appointment, the smell of antiseptic, or even a random moment alone
in the kitchen could bring the whole sequence back with such force that he became overwhelmed by
emotion and had to sit down until it passed. He did not describe these episodes in dramatic
language; instead, he spoke with visible effort, as if the recollection itself demanded too much
from him. The memory could arrive with a flat, distant numbness one moment and then become immediate
and engulfing the next, leaving him unable to continue speaking.

Kwame had also come to interpret his bodily reactions with deep distrust. He was certain that his
body kept reacting as if he were still in danger even when he was safe, and that this made it
impossible for him to feel secure. He said he could no longer trust his own body, because it seemed
to tense, alarm, and misfire without warning. What troubled him most was not only the physical
unease itself but the meaning he had attached to it: he believed he would never be able to feel
normal emotions again, that the illness had taken something essential from his capacity to respond
like himself. That belief was especially painful for a man who had spent his life relying on
composure, self-control, and clear judgment. Since the illness, he had found himself living more
narrowly, avoiding situations that left him too exposed to reminders of medical vulnerability, and
his social world had become smaller and more cautious. The solitude of the house that he once
handled easily now sometimes felt amplifying, making every bodily sensation more noticeable and
every unexplained pang harder to dismiss.

By the time he sought help, the distress had become impossible to ignore. He came because he was
tired of feeling permanently braced, tired of losing his concentration at work, and frightened by
how powerfully the memories could seize him months after the illness had passed. More than anything,
he wanted to know whether his mind and body could ever feel trustworthy again. He was not asking for
a return to the man he had been before in any abstract sense; he was hoping for relief from the
constant sense that danger was still near, and for the possibility that he might once again feel
something ordinary and human without fear that it would vanish.
