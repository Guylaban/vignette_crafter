Mere was 46, a married veterinarian originally from Oceania, who had built a steady life in the
United States before a work assignment took her into a war zone where that steadiness was abruptly
shattered. She had been helping with animals in a city already worn down by conflict, moving between
improvised clinics and damaged streets, when the event occurred that would later return to her in
fragments. In her telling, it was not one single clean sequence but a series of disconnected pieces:
the sudden crack of loud noises overhead, the jolt of being touched without warning in the
confusion, the hard knowledge that she was in danger, and then the helpless scramble to get herself
to safety. Even now, when she tried to speak about it, she often stopped and said that the memory
did not arrive as a coherent story. It came like a film that kept starting up on its own, with
flashes she could not line up properly, images and sounds breaking apart and then snapping back with
disturbing force.

In the aftermath, Mere became preoccupied with what had been done to her and by whom, and with how
little anyone seemed able to put right. She had come to believe that what happened to her was deeply
unfair and nothing had been done about it, and that sense of injustice stayed close to the surface
whenever the memory returned. She also felt intense shame when she remembered the event, a shame she
did not find easy to explain even to herself. At times, she would sit silently, jaw tight, as though
she were trying to contain something humiliating and private. The memories did not come as a single
recollection but as repeated, involuntary returns: a burst of sound, a hand reaching unexpectedly, a
sensation of being cornered, and then her mind racing back through the same frightening sequence
over and over. She could not settle the story into order, and the disjointed quality of the memory
made it feel even more disturbing and unfinished.

Over the months that followed, Mere’s sense of safety narrowed. She began sleeping badly because she
felt she needed to stay alert, and she was often up later than she intended, listening for anything
unusual before allowing herself to drift off. Even in her own home, with her husband nearby, she
said she was unable to relax even when she knew she was in a safe place. She described herself as
constantly scanning her surroundings for signs of danger, a habit that extended into ordinary
routines that had once felt automatic. As a veterinarian, she still had to be around animals,
clients, and the ordinary bustle of a busy clinic, but she was now unusually watchful in spaces that
involved unpredictable movement. She noticed herself tensing when someone approached from behind in
an exam room or reached toward her without warning, and she found herself positioning her body so
she could see what was coming. At home, and especially in the car, the same alertness persisted.
Driving with other people had become difficult because she felt more exposed when someone else was
in the vehicle with her, as though she could not fully monitor what might happen next. Even a
routine ride to work could leave her on edge, eyes moving from mirrors to road to passengers, as if
any lapse in attention might allow something bad to happen before she could stop it.

Loud noises also remained difficult to tolerate. Sudden bangs, sharp mechanical sounds, or anything
resembling the unpredictable noise of conflict could make her startle and then freeze for a moment,
her body instantly on alert. She did not describe herself as panicked in a dramatic way; rather, she
seemed perpetually braced, unable to settle back down after the initial jolt. This persistent
readiness had spilled into family life as well. Her husband had begun to notice how careful she was
about where she sat, how quickly she withdrew from startled physical contact, and how often she
seemed to be checking her surroundings before she could join a conversation. Mere herself said she
hated that she could not trust herself to stay calm or to keep bad things from happening. She was
certain that she could not rely on herself, and that conviction left her feeling smaller and less
capable than she had once been.

By the time she sought help, Mere was exhausted by the way the event kept reclaiming her attention
and by the life she had organized around staying braced for it. She wanted to be able to sleep
without feeling she had to stand guard, to ride in a car with her husband without monitoring every
movement, and to tolerate an unexpected touch or loud noise without immediately feeling pulled back
toward that war-torn street. What finally brought her to treatment was not a single crisis but the
accumulation of these daily restrictions and the fear that she would remain trapped in this state
indefinitely. More than anything, she hoped to regain enough steadiness to feel safe in ordinary
life again, while fearing that she might never fully escape the sense that danger could return at
any moment.
