Daniel, a 57-year-old North American interior designer who had been divorced for several years,
first experienced the trauma in what should have been a routine drive home after a late client
meeting. Another car came out of nowhere at an intersection and hit his vehicle hard enough that, in
his memory, everything seemed to fracture at once: the violent impact, the twisting metal, the awful
sense of being trapped inside a body and a car that would not respond the way it should. In the
aftermath, what stayed with him was not only the physical shock of the accident but the image of
cars colliding and crumpling, a scene that later returned to him with unwanted force whenever he saw
damaged vehicles on the road or in news footage. Even months later, he could feel his stomach
tighten when he passed a fresh crash on the highway and caught sight of two cars pulled to the
shoulder, their position enough to call the whole event back with startling immediacy.

Before the accident, Daniel had built his life around precision, aesthetics, and a strong sense of
control. He moved through work with confidence, meeting clients, sketching plans, and arranging
spaces with the assumption that his body and mind would cooperate with him. Afterward, he began to
see himself differently. He found himself listening for car sounds in a way that felt involuntary,
tense whenever engines revved too close, when tires squealed at an intersection, or when traffic
noise rose abruptly outside his office windows. Those ordinary sounds no longer felt neutral to him;
they carried the possibility of impact. He was especially unsettled by discussions about accidents.
If a colleague mentioned a fender bender, or if a client casually told a story about a highway
pileup, Daniel would grow quiet and drift away from the conversation, feeling as though his
attention had been pulled back into the scene he could not shake. He did not merely dislike these
moments; he experienced them as dangerous intrusions, as if hearing about another crash could drop
him right back into the one that had changed his life.

As the weeks passed, Daniel became increasingly preoccupied with the sense that his own body could
not be trusted. He noticed every ache, every hesitation in movement, every flutter or stiffness, and
interpreted them with growing dread. A sudden twinge in his chest or a moment of lightheadedness
would leave him wondering whether something inside him had been permanently altered by the accident.
He had come to believe that he could no longer trust his body to remain steady or predictable, and
that belief made even ordinary activities feel less secure than they once had. At times he would
stand still in his own kitchen or in the middle of a project site and feel detached from the
confident, capable man he remembered being. He told himself that he did not know himself anymore,
that something essential had shifted in him since the crash, leaving him unfamiliar to himself in
ways he struggled to put into words. More than anything, he feared that the accident had changed him
for the worse in a permanent way, and that there would be no return to the person he had been
before.

Those convictions shaped the way he moved through daily life. As an interior designer, Daniel still
needed to visit offices, showrooms, and clients’ homes, but he did so with more strain than he let
on. A site visit that required driving across town could leave him visibly tense before he even left
the parking lot, especially if traffic was heavy or an ambulance sound cut through the street. He
learned to scan roads for signs of collisions and to feel relieved only when he had passed safely
through an intersection. He also found himself withdrawing from conversations where accidents might
be mentioned, including during work lunches or informal gatherings that used to be easy for him.
Over time, these habits narrowed his life. He became more guarded, less spontaneous, and more likely
to decline invitations that involved long drives or unpredictable routes. Friends and former
colleagues noticed he seemed changed, though Daniel experienced it more privately as a loss of
continuity with his former self.

By the time he sought help, Daniel described feeling worn down by how often reminders of the crash
entered his day. The sight of damaged cars, the sound of traffic, and even a casual conversation
about an accident could bring on a surge of dread and a painful sense that he was no longer the
person he had once been. He came to treatment not because the memories had faded, but because they
had not. He was hoping for relief from the constant shock of being pulled back into that
intersection, and he was most afraid that he would remain trapped in the belief that his body was
unreliable and that the worst version of himself had become permanent.
