Malia, a 35-year-old accountant of Pacific Islander background, first came to therapy after months
of feeling as though ordinary life had become difficult to inhabit. The robbery had happened on an
evening when she left her office later than usual, carrying her laptop and paperwork through a
nearly empty parking structure. She remembered hearing footsteps behind her before she saw anyone,
the sound steady and close enough to make her glance back. The man who approached had already become
the center of that moment by the time she turned. She could still picture his face in the brief,
terrifying exchange that followed, and those details came back to her in fragments that felt at once
vivid and impossible. In the days and weeks afterward, the memory did not sit in the past for her in
any orderly way; when it returned, she sometimes felt the taste she had in her mouth during the
event as if it were happening again, and she was startled by how much of it seemed to place her body
back into the same position she had been in then, frozen and bent toward the pavement. At other
times she would think about it and feel as if it had happened to someone else entirely, so extreme
that she could hardly believe it had actually happened to her.

As the months passed, Malia began to organize her life around the possibility of something sudden
and threatening. She dwelled on what she had done wrong, replaying each step of that evening and
then expanding the thought into a long chain of prevention: she should not have stayed late, should
not have walked out alone, should have noticed sooner, should have chosen differently. She was
convinced that the event had happened because of the way she acted and because of the sort of person
she was, as though some defect in her judgment or character had singled her out. She also came to
believe that the world itself was dangerous in a general, encompassing way, not just that night but
in streets, parking lots, hallways, and any place where a stranger might be close enough to matter.
Even when her husband and sister told her she was safe, she found it impossible to believe them;
safety, once announced by others, did not settle into her body as reassurance but remained thin and
unconvincing, something she could not quite take in.

At work, the consequences became especially clear. Malia had always been efficient and steady in her
role as an accountant, comfortable with routines, deadlines, and long stretches at her desk. After
the robbery, she noticed that when the office stayed late or the building emptied out, the sound of
footsteps in the corridor or behind her in the parking area made her shoulders tighten before she
could stop it. She would listen for them, then look over her shoulder, and if someone came too near
unexpectedly and touched her arm or brushed past her without warning, she would jolt hard enough to
feel embarrassed. She began taking longer routes, waiting for others to leave first, and once she
had time to think about it, she would deliberately try to leave her body or blank out, withdrawing
into a kind of mental distance that made the moment more bearable. Yet afterward she would return to
the same questions, turning them over again and again: how it could have been prevented, what she
had missed, what she should have done differently. If a meeting ran late or a ride home took longer
than expected, she found herself assuming something bad was happening. Delays no longer felt
ordinary to her; they became ominous gaps in which her mind moved immediately toward danger.

The robbery’s aftermath also altered how she experienced her own body and mind. If a memory surfaced
suddenly, it did not feel like a simple recollection. The taste returned with it, and the sense of
her body locking into the same position as before could be so strong that she would stop mid-task,
staring at nothing until the feeling passed. These episodes left her uneasy even in familiar places,
and she described herself as unable to relax even when she knew she was in a safe place. She could
be at home with her husband, doors locked, lights on, and still remain tense, as though her body had
not received the message that the danger was over.

What finally brought Malia to seek help was the accumulation of these small daily moments: the
corridor at work, the parking structure, the unexpected brush of a stranger’s hand, the repeated
assumption that any delay meant something terrible had begun. She said she was tired of living as if
the robbery were still unfolding around her, tired of watching her attention narrow toward every
sound behind her. More than anything, she hoped to be able to trust safety again, or at least to
believe it enough to move through her life without constantly bracing for the next footsteps.
