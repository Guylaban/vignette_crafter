Minh was a 22-year-old South Eastern Asian student who came to treatment after months of trying to
carry on with classes while feeling increasingly unable to move through ordinary life without alarm.
Before the war, he had been proud of the fact that he had volunteered as a relief worker, seeing it
as the most important role in his life at that time. He had gone where supplies were needed, helping
distribute food and basic necessities in areas where the fighting had already torn through
neighborhoods and left people frightened and displaced. What had begun as purposeful work became, in
his memory, inseparable from the day armed men moved through the area and everything turned abrupt,
chaotic, and dangerous. He described being forced to stay alert to every sound and every figure in
uniform, and he had come away from that period with the sense that danger could appear without
warning and that he had been powerless to prevent it.

Since returning to the United States and resuming his studies, Minh had found that certain moments
in daily life could suddenly pull him back into that time. If he noticed police nearby, or saw
military or police uniforms on campus or in the city, his attention snapped toward them immediately.
He felt the same surge of unease if he thought someone was watching him, even briefly, as though
another set of eyes on him meant something bad was about to happen. These moments were especially
difficult in crowded hallways, at the student center, and walking past security staff outside
buildings, where he became intensely aware of who was looking at him and from what distance. He said
that uniforms were not just clothing to him anymore; they were the visual signal that made his body
go on alert before his mind had fully caught up.

Minh had gradually altered the way he moved through the world. He began avoiding social contact with
people he had known before the event, even though some of them were classmates or acquaintances from
earlier in his studies. Seeing familiar faces made him uneasy because they reminded him of who he
had been before the war zone, before he had become someone who felt permanently changed. He also
found himself avoiding eye contact with other people, especially in public or in classrooms, because
direct looks made him feel exposed, as if he were being scrutinized and judged. Over time, he sat in
the back of lectures, kept his gaze on his desk, and took routes across campus that let him pass
more anonymously. These habits narrowed his life in ways he did not initially intend: conversations
became shorter, invitations harder to answer, and the distance between him and other students
widened. He was not indifferent to people; rather, he had come to believe that being seen too
clearly made him vulnerable.

What distressed Minh most was the way his body continued to react as if it were still in danger even
when he knew, intellectually, that he was back in a safe place. He described the experience with
frustration and shame, saying that his heart and muscles behaved as though the war zone were still
around him, and that he could not simply tell himself to calm down. This mismatch between where he
was and how his body reacted left him feeling trapped inside a permanent state of readiness. He had
also come to believe, more painfully over time, that he had failed in his most important role in
life. As a relief worker, he had wanted to be useful and protective, and instead he left with the
conviction that he had not been able to stop bad things from happening to the people he was there to
help, or to himself. That sense of helplessness sat heavily with him, especially when he thought
back on the expectations he had placed on himself before deployment.

By the time he sought help, Minh was still trying to attend classes, but the effort of navigating
campus had become exhausting. The presence of police near a building entrance, or a student in a
military-style jacket, could derail his concentration for the rest of the day. He had started
minimizing time in shared spaces and avoiding classmates he used to know, which left him feeling
increasingly isolated and far from the person he had once imagined himself becoming. He came to
treatment because he was tired of organizing his days around fear and because the reactions were no
longer something he could hide from himself. What he hoped for, more than anything, was to feel safe
in his own body again and to believe that his life had not been defined entirely by failure.
