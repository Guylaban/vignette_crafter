Megan O’Connor, a 35-year-old married counselor of European American background, had been described
by colleagues and by her husband as thoughtful, steady, and careful before the incident that altered
the shape of her life. She had built a career helping other people make sense of distress, and for
years she had taken quiet pride in being the person who could stay present when others were
frightened or overwhelmed. That sense of purpose changed on an afternoon that still returned to her
in disjointed flashes. She had been driving home after work when a brief lapse in judgment led to a
serious injury to another person. When she tried to tell the story later, even to herself, it would
not settle into a clear sequence. She remembered scattered pieces: the sensation of being behind the
wheel, the sudden sharpness of the moment when things went wrong, a later image of emergency
vehicles, and the hard, sickening fact that someone had been badly hurt. The memory came back to her
in disconnected fragments that did not fit together, leaving her unable to form a coherent account
of what had happened, even though she kept trying.

In the weeks that followed, Megan found that she could not keep the event at a distance. She would
force herself not to think about it, pushing away any image or sentence that threatened to bring it
back, but the effort itself became part of her daily life. At work, when a client mentioned an
ambulance, a hospital visit, or a recent physical examination, her attention would tighten and she
would feel the urge to look away from the image that formed in her mind. She began avoiding medical
cues in ordinary life as well, choosing routes that did not pass the local hospital and feeling a
jolt of dread if she saw an ambulance on the road. She disliked having to confront anything that
reminded her of the aftermath, because those reminders pulled her straight back into the scene she
was trying so hard not to picture. She also noticed that eye contact with other people had become
strangely difficult. In sessions, in meetings, even with friends, she often looked at a point just
beside the other person’s face rather than meeting their gaze. It felt safer not to be fully seen,
especially when she was worried that her own face might reveal too much of what she felt she had
done.

The emotional impact remained intense and raw. When the event surfaced, Megan was flooded with fear,
horror, anger, guilt, and shame, sometimes all at once and sometimes in a wave so strong that it
left her hollowed out afterward. What frightened her most was not only the memory itself but the way
it seemed to strip away the sense that her life had any meaning left in it. She had once understood
herself as someone who helped others, but after the accident she came to believe that she had failed
in a fundamental way. She was convinced that the event happened because of the way she acted, and
she returned to the same thought again and again: she should have protected others and she failed.
This belief sat heavily over her marriage and her work. Her husband noticed that she had become
quieter and less present at home, and she found herself going through the motions of daily tasks
without the same sense of connection or direction she once had.

Sleep became difficult as the weeks turned into months. Megan would lie in bed replaying stray
details, trying not to think about the accident and then becoming more alert when the effort did not
work. Some nights she had trouble falling asleep; on others, she woke and stayed awake, watchful and
tense, as if something bad might happen if she let herself drift off. Even in ordinary settings she
felt superalert, scanning for what might remind her of the event or what might signal another
mistake. That watchfulness made simple routines feel strained. At work, when clients spoke about
accidents, hospitals, or injuries, she felt herself stiffen and turn inward. She remained competent,
but the effort of holding herself together was obvious to those close to her, and it left her
drained by the end of the day.

By the time she sought help, Megan was no longer only distressed by the memory of what happened; she
was frightened by how much of her life had become organized around avoiding it. She had not wanted
to come in, partly because speaking about the accident felt intolerably close to reliving it, and
partly because she was ashamed of how completely she blamed herself. What finally brought her to
treatment was the combination of worsening sleep, the persistent sense of being on guard, and the
painful realization that she no longer felt her work or life had the meaning they once had. She said
she was not sure she could make sense of the story, but she hoped, quietly and with hesitation, that
she might find a way to remember it without being overwhelmed by grief and without feeling that she
had failed beyond repair.
