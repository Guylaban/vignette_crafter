Musa was 46, a married African American man who had spent most of his adult life building a careful,
organized career as a curriculum designer, the kind of work that suited his preference for
structure, clear language, and planning ahead. For years he had been known as measured and
dependable, someone who could take a chaotic set of educational requirements and turn them into
something coherent. That steadiness had been shaken after a police-related traumatic event that he
still found difficult to tell in a linear way. When he tried to describe it, the words seemed to
catch in his throat, as if the event existed in his body more clearly than it did in language. He
could say pieces of it, but never enough to make it feel complete. What remained most vivid was the
emotional force of it: at times the fear returned in full, sudden and raw, without a clear image
attached, only the sense that something terrible was happening again.

The event had unfolded quickly and with a kind of authority that made resistance feel impossible.
Musa had been caught up in an encounter with police that left him feeling trapped, watched, and
powerless, and the details he could tolerate saying out loud were always limited. He would start to
explain what happened, then stop, pressing his lips together as if pushing the rest back down. At
those moments he looked physically overwhelmed, as though simply approaching the memory brought a
wave of emotion that was too much to stay with. In session he often said he “couldn’t get it into
words,” and that the memory was “just there” in his body before he could make sense of it. The most
distressing part was not only what happened during the event, but that reminders of it could return
with no warning and leave him flooded with the same feeling he had had then, even when he could not
summon a clear picture.

In the weeks and months that followed, Musa began to notice that his body reacted before he had time
to think. A sudden siren on the street, a hard knock at the door, or a burst of commotion in public
could make him jump so sharply that he felt embarrassed by how obvious it was. He described himself
as “always on,” scanning automatically when he was outside, noticing uniforms, flashing lights, and
the tone of voices around him. He moved through stores, parking lots, and sidewalks with a
watchfulness that felt exhausting but necessary to him, as if any moment of not paying attention
might leave him vulnerable again. He was not dramatic about it; rather, he spoke of a quiet,
constant guarding, a readiness in his shoulders and chest that never fully settled. Strong physical
reactions also came when something reminded him of the event in less obvious ways. Even a sudden
authoritative voice in a hallway or an unexpected approach behind him could bring a surge of tension
through his body, making his heart race and his muscles tighten before he had fully registered what
had happened.

At work, where he spent much of his day designing curriculum and attending meetings, the police
encounter had a concrete effect on how he moved through the world. He found himself choosing routes
and schedules with more caution, and the office environment became another place where he watched
his surroundings closely. If he heard loud voices in the corridor or a sharp bang from the
neighboring room, he would startle and lose his concentration. He began to prefer sitting where he
could see the door, and he noticed himself staying alert even during routine conversations. The
changes were subtle enough that colleagues may have read them as simple distraction, but for Musa
they were part of a broader sense that he could not afford to let his guard down, even in a setting
that had once felt predictable.

His marriage also felt the strain of what he was carrying. His wife knew he had been changed by the
event, but he still struggled to explain it fully. When she asked what he was thinking, he often had
no useful answer beyond saying he was fine or tired. He disliked how quickly the memory could
overwhelm him, and he had started to avoid trying to talk through it because once he began, the
intensity rose so fast that he had to stop. That left him feeling isolated, with the sense that the
most important part of his experience remained locked away from even the people closest to him. He
came to treatment because the startle responses, the constant alertness, and the sudden waves of
emotion had become harder to manage, and because he was tired of feeling as though his life was
being organized around the possibility of threat. What he hoped for, beneath the exhaustion and
embarrassment, was to be able to speak about what happened without being overtaken by it, and to
move through his own home, his neighborhood, and his workplace without feeling perpetually braced
for the next reminder.
