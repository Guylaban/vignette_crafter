shattered the ordinary rhythm of her family’s life. One evening he had been laughing at the kitchen

doctor walked in. She stayed close to Sami, watching every movement, but she also felt acutely aware
of how little she could do. When he became more frightened, she felt herself freeze, as if she were
supposed to somehow make it stop and could not. In the months that followed, she came to see that
moment as the point where something in her changed. Even after Sami recovered enough to come home,
she could not return to feeling that the world was predictable.

By the time she began working in a warehouse, Layla was still carrying the strain of those months.

the concrete floor, and she found herself on edge whenever a sudden sound cut through the hum of the
building. A cart slamming into a metal frame, a sharp alarm, or the brief, startled cry of another
worker could send a jolt through her body so quickly that her chest tightened and her hands went
cold. She was embarrassed by how intense these reactions felt, especially because other people
barely seemed to notice the same noises. At work she tried to push through, but she often caught
herself losing her place in a task because her attention slipped away; one moment she would be

without taking in what she was seeing. The warehouse required constant focus, and she feared being
seen as careless or not strong enough for the job, even though she was trying hard.

What frightened Layla most was how easily her mind went back to the hospital whenever something in
the present resembled that time. If she saw a child with an IV pole in a waiting room, or heard

would feel it physically first: her stomach dropping, her heart pounding, and a prickling tension
moving through her arms and shoulders. She described those moments as if her body remembered before
her mind caught up. Her emotions also rose quickly and intensely; she could feel fear, horror, and a

protected them somehow, that a better sister would have been calmer, stronger, or more useful.

moment her family needed her most.

Over time, Layla’s view of herself became harsher. She often told herself that she was weak because

she remained convinced that bad things could still happen without warning and that she would be
unable to stop them. That sense of helplessness had become part of how she interpreted ordinary
life: if a text message from her mother came in after a delay, or if someone looked worried, she

when nothing obvious was happening, and she hated how quickly her body seemed to react before she
could settle herself.

relationship had become one of the few places where she felt somewhat understood, although even

overwhelmed by reminders of Sami’s illness, and what she feared most was that she would always
believe she was weak and powerless whenever life suddenly turned frightening again.
