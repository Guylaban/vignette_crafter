Marisol Bennett, a 41-year-old European American woman, had built her adult life around being the
one who moved toward emergencies rather than away from them. As a firefighter, she was used to
smoke, alarms, sirens, and the controlled urgency of other people’s crises. What changed was not a
scene at work but her own body, when she became seriously ill and found herself on the other side of
the stretcher. During the period when she was medically unstable, she was taken into hospital spaces
that felt brutally unfamiliar to her despite her professional familiarity with emergencies. The
smell hit her first: antiseptic and hospital disinfectant, sharp and synthetic, clinging to the air
and to her clothes. She could still remember looking past the curtain and catching sight of needles,
drips, tubing, and the metal and plastic of medical equipment clustered around her bed. When staff
began examinations and procedures, she experienced them as helplessly intrusive, as if her body were
no longer fully her own.

In the months after that illness, Marisol found that hospitals and ambulances no longer registered
as neutral places of care. The sight of a medical building, the faint smell of antiseptic in a
corridor, or even passing an ambulance on the street could pull her attention so forcefully that she
had to redirect herself immediately, thinking of something else before the feeling overtook her. At
work, this became difficult to manage. She noticed the ambulance bay outside the station, the
equipment trays in the vehicle, and the examination of injured patients with a level of vigilance
she had not previously experienced. She began avoiding medical cues whenever she could, stepping
back from hospital transports and steering clear of physical examinations unless there was no
alternative. Even routine encounters that involved medical equipment or the prospect of a procedure
carried a charge that made her want to leave the area, and her avoidance gradually altered how she
moved through both work and ordinary life.

Marisol described herself as constantly scanning her surroundings for signs of danger, a habit that
had become almost automatic. In public places she checked exits, watched other people’s hands, and
tracked any hint of a smell that reminded her of the hospital. Her body felt constantly tense and
ready to react, as if it had remained braced for the next bad turn. This tension was not abstract;
when something reminded her of the illness, she could feel a strong physical reaction in her chest
and stomach, a sudden surge of alarm that left her needing a moment to steady herself. The response
could be set off by a familiar institutional smell, by seeing needles in a clinic room, or by the
table and lights of a medical examination. She often caught herself waiting for something terrible
to happen, even in ordinary settings that had no obvious danger.

What troubled Marisol just as much was the way the illness returned to her in memory. She said that
when she tried to think about what had happened, the details seemed to come back in fragments rather
than in a clear story. She could not seem to put the frightening event into words in a way that felt
complete, and this left her frustrated and unsettled. At times, remembering it brought back the same
terror she had felt while it was happening, so that the memory felt less like an account from the
past than a sudden return of the original fear. There were moments when her body seemed to reenact
the experience: if she thought of being strapped down, confined, or positioned for treatment, she
felt herself physically in the same posture she had been in during the illness, as though the
sensation had bypassed language and gone straight to her muscles and breathing.

Over time, these reactions narrowed her world. She avoided anything likely to stir strong emotion,
not wanting to set off the wave of fear or the bodily surge that could follow. She became more
guarded in conversations that touched on health, mortality, or vulnerability, and she found herself
using distraction almost reflexively when memories or reminders surfaced. At home, her husband
noticed that she would go quiet or abruptly change the subject when medical topics came up, and he
came to understand that even a routine appointment could leave her shaky and keyed up for the rest
of the day. She had always relied on her physical confidence and her capacity to stay composed under
pressure; now she found herself measuring each environment for signs that it might contain another
reminder.

Marisol finally sought help after realizing that the pattern was no longer staying in the background
of her life. The combination of hospital smells, medical equipment, examinations, and the body-level
terror that came with reminders had made both work and everyday care feel harder to tolerate. She
came to treatment hoping to be able to face necessary medical settings without feeling trapped in
the same terror again, and at the same time she was afraid that the response would never fully quiet
down.
