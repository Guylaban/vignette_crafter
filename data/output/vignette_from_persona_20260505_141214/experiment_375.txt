Marisol, a 19-year-old Latin American woman who worked as a train conductor, came to treatment
describing a childhood marked by a single, vivid scene that still returned to her with unsettling
force. When she was younger, she witnessed domestic violence at home, an event she now struggled to
place in a clear order when she tried to tell it. In memory, it did not unfold like a story with a
beginning and end. Instead, pieces came back disjointedly: a burst of shouting, a body moving too
fast, a taste she could still identify when the memory arrived, and then the awful sense that the
scene was happening again in the room with her. She would say that it did not feel finished or
safely in the past; it felt immediate, as if the moment had never truly left her.

Certain sensations in the present now pulled her back into that experience. Darkness was especially
hard for Marisol, and she had come to dread dark spaces and the hours after sunset. At night, when
her apartment was quiet and the lights were off, she found herself listening for what might break
the silence. Loud noises were another sharp trigger. A slammed metal door at the station, a sudden
shout across the platform, or the clatter of equipment could make her flinch and freeze before she
had time to think. She also became alarmed by her own body in a way that felt difficult to explain
to others. If her heart began racing or pounding, she immediately felt on edge, as though something
was about to happen and she needed to brace for it. On workdays, when she was moving through the
train cars, she noticed herself scanning constantly for unexpected sound, her attention narrowing at
every abrupt bang or raised voice.

Over the years, Marisol had built a life around staying occupied. She described working hard at
keeping busy with other things, filling every spare stretch of time with tasks, errands, or extra
responsibilities so she would not be left alone with what came up in her mind. She also found
herself thinking again and again about how life would have been different if the event had not
occurred, turning over alternate versions of her childhood and the person she might have been. Food
had become part of the same effort to cope; she often restricted what she ate or skipped meals,
telling herself that eating less gave her some kind of control when other things felt unsettled.
These habits had taken shape quietly over time, until they became part of her daily rhythm.

At night, Marisol’s sense of danger sharpened. She found it harder to feel safe after dark than
during the day, and she often checked the doors and windows repeatedly before trying to sleep. Even
after confirming they were locked, she would return to check again, especially if she heard
something outside the building or a sudden noise in the hallway. She was most unsettled by
unexpected sounds, and her body seemed to react before her thoughts could catch up. At home she kept
the television on for company, but on some nights that only made her more vigilant, because any
abrupt sound would pull her attention immediately. At work, the train yard and station platforms
meant she was constantly exposed to clanging metal, brakes, voices, and sudden movement. She tried
not to show how tense she felt, but she often moved through her shifts with an internal readiness
for something alarming to happen.

Marisol had also come to believe harsh things about herself and about the world. She felt certain
that she could not stop bad things from happening to her, no matter how careful she was. She
believed she should have protected others and failed, and she blamed herself for the event as though
the way she acted somehow made it happen. These thoughts were not abstract; they surfaced when she
remembered being small and powerless, and they left her with a painful sense that she had not done
enough. What disturbed her most was that the memory could still arrive with such force, along with
the taste she associated with it, and place her right back in the moment. She no longer trusted her
own body when her heart raced, and she no longer trusted quiet or darkness to remain safe.

By the time she sought help, Marisol was exhausted by the amount of effort it took to keep
functioning. She had managed to keep working, but the constant alertness, repeated checking, and
avoidance of being still were wearing her down. What finally brought her in was the feeling that she
could not keep living as if every loud sound, every dark space, and every racing heartbeat might
mean danger was coming back. She hoped for relief from the sense that the past was still happening
to her, and she feared that if nothing changed she would continue living as though she were always
waiting for the next thing to go wrong.
