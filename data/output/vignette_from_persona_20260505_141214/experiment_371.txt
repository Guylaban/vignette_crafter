Javier Morales was a 22-year-old single HVAC technician of Latin American background who came to
treatment after months of trying to manage on his own. The event that had changed things happened at
work, during a routine service call that he still experienced as anything but routine. He had been
working in a cramped mechanical space when a sudden accident led to serious injury to another
person. Javier described the moment as fast, chaotic, and impossible to undo: one second he was
doing what he thought he was supposed to do, and the next there was someone badly hurt and shouting,
and he was left staring at what had happened with the sense that he had failed to stop it. Since
then, he had spent a great deal of time going back over the details in his head, replaying every
step and every decision, trying to make sense of how it had happened and what he should have done
differently. He could not settle on an explanation he found acceptable. What stayed with him most
was the conviction that he would never forgive himself for not doing more to stop it.

In the weeks that followed, Javier began to experience the memory as something oddly separate from
him, as though it belonged to another person rather than to his own life. He said that when it came
into his mind, it did not feel like a normal recollection but like being forced back into a scene he
never wanted to enter again. At times the memory arrived without warning, intruding while he was
trying to work, drive, or fall asleep, and he felt powerless to keep it out. Certain sensory details
brought it back sharply. He could smell the event in a way that made no sense to other people but
made complete sense to him: the sharp, metallic, mechanical odors from the worksite seemed to return
all at once when he thought about what happened, and that smell was enough to make his stomach
tighten and his chest feel uncomfortable. He found himself physically reacting before he could
think, with a sudden rush through his body whenever something reminded him of the injury or the
setting in which it occurred.

Javier’s day-to-day life gradually narrowed around what felt manageable. He had come to believe that
he could no longer trust his own body, especially in moments that required quick reactions or steady
hands, and that feeling made ordinary tasks at work feel more threatening than they had before. As
an HVAC technician, he could not avoid the practical demands of the job, but he tried to keep
himself in what he experienced as a very limited safety zone: familiar places, predictable routes,
and situations with as little surprise as possible. Anything that seemed stressful, even mildly so,
he started to avoid when he could. He became reluctant to take on extra responsibilities, stay late,
or get involved in anything that might add pressure. He also avoided dates and anniversaries
connected to the event, tracking the calendar with a kind of dread and noticing how the days leading
up to it made him more on edge. At work, silence became especially difficult. If he was in a quiet
basement, a shut-off building, or a space where the usual hum of tools and ventilation suddenly
stopped, he felt as if something dangerous was about to happen. That unusual quiet made him scan the
environment, tense his shoulders, and wait for the next bad moment.

Over time, Javier noticed that he seemed unlike the person he had been before. He spoke of himself
with embarrassment and grief, saying the person he had become was unrecognisable to him. He had once
thought of himself as capable and dependable, but now he felt altered in a way he could not fully
name. The change was not dramatic in how others saw him, but it was vivid to him in private,
especially when he compared his current caution and self-doubt with how he had once approached work
and daily life. He continued to do his job, but with a narrower sense of ease, and the consequences
of his avoidance began to show in his relationships and in the strain of carrying everything alone.
He did not want to burden family or friends with the details, yet the effort of holding it in made
him increasingly isolated.

By the time he sought help, Javier was exhausted by the loop of remembering, analyzing, bracing, and
avoiding. He came because he could no longer tolerate how often the memory forced its way into his
mind and because the dates, the silences, and the workplace reminders were making ordinary life feel
trapped inside the perimeter of that one day. What frightened him most was the sense that he might
remain this way, permanently unable to trust himself, permanently defined by what happened, and what
he hoped for most was some way to feel like himself again without having to keep living inside the
accident.
