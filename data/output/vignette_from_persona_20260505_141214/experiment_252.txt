At 71, Tane had lived most of his adult life in the United States after growing up in Oceania, and
by the time he came to treatment he was still working part-time as a cashier, usually standing
behind the register in a small neighborhood shop with his wife keeping the books at home. He had
always seen himself as steady and useful, someone who did not make a fuss, and for years that had
been enough. Then, several months earlier, he witnessed a violent incident in a parking lot outside
the store after his shift, when a commotion drew him to the doorway and he saw a young man collapse
while others shouted and scattered. The scene had moved quickly and felt chaotic, with bodies
turning, a door slamming, and people rushing in ways that made it hard for Tane to take in what he
was seeing. In the aftermath, what stayed with him was not a full, ordered account but fragments:
the sharp sound of the door, the sudden movement near him, the sense of being too close to something
he could not stop. When he tried later to describe what happened to his wife, and again to the
police, he would lose the thread partway through, pausing and starting over, unable to shape it into
a clear story even for himself. He knew pieces were missing. He could say that he had witnessed
something terrible, but the rest felt broken apart, as though his mind had kept only scattered
images and sensations and left the meaning behind them out of reach.

In the weeks that followed, Tane found himself watching people more closely than he used to. In the
store, when customers came in, he tracked their hands, their faces, and how they moved toward the
counter. Unexpected movement near him made him react as if he were in danger, and he would jerk back
from even a quick step in his peripheral vision. Doors became difficult. The front door of the shop,
with its constant opening and slamming as customers came and went, made him tense before he could
stop himself, his shoulders rising and his eyes going to the entrance every time the handle turned.
He also noticed that babies unsettled him in a way he could not easily explain. A baby crying in the
aisle, or a family coming in with an infant, could make his chest tighten and his attention lock
onto the sound. He did not tell people this was frightening; instead, he simply found reasons to
move away, to busy himself with the till, to step to the stockroom, or to ask his wife to handle
certain customers when he was home. He had come to dread feeling uncomfortable in public because
once that unease started, it seemed to settle into his body and linger.

Tane’s days became narrower. At work he remained dependable, but the effort of staying superalert
left him drained, and he began arranging himself so he could see the doorway at all times. He
watched for signs that someone might harm him, scanning expressions and body movements in the hope
of catching trouble before it reached him. He did this without feeling proud of it. It was simply
how he moved through the world now, as though he could not afford to let his attention soften. At
home, the same watchfulness followed him into ordinary routines. If a car door slammed outside or a
baby cried in a nearby apartment, he startled and went rigid, needing a moment to orient himself. He
did not talk much about feeling dead inside, but his wife noticed that he sat quietly for long
stretches, responding with less warmth than before. Tane himself used the phrase in a low voice
during intake, as if embarrassed by it, saying that it felt as though some part of him had gone flat
and empty after what he saw.

What troubled him most, besides the unfinished nature of the memory, was his conviction that the
people and institutions that should have protected others had let them down. He believed there
should have been consequences for those responsible, and that the failure to hold anyone accountable
made the whole thing feel even more wrong. He did not speak about this with rage so much as a tired
disbelief, as if he were still trying to understand how something so public could happen and then be
left without a proper response. That sense of being let down sat alongside his other reactions, and
he carried all of it while continuing to work the register, where doors kept opening and slamming,
babies cried in carts, and each sudden movement in his narrow field of view reminded him how alert
he had become.

By the time he sought help, it was not because he had stopped functioning, but because he was tired
of living on guard and tired of being unable to tell the story clearly even when he wanted to. He
came in at his wife’s urging, saying he wanted to sleep and work without feeling constantly braced
for the next jolt. What he feared most was that the broken, unfinished quality of the memory meant
he would never make sense of what he had witnessed; what he hoped for was some way to carry it
without feeling so empty and watchful all the time.
