Michael, a 37-year-old North American man who worked as a database administrator, described his life
as having divided cleanly into “before” and “after” the illness that nearly killed him. Before that
time, he had been recently divorced and living alone, keeping a careful routine that made work and
home feel manageable. The illness began abruptly enough that, when he tried to explain it, he still
sounded unsettled by how ordinary the day had seemed at first. What had started with breathlessness
became a medical emergency, and he remembered the frightening loss of control in stark, physical
fragments: struggling to get air, being rushed through examinations, and seeing needles, tubing, and
other medical equipment moving in and out of his body while he was too frightened and too weak to do
anything but endure it. Those images stayed vivid for him, and even now he could recall the feel of
shortness of breath as the moment his mind linked his own body with danger.

In the weeks that followed, Michael found himself responding intensely to anything that resembled
that period. He noticed that if he became winded climbing stairs, on a brisk walk, or even after
hurrying across the parking lot to the office, he would suddenly feel a wave of alarm. The sensation
of not getting enough air was not just uncomfortable to him; it had the quality of a warning that
something terrible was about to happen again. He also became uneasy in medical settings and had a
strong urge to avoid hospitals, ambulances, and physical examinations whenever possible. Even seeing
medical equipment was enough to unsettle him. Needles, IV drips, and the sight of carts or tools in
a clinic waiting room could make him tense up before anyone had touched him. He found procedures
especially difficult, because they placed him back in the role he had most dreaded during the
illness: lying still while others examined him, with no clear sense that he could protect himself.

As the months passed, Michael developed a set of habits meant to help him feel more in control. He
began restricting his eating strictly, telling himself that if he could keep his intake tightly
regulated, at least one part of his life would feel orderly and contained. He also started avoiding
exercise. He had once tried to keep himself healthy through regular physical activity, but now
exertion had become linked in his mind with breathlessness, and breathlessness with emergency. Even
minor workouts felt risky to him, so he drifted away from them entirely. At work, this showed up in
small but important ways: he would choose routes that kept him away from the hospital building near
his office, and he became reluctant to schedule routine checkups, even when he knew he probably
should. The avoidance was not dramatic, but it slowly narrowed his world and made ordinary errands
feel more complicated than they used to be.

What Michael spoke about most readily in the sessions was the way his body stayed on guard. He said
he felt constantly tense and ready to react to danger, as if some part of him were still waiting for
the illness to return. He would notice his muscles braced before he even understood what had set him
off. When something reminded him of the original experience, his body could react strongly and
suddenly. A sensation of tightness in his chest, an unexpected shortness of breath, or the sight of
medical gear could send him into a state of immediate physical alarm, and he described these moments
with frustration because they seemed to happen before he had time to think. Alongside that, he was
aware of intense negative feelings that he found difficult to sit with: fear, horror, anger at how
vulnerable he had been, and shame that his body had become so unreliable to him. He did not present
these feelings as abstract emotions but as a daily burden he carried in silence, especially since
his divorce had left him with less immediate support at home.

Recently, Michael sought help after realizing how much energy he was spending organizing his life
around the possibility of another medical crisis. He had delayed appointments, avoided exercise, and
monitored his body closely, yet still felt he could never fully relax. What finally pushed him to
come in was the sense that he was living as though his body were always one step away from failing
him again. He said he wanted to understand why a breathless moment or a medical setting could still
make him feel so physically overwhelmed, and he was most afraid that he would be caught unprepared
if his body signaled danger again. At the same time, he hoped that treatment might help him move
through ordinary life without feeling forced to brace himself for another emergency every time he
became short of breath or saw the equipment of medicine.
