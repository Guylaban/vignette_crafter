Malia, a 66-year-old divorced woman of Oceanic background who worked for many years as a curriculum
designer, came to describe her wartime experience in the language of fragments. She said that what
happened still did not feel fully believable to her, as if it belonged to someone else’s life, or to
a story so extreme that it was difficult to accept it had happened to her at all. When she tried to
speak about it, she often knew the facts but could not find the words to hold them together. The
memories arrived in disconnected pieces: a flash of sky, the thudding approach of a helicopter, the
sense of people around her looking to someone else for orders, and her own body bracing before she
could think. Even now, the event came back uninvited, sometimes in the middle of ordinary moments,
with no warning beyond a sudden intrusion of the scene into her mind.

She linked the trauma to combat conditions in which she had felt trapped beneath other people’s
power and decisions. What stayed with her most vividly was not a single complete narrative but the
experience of being in a situation where someone else held authority over whether she and others
were safe. The imbalance of power was what made certain moments in the memory so potent for her now.
She said that when she finds herself around people who seem to have control over her options, she
immediately feels the old tightening in her chest and wants to get away. She has come to watch for
that feeling carefully in everyday life, and she dislikes how quickly it can appear even in settings
that are objectively ordinary. Helicopter or aircraft sounds are especially jarring for her. The
sound can catch her off guard outdoors or even when she is near a road and hears a low mechanical
roar overhead, and in that instant she is back in the sensory world of the event, alert and tense
before she has time to orient herself.

Over the years, Malia developed habits meant to keep those reactions from overwhelming her. She
began avoiding going out unless she had to, gradually shaping her days around what she believed
would be the least exposing route and the shortest possible time away from home. When she does
leave, she makes sure to stay close to an exit, even in places she knows well, positioning herself
where she can leave quickly if she feels trapped. In meetings, at appointments, or even in a
restaurant, she scans for doors and calculates how to reach them. She has also tried not to allow
herself to feel strong emotion, telling herself that if she starts to feel too much, the memories
will become harder to contain. She learned to keep conversations contained and to move on quickly
when topics approached anything that stirred up fear, anger, or sadness. Those efforts have narrowed
her life in practical ways. As a divorced woman living alone, she has grown increasingly reluctant
to make plans that involve unfamiliar places or crowded environments, and the shrinking of her
routine has left her feeling increasingly apart from other people.

Malia’s current work as a curriculum designer has remained meaningful to her, but even there she
notices the same alertness. In staff meetings and consultations, she is especially uneasy when a
supervisor or senior administrator speaks in a way that reminds her of being under someone else’s
control. She may focus on the door, sit upright, and rehearse in her mind how she would leave if
needed. She worries quietly that she cannot trust herself to do the right thing if pressure rises
too quickly. That self-doubt has become part of how she sees herself now: someone who cannot rely on
her own judgment when a situation becomes too charged. She also experiences herself as set apart
from others, as though the version of her life shaped by combat has left her on the edge of ordinary
social ease. Even when she is with colleagues or family, she can feel as if she is standing slightly
outside the group, watching rather than belonging.

She sought help after noticing how much her world had narrowed and how often she was living on
guard. The sudden memory intrusions, the reactions to helicopter sounds, and the dread of being in

aware of how much she was missing by staying in. What brought her to treatment was not a single
crisis but the accumulation of years spent bracing herself, avoiding strong feeling, and arranging
her days around exits and escape routes. She came hoping to feel less isolated and less controlled
by the old fear, though what she feared most was that if she let her guard drop, the memory would
return in full and she would again be left with the same helplessness she remembers from the event
itself.
