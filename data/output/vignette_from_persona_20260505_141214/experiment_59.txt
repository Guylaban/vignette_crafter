she was about to lose control. In that moment and in the days that followed, the event had not
settled into the past in the way she expected; instead, it remained vivid and unfinished, returning
to her in fragments that felt as if they were happening again. When she tried to talk about what had
occurred, she could say the facts in a flat, careful way, but she often stopped mid-sentence because
she knew what happened yet could not find words that felt adequate to describe it. What she did say
was that when the memory came back, it brought a wave of rage so intense that she had to clench her
jaw and shut the thought down before she could go further.

In the months after returning home, Aroha found that sirens in the community—an ambulance passing
outside her apartment, the wail of emergency vehicles near the hospital where she worked, even brief
alarms in clinical areas—cut through her with immediate force. Loud noises in general, especially
sudden clatters on the ward or a dropped tray, left her jolting and rigid, her attention snapping
outward before she could decide what was happening. She also noticed that her own body had become a
signal she mistrusted. If her heart began pounding after climbing stairs or hurrying between
patients, she became acutely aware of the sound and sensation of it, and the racing itself seemed to
transport her back into that night with a disturbing vividness. At times she would suddenly feel and
act as though the original danger were unfolding again, going tense, scanning, and shifting into a
state she herself recognized as not fully in the present. She described these moments as frightening
because they seemed to arrive without warning and with the force of certainty, as though her
surroundings had changed before she had time to think.

Aroha had tried to keep life moving, but her days gradually narrowed around what she could not bear
to face. She stopped exercising, even though she used to rely on it to clear her mind, because
exertion made her heart pound and left her feeling too close to the sensations she feared. She also
began avoiding situations that might make her feel happy or hopeful. She noticed herself backing
away from plans with her partner, declining invitations, and muting any anticipation of good news
because joy felt dangerous to her, almost like setting herself up for something to be taken away.
She could not have explained this neatly, only that she did not trust ease anymore. Her relationship
became strained in quiet ways; her partner wanted to celebrate small milestones, but Aroha often
seemed distant, preoccupied, or unwilling to lean into moments that asked her to relax. She knew
this disappointed him, and she felt guilty, yet the pull to keep herself braced remained stronger
than the wish to be fully present.

At work, her vigilance was exhausting but constant. As a nurse, she was expected to move efficiently
through a busy environment filled with monitors, alarms, intercoms, and sharp bursts of sound, and
Aroha found herself constantly scanning her surroundings for signs of danger. She watched doorways,
listened for changes in tone, and repeatedly checked where people were positioned around her.

startled; she spoke more sharply than she intended, snapped at colleagues, and then withdrew with
shame. She did not see herself as an aggressive person, which made these episodes particularly
upsetting, but in the moment she felt driven by a raw, combustible edge that matched the rage she
experienced when the memory surfaced. Even ordinary noise on the ward could leave her feeling keyed
up and ready to react before she had identified what had triggered her.

By the time she sought help, Aroha was still functioning, but only by keeping herself tightly
controlled and increasingly cut off from the parts of life that once felt restorative. What finally
brought her in was the sense that the symptoms were spreading into every corner of her day: at work,
in her relationship, and in the small private moments when a siren outside the window or the thud of
her own heart would yank her back into the event. She wanted to understand why her mind would not
let the experience stay in the past, and she was most afraid that she would keep living in this
narrowed, watchful state indefinitely. At the same time, she hoped that treatment might help her
feel safe enough to exercise again, to accept happiness without bracing for impact, and to talk
about what happened with words that did not disappear the moment she tried to speak them.
