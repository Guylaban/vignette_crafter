Robert, a 65-year-old North American man who had spent most of his adult life as a prosecutor, came
to treatment after several months in which ordinary moments had begun to feel charged with danger.
The event he kept returning to occurred years earlier, while he was leaving a courthouse after a
long day and encountered police officers during what he described as a chaotic, forceful
interaction. What stayed with him was not only the physical roughness of the encounter but the sense
that authority had turned suddenly and inexplicably against him. He had expected order and
protection in that setting; instead, he found himself caught in police violence, disoriented by
shouted commands, incomprehensible chatter, and the sense that no one was speaking in a way he could
reliably understand. In the aftermath, he became preoccupied with how quickly people in uniform
could become threatening, and with how easily misbehavior in others might signal that a situation
was about to get out of control.

Over time, Robert found himself reacting to small, concrete reminders of that experience in ways
that surprised him. If he passed a police car parked outside the courthouse, or saw officers
standing near an entrance, his body tightened and his attention narrowed. Even the sight of police
in a grocery store parking lot could make him feel as if he needed to stay on alert and watch every
movement. He also found that crowded places with overlapping voices were difficult for him to
tolerate. In restaurants, waiting rooms, and hallways, when people spoke at once and their words
blended into incomprehensible chatter, he felt unsettled and on edge, as though he could not tell
what was happening around him quickly enough. He began noticing that his discomfort was especially
strong when other people were behaving badly in public — cutting lines, arguing loudly, acting
thoughtless or aggressive. Those moments seemed to crystallize the same uneasy certainty he had felt
during the original event: that other people’s misconduct could suddenly turn an ordinary scene into
something unsafe.

At home, Robert tried to manage these reactions in ways that made sense to him at the time. He kept
loud music or the television on much of the evening because silence left him too aware of his
surroundings. He described needing the sound as a kind of buffer, something to fill the space and
keep his attention from settling on every distant footstep or unexpected noise. He also found
himself repeatedly asking his wife, and sometimes other people close to him, to reassure him that he
was okay. He would check whether he seemed normal, whether he looked frightened, whether anything
about his behavior suggested that he was losing control. He knew he was asking the same question
more than once, but he said that the reassurance gave him brief relief, even if the feeling never
lasted long. In public, he constantly scanned his environment for signs of threat, noting exits,
watching hands, and tracking who was standing too close. He watched other people closely for
indications that they might harm him, especially when someone looked impatient, angry, or
unpredictable. He had come to believe that staying mentally ahead of others was the only way to
avoid being caught off guard again.

These habits had begun to shape his daily life in concrete ways. As a prosecutor, Robert could not
avoid spaces where authority, conflict, and scrutiny were present, and he noticed that court
corridors, police stations, and tense meetings left him exhausted. He would arrive early so he could
assess the room before others entered, and he sat in ways that let him keep the door in view. At
home, his wife noticed that he was more preoccupied and less able to relax in the evening. He often
stayed up longer than he wanted, then lay awake after going to bed, troubled by trouble falling or
staying asleep. He would drift off only to wake alert, instantly checking the room and listening for
activity outside. The next day he would appear tired but determined to keep going, telling himself
that he had spent decades handling crises and should be able to handle this too.

By the time he sought help, Robert was still functioning but increasingly worn down by the effort of
staying watchful. He came to therapy because the constant scanning, the need to keep asking for
reassurance, and the disrupted sleep had become difficult to ignore, and because he no longer
trusted himself to simply move through public spaces without calculating every possible threat. What
frightened him most was the sense that he could never fully let his guard down, especially around
police or in a crowd where voices blurred and tempers rose. What he hoped for, more quietly, was the
chance to sit in a room, at home or elsewhere, and not have to keep proving to himself that he was
safe.
