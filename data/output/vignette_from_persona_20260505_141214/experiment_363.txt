working at the time she came for help. Before the assault, she had seen herself as someone who could
get through things on her own, and she had built much of her identity around being capable and
steady. That understanding of herself changed abruptly after a frightening incident that took place

returned to her later with the same force. She remembered the sound of shouting in a language she
now associated with danger, and the sharp smell of alcohol hanging in the air. Since then, those
exact cues have become potent in a way that feels to her almost physical. If she hears Arabic being
shouted somewhere nearby, even across a parking lot or through thin apartment walls, her body reacts
before she has time to think. If she catches the smell of alcohol on someone passing by, she can
feel herself stiffen and go still.

In the weeks after the assault, Maya began to find that reminders could arrive without warning. A
sound in the environment, a smell, or an unexpected moment after dark could suddenly bring back the
full memory before she had any chance to prepare herself. She described it as if her mind did not
ease into the memory but dropped her straight back into it. At times, when she remembered what
happened, she would also hear fragments of what she had heard during the event, especially the
shouting, as though it were happening again just beside her. On other occasions, when the memory
came up, she could smell the alcohol again, and that sensation made the event feel immediate and
real rather than distant. There were moments when she would suddenly feel or act as if the assault
were happening again, becoming rigid, scanning, and unable to settle until the wave passed. These
episodes have been unsettling enough that she has started to worry about being caught off guard
anywhere, even in places that should be safe.

Over time, Maya’s days narrowed around what she tried to avoid. She stopped going out at night and
became careful about being outside after dark. What had once been a normal part of life now felt
exposed and risky to her, especially because the assault itself had happened in the dark and because
she found the evening hours more difficult to tolerate. She also began avoiding dates and
anniversaries connected to the event, becoming tense as those times approached and trying not to
mark them. She could not bear to look closely at her own body or pay attention to it, and she found
herself avoiding mirrors and any situation that made her notice herself too much. This was not only
about discomfort; it felt to her as though looking would force her to confront something she was not
ready to face. She had come to feel that her body itself held too much of what happened.

Maya’s sense of safety in the present has been shaped by constant watching. She listens for sounds
that might mean someone is entering the home, especially when she and her husband are at home and

even during brief trips outside during the day. These habits have become part of how she moves
through the world, and they have made ordinary outings exhausting. Because she is unemployed, much
of her time is spent at home, yet even there she remains on guard, listening for noises at the door
or in the hall and trying to keep herself prepared. Her husband has noticed that she is harder to
reassure and more reluctant to leave the house after sunset, and this has changed the rhythm of
their shared life.

As time has gone on, Maya has become increasingly discouraged by the sense that the assault altered
her permanently. She said her beliefs about how the world works had been completely destroyed, and
she no longer felt that the world was predictable or fair in the way she once assumed. She also
believed she was no longer capable of doing the things that defined her. The version of herself that
she remembers from before the assault feels, to her, like someone she cannot fully return to. At the
same time, she has been stuck with the conviction that somebody else would have stopped the event

danger is always close, tired of bracing herself every time she hears shouting, catches the smell of
alcohol, or has to step outside after dark. What she most hopes for is to feel like herself again,
and what she most fears is that the assault has changed the rest of her life in a way she will never
be able to undo.
