Leilani Katoa had spent most of her adult life working as a police officer, a role she had carried
with discipline and pride in her mid-sized American city, where her days were usually shaped by
routines, radio calls, and the practical demands of keeping order. At 71, she was still married,
still observant, and still accustomed to being the one who stayed calm when others could not. That
sense of steadiness changed after an accident involving someone she loved, an event that left her
with a kind of memory she described as startlingly exact, as if the worst moment had been preserved
in her mind with painful clarity. She could still call up the scene as pictures first: the damaged
car, the angle of the impact, the look of confusion and shock that followed. Then came the sounds,
just as sharply, intruding on her mind when she least expected them. She would be standing in her
kitchen or sitting in the police station break room and suddenly hear again the sounds she
associated with that frightening event, not as a story she told herself but as a brief, unwelcome
replay inside her head.

In the weeks after the accident, Leilani found that certain moments in ordinary life could pull her
back to that night with almost no warning. Passing a collision on the road, seeing cars hit by
accident, even a brief glimpse of a crumpled fender on the way to work could freeze her attention
and make her feel she was back in the aftermath of the crash. She began to notice that the smell of
alcohol had become difficult to tolerate. If she caught it on someone’s breath during a social
visit, or drifted past it in a restaurant or at a gathering, her body would tense and her mind would
immediately go to the accident and the sequence of events she could not get out of her head. She
also found that conversations about accidents were no longer neutral. At work, when another officer
or a colleague talked through a traffic incident, Leilani would grow quiet, listening only until she
felt she could safely step away. In a profession where accident scenes and discussions of collisions
were part of the landscape, this created a strain that she did not initially tell anyone about. She
was still showing up, but she was increasingly careful about which calls she volunteered for and
which conversations she tried to avoid.

What troubled Leilani most was how clear the memory remained. She did not experience it as vague or
distant; instead, she described it as vivid and detailed, like a series of images she could not shut
off once they appeared. The clarity of the scene seemed to leave no room for reinterpretation. She
had come to believe that the event happened because of the way she acted, turning over each decision
and each moment in her mind as if she might find the point where things could have been different.
Alongside that, she was certain that she should have protected others and failed. As a police
officer, she had long seen herself as someone responsible for anticipating danger and shielding
people from harm, and this belief sat heavily on her now. Even when others reassured her that no one
could have controlled everything that happened, Leilani remained troubled by the thought that she
had not done enough, not acted quickly enough, or not noticed some sign in time.

That sense of responsibility was tied to a deeper fear that surfaced in quiet moments: she could not
stop bad things from happening to her. The accident had shaken her confidence in the basic
predictability of daily life, and she moved through the months after it with the uneasy feeling that
danger could arrive again without warning. This was especially hard at work, where she encountered
the very kind of incidents that could set off her memories. A call involving a crash scene could
leave her focused on the images from the earlier event for the rest of the shift. The smell of
alcohol at an accident report or in a nearby bar during an after-hours discussion could make her
want to leave immediately, not because she lacked professionalism, but because the sensation was so
closely tied in her mind to what she had lived through.

By the time she sought help, Leilani had grown tired of keeping these reactions to herself and of
carrying the private certainty that she had failed in a way that could not be repaired. She
presented as composed but burdened, speaking with the careful precision of someone used to control,
yet clearly distressed by how often the accident returned to her in images and sounds. What brought
her in was not one dramatic breakdown, but the accumulation of these moments: the repeated jolts
when a crash passed her eye line, the involuntary reaction to alcohol on someone’s breath, the
discomfort of hearing accident talk at work, and the exhausting conviction that she should have done
more. More than anything, she hoped to find a way to live with the memory without feeling as though
it was waiting in every road, every conversation, and every smell, ready to pull her back into the
same frightening scene again.
