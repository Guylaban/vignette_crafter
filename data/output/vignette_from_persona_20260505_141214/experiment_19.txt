Anjali had been healthy and self-reliant for most of her adult life, the kind of woman who took
pride in being able to fix a leak under a sink or replace a faulty valve without asking for help. At
45, single and working as a plumber, she had built a life around practical competence and physical
stamina, moving through basements, crawl spaces, and apartment hallways with the easy confidence of
someone who trusted her body to carry her through a long day. That changed during a life-threatening
illness that began with dizziness and breathlessness she could not make sense of at first. She
remembered the sensation as deeply unsettling: a sudden lightheadedness that made the room seem to
tilt, followed by the frightening feeling that she could not get enough air. When she was taken to
hospital, the smell of antiseptic seemed to sharpen the whole experience, lodging itself in her
memory as tightly as the monitors, the urgency in people’s voices, and the sense that she was no
longer in control of what was happening to her body.

In the weeks and months that followed, Anjali found herself reacting strongly to the same bodily
states that had marked the onset of the illness. If she stood up too quickly on a job and felt even
a moment of dizziness, she would freeze, gripping the edge of a counter or a railing until the
feeling passed. Shortness of breath became especially alarming when she was carrying equipment,
climbing stairs, or working in a hot confined space. She described these moments as if they arrived
with a jolt of certainty: something terrible was about to happen, and she needed to pay attention
before it was too late. The smell of hospital disinfectant was equally potent. When she had to walk
past a clinic entrance on her way to a supply store, or when a maintenance call brought her near an
ambulance bay, she noticed the sharp, clean odor and immediately felt pulled back into that period
of illness. Even the suggestion of a physical examination brought tension, and she began arranging
her days to avoid situations that would place her in medical settings at all. Over time, she stopped
going to routine medical appointments and delayed any psychological support as well, telling herself
she could not afford to be “in the system” unless she was truly forced.

As months passed, Anjali’s habits narrowed further around protecting her body from strain. She had
come to believe that she had to be especially careful because you never knew what could happen next,
and she moved through daily life with that watchfulness always in the background. She avoided
lifting that felt too heavy, took fewer jobs that involved climbing or prolonged exertion, and
turned down work that might leave her breathless or faint. In her mind, the body no longer felt like
a dependable tool; it felt unpredictable, capable of failing without warning. That sense of
unreliability was deeply personal for her, because physical competence had always been central to
how she saw herself. Now, if she felt even mildly unwell, she would scan herself repeatedly, trying
to decide whether the sensation was manageable or the beginning of another crisis. The vigilance was
exhausting, but she did not trust herself to ignore it.

Her mistrust extended outward as well. During the illness, she had experienced how dependent she was
on other people’s judgments and actions, and she had left the hospital with a settled belief that
she could not rely on other people to keep her safe. Since then, she has tended to keep worries to
herself, assuming that others will not understand the seriousness of what she feels or will not
respond in time. At work, this has meant trying to push through on her own, even when she is
frightened by a symptom, rather than asking a colleague to take over. The consequences have been
concrete: her avoidance of medical care has delayed assessment of other problems, and her avoidance
of physical stress has reduced the kinds of plumbing jobs she will accept, affecting both her income
and her sense of competence. She still works, but more guardedly, with less flexibility than before,
always calculating whether a task might leave her dizzy or short of breath.

By the time she sought help, Anjali had become increasingly distressed by how much of her life was
organized around not encountering hospitals, ambulances, examinations, or bodily strain. What
finally brought her in was the growing fear that she was living in a narrowed, cautious version of
herself that she did not know how to expand. She did not come because the fear was gone; she came
because it was starting to shape too much of what she could do. What she most hoped for was a way to
feel safe enough in her own body to work, move, and function without constantly bracing for the next
sign that everything might change again.
