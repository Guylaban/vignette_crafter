Niko was 21, a European American man working as a public defender, and he had been in a relationship
for just over a year when he came for help. He described himself as someone who had learned early to
keep moving, to stay useful, and to avoid being a burden, but after fleeing as a refugee and
arriving in the United States, he found that ordinary life did not feel ordinary to him anymore. The
event he kept returning to happened during the escape: the crowded, disorganized stretch of travel
in which he was surrounded by unfamiliar people, noise, and uncertainty, unable to know what would
happen next. He did not talk about the full details with others, and even in session he kept
circling the edges of it, as if naming too much would make it more real. What stayed with him most
clearly were pictures rather than a full story — fragments of faces, a cramped space, and the look
and feel of being trapped in a moment that could not be escaped. When the memory surfaced, he often
felt his body slip back into the same position it had been in then, tightening in the same way, as
though his muscles remembered before he could fully describe it. At other times, the event came back
not as an image but as a wave of the same terror, abrupt and complete, with no clear scene attached
to it.

In the months after arriving, Niko began watching his own thoughts closely, worried that if he let
his mind drift in the wrong direction he might go “crazy” or lose control. He described checking
himself constantly, almost second by second, for signs that something was wrong with him. He also
made a point of not talking about what had happened or what came after it. Friends and his partner
noticed that he would shut down when the conversation moved anywhere near the journey, immigration,
or people being trapped or detained. He was also careful to avoid reminders that came from other
people, including comments, news stories, or casual questions that touched on refugees or border
crossings. He said it felt safer to steer away quickly than to risk being pulled back into the
event.

At work, these patterns became harder to hide. As a public defender, Niko spent his days in offices,
hallways, and courtrooms where unexpected noises were common. A phone dropping in a neighboring
cubicle, a file cabinet slamming, or a door snapping shut made him jump harder than the people
around him. He knew the reaction was out of proportion, which embarrassed him, but he could not stop
it. In the quiet moments between meetings, when the office went unusually still, he found himself on
edge, waiting for something to happen. Silence did not soothe him; it made him feel that danger was
about to break in. In court, he sometimes reacted as though he were in immediate trouble even when
nothing was wrong, feeling his body tighten before he had time to register that he was safe.

Niko had also come to believe that the world itself was dangerous. He said he had to be on guard all
the time and especially careful because you never know what can happen next. That belief showed up
in the way he moved through the day: scanning rooms, noticing exits, and feeling that he could not
fully relax even when he was with people he trusted. His partner had tried to be supportive, but
Niko’s reluctance to talk meant that she often only saw the edge of what he was carrying. He would
go quiet if she asked questions about the past, and he preferred not to have reminders of it in the
home. Over time, this made him feel more separate and more tired, as if he were always preparing for
the next unknown thing while trying not to let anyone see how much energy that took.

By the time he sought treatment, the symptoms had become impossible to ignore. His PCL-5 score
reflected how much distress he was experiencing, but what brought him in was more immediate: he was
exhausted by the constant sense that something bad might happen, unsettled by how easily ordinary
sounds threw him, and frightened by the moments when the memory arrived as raw emotion or body
sensation without warning. He said he wanted help because he was tired of monitoring himself so
closely and tired of living as if the danger had never ended. What he hoped for was not to forget
what happened, but to be able to sit through a quiet room, hear a sudden noise, and stay present
with his partner and at work without feeling as if he were back in the moment of escape.
