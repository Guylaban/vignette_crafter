Arjun was 29, married, and worked as a painter, usually taking whatever jobs came through his
contacts in the South Asian community and the wider neighborhood where he and his wife rented a
small apartment. The assault that changed things happened on an evening when he had finished a job
and was walking back to his car with his tools. He had been in a street he knew well enough to
assume was safe, still carrying the ordinary fatigue of work, when two men moved in close and
demanded his wallet and phone. In the sudden confusion of the robbery, he had been shoved hard, felt
his body brace, and saw only fragments of what happened next: one face too near, a hand reaching,
the flash of movement by the curb, the harshness of voices cutting over one another. He got away
with little physical injury, but afterward he could not put the event together in a clear sequence.
When he tried to tell his wife about it, he spoke in pieces, stopping and starting, aware that parts
were missing. That incompleteness troubled him, and so did the feeling that he should have done
something different. He found himself replaying the moment with a heavy, stubborn guilt, as though
his own choices had somehow made the robbery worse.

In the weeks that followed, Arjun began waking from nightmares with his heart already racing, as if
he had not fully left the scene behind. On those mornings he would sit up in bed disoriented,
needing a few seconds to understand where he was, and his wife would notice that he was tense and
hard to settle. During the day, memories of the mugging could come to him without warning, not as a
full story but as a sudden sharp return of the fear and confusion from that night. Certain places
became especially difficult. Walking the block outside their apartment, standing near parking lots,
or passing through the same stretch of street where the mugging happened could make him feel the
event pressing in on him again. He would slow down, look around, and keep checking the people behind
him and beside him, scanning constantly for anything that seemed off. In those moments his body felt
wound tight, ready to react before he had fully decided what he was reacting to.

Arjun also found that when he tried to sit quietly and remember the robbery in a deliberate way, the
memory would not come smoothly. He could access isolated images and sensations, but not the whole
event, and that partial quality made him uneasy. He would think about the missing pieces afterward
and feel a surge of guilt, as if failing to remember clearly meant he had failed in some deeper way.
Over time, that mistrust spread beyond the robbery itself. He had come to believe that people could
not be trusted, and that he could not rely on other people to protect him if something happened
again. Even in ordinary interactions, he kept a guarded distance. At work this showed up in small
but noticeable ways: he became more irritable when someone stood too close while he was painting,
and if a client changed plans at the last minute or a coworker approached him unexpectedly, he could
snap in a way that surprised even him. He knew his anger came out quickly, and he disliked how
aggressive it sometimes sounded, but in those moments he felt keyed up and ready for trouble.

The changes had also made him feel set apart from others. He no longer experienced the same ease
being around neighbors or taking jobs in unfamiliar parts of the city. He noticed himself declining
invitations and choosing routes that kept him in more familiar spaces, and that narrowing of his
world left him feeling isolated. His wife tried to reassure him, but Arjun did not fully believe
reassurance would hold if something happened again. What brought him to seek help was the
accumulation of these moments: the nightmares that jolted him awake, the way certain streets made
his chest tighten, and the growing strain in his marriage when she saw how often he was on edge or

movement for danger. What frightened him most was the sense that he could not trust people or his
own recollection of what happened, and what he hoped for, more than anything, was to feel safe
enough to live and work without constantly bracing for the next threat.
