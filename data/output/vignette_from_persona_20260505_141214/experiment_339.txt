Michael, a 55-year-old European American man who worked as a counselor and had lived alone for many
years, was referred for help after he became increasingly distressed following a man-made disaster
he had witnessed years earlier. At the time of the incident, he had been in a public building when a
sudden violent disturbance unfolded around him. He later described the event in practical fragments
rather than as a coherent story, focusing on the sounds of objects being hurled, the sharp burnt
smell that seemed to fill the air, and the sense that smoke was close enough to sting his nose and
throat. What had stayed with him most was the speed with which something ordinary became dangerous:
people shouting, things crashing, the acrid smell of burning, and his own body reacting before his
mind could fully make sense of what was happening. From the beginning, the event felt to him like
something that should never have occurred in a place meant for safety and routine.

In the weeks that followed, Michael noticed that certain sensory cues could bring the experience
back with startling force. The smell of smoke or even a burnt smell in a kitchen or from someone’s
trash fire would stop him in his tracks, and he would feel abruptly back in that moment, not so much
seeing a clear picture as getting pulled into the same emotional intensity. At times he would be hit
by the full feeling of terror without a clear image at all, as if his body recognized danger before
he could. He also found that when he tried to remember the event, it did not feel entirely like his
own memory. He could describe it, but the recollection seemed oddly detached, as if it belonged to
someone else and he was only reporting on it from the outside. The most upsetting part was that his
body still reacted as though the danger were immediate: his heart would race, his muscles would
tense, and he would become convinced that he was not fully safe even in familiar settings. He came
to believe that his body kept responding as if the threat were still present, which left him feeling
unsettled and wary of his own physical reactions.

Michael’s daily life gradually narrowed. As a counselor, he was expected to be attentive, calm, and
emotionally available, but he began to spend much of his time monitoring his own thoughts for signs
that he was “going crazy.” He would catch himself checking whether a stray image or sensation meant
he was losing control, and this internal scanning made ordinary moments feel tense and unstable. He
also became increasingly reluctant to think about the event, certain that if he let himself go there
mentally, he would not be able to handle it. When memories or reminders surfaced, he would cut them
off quickly, changing the subject, turning to paperwork, or distracting himself with routine tasks.
Over time, he also started avoiding seeing friends. Social plans that once would have been easy to
keep became something he put off, then declined altogether. He told himself he was simply busy, but
privately he feared being around others when he felt so on edge and afraid that something could set
him off unexpectedly.

The sense that danger could appear at any moment remained strong. Michael described himself as
having to be especially careful because you never knew what could happen next. That sense of
constant vigilance showed up in small, everyday ways: he noticed exits, listened for sudden noises,
and became uneasy in situations where he could not predict what might happen. Throwing objects was
particularly disturbing to him, not only because it echoed the chaos of the disaster, but because it
seemed to contain the same abrupt force and loss of control he had witnessed. Even a colleague
tossing something across a room or a client making a sudden angry gesture could cause a spike of
alarm that he felt in his body before he could reason it through. These moments were not dramatic by
themselves, but they were potent because they matched the quality of the original event: sudden,
physical, and impossible to ignore.

By the time he sought help, Michael was exhausted by how persistently the experience still intruded
into his life and by the effort of keeping it at bay. He had managed to continue working, but the
strain of hiding his reactions, avoiding friends, and constantly checking his own mind had become
harder to sustain. He presented not because one single crisis had occurred, but because he was tired
of feeling as though he had to live one step ahead of danger and one step ahead of his own thoughts.
What frightened him most was the possibility that he would never again feel fully at ease in his own
body or trust his mind to stay with the present. What he hoped for, though he said it cautiously,
was to be able to remember what happened without being overwhelmed by it, and to move through
ordinary days without smoke, burnt smells, or a sudden thrown object making the world feel unsafe
again.
