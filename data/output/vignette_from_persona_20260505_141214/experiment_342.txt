Mina, a 30-year-old Middle Eastern woman, came to treatment several years after leaving a marriage
that had felt to her, in some quiet and familiar way, like a life built around monitoring danger.
She had grown up in a home where domestic violence was witnessed repeatedly as a child, scenes of
shouting, sudden movement, and bodies crowding doorways becoming part of the background of daily
life. Even now, she struggled to tell that history in a smooth, linear way. When asked about it, she
would pause, look down, and say that she knew what had happened, but that the frightening parts did
not come out in words. At times the memory would rise so sharply that she had to stop speaking
altogether, her face tightening and her breathing changing as if the scene were happening again in
the room with her. What she could describe was fragmentary: the sense of being small and trapped in
the household space, the abruptness of movements that could turn an ordinary moment into something
dangerous, and the lasting impression that people closest to you could not be counted on to behave
safely.

As a child, Mina learned to keep herself quiet and contained, and as an adult she had come to
believe that she could not rely on herself either. She often doubted whether she would do the right
thing, describing a chronic mistrust in her own judgment that made decisions feel heavier than they
seemed to other people. That self-doubt carried into her marriage and later into her divorce, though
she did not present the divorce as a separate trauma so much as part of a longer pattern in which
she had tried to make herself smaller and more controlled. She described making herself “very
careful” with her feelings, trying hard not to show much emotion because once she started, she
feared it would spill over and she would lose control. In session she spoke in a measured,
restrained way, as if keeping a tight lid on her reaction were something she needed to do to stay
safe.

In the years after leaving home, Mina developed a set of habits that made sense to her as
preparation. She mentally rehearsed worst-case scenarios so she would not be caught off guard,
running through what she would do if someone suddenly came toward her, if an argument broke out, or
if she found herself unable to get out quickly. At home she repeatedly checked doors and windows,
sometimes returning to them several times after already making sure they were locked. She said it
was the only way she could settle enough to sit down. Even then, she felt safe in only a very
limited number of places, and wide-open or exposed spaces were especially hard for her. She
described open spaces not simply as uncomfortable but as places where she felt vulnerable and too
visible, where there was nowhere to orient herself if something changed too quickly.

Her work as a bartender made some of these experiences difficult to hide. The bar was often busy,
with people moving fast behind her and around her, and sudden gestures from customers or coworkers
would put her immediately on guard. A hand reaching across the counter too quickly, a person
stepping in too close, or a chair scraping back abruptly could make her body tense before she had
time to think. She did her best to control her expression so that none of this showed, but she left
shifts feeling drained from holding herself so tightly together. There were also moments when she
could not help noticing how much the open layout of some evenings, especially when the bar felt
crowded and exposed, unsettled her. She kept track of exits, trusted only a few familiar corners,
and preferred settings where she could see who was coming and going.

By the time she sought help, Mina was no longer only managing old memories but living inside them in
a practical, everyday sense. She was afraid of sudden movement, afraid of being in the wrong place
without warning, and afraid of her own uncertainty when she had to make decisions quickly. She
wanted therapy because the checking, the vigilance, and the effort to keep her emotions contained
had become exhausting, and because she was tired of feeling that other people could not be trusted.
More than anything, she hoped to feel less trapped by the sense that danger might appear at any
moment, and to find some way of living that did not require her to rehearse catastrophe every day
just to get through it.
