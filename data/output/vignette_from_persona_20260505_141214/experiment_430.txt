Ravi was 50, a South Asian American animator who had spent much of his working life creating
characters and motion on a screen, but the event that now organized his days had nothing animated
about it. Several years earlier, his young child had become suddenly and gravely ill, and the
ordinary sequence of family life had been replaced by emergency appointments, hospital corridors,
and long stretches of waiting beside a bed that felt far too small for the fear in the room. Ravi
remembered the antiseptic smell most vividly; even now, a whiff of bleach or hospital disinfectant
could pull him back into that period with startling force, as if he were again standing at the edge
of a treatment area, watching staff move quickly and speaking in the clipped, efficient language of
urgent care. He had gone from trying to follow explanations he barely understood to sitting through
examinations and procedures in which his child was touched, measured, and checked while Ravi could
only watch, tense and helpless, for signs that the next answer would be worse than the last. He had
come to associate medical settings not with recovery but with the possibility that everything could
tip without warning.

In the months that followed, Ravi noticed that certain situations began to feel intolerable in a way
they had not before. If he had to undergo a medical examination himself, or even anticipate one, his
body tightened and his thoughts narrowed around the memory of being in hospital with his child. He
found himself watching the face of the clinician, listening for changes in tone, and bracing for the
kind of news he had once feared most. The smell of antiseptic in a clinic waiting room could make
his stomach drop before anyone had even called his name. At home, sleep became another vulnerable
place. Sleeping alone, or in an unfamiliar place, left him unsettled and watchful, as though being
unaccompanied at night meant there would be no one nearby if something terrible happened again. He
was more comfortable when his wife was beside him, and he felt especially on edge when travel or
routine disruptions meant he might be away from his own bed. In those moments, he was not simply
uneasy; he became certain that night-time distance from others left him exposed to the same kind of
shock and dread he had once lived through.

Ravi’s mind, meanwhile, kept returning to the period of his child’s illness in ways that felt
intrusive and effortful at the same time. He could spend hours mentally rehearsing worst-case
scenarios, turning them over in advance so that, if another crisis came, he would not be caught off
guard. He told himself that if he imagined every possible complication, he might feel prepared, even
if the exercise left him exhausted. At other times his thoughts took on a sharper, angrier edge: he
would ruminate about how to get even with the assailant, as he called the doctors or hospital system
in the most heated moments of his mind, replaying what had happened and imagining revenge in a way
that gave shape to his helplessness. The thoughts did not lead him to action, but they could occupy
him for long stretches, especially when he was alone after work and the house was quiet. Alongside
this, he repeatedly asked others to reassure him that he was okay. He sought this from his wife,
from family, and sometimes from physicians, needing them to say plainly that he was not missing a
hidden danger, that his own body was fine, that what he was feeling was not a sign that another
catastrophe was starting.

These habits altered his daily life in concrete ways. As an animator, Ravi worked in a field that
required concentration and imagination, yet he found himself distracted by what he might have
forgotten to check or what could go wrong if he did not stay alert. He could still do the work, but
the effort of keeping his thoughts from spiraling often followed him into the studio and back home
again. He became more reliant on reassurance from the people around him, which his wife understood
at first but gradually found draining when he asked the same questions more than once. Travelling
for work or staying elsewhere felt harder than it used to, because the prospect of sleeping alone in
a hotel room made him uneasy before he even arrived. He had started to narrow his world around
places and routines that felt controllable, and his life had become organized around anticipating
what might happen next rather than settling into the present.

Ravi finally sought help after a stretch in which the familiar triggers began occurring more often
and felt harder to shrug off. A clinic visit, the smell of antiseptic, and another night away from
home had left him feeling overwhelmed and ashamed of how much fear still lived in him years after
his child’s illness. He came to treatment wanting relief from the constant mental rehearsal and the
need to keep asking if he was safe, but what frightened him most was the sense that he could never
fully relax again, and what he hoped for was a way to sleep, work, and be with his family without
feeling as though disaster were waiting just beyond the next doorway.
