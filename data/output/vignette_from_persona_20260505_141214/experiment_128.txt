as a social worker, a role she had taken pride in for its steadiness, empathy, and practical
problem-solving. By the time she sought help, she spoke softly but with clear urgency about how

the moment her life split into “before” and “after.” During that period, he had become acutely
unwell and required repeated hospital care, with the ordinary terrain of corridors, waiting rooms,
and treatment bays becoming the setting in which she felt most powerless. The smell of antiseptic,
the sight of needles and drips, and even the sound of medical equipment would now pull her back
toward that time with startling force. She remembered being beside him while staff prepared
injections and infusions, and she had tried to stay composed for him, but inside she felt as though
she was watching something unbearable unfold and could do nothing to stop it.

In the months after that illness, Mariam found that memories did not always arrive as clear scenes.
Sometimes they came as a wave of emotion with no picture at all, a sudden bodily flood of dread,
grief, and helplessness that could hit her while she was doing something ordinary. At other times,
the memory forced its way into her mind when she did not want it there, appearing uninvited and
making it hard for her to continue what she was doing. When she did remember the period in full,
what came most vividly was the overwhelming grief she felt at seeing her child so ill and feeling
responsible for remaining steady. She said there were moments when she felt she had acted in ways
that went against who she was, not because she had wanted to, but because fear had made her brusque,
distracted, or emotionally unavailable when she wanted to be calm and caring. She had come to
believe that those moments reflected something changed in her character, and this belief sat heavily
with her.

As time went on, Mariam began to avoid anything that reminded her of the hospital period. She
stopped going out as much as she once had, explaining that even routine outings could leave her
feeling exposed and on edge, as though something might happen unexpectedly and she would not be able
to handle it. She also made a point of steering clear of hospitals, ambulances, and physical
examinations whenever possible. She would arrange her appointments reluctantly, often delaying them,
and had become uneasy about entering medical settings even for herself. Her social world narrowed
gradually. As a widow, she already lived alone, but now she declined invitations more often and
spent longer stretches at home. This was not framed as fear in the moment so much as a quiet
determination to stay away from places and situations that might bring the old feelings rushing
back. At work, the trigger could arrive in more indirect ways as well. As a social worker, she had
encountered many conversations about loss over the years, yet after her son’s illness she found
herself bracing whenever people talked about losing loved ones. What once had been part of her
professional life now opened an internal door she struggled to keep shut.

Mariam had also developed the habit of trying to push thoughts out of her mind when they surfaced.

away. She described feeling that she had permanently changed for the worse, as though the experience
had altered something basic in her and left her less resilient than she had been before. Beneath

from happening to her. That conviction made the world feel less predictable and left her wary even
in settings that others considered ordinary.

What finally brought Mariam to seek help was the growing burden of these reactions in daily life.
She was tired of avoiding places, tired of feeling ambushed by images and sensations she did not
invite, and tired of carrying the grief and self-criticism in silence. She said she wanted to regain
some sense of safety, but she also feared that if she did not speak about it now, the memory would
continue to force its way in and keep shrinking her world.
