working around rivers, wetlands, and field sites, the kind of career that made him calm in the
presence of weather, machinery, and long silences. He had been married for decades, and until
recently his days had followed a steady rhythm of early mornings, reading reports at the kitchen
table, and occasional visits to outdoor sites where he still liked to look over water quality notes
and habitat maps. That steadiness was broken by an accident that had stayed with him with a force
that surprised even him. While helping at a site where equipment and people had to move carefully in
a confined area, he had made a mistake that led to a serious injury to another person. He was the
one who had set the events in motion, and that fact sat with him in a particularly punishing way:
not only that someone had been badly hurt, but that it was his error, his decision in that moment,
that had made it possible.

In the weeks that followed, Khalil found that the event would come back to him with a vividness that
made it feel less like a recollection than a replay. When he tried to remember it, he often felt as
if it was happening right then, with the same rush of fear and helplessness that had seized him in
the moment. Even though he could say in plain terms what had occurred, he noticed that the story
would not come out easily when he tried to tell it; he knew what happened, but the words seemed to
stop short of the experience itself. He would begin to explain and then pause, searching for
language that felt adequate, only to end up saying that he “could not put it right,” as though the
event were too extreme and too strange to fit into ordinary speech. At times he had the unsettling
sense that it was almost unreal, so severe that it was hard for him to believe it had actually
happened to him, as if it belonged to someone else’s life and yet remained inseparable from his own.

Since then, certain moments in ordinary life had become loaded with the accident. A heavy-breathing
sound could bring him up short, especially if he heard it suddenly nearby, in a quiet room or while
someone was resting close to him. The sound would seize his attention immediately, and he would find
himself listening with a fixed intensity, as if checking whether the breath belonged to someone in
danger. He was equally unsettled by physical affection. A hug from his wife, though meant warmly,
could make him stiffen before he could stop himself. He did not welcome his reaction, and he felt
embarrassed by it, but the closeness of another body and the sensation of being held at once
reminded him how fast a person’s condition could change, how quickly a moment that looked ordinary
could become catastrophic.

Even feeling happy or relaxed had become complicated. There were moments when he might be sitting

would be followed by a sudden jolt of unease. He had come to believe that letting himself settle too
fully into calm was risky, almost as if happiness created room for something terrible to return. As
a result, he often found himself pulling back from ease just as it was beginning, not because he
wanted to but because the feeling itself had become a point of vulnerability. His wife noticed that
he was less able to linger in ordinary pleasure, and he noticed it too, though he struggled to
explain it without sounding as if he were choosing it.

more guarded in situations where breathing sounds, closeness, or moments of comfort might
unexpectedly arise. He was still functioning, but with less spontaneity and more self-monitoring
than before. He avoided conversations about the accident whenever possible, partly because speaking
about it made the memory surge forward with such force and partly because he feared the inadequacy
of his own words. This left him feeling isolated even in the presence of people who cared about him.
In the home he shared with his wife, the change was especially painful because the gestures that had
once conveyed reassurance now sometimes made him recoil or go rigid, and he disliked the look of

was troubled not only by the intensity of the memories but by how often they intruded into moments
that should have been restful or tender. What he wanted most was to be able to think about the
accident without being thrown back into it, to speak of it in words that felt possible, and to
return to his wife’s embrace and to moments of happiness without feeling that he had stepped too
close to danger.
