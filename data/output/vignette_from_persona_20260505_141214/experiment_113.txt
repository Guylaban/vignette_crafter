Levi was 46 years old, an auditor by trade, and when he first came to treatment he spoke in the
careful, measured way of someone used to checking figures twice before trusting them. He was
originally from an Oceania background and had lived in the United States for years; until recently
he had been widowed and living alone, keeping to a narrow routine that made the world feel easier to
sort. He traced the beginning of his difficulties back to the natural disaster that took his wife
from him and left his life permanently altered. On that day the weather had turned with frightening
speed, and what had started as an ordinary stretch of time became a sequence of chaos he still
struggled to make fully real in his mind. He remembered the force of it, the abrupt loss of control,
and the helplessness of being unable to stop what was happening. Later, even when he tried to tell
the story, it seemed to him so extreme that it was hard to believe it had actually happened to him.

In the months that followed, Levi found that the event returned to him in fragments rather than as a
steady narrative. When he thought about the day, he could sometimes smell the same damp, muddy odor
that had hung in the air afterward, and at times he could taste what he had tasted then, a metallic,
dirty flavor that came back with the memory. These sensations made the disaster feel immediate
again, as if it were not something behind him but something that could break open at any moment. He
noticed that the first hours after dark were especially difficult, and if he woke in the night or
found himself in a dark room, his body would tense before he had even fully oriented himself. Night-
time no longer felt like rest; it felt like a vulnerable interval in which something dangerous might
be waiting.

Over time Levi’s world became smaller. Physical locations could set him off in a way that surprised
other people but made perfect sense to him, because unfamiliar places left him unable to assess what
might happen there. He began declining invitations to new restaurants, new neighborhoods, and even
new routes when he drove to work, preferring the same streets and the same parking lot outside the
office building. In his job as an auditor, that narrowing showed itself in practical ways. He became
uneasy when assignments took him somewhere he had not been before, and he would sometimes hesitate
before entering an unfamiliar office, scanning exits and watching the room as if a risk he could not
name might suddenly reveal itself. He did not describe this as fear so much as a refusal to put
himself in a situation where danger could not be measured.

At home, he was troubled by quiet. Unusual silence made him feel that something dangerous was about
to happen, and delays of any kind were difficult for him to tolerate. If a text message went
unanswered, if a meeting ran longer than expected, or if a family member was later than planned, he
immediately assumed something bad was happening. These moments could pull him into a state of tense
waiting, listening for sounds that would tell him what he needed to know. At the same time, he had
become someone who avoided talking with others about the disaster or its aftermath. He would change
the subject when friends asked how he was doing, and even with people who cared about him he found
it easier to keep the event sealed off than to hear himself speak it aloud.

Levi also found that he had begun avoiding relaxing, because relaxation itself felt dangerous to
him. When he let his guard down, even briefly, he feared that something could happen while he was
not ready for it. He filled the empty spaces in his day by smoking more often, stepping outside for
cigarettes when he felt himself starting to drift into stillness. The habit gave him something to do
with his hands and a reason not to settle into the quiet that made him uncomfortable. Yet despite
these efforts, he described himself as feeling dead inside, as if some essential part of him had
gone cold after the disaster. He also spoke with a bleak conviction that he could not stop bad
things from happening to him and that the world was a dangerous place, a place that could turn
without warning and take away whatever mattered most.

He sought help after realizing how much his life had contracted around these fears. What brought him
in was not one single crisis but the accumulation of small losses: the missed social invitations,
the tension at work when he had to go somewhere unfamiliar, the sleeplessness that came with waking
in the dark, and the persistent sense that he was always bracing for the next bad thing. Levi said
he did not want to keep living as if danger were around every corner, but he was equally afraid that
if he let himself relax or speak about what happened, something terrible would catch him off guard
again. More than anything, he hoped for a way to feel safe enough to breathe without waiting for the
world to break.
