months earlier with contacts that at first seemed intrusive but manageable: repeated messages,

lit moments that arrived out of sequence. She remembered the sound of heavy breathing close to her
phone or just beyond a door, the kind of breath that made her body freeze before her mind had fully
caught up. She remembered scanning for the police with a surge of relief and dread, because
authority presence meant the possibility that someone else had finally believed her, but it also
confirmed how serious things had become. She also remembered the sickening certainty that she was
being followed or tracked, a conviction that made even ordinary errands feel like she was moving
through a map someone else had already studied.

In the months that followed, Megan found that her reactions were no longer tied only to the known
facts of the stalking but to moments that resembled it in the most ordinary ways. If she heard heavy
breathing in a quiet room, or behind her in a store aisle, her attention snapped toward it before
she could decide to look. If she was walking to her car after school and noticed a person nearby she
did not recognize, she felt threatened in a way she found hard to dismiss, even when nothing overtly
alarming had happened. Unusual silence bothered her almost as much as noise; when the house was
still at night or the hallway outside her classroom went abruptly quiet, she felt as if something
dangerous was about to happen. She described living with a constant internal insistence that she had
to be on guard all the time. At school, this meant checking the parking lot before leaving,
lingering near other staff members, and making sure her classroom door was locked. At home, it meant
repeatedly glancing out windows and asking her boyfriend to listen when she thought she heard
someone outside. She disliked how tense she had become, but she was convinced that letting her guard
down would be irresponsible.

Megan also struggled with the way the memories came to her. She said she could describe the broad
outline of what had happened, but not in a smooth way. Some moments were extremely clear — the sound
of breathing, a message appearing at the wrong hour, the sense that she had been found again — while
other parts were blank, as if the story had gaps she could not fill. Fragments would surface without
warning and disappear just as fast, leaving her shaken and annoyed with herself. When people asked
her to explain what the stalking had been like, she knew what happened but felt unable to find words
that matched the experience. The events felt too disorganized and too personal to tell cleanly, and
she often stopped mid-sentence, frustrated by her own silence. She noticed that this made
conversations with loved ones harder, especially when they wanted details she could not easily
supply.

As time passed, Megan came to believe that people could not be trusted. The realization had not made
her suspicious in a dramatic way; instead, it settled into her as a steady expectation that
strangers might approach with hidden intent, even in ordinary situations like a checkout line, a
sidewalk, or the school gate. She no longer experienced the world as reliably public or safe. The
stalking also left her with a bleak sense that her life had been destroyed by the trauma. She said
this carefully, not as an exaggeration but as her honest account of how different everything felt to
her. Teaching, once a source of competence and routine, now required a level of vigilance she found
exhausting. She kept functioning, but in the background of her work she was constantly tracking
entrances, voices, and movement. In her relationship, she tried to appear steady, yet she often
needed reassurance after hearing footsteps in the building or noticing a car that seemed to linger
too long.

By the time she sought therapy, Megan was no longer only frightened by what had happened; she was
frightened by how much of her life now felt organized around staying safe. She wanted help because
she was tired of living as though danger could appear in the form of a breath, a stranger’s
approach, or an empty silence. At the same time, she was ashamed of how hard it had become to trust
her own judgment. What she hoped for was not to forget what happened, but to stop feeling as if it
were still happening somewhere just beyond her awareness.
