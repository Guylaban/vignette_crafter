Anna had always taken pride in the predictability of her life in Portland: the steady rhythm of her
work as a graphic designer, the bike rides she used for errands, the restaurants and coffee shops
she and her husband liked to try on weekends. That changed after a man she had briefly known began
stalking her. At first his attention seemed merely intrusive, but it steadily became more persistent
and unnerving. He appeared where he should not have been, and she became aware of how often she was
noticing him nearby, or thinking she had seen him across a street or at the edge of a crowd. What
frightened her most was not a single dramatic incident but the accumulating sense that she was being
watched and tracked, that her own movements were no longer private. The experience was tied in her
mind to ordinary places and ordinary ways of getting around, especially her bicycle, which she had
once used casually and now associated with the possibility of being followed.

In the weeks that followed, Anna found herself changing small parts of her day without fully
deciding to. She stopped going near the place where the stalking had first become most intense, even
when it was only a route past a familiar block or a building she used to pass without thinking. Her
office was not far from that area, and she began taking longer, less direct paths to work so she
would not have to ride through it. On her bicycle she became acutely aware of movement behind her,
of someone matching her pace, of wheels, footsteps, or a voice that might be meant for her. Even
when nothing was wrong, she often reacted as if danger were already present. A man walking a few
meters behind her, a car slowing near the curb, someone pausing outside the café where she was
sitting could send her attention sharply outward, her body bracing before she had time to decide
whether there was any real threat.

At work, these habits became harder to hide. Anna’s office is in a busy open-plan studio downtown,
with a glass door that opens directly onto the street. She had come to need to know where the exits
were in any room before she could relax enough to stay there, and in meetings she would sit so she
could see the door. If she arrived early to a client presentation, she would choose a chair nearest
the exit and keep track of who came and went. She constantly scanned the environment for signs of
threat: a person lingering outside the window, a bicycle leaning too long by the entrance, someone
standing still while others moved past. Because she was always identifying escape routes before she
felt comfortable anywhere, she found herself less able to settle into the work that used to absorb
her. She still completed her projects, but the effort of staying alert ran beside everything else,
and colleagues noticed that she was more likely to excuse herself early or avoid after-work
gatherings entirely.

Over time, the world came to feel less reliable to her. Anna was certain that the people responsible
had faced no consequences, and that conviction sharpened her sense that harm could happen without
warning and without anyone intervening. She had come to believe that people were not what they
seemed, that someone could look ordinary and still be watching, waiting, or following. This made
even brief interactions feel loaded. A stranger asking for directions, someone standing too close in
line, a cyclist pulling up beside her at a light—small moments that others would dismiss felt to
Anna like possible openings for something worse. She described herself as jumpy and easily startled
now, especially if someone approached her from behind or if a sound broke through her concentration.
Her body seemed to react before her mind could catch up, and that reaction embarrassed her as much
as it unsettled her.

Anna did not seek help immediately. For months she managed by building her routines around caution,
but the cost was becoming harder to ignore. She avoided neighborhood streets she had once liked,
took increasingly convoluted routes on her bike, and arranged her day around places where she could
see an exit. The changes narrowed her world and made social life more complicated; she declined
invitations that would require her to arrive somewhere unfamiliar or stay in crowded spaces. What
brought her to therapy was the realization that she was living as though every ordinary outing
required surveillance and preparation. She came in describing how exhausting it was to keep
scanning, to keep calculating where she could get out, and how frightened she felt by how quickly
her body now jumped into alertness. Anna hoped for relief from that constant sense of danger, but
she was equally afraid that if she let her guard down, she would be proving herself wrong at exactly
the moment she most needed to be careful.
