Michael had been 48 when the loss that changed the center of his life happened, a North American
shopowner who had spent most days in the same small business he and his wife had built together. For
years he had been the steady one in the family, the man who kept the doors open, remembered
customers’ names, and came home tired but unshaken. Then his wife died, and the event landed in his
life not as an abstract bereavement but as a rupture that he experienced as if the world had split
into before and after. In the months that followed, Michael found that anything connected with
losing someone could hit him with unexpected force. If a customer mentioned a parent who had passed
away, or a neighbor stopped by talking through a funeral arrangement, he would feel a hard
tightening in his chest and want to get away from the conversation. Funerals, cemeteries, and other
grief-related gatherings were especially difficult; he would avoid them if he could, telling himself
that he had already done enough “showing up” and could not bear being around more loss. Even
reminders of other victims, whether on the news or in passing stories, could leave him unsettled in
a way that felt immediate and personal.

Michael had tried to manage this by not thinking about the event and by smoking more than he used
to. He described cigarettes as something to hold onto when his mind started drifting toward his
wife, or when a quiet moment in the shop gave him too much room to remember. He did his best to push
away anything that reminded him of someone he had lost. He changed the channel if a memorial came on
television, stepped out of rooms when grief came up in conversation, and found excuses not to attend
gatherings where candles, flowers, or photographs of the dead might be present. Over time, this
narrowed his world. He still opened the shop every day, but he was more guarded with customers and
less likely to linger after closing. At home, he noticed that his wife’s absence was everywhere, yet
he worked hard not to look directly at it. Friends and relatives began to notice that he was missing
family events more often, and he knew his distance was being read as coldness, though from his own
perspective it was the only way to keep himself from being overwhelmed.

What frightened Michael most was how easily ordinary sensations could throw him into a state of
alarm. A dropped box in the stockroom, a car backfiring outside the shop, or even a chair scraping
unexpectedly across the floor could make him jump before he had time to think. If someone moved
suddenly near him, he reacted as though he were in danger, turning sharply and bracing himself,
embarrassed by how quickly his body seemed to betray him. He did not describe these moments as
exaggerated; to him they felt entirely reasonable in the split second they happened, as if his body
had learned to expect that something terrible might come without warning. Alongside that, he carried
a steady weight of negative feelings—fear, horror, anger, guilt, and shame—which he experienced at
different times and often without relief. Some days the anger was directed at the unfairness of the
loss, other days the guilt was sharper and more private, leaving him ashamed of how much he could
not control his own reactions.

The memory itself remained unusually alive for Michael. When it came, it did not feel like something
that belonged to the past. He would say that it felt as though it was happening right now, in the
present tense, with his wife’s absence and the circumstances of her death pressing in on him all
over again. The details were painfully clear to him, and he could recall the frightening moments
with a vividness that made them hard to set down. He described the memory as if it were a film that
plays over and over, returning without invitation and showing him the same scenes with the same
force each time. At night, or during pauses in the day when the shop was quiet, the images could
come back with such clarity that he would feel pulled back into that period, as though there were no
distance between then and now.

By the time he sought help, Michael was increasingly concerned by how much his life had been
organized around avoiding reminders and getting through the day without being thrown by the next
sound, image, or conversation. He came in after his wife’s death had begun to shape not only his
private moments but also his work and relationships, and after he realized that the effort to keep
grief at arm’s length was leaving him isolated and worn down. What he hoped for, though he said it
cautiously, was to be able to remember her without feeling as if he were right back in the middle of
the loss, and to walk into his shop, his home, or even a funeral without bracing for the next wave
of fear, shame, or the sudden sense that everything was happening all over again.
