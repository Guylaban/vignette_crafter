Malia, a 58-year-old divorced psychiatrist of Oceanian background, sought help after several months
in which an old accident had begun intruding on ordinary life in ways she found increasingly
difficult to manage. Years earlier, she had been in a life-threatening car accident that unfolded so
quickly she still could not place the pieces in a reliable order. What she remembered did not come
to her as a clean story but as disconnected flashes: the impact, the violent jolt of metal, a sense
of cars hitting by accident that still returned with alarming force, and then a confusing stretch in
which the sequence made little sense even to her when she tried to explain it. She could not tell a
clear story of what happened without stopping, restarting, and losing track of where one moment
ended and another began. Even now, she said, something in her surroundings could bring the whole
thing back without warning, as if a switch had been thrown.

In the months after the crash, Malia found herself caught by unwanted thoughts that arrived
uninvited and at odd times. They were not tied to any single scene so much as to the shock of the
event itself and the image of cars being struck, and they could surface while she was working,
driving, or simply moving through the day. As a psychiatrist, she was used to listening closely to
other people’s accounts of fear and loss, but she noticed that her own mind had become less
dependable when these thoughts came. She had come to believe that something permanent had happened
to her, that she had changed for the worse in a way she could not reverse. She also found herself
doubting her own judgment more than before, worrying that she could no longer trust herself to do
the right thing in a moment that mattered. The crash had left her with a conviction that the world
itself was dangerous, not in an abstract way but in the everyday sense that harm could appear
without warning and change everything in a second.

Certain situations brought the accident back with particular sharpness. Trauma-related events,
especially anything that resembled the original incident, could overwhelm her with the same bodily
surge of fear and disorientation she felt at the time. A sudden brake squeal outside her office
window, the sight of a damaged vehicle on the street, or hearing about a collision in the news could
all pull her back into the memory before she had time to prepare. She described these moments as if
the memory were not recalled but forced upon her. At work, when she saw patients after a commute
through busy traffic, she sometimes arrived already tense, because the day might begin with a near
miss in the parking lot or a glimpse of a crash scene on the road. She had begun choosing routes and
timing her driving more carefully, but even then she could not keep the memory from arriving when it
wanted.

Over time, Malia organized her life around keeping a sense of control, especially over her emotions.
She tried hard to contain visible distress, both at work and at home, because any strong feeling
seemed risky to her. She did not want to be seen as unraveling, and she especially did not want to
experience the sense of being overtaken by fear that had followed the accident. This effort to stay
composed came at a cost in her relationships. Since her divorce, she had become more withdrawn in
intimate situations and avoided sex, not because she no longer desired closeness, but because sexual
contact left her feeling vulnerable in a way she could not tolerate. She also avoided going to sleep
or lying in the dark. Nighttime brought its own apprehension: when she lay down, the room felt too
exposed, and she preferred to remain in positions where she could see what was around her. She felt
safest with her back against a wall, able to scan the room and know who or what was there. Even
then, she woke during the night to check whether she was safe, often rousing herself fully enough to
listen, look, and orient to the room before trying to settle again.

Her startle response became noticeable in daily life. She felt jumpy and easily startled by sudden
sounds, whether in the clinic, at home, or in public places. A door closing sharply or an unexpected
call of her name could make her body react before her mind caught up. This vigilance was
particularly hard for her in settings where she could not choose her seat or her angle of view. At
work, in meeting rooms or during consultations outside her office, she found herself scanning for a
place with a clear line of sight and a wall at her back, a small arrangement that helped her feel
less exposed. These habits were not dramatic, but they shaped her movements through the day and made
her feel more constrained than she wanted to admit.

By the time she sought therapy, Malia was tired of sleeping lightly, checking her surroundings, and
feeling ambushed by memories that arrived in fragments. What finally pushed her to ask for help was
the sense that she was living as if the crash were still waiting to happen again, and that she could
no longer simply outthink or outwork that reality. She hoped to regain enough steadiness to sleep
without fear, to sit through the night without checking the room, and to trust herself again in the
ordinary places where she now felt on guard. Most of all, she wanted some reassurance that the
accident had not irrevocably taken something essential from her.
