exposure that later brought her to treatment happened at work and cut sharply across that identity.

stayed with her most was not only the exposure itself but the sense that her own body had turned

control. In the days and weeks that followed, she noticed that anything that reminded her of
violence could jolt her with a sickening immediacy. Seeing violence in a film or hearing about it in
passing would make her tense and pull inward, as if danger had moved close again. Blood, which had
once been part of the ordinary landscape of her profession, became a visual and bodily cue she could
not easily dismiss; even a glimpse of it could bring back the queasy feeling of being physically
unwell and make her feel abruptly on edge.

As the months passed, Margaret found herself arranging her life around keeping those reactions from
happening. In therapy, she listened carefully, but then went home and tried not to think about what
had been discussed, telling herself she could not afford to dwell on it because once she started,

especially difficult. Where she had once thought comfortably in terms of schedules, family events,

could no longer trust.

Over time, Margaret’s view of herself grew harsher and more disorienting. She had come to believe

fragile in a way she did not recognize. She also spoke with a growing sense that nothing in her life
