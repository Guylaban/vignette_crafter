Michael was 46, a married graphic designer from a European American family, and when he first came
to treatment he described his life as if it had been split cleanly in two: before the police
encounter and after it. The event itself had taken place during what should have been an ordinary
evening. He had been stopped by police, and what began as a tense interaction escalated into a
frightening scene in which he was left exposed to authority figures he could not reason with or get
away from. He did not describe every detail in a tidy sequence; instead, when he tried to tell it,
he would lose the thread partway through, stopping and starting as if the memory itself pulled him
under. What stayed with him most vividly were flashes of visible injuries and wounds on others
nearby, sights that lodged in him with a force he had not expected. Even now, if he tried to explain
what had happened, he often found himself unable to finish, his voice trailing off as the feeling of
it became too much to hold and speak at the same time.

In the months that followed, Michael noticed that the memory could arrive without warning. He might
be at home, in the middle of a normal task, and something in the surroundings would suddenly bring
back the full scene so quickly that he would have to stop what he was doing. He described the return
of the memory as overwhelming, not just upsetting, but so flooded with emotion that he had to cut
himself off from it. He became especially wary of police presence; even seeing officers in person,
on television, or outside his window could make him feel instantly on edge. The sight of injuries on
other people carried the same charge. A scraped knee, a cut hand, or blood visible on someone else
could jolt him back into that night in a way that felt immediate and physical, as if his body had
already started responding before he had a chance to think. He said he hated that this could happen
anywhere, at any time, and that he never knew what ordinary object or image might suddenly open the
door to the memory.

Living with that unpredictability changed the way Michael moved through the world. He had come to
avoid other people more than he used to, keeping his contact limited and creating distance whenever
he could. At work as a graphic designer, he increasingly structured his day around remaining
mentally occupied, filling every gap with tasks so there was no quiet moment for his mind to drift.
Outside of work, he spent long hours on his phone, on games, or scrolling the internet, not because
he enjoyed all of it, but because being absorbed in a screen made it easier to keep the unwanted
thoughts from breaking through. He no longer felt comfortable in many places unless he had first
identified escape routes and checked how he would get out if he needed to leave quickly. In
restaurants, meeting rooms, and even in his own house, he would take in doors, hallways, and exits
before he could settle enough to sit down. He said he knew, intellectually, that he was in safe
places, but he was unable to relax there anyway. His body stayed tense and ready, as if it had not
received the message that the danger was over.

Over time, Michael’s sense of what his life was supposed to mean became badly shaken. He spoke with
a hard, flat conviction that nothing in his life felt meaningful anymore and that the trauma had
destroyed the life he thought he had been living before. What angered and haunted him just as much
was his belief that what had happened to him had been deeply unfair and that nothing had been done
about it. He carried that injustice with him, revisiting it in his mind with no sense that it had
been answered or made right. Although he remained married, the pattern of withdrawing, staying on
his phone, and avoiding people had changed the atmosphere at home, leaving his wife frustrated by
how difficult it was to reach him. By the time he sought help, it was not because the memories had
vanished but because they had become impossible to keep managing alone. He wanted relief from the
constant state of alertness and from the way the memory could seize him so completely, and he was
most afraid that this would remain the rest of his life—that he would never again feel safe enough
to let his body stand down, never mind feel that his life meant anything at all.
