once too vivid and strangely unreal. He would think about the night and feel as though the whole

sudden rush of the same fear and helplessness he had felt during the flooding, as if his body
recalled the event before his mind could assemble an image. More than once, when the memory broke
through, he noticed the stale taste of floodwater in his mouth so distinctly that he would spit into
the sink and rinse his mouth several times, unsettled by how closely his present body seemed to
reach back into that night.

As months passed, Minh became increasingly aware of the way ordinary sensations could pull him back.

made him stop and worry that something was wrong with him. If he woke feeling physically unwell,
even mildly tired or off, his attention would narrow immediately to the possibility that something
bad was happening again, and he would spend time checking himself rather than settling into the day.

could steady himself. He was careful not to say much when those moments happened. He worried that
other people would not understand, and that what had happened to him had made him too different from
everyone around him to explain easily.

seemed, that ordinary friendliness could hide a lack of real understanding, and that even well-

secretive, but because he was increasingly certain that others could not hold the reality of it

unbearable. He felt isolated and set apart in meetings, in after-work lunches, and even in casual
messages from friends who had no trouble moving on to weekend plans. He would see their ease with
ordinary life and feel that he no longer belonged anywhere in quite the same way. The disaster had
left him with a private, unshareable knowledge, and he often found himself sitting alone in his
apartment after work, aware that he had a steady job and a clean room and people around him in the
city, yet still feeling as if he stood just outside the life other people were living.

He sought help when the disorientation and the body-based reminders became harder to ignore and he
realized how much he had organized his days around trying not to be caught off guard. What brought
him in was not only the fear that the memories might keep breaking in, but the growing loneliness of
feeling unlike everyone else and unable to trust that others would truly see what he had lived
through. What he most hoped for was a way to make sense of the event without feeling as though it
belonged to someone else, and a way to move through his work and daily life without every neck ache,
moment of feeling ill, or mention of a storm opening the same old wound.
