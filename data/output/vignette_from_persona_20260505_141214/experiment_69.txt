Darnell, a 62-year-old African American man, had spent most of his adult life in bomb disposal work,
a profession he once described with pride as exacting and necessary, the sort of job that demanded
calm hands and absolute concentration. The incident that changed that steadiness came during a
response to a suspected chemical device at work, when a toxic exposure left him abruptly overwhelmed
by the smell and the physical reality of something in the air that was not supposed to be there. He
remembered the scene in fragments: the worksite, the urgency, the clatter and shouted instructions
around him, then the sudden tightening in his chest and the unmistakable sense that his body had
been placed in danger before he had fully understood what was happening. In the months that
followed, Darnell found that ordinary loud noises could send a jolt through him, especially sharp
bangs at work that sounded too much like the abrupt violence of that day. The smell of hospitals or
antiseptic also became difficult for him to tolerate; if he passed through a clinic corridor or
caught that clean, medicinal odor in a medical setting, his attention narrowed and he felt pulled
back to the period when he was being checked and monitored after the exposure.

He began changing the way he moved through daily life almost without noticing it at first. At work,
he was still highly functional, but he overworked himself, taking on extra tasks and staying busy
well beyond what was required because he did not like the moments between demands, when his thoughts
had room to catch up with him. He had come to rely on constant activity as a way to keep from being
alone in a room or building, and he disliked the silence that settled when a job ended and everyone
else had gone home. If he finished late and found himself alone in the workplace, he would keep busy
with paperwork or minor checks, preferring the structure of motion to the unease of sitting still.
He also avoided hospitals, ambulances, and physical examinations whenever he could. Medical settings
felt especially difficult after the exposure, and he would rearrange appointments or delay care
unless something made it impossible to postpone. His avoidance had practical consequences: he was
less willing to seek routine medical attention, and the prospect of being examined in a clinical
room, under fluorescent lights with that antiseptic smell, was enough to make him tense before he
even arrived.

What Darnell noticed most in the present was how much of his attention now went to keeping ahead of
danger. He constantly scanned his surroundings for signs of it, even in places where he knew,
rationally, that nothing was likely to happen. When he entered a building, he immediately identified
escape routes before he felt comfortable enough to settle, mentally mapping exits, hallways, and the
fastest way out. He did this in restaurants, at work, and even in his own family’s social
gatherings, where he would choose a seat that let him see the door. His wife had learned that he
preferred to stand rather than sit for long periods in unfamiliar spaces, and that he often
positioned himself near an exit without explaining why. He was also startled when someone touched
him unexpectedly. A hand on his shoulder from behind, a casual tap from a coworker, or even an
affectionate gesture caught him off guard could make him react with visible alarm before he had time
to compose himself, as if his body responded before his mind could catch up. He found these
reactions embarrassing, but they seemed to him unavoidable, the kind of reflex that arrived fully
formed and left him trying to regain his composure.

By the time he came for help, Darnell had become tired of organizing his days around staying alert
and staying busy. He could still do his work, but it had become harder to ignore how much effort it
took to maintain that pace and how often he found himself planning routes, checking surroundings,
and bracing for unexpected contact or noise. He spoke as someone who did not want to lose his
competence or his place in the world, yet who was increasingly weary of living as though he were
always one moment away from danger. What finally brought him in was the sense that these patterns
had narrowed his life enough that even a man trained for high-risk situations no longer felt fully
at ease in ordinary ones. He wanted relief, though he was also worried that if he stopped working so
much or let himself be still, the thoughts and sensations he had been outrunning would come rushing
in.
