Marisol had been 38 when her life changed in a way she still struggled to put into words. She was
working at the factory on an ordinary shift when she got the call that her younger brother had died
unexpectedly. What she remembered most vividly was the sound of sirens that later cut through the
streets as she made her way to where her family had gathered, the harsh wail seeming to freeze
everything around her. In the days that followed, the practical details of death came quickly and
with a kind of brutal finality she had not been prepared for: funeral arrangements, the cemetery,
the sight of the grave, the conversations that revolved around loss and what it meant to say
goodbye. Marisol found herself standing in those places feeling as if she were moving through
someone else’s life, listening to relatives speak and hearing only fragments, because her attention
kept snapping back to the fact that her brother was gone and there was no way to undo it.

In the months after the funeral, Marisol began to organise her days around anything that would keep
her away from reminders of death and grief. She stopped going to cemeteries altogether and made
excuses when friends or cousins invited her to memorials, wakes, or family events where people would
speak about loved ones who had died. Even ordinary conversations became difficult if someone
mentioned losing a parent, sibling, or partner, because the topic seemed to land in her body before
she could answer. At home and at work, she found herself trying to avoid photographs, songs, or
objects that made her think of her brother, as though anything connected to him might pull her back
into the same helpless moment. She had come to believe that if she let herself think too much about
what had happened, she would be overwhelmed by it all over again, so she worked hard to keep grief
at a distance even as it seemed to appear everywhere.

Sirens remained one of the most powerful reminders. When an ambulance passed the factory or sounded
in the distance while she was on break, Marisol would tense immediately and feel the event rushing
back at her with disturbing clarity. Her mind would return to that day and begin to replay the
details over and over, as if by examining every minute she might discover where things had gone
wrong or how she could make sense of the suddenness of his death. Instead of bringing relief, the
replaying left her more unsettled, because each time she reached the same ending: she could not
change what had happened, and she could not bring him back. She also found herself repeatedly asking
people close to her if she seemed all right, if they thought she was coping, if they could reassure
her that she would somehow be okay. For a little while, hearing others say that she would get
through it helped her hold herself together, but the relief never lasted, and before long she would
be asking again. Marisol knew this made her feel dependent in a way she disliked, yet she could not
seem to stop searching for certainty from other people.

As time passed, Marisol’s view of herself grew harsher. Before the death, she had thought of herself
as a happy person, someone who laughed easily with her siblings and cousins and who could find
pleasure in simple things after a long shift. Now she felt that that version of herself was gone.
She often told herself that she used to be happy but was miserable all the time now, and that her
life had been destroyed by the trauma. She also felt cut off from others in a deeper way, as if she
no longer belonged anywhere. Family gatherings that once felt warm now left her with the sense that
everyone else had a place in the world except her, and that she was somehow outside of it, unable to
rejoin the life she had known before. At the factory, she still showed up and completed her work,
but the routine felt hollow. When a siren sounded outside the building, she would lose track of her
concentration for a moment, her body tightening as she waited for the familiar surge of dread.
Coworkers noticed that she kept to herself more and declined invitations after work, and she noticed
it too, but withdrawal had become easier than trying to explain what she felt.

By the time Marisol sought help, she was exhausted by how much effort it took to get through each
day. What finally brought her in was the fear that she would remain trapped in this state, forever
orbiting the same loss and never feeling like herself again. She was not sure she wanted to relive
the death, but she wanted relief from the relentless return of it, and from the sense that her world
had narrowed around everything she was trying to avoid. More than anything, she hoped to find some
way to think about her brother, her life, and herself without feeling that the whole thing had been
ruined beyond repair.
