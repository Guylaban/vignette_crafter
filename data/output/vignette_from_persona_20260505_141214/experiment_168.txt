Marianne was 54, a European American woman who had spent most of her adult life in kitchens, and
when she first came for treatment she described herself as still doing the work she knew best but no
longer moving through the world in the same way. Before the assault, she had been the kind of chef
who could keep several things going at once, calling orders across a hot line and moving through a
crowded kitchen with easy authority. The assault happened after work, when she was alone with a man
she had known well enough to trust. She had believed she was in a safe, ordinary situation, in a
place and with a person that should not have required caution. Instead, she was attacked, and
afterward what stayed with her was not a full, orderly story but a series of harsh fragments: the
sense of being pinned, the shock of realizing he was the one who had hurt her, and a bodily memory
that returned in pieces she could not easily put into words. When she tried to speak about it, she
often stopped and said that the event did not come out as a narrative in her mind; it came back as
sensations and disconnected flashes that would not fit together into a beginning, middle, and end.

In the weeks and months that followed, Marianne found that certain kinds of conversation now made
her tense before she fully noticed it. Discussions that drifted toward assault, betrayal, or women
being unsafe would leave her quiet and watchful, as if the topic itself were too close to something
unstable. She also became uncomfortable when someone tried to get emotionally close to her. She had
once been warm and open with her husband and a few close friends, but now she felt exposed when
another person pressed for intimacy or asked her to explain how she was doing. She found herself
pulling back, not because she wanted to be alone, but because emotional closeness made her feel as
though she were being asked to trust the wrong thing again. Being alone with men was especially
difficult. At work, she still managed the kitchen, but she arranged herself so that there were
always others around when possible, and she felt uneasy in the practical moments that used to seem
ordinary, such as stepping into a storage area with a male line cook or finding herself briefly
isolated in a back corridor with a deliveryman. She did not describe herself as irrational; rather,
she had come to believe that being careful was the only sensible response, because someone could
appear familiar and still be dangerous.

Her daily life became organized around scanning. In the restaurant she was superalert to who was
entering the kitchen, where men were standing, and whether anyone was moving unexpectedly near her.
A sudden hand reaching past her at the pass, a colleague turning sharply behind her, or an
unfamiliar man approaching the front of the restaurant would make her react as if she were in danger
before she had time to sort out what was actually happening. She was embarrassed by how quickly her
body braced itself, but she could not stop it. Even in ordinary settings, if a stranger approached
her in a parking lot, at a store, or near her workplace, she felt threatened in a way that was
immediate and hard to shake. Marianne said she was not “looking for trouble,” but she was always on
guard for it. That watchfulness had become part of how she crossed a room, checked a doorway, or
assessed the people around her.

What troubled her most in the therapy room was how the event lived in her body. When a memory
surfaced, it did not arrive as a clear scene that she could tell from start to finish. Instead, she
would get disconnected bits—a smell, a position, a flash of the room—and sometimes her body seemed
to drop back into the same position it had been in during the assault, as though she were folded
into the memory rather than remembering it from a distance. She spoke with frustration about not
being able to put the frightening event into words, and she seemed disturbed by how incomplete and
physical the recollections felt. This was especially painful because it confirmed what she had come
to believe about other people: that someone could be trusted right up until the moment they were
not. She often repeated that you can never know who will harm you, and that people cannot be
trusted, even those who seem familiar, kind, or ordinary.

By the time she sought help, Marianne was still working, still married, and still managing her
responsibilities, but with a narrowed sense of safety and a growing exhaustion from living on alert.
Her husband had noticed how guarded she had become, and Marianne herself was tired of the distance
that had opened between the life she used to have and the one she now inhabited. What finally
brought her in was the fear that she would never again feel comfortable around other people,
especially men, or in the close, busy environments that used to define her. She wanted help because
she was frightened by how much of her day was spent bracing for harm, and she was hoping,
cautiously, to find a way to move through the world without feeling that danger could appear from
any stranger, any conversation, or any moment of unexpected closeness.
