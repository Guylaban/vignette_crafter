Nandita Rao was 66 and had spent most of her adult life in South Asian family households where
everyone’s roles were clearly understood, and where she had learned to keep moving even when upset.
She worked as a security guard at a large apartment complex, a job that suited her preference for
routines, observation, and quiet responsibility. She and her husband had raised their family in the
United States, and when she first came for help she was still going to work, though she described
feeling as if she were “always braced for something.” The event that changed things had occurred
months earlier, when a close loved one was killed in a car accident. Nandita had received the news
as a shock, and from the first moments afterward the details had fixed themselves in her mind with
an unwanted force. She kept seeing the car struck in the crash, the crushed shape of it, and the
unbearable certainty that life had been altered in an instant. At the funeral, and later at the
cemetery, she found herself overwhelmed by the reality of the loss, as though the grief around her
made the death even harder to bear.

In the weeks that followed, she noticed that ordinary conversation could suddenly become unbearable
if someone mentioned an accident or a crash. A brief discussion at work about a roadway collision
outside the complex was enough to make her go quiet and look down at the desk, waiting for the image
to rise again. Similar images came with a vividness that unsettled her; when she thought of cars hit
by accidents, she did not experience them as distant news but as scenes that seemed to flash into
the room in front of her. The memory of the accident did not stay in the past for her. When it
returned, she felt as if she were back in the moment of learning what had happened, with the same
pressure in her chest and the same stunned disbelief. She often had to stop whatever she was doing
because the feeling of being overwhelmed became too strong. What troubled her most was how real the
memory seemed compared with ordinary memories of daily life. She said the crash scene had a sharper,
more forceful quality than anything else she could call to mind, as if it were still happening
somewhere just beyond her.

Nandita came to believe that she had failed in some essential way. She said she should have
protected others and failed, and she kept searching backward through the day of the accident,
convinced that the event happened because of the way she acted. She would not fully explain every
detail, but her self-blame was clear and persistent, and it left her feeling undeserving of comfort.
Alongside that, she feared that she would never be able to feel normal emotions again. This was not
a dramatic claim to her but a bleak conclusion drawn from her own experience of being unable to
settle into sadness, relief, or even moments of ordinary warmth without feeling cut off from
herself. She described this in a flat, distressed way, as though her emotional life had been damaged
beyond repair.

Over time, she began avoiding anything that reminded her of the person she had lost. She steered
away from grief-related events, finding excuses not to attend gatherings where funerals, cemeteries,
or remembrance rituals might be discussed. At work, she became careful to avoid disagreement or
confrontation, even in situations where she would previously have spoken up. If a tenant or coworker
seemed likely to challenge her, she would withdraw rather than risk conflict, telling herself that
she did not have the strength for another upsetting experience. She also tried hard not to think
about the trauma, pushing the memory away whenever it surfaced. This effort sometimes left her
exhausted, but she felt it was the only way to keep functioning. Still, the cost showed itself in
her daily life: she became more distant with people around her, less willing to stay for
conversations after shifts, and less able to tolerate the emotional demands of family occasions.

Her body seemed to live in a state of unfinished alarm. Nandita described being constantly tense and
ready to react to danger, even when she was sitting at the security desk in a quiet lobby. She found
herself scanning her surroundings for signs of danger, checking movements in the parking area,
watching doorways, and reacting strongly whenever something reminded her of the accident. A sudden
sound, an unexpected image of a damaged car, or a mention of a funeral could bring on a powerful
physical response that startled her with its intensity. By the time she sought help, she was no
longer only mourning; she was frightened by how much of her day had become organized around bracing
against reminders. She came in hoping for relief from the constant alertness and the intrusive,
vivid returns of the crash, but what she feared most was that the memory would keep arriving with
the force of the present, and that she would remain trapped in the feeling that she had failed the
