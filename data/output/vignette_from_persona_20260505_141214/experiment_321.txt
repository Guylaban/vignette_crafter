and that she had none. In the aftermath, sirens cut through her like a physical blow; even now, the
wail of an ambulance or police vehicle could make her chest tighten before she had time to think.
The smell of smoke or fire had become just as potent. If she caught even a faint burning odor
drifting in from a kitchen, a neighbor’s fireplace, or someone passing with a lit cigarette, she
would freeze for a moment and feel herself back in that frightened state, as though the air itself
were warning her that danger was near.

In the months that followed, Asha began changing the way she moved through the world. She stopped
going to or even near the place where the event had happened, first telling herself it was
temporary, then realizing that the route, the neighborhood, and even the possibility of seeing the
site from a distance brought on a wave of dread she did not want to endure. The decision had
practical consequences. She had once been the sort of person who moved confidently between her home,
her dental practice, and the parts of the city she knew well, but now she arranged her days so that
she would not need to pass that area. She would take longer routes, cancel errands, or ask others to
go for her if there was any chance she might come close to it. What she found most unsettling was
the sense that she was not as free as she had been; a place in the city had acquired the power to
dictate her movements.

Asha also became someone who watched constantly. In the waiting room of her practice, in the car, in
grocery stores, and walking through parking lots, she scanned for signs that something was wrong.
Her eyes went automatically to doorways, windows, and anyone moving quickly or standing too still.

effort of keeping watch seemed to take up a great deal of her attention. She made a point of
positioning herself near exits whenever she could. In restaurants she chose seats where she could
see the door; in her office she was careful about where she placed herself and where she asked

discomfort sharpened. Over time, this altered her routine in quiet but tangible ways: she found
herself declining invitations, arriving early so she could choose her place, and leaving situations
sooner than she otherwise would have.

Even at work, where she had long been competent and composed, the old steadiness no longer came as
easily. As a dentist, she spent her days in rooms where patients placed themselves in her care, and
that arrangement sometimes felt uncomfortably close to the kind of imbalance that now frightened
her. Being in a situation where someone else had power over her could stir a silent alarm in her
mind, especially when she was unable to see what was happening behind her or when she felt
physically constrained. She did not say that she no longer enjoyed her work, but she had become more
careful about the settings in which she was willing to be. She preferred to know who was nearby, to

every outing. What finally brought her in was the accumulation of these small restrictions and the

for relief from the sudden surge of fear that still arrived with sirens or smoke, and she wanted to
feel able to move through her city, her practice, and her daily routines without always searching
for the nearest exit or bracing herself for someone else to take control.
