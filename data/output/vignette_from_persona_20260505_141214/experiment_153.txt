Maya, a 22-year-old South Asian woman who had moved into a small apartment on her own not long after
finishing school, was working as a delivery driver when the robbery happened. It was late enough
that the streets were thinning out, and she had pulled over after a drop-off when two men came up
quickly, one of them blocking her path while the other reached for her bag and phone. She remembered
the shock of being told, in a voice that left no room to argue, to hand things over and keep still.
What stayed with her afterward was not a single continuous story but a handful of hard-edged
moments: the man’s hand coming toward her, the feeling of standing there unable to stop it, the
sudden sight of their faces too close, and then the sound of their voices, sharp and threatening,
repeating in her head later even when the scene itself would not come back clearly. In the days
after, she found that she could sometimes feel the same wave of terror return all at once, full-
force and body-deep, without any clear picture attached to it, as if her mind were throwing her back
into the moment while withholding the rest.

At first Maya tried to treat the event as something she should be able to put behind her. She told
herself that it had not really happened, or that it had not been as bad as it felt, especially when
friends asked why she seemed so shaken. That way of talking to herself let her get through some
conversations, but it did not stop the sense that the robbery was still present in her mind. When
she did think about it, the memory arrived in fragments, like snapshots: the empty stretch of curb,
the men moving in too fast, her own hands frozen at her sides, and the harsh sound of their words.
Sometimes the sounds came back before anything else, and she would hear them in her head with the
same frightening force as if they were still spoken beside her. She kept returning to the moment
when she realized she had not done more to stop what was happening, and she became stuck on the
thought that she would never forgive herself for that. In her mind, that passivity was tied to a
deeper belief that she could not stop bad things from happening to her, no matter how careful she
tried to be.

As the weeks passed, Maya started shaping her life around that sense of danger. Driving, which had
once been the center of her work, became something she had to brace herself for, and being a
passenger felt almost as difficult as being behind the wheel. She began taking drugs at times to
blunt the edge of what she was feeling, especially before shifts or after she had to go out at
night. Even then, she rarely felt settled. Before she could stay anywhere with any ease, she needed
to identify exits and think through how she would get out if something went wrong. In restaurants,
stores, and parking lots, she quietly mapped escape routes before she could relax enough to focus on
anything else. At home, even in her own apartment, she could not fully let her guard down. She would
wake during the night to check whether she was safe, listening for movement in the hallway or on the
street below, and often she found that she could not relax even when she knew there was nothing
immediate to fear. Her body stayed tense, as though it had not been told the danger was over.

Certain moments in daily life still set off a sharp reaction. If someone near her seemed
suspiciously watchful or moved in a way she could not read, she became instantly alert, her
attention narrowing onto them until she could no longer think about anything else. Being forced to
do something, even in ordinary situations, could bring back the same trapped feeling she had
experienced during the mugging. Police presence was especially hard for her; seeing officers near
her route or around her building made her uneasy in a way that was difficult to explain, because it
carried the same sense of authority and powerlessness she had felt when she was being ordered around
that night. At work, these reactions had begun to matter. She had started choosing routes and
deliveries that seemed safer, and there were times she hesitated to take a job if it meant driving
in unfamiliar areas after dark. The result was that her world had become smaller and more
controlled, and she felt set apart from other people because they did not seem to live with the same
constant calculation.

By the time she sought help, Maya was tired of managing everything alone. The robbery still came
back in pieces, her sleep was broken by checking and vigilance, and she was worried by how much of
her day was spent scanning for danger or trying to push the event away. What finally brought her in
was the sense that she could no longer keep pretending it had not happened. She wanted relief from
the feeling that she was always one step away from something bad, and she was most afraid that she
would remain isolated, helpless, and unable to trust that she could be safe anywhere.
