trying to carry on as if nothing had changed. The exposure occurred during a routine workday abroad,

wrong. Even now, when the memory returns, she can taste that same metallic-chemical flavor so
vividly that she recoils and swallows hard, as if trying to clear her mouth. When she first tried to
describe the event to colleagues and later to family, she lost the thread partway through, stopping
mid-sentence and apologizing for not being able to finish. She found herself minimizing it, telling
herself that it had not really happened the way she remembered or that it had not been that bad,
especially when others around her seemed to move on quickly. At the same time, she did everything
she could not to think about it, deliberately pushing away any thought of the incident whenever it
surfaced.

In the weeks and months that followed, Meera’s life narrowed around what might be stressful. As a
diplomat, she had once prided herself on being composed under pressure, moving easily between
meetings, briefings, and public events. After the exposure, she began avoiding anything that felt
even vaguely demanding, saying no to additional responsibilities and steering clear of situations
that might leave her feeling trapped or overextended. This was not a dramatic withdrawal at first;
it was a steady trimming back of her world. She became more guarded in daily routines, especially in
the kinds of professional settings where she could not easily control what was around her. In the
office, she would notice herself scanning the room before she settled in, checking the people near
the doors, the air in the corridor, the objects on the table, as if danger might announce itself in
some small detail. She described being unable to fully relax even in ordinary moments, with her body
feeling constantly tense and ready to react. The tension left her exhausted, yet also strangely
unable to stand down, as though she had to stay prepared for something to go wrong again.

Meera’s irritability also became more visible to those around her. Small disruptions at work that
she would once have handled with patience now provoked sharp replies or angry outbursts she later
regretted. She could hear herself sounding harsher than she intended, but in the moment she felt raw
and cornered. She was aware that this made her less easy to be around, which added to her reluctance
to let people ask questions about what had happened. Socially, she became more selective and more
isolated, not because she wanted to be alone, but because any gathering could carry the possibility
of stress, and stress felt dangerous in a way it had not before.

What brought the event back most powerfully were sudden reminders in the environment. A faint smell

something slightly metallic could bring the entire memory back without warning, not as a distant
recollection but as a full and immediate return of the scene. In those moments Meera would go quiet,
visibly startled, and appear momentarily elsewhere, as if the present had been replaced by the
original event. She often could not explain to others why she had gone still or tense, and she had
trouble putting the experience into words even when she wanted to. Her difficulty describing it made
her feel embarrassed and more determined to avoid discussion altogether.

By the time she sought help, Meera had grown tired of living around the edges of fear while
insisting to herself that she was fine. She was still working, but with increasing effort and

speaking, and not being caught off guard. She wanted relief from the constant readiness in her body
and from the abrupt intrusions that could seize her without warning. More than anything, she hoped
to feel able to move through her work and daily life without bracing herself for the next reminder
