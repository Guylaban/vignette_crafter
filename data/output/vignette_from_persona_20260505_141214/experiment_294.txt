Mireille, a 63-year-old African American woman who had come to the United States years earlier as a
refugee from violence in her home country, described the original event in a way that remained vivid
and unfinished even after all this time. She had been forced out of her home by armed men and made
to move when she was told to move, to keep quiet when she was told to keep quiet, and to follow
orders without knowing where she was being taken or whether she would see her family again. The
details that stayed with her were not abstract images but sensory facts: the sting of heat, the
rough ground underfoot, the helplessness of being directed through an open, natural landscape that
looked ordinary to other people but was forever linked in her mind with not being allowed to choose.
In the present, even a walk in a park near her apartment could turn tense if the trees and uneven
paths reminded her too closely of that setting; she would feel her body tighten and her attention
narrow, as though she were once again waiting for someone to tell her where to go. What unsettled
her most was not only the memory itself but the feeling of being made powerless by another person’s
command.

As a young woman, Mireille learned to keep moving, and after resettlement she built a careful life
around work and self-reliance. She studied accounting, eventually becoming a financial analyst, and
took pride in handling numbers with precision because numbers did not shout, corner, or demand
obedience. But over the years she found that certain moments at work pulled her sharply out of the
present. If a supervisor approached her desk with a firm tone, if a manager stood over her while
giving instructions, or if anyone in a uniform came into her line of sight in the building lobby,
she felt her chest tighten and her thoughts go immediately to danger. Police officers were
especially potent; seeing them near her train station on the way to work, or outside her office
building, could make her step aside, lower her eyes, and wait until they were gone. She did not
describe these reactions as irrational. To her, authority carried the possibility of being
controlled, and she had learned from experience that control by others could become terrifying very
quickly.

Over time, Mireille began arranging her life to avoid anything that felt even remotely like
confrontation. At work she would agree quickly in meetings, even when she privately disagreed,
because the prospect of an argument left her feeling exposed and uneasy. If a colleague spoke
sharply or if a form needed to be corrected in front of someone else, she would find reasons to
postpone the conversation or send an email rather than speak face to face. She had come to rely on
quiet compliance, not because she believed it was ideal, but because disagreement felt like standing
at the edge of an open door she did not want to cross. These patterns extended beyond the office. In
her small circle of relatives and friends, she tried to keep peace at any cost, changing the subject
when conversations became tense and withdrawing from situations where she thought someone might
challenge her. She was single and lived alone, but her concern for people she loved was constant and
consuming; she worried obsessively that something terrible might happen to them, replaying ordinary
delays as possible emergencies. If a niece did not answer her phone, Mireille might sit with her
device in hand for hours, imagining accidents, arrests, or violence, unable to settle until she had
made repeated attempts to reach her.

By the time she sought help, these fears had narrowed her world in quiet but significant ways. Her
route to work changed depending on whether she expected to pass a police car or a cluster of
uniformed security guards. She avoided social events that might involve disagreement, and she had
stopped joining friends for walks in natural settings, even though they once appealed to her,
because the open air and unfamiliar terrain could suddenly bring the old fear back. What finally
brought her to treatment was the cumulative strain of living as though every forceful voice, every
authoritative figure, and every quiet lapse in contact might signal impending danger. She reported
feeling exhausted by the constant vigilance and frightened by how much of her life had become
organized around staying out of anyone’s way. What she hoped for was not to erase the past, but to
be able to move through her work, her relationships, and the world without feeling that any command,
confrontation, or uniform could take her back to the moment when she had no say over what happened
next.
