as a refugee years earlier, and she came to treatment only after a long stretch of trying to manage
on her own. She was married, served as a pastor, and was known in her congregation as steady and
dignified, someone who continued to show up for others even when she herself felt increasingly worn
down by what had happened to her. The event she described from her refugee flight remained vivid in
the way she told it: there had been a bus, crowded and uncertain, with people pressed together and
no easy way to tell who was safe. At one point, weapons were visible among the men around her,
objects that could be used as weapons hanging in plain sight and making the air feel dangerous in an
instant. She remembered the sudden shifts of bodies, the abrupt movements of others, and the sense
that everything could change without warning. Even now, when she tried to explain it, she sometimes
paused as if the story itself were hard to hold in her hands; she said it felt so extreme that it
was difficult to believe it had actually happened to her.

In the years that followed, Mariam found that the memory did not stay neatly in the past. It would
force its way into her mind when she least wanted it, arriving with no invitation and no
preparation. At times, when it came back, she also heard pieces of what had been heard during the
frightening event, as if the sounds had been preserved intact inside her and could return with the
force of the original moment. She did not speak about the event often, partly because she did not
want to revisit it and partly because she feared that if she started thinking about it, she would
not be able to handle what came next. She carried a deep sense that what had happened to her had
been profoundly unfair, and that nothing had been done about it. That unfairness sat beside another
painful belief: that after such treatment, she felt less like a person and more like an object,
moved through events without being seen as fully human.

Her daily life remained organized around trying not to be caught off guard. On public transport,
especially on bus rides, she became tense as soon as she stepped aboard, watching the aisle and the
seats with careful attention. Sudden movements by other passengers put her immediately on guard, and
she found herself tracking people closely for signs that they might harm her. Even walking in
familiar places, she often looked behind her to check whether someone might be following. In the
practical life of an older pastor, this vigilance had a cost. She could still fulfill
responsibilities, but she did so with a guardedness that was visible to those who knew her well, as
though part of her remained oriented toward the possibility of danger rather than the task in front
of her. She also became deliberate about how she dressed, choosing concealing clothing so that she
would not be noticed. She described this as a way of staying safer, of not drawing attention, of
moving through the world with as little visibility as possible.

Mariam also kept herself from talking with others about what had happened or about what came after.
In her role as clergy, she was accustomed to listening to other people’s suffering, yet when it came
to her own experience she held back, giving partial answers and changing the subject. She did not
seek medical or psychological help for a long time, and she spoke of that choice with the same
carefulness: she had not wanted to open something she believed could overwhelm her. Her congregation
continued to see a composed pastor, but privately she was burdened by moments in which she began to
feel detached or unreal, as though she were slipping away from herself. Those episodes frightened
her because they seemed to confirm how fragile her sense of being present had become. They could
occur suddenly, leaving her unsure of her footing in a room or in a conversation.

She eventually sought help when these experiences became harder to ignore and more difficult to fold
into the disciplined routines that had carried her through so many other hardships. What brought her
in was not a single dramatic crisis, but the accumulation of being on guard, being pulled back into
the memory, and feeling increasingly diminished by it. She said she was tired of living as though
danger were always one sudden movement away, tired of feeling that she had to remain hidden, and
tired of carrying a story that felt at once impossible and inescapable. What she most wanted was to
be able to move through bus rides, daily errands, and her work as a pastor without constant
scanning, and to feel like herself again instead of someone who had been reduced to an object by
what she survived.
