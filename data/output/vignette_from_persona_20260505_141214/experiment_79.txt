Sokha was forty-seven, a married park ranger who had spent most of his adult life working outdoors
in a state park where the work was steady and physical and, most days, quietly routine. That
stability had been shattered when his young child became suddenly and life-threateningly ill. He had
brought the child to the hospital after a day that began with ordinary family life and ended with
panic in a fluorescent-lit emergency department, where the smell of antiseptic hung in the air and
staff moved briskly between rooms while he tried to understand what was happening. He remembered the
child looking small against the bed rails, the monitor sounds, and the strange unreality of being
told that things were serious enough to require urgent treatment. What stayed with him most was the
helplessness of that time, the sense that his attention had to stay fixed on every change in the
child’s condition because something terrible could happen if he missed even a small sign.

Afterward, Sokha found himself reacting sharply to the most ordinary bodily sensations. If he felt
dizzy or lightheaded, even briefly, his mind would go straight back to the hospital, and he would
become tense and watchful, certain that the feeling meant something was wrong again. He noticed that
the smell of hospitals or antiseptic could bring the whole period back in a rush, as if he were once
more standing in that corridor trying to catch the eye of a nurse. The smell of alcohol had become
just as powerful for him, not because it was part of his everyday life before, but because it now
carried the same sharp, clinical association; if he passed someone using hand sanitizer or
encountered the smell in a store, he would feel a jolt of dread and a tightening in his chest, and
he would want to get away. These reactions were especially hard for him because his work as a park
ranger sometimes required him to accompany visitors, give first aid, or deal with minor injuries,
and he had come to dread situations where a person might mention a clinic, a hospital, or even a
bottle of disinfectant in a backpack. In those moments he often became preoccupied with getting out
of earshot or changing the subject quickly, as if avoiding the reminder could keep the memory from
taking hold of him again.

Over time Sokha also began spending long stretches replaying the event in his mind, turning it over
with a harsh, restless logic. He would dwell on what other people had done to him during that
period, especially the sense that others had not understood how serious it was until he was already
overwhelmed. At other times the rumination became more vengeful in tone; he would imagine how he
might get even with the people he felt had wronged or failed him, though the target of that anger
shifted depending on what he was thinking about at the moment. These thoughts did not leave him
feeling settled. Instead, they kept him emotionally tied to the episode, returning him again and
again to the unfairness of what had happened and to the humiliating feeling of having had so little
control. He did not talk much about these thoughts, partly because he did not want to be judged and
partly because being questioned about the event by others only intensified his distress. When
friends or coworkers asked how his child was doing or tried to bring up the hospital period, he
often shut the conversation down, changed the subject, or withdrew. He found such reminders hard to
tolerate and preferred not to be reminded by anyone else, even people who meant well.

As the months passed, these reactions narrowed his world in quiet ways. At work he remained
functional, but he had become more guarded in situations that might bring the memory close, and he
was aware that he now organized parts of his day around not encountering certain smells or
conversations. At home, he seemed preoccupied even when sitting with his wife, and she had noticed
how quickly his attention could be pulled away by a dizzy spell or a whiff of alcohol-based cleaner.
Sokha had finally agreed to seek help because he was tired of living as though he were still braced
in that hospital corridor, waiting for another crisis. What he wanted most was to be able to be near
his family and continue working without feeling ambushed by the smell of antiseptic, a moment of
lightheadedness, or someone else’s well-meant question. What frightened him most was the sense that
these reminders could still seize him as powerfully as they had at the hospital, and that he might
never feel fully settled again.
