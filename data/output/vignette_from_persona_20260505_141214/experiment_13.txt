Daniel, a 32-year-old European American man who worked as a tutor, came to treatment after months of
living as though ordinary life might again turn into something unmanageable. Before the stalking
began, he had described himself as steady and private, someone who spent most of his days helping
students prepare for exams and moving between tutoring appointments and home with his wife. The
stalking started with small intrusions that at first seemed easy to dismiss: repeated appearances by
the same man near places Daniel frequented, messages that implied he was being watched, and a sense
that his routines were no longer his own. Over time the pattern became impossible to ignore. Daniel
found himself scanning parking lots before getting out of the car, checking windows and doorways,
and wondering whether the figure he had glimpsed earlier in the day was still nearby. What had once
felt like coincidence came to feel deliberate, and he began to live with the constant impression
that he was being followed or tracked.

The period after the stalking ended in the practical sense did not bring relief in the way Daniel
had expected. Instead, he noticed that his attention kept catching on signs of potential threat,
even in places where nothing was actually happening. A motorcycle passing close by could jolt him
hard enough that he would stop speaking mid-sentence and look up sharply, as if bracing for the next
approach. He reacted the same way when someone’s voice rose, even if the anger was not directed at
him and even if the person sounded calm rather than explosive; the tone alone could make him tense
and watchful, as though anger in another person might signal danger. He came to trust unfamiliar
places less and less, especially if he could not immediately assess exits, sightlines, or who might
be there. This meant he started declining new tutoring assignments in settings he did not know well,
choosing instead to work only in locations that felt predictable, which slowly narrowed his
professional flexibility.

At home, Daniel’s wife noticed that he rarely seemed settled. He had trouble falling asleep and,
when he did sleep, he would wake repeatedly, listening for sounds outside the bedroom or replaying
the day’s movements in his mind. He began to avoid relaxing altogether because stillness felt unsafe
to him; sitting with a book, lying on the sofa, or letting himself drift into a quiet evening
brought on a feeling that he was exposing himself to danger. Even in moments that should have been
restful, he stayed keyed up, as if his body had not received the message that the immediate threat
was over. He often described these reactions with embarrassment, saying that he knew nothing was
actually wrong in those moments but that his body behaved as if he were still in the middle of being
hunted.

Daniel’s thoughts about the experience grew harsher as the months went on. He told himself that he
deserved to suffer because of what had happened, and there was a bleak, self-punishing tone to the
way he talked about coping. He doubted that he would do the right thing if confronted with a
difficult situation again, and that uncertainty made him increasingly cautious in ways that spilled
into everyday life. He became less willing to trust people’s intentions, saying that people were not
what they seemed and that people could not be trusted. This mistrust extended beyond strangers to
social situations more generally, and he found himself watching people for inconsistencies, scanning
for signs that they might be hiding something. Emotional closeness also became harder to tolerate.
With his wife, he remained loving in practical ways, but he pulled back from intimacy and avoided
the kind of open emotional contact that used to feel natural. He seemed to worry that letting
himself be close would make him vulnerable again, though he did not frame it as fear so much as a
grim decision to keep a safer distance.

By the time he sought help, Daniel’s days had become smaller and more managed around these concerns.
He would take familiar routes, avoid unfamiliar streets, and plan his tutoring schedule around
places he knew well. He had begun to notice the cost of this pattern in his work and in the strain
it placed on his marriage, yet he also felt he could not simply relax or trust his own judgment
enough to do otherwise. What finally brought him to treatment was the growing sense that he was
living on constant alert and could no longer tell whether he was protecting himself or disappearing
into fear. He arrived hoping for some way to feel safe again without having to keep shrinking his
life, though what frightened him most was the possibility that he would never fully trust other
people, or himself, enough to let his guard down.
