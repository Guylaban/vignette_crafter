Khan, a 60-year-old South Eastern Asian man who had worked for many years as a hotel manager, came
to treatment after months of feeling unable to settle back into ordinary life with his partner. The
event he described had happened at work, in the setting that had once felt most orderly and
predictable to him. A chemical leak in the hotel’s maintenance area exposed him to a toxic substance
before he understood what was happening. He remembered the moment in fragments that still arrived
without warning: the sharp, bitter taste that seemed to flood his mouth as soon as he was near it,
the sudden sense that something was dangerously wrong, and the shock of not being able to do
anything useful except get himself away from the area. Even now, when the memory forced its way into
his mind, he said it did not come as a story from the past but as something that seemed to happen
again in the present, with the taste returning so vividly that he would briefly feel transported
back into that scene. What frightened him most was the feeling that his body had failed him in a way
he could not anticipate or control. He had come to believe that he could not stop bad things from
happening to him, no matter how careful he tried to be.

In the weeks after the exposure, Khan began to move through his days as if danger might be waiting
in ordinary places. At work, he found himself constantly scanning hallways, service corridors, and
storage areas for signs that something had gone wrong. If a delivery was late, or a repair took
longer than expected, he immediately assumed that something bad was happening somewhere out of
sight. When guests or staff mentioned the incident, or when anyone referred to chemicals, cleaning
supplies, or ventilation, he felt a rush of discomfort and pulled away from the conversation. He
started avoiding reminders whenever he could, stepping out of meetings if the topic turned to
maintenance or safety procedures, and changing the subject when others brought up the exposure as
though speaking of it casually. These moments left him feeling exposed and ashamed, as though his
body and his work no longer belonged to him in the way they once had.

At the same time, he became preoccupied with his own physical state. Khan said he constantly checked
his body for signs of danger or injury, reading every throat sensation, chest tightness, or stomach
feeling as though it might be the first warning of another toxic event. He watched himself closely
for changes and often found that his body felt tense and ready to react before anything had actually
happened. In the hotel, this meant that he could be standing at the front desk or walking through
the lobby while part of his attention was still fixed on whether he felt “normal” inside. When a
guest was delayed, or a maintenance report did not arrive on time, he would feel his body brace as
if something threatening were about to unfold. He described these reactions as false alarms that
arrived with little warning, leaving him startled by his own body’s urgency. Over time, the strain
of staying on guard made everyday interactions more exhausting, and his partner noticed that he was
less present at home, often distracted and difficult to reassure.

Khan’s relationship with his own body had also changed in a more painful way. He said he could no
longer trust it, because it seemed capable of turning against him without notice. That belief was
strongest on days when he felt a twinge of nausea, a tightening in his throat, or a wave of heat in
his chest; instead of passing over these sensations, he treated them as signs that something was
wrong. The result was a constant readiness, as though he needed to be prepared for an emergency at
any moment. He found himself checking the environment before entering rooms, watching people’s faces
for signs of alarm, and keeping track of exits and staff movements in a way that made the hotel,
once familiar and competently managed, feel unpredictable. At home, he was no easier to settle. He
said he no longer felt that he belonged anywhere in the way he once had, not fully at work and not
comfortably at ease even in the relationship that had sustained him. The feeling of being out of
place carried a quiet sadness, and it made him less willing to explain what was happening to him.

By the time he sought help, Khan was exhausted by the combination of intrusive memory, constant
alertness, and the conviction that his body could not be relied upon. What brought him in finally
was the fear that he could not keep functioning at work if every delay, every mention of the
incident, and every unfamiliar taste or bodily sensation continued to pull him back into that
exposure. He hoped treatment might help him feel safe in his own body again, but he was most afraid
that the sense of danger would never fully leave him and that he would remain someone who no longer
knew where he belonged.
