Daniel was 53, a married social worker, and by the time he came for help he was describing a life
that had become carefully narrowed around a single night he had never managed to leave behind. The
assault had happened years earlier, when he was alone in a place that should have felt ordinary and
contained. What remained most vivid for him was not a coherent story he could tell easily, but a set
of fragments he could still feel in his body: the crushing terror of being trapped, the humiliating
sense of being overpowered, and the immediate shame that followed when he tried to make sense of
what had happened. Even now, when he tried to speak about it, Daniel would pause and search for
language, saying he knew exactly what had happened but could not find the words that felt adequate
to describe it. In sessions, his voice would tighten and he would fall back on short, factual
statements, as though anything more detailed might expose him to the same mortification he felt
then.

In the months after the assault, Daniel found himself trying to force the memory away whenever it
rose up. He would push it out of his mind, only to have it return later in a flood of terror that
felt indistinguishable from the original moment. At times, recalling it brought back the same raw
panic he had felt during the assault, as if the event were not over but still unfolding somewhere
just beyond the present. He also noticed that his mind kept returning to thoughts of revenge. He
would ruminate about how he might get even with the assailant, not because he expected to act on it,
but because the thoughts gave him a fleeting sense that he had not been made entirely helpless. He
was ashamed of these thoughts too, and the shame seemed to accumulate alongside the memory itself.

Over time, Daniel began organizing his days around the sense that danger could appear without
warning. He frequently looked behind him when he walked, scanning for anyone who might be following
him. If he arrived somewhere and the route or layout was unfamiliar, he would avoid going in at all,
telling himself he could not assess the danger quickly enough. At work, this meant turning down
assignments that would have taken him into new neighborhoods or unfamiliar buildings. As a social
worker, his job required him to move between offices, homes, and community sites, and he found
himself planning routes obsessively or asking for details long before leaving the office. What had
once been routine visits became tense calculations about entrances, exits, and who might be nearby.
If a meeting ran longer than expected or a client was delayed, his mind jumped immediately to the
thought that something bad was happening. He would stare at his phone, growing increasingly
unsettled by each passing minute, certain that delay meant threat until proven otherwise.

His avoidance extended into enclosed spaces as well. Daniel came to dread elevators, small
conference rooms, and any place that felt confined or hard to leave. In such spaces his body would
tense, and he would count the seconds until he could get out. He began choosing stairs when he could
and standing near doors in meetings, even when this made him look distracted or disengaged. At home,
his wife noticed him circling his routines so that he would not have to enter places that felt boxed
in, and she also noticed how little he wanted to talk about the assault. When she asked, he would
shut down or change the subject, not because he did not trust her, but because the shame came so
quickly that he felt almost physically unable to stay with the conversation.

By the time he sought treatment, Daniel’s world had become smaller and more exhausting. He was still
working, but with increasing effort, and he felt embarrassed by how often he checked over his
shoulder or hesitated at a doorway like someone much younger and less in control. What brought him
in was the accumulation of these moments: the strain on his marriage, the narrowing of his work, and
his growing fear that he would keep living as though the assault were happening all over again. He
said he wanted to be able to name what happened without feeling overwhelmed, to sit in a room
without automatically looking for a way out, and to stop spending so much of his day braced for
something bad. More than anything, he was afraid that if he did not get help, he would remain
trapped in the same shame and terror that had followed him for years.
