up around the sea and now worked as a fisherman in the same coastal community where she had always
lived. Before the event, she had been known as steady and hardworking, someone who could get up

violent incident in her village one evening when she was still younger, an event she describes only
in fragments because the details do not come to her as a complete picture. What stays with her is
the force of it: the sound of rushed movement, footsteps coming quickly behind and nearby, and other
related sounds that now feel loaded with danger in a way they did not before. She remembers the
sudden surge in her body before she could think, the jolt of being thrown into alertness, and then
the horrible sense that something terrible was unfolding in front of her and that she had not been
able to stop it. When she recalls it now, she often does not see a clear image at all; instead she
is overtaken by the same emotional intensity, as if the fear and helplessness arrive full strength
without a visual memory to contain them. At other times, the memory forces its way in when she is
not wanting it, interrupting ordinary moments with a rush of guilt. She has come to believe that she
should have protected others and failed, and this belief still lands on her with particular force
when she thinks back on what happened.

Since then, Malia has found herself scanning for danger in ways that feel immediate and bodily. She
says her body reacts before her mind has time to catch up, and that she often knows she is
frightened only after her muscles have already tightened and her chest has already gone tense. She
becomes especially unsettled when something takes longer than expected, because the delay itself
makes her assume that something bad is happening. On the fishing boat, if a crew member is late
returning with gear or if a routine task takes longer than usual, she finds herself watching the
horizon and looking around with growing unease, certain that the delay means trouble. The same kind
of reaction happens on land. A bicycle passing close by can make her freeze or jerk away, not
because the bicycle itself is dangerous, but because it carries the same sense of sudden approach
that now feels threatening. Footsteps behind or near her are particularly difficult; if she hears
them on the road, in a yard, or near the dock, she becomes tense and checks who is there. Related
sounds can have a similar effect, enough that she has begun to notice how quickly her body prepares
for danger before she has decided what she is reacting to.

In the months after the event, Malia tried to keep herself busy. She began working excessively,
taking on more hours whenever she could so she would not be alone with her thoughts. The long shifts
on the water and around the boats gave her structure and kept her from sitting still with the
memories, but they also left her exhausted. Outside of work, she increasingly spent long hours on
her phone, games, or the internet, scrolling until late because being absorbed in the screen felt
easier than being left alone with what came up in silence. This has affected her relationship as
well. She avoids sex, and she does not describe this as lack of interest so much as a wish to keep
distance from experiences that make her feel too exposed and too aware of her own body. She has been
embarrassed by how difficult it has become to feel at ease even with someone she cares about, and
she worries that her partner does not fully understand why she pulls away.

What troubles Malia most is the conviction that she is somehow unsafe in her own body. She says it
feels as though her body keeps reacting as if she is still in danger even when she knows,
rationally, that she is not. That disconnect leaves her frustrated and ashamed, especially when the
reaction is immediate and seems to happen without permission. She also fears what her own anger
might become. In quieter moments she worries, with a seriousness that frightens her, that she will
not be able to control her anger and will do something terrible. She does not describe acting on
this fear, but the fear itself has become a prominent part of how she monitors herself, as if she
must constantly watch for any sign that she might lose control.

By the time she sought treatment, Malia had reached a point where the effort of managing her days
had become too heavy to keep carrying alone. The triggers were showing up too often in ordinary
life, her concentration was slipping at work, and the constant push to stay busy was no longer
enough to keep the memories out. She came hoping for some relief from the sudden floods of guilt and
fear, and with a guarded wish that she might one day move through her work, her relationship, and
the spaces of her daily life without feeling braced for danger at every delayed step or nearby
footfall.
