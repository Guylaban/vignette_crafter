the United States after emigrating from the Middle East years earlier. Before the earthquake, he
described himself as easygoing and generally cheerful, the sort of person who could settle into
work, family routines, and weekend plans without much strain. The disaster had come suddenly while
he was at home with his wife, and in the first violent seconds he could not make sense of what was
happening. The floor jolted hard enough that furniture lurched and objects crashed, and the house
filled with sharp, overlapping sounds that he later found difficult to place in order. He remembered
the sequence in fragments: the first jolt, his wife calling out, something heavy striking the wall,
then the sound of sirens outside and the frantic movement of people trying to get out. Even now,
when the memory surfaced, it did not return as a tidy story but as pieces that felt out of order, as
if he were trying to assemble a broken recording.

In the weeks that followed, Omar found himself pulled back into the event by sensory flashes that
felt startlingly immediate. He would hear, in his mind, the same noises that had been there during
the quake—sirens, alarms, the scrape and crash of objects—so vividly that for a moment he was not
sure whether they were present or remembered. At times the recollection came on all at once, and the
room around him seemed to disappear; he described those moments as if the event were happening again
across all of his senses, with the same pressure in his chest, the same sense of urgency, and the
same helplessness. He was unsettled by how incomplete the memory was in some respects and how
overwhelmingly real it could feel in others, especially when the sounds returned without warning.

Afterward, Omar began moving through daily life as if danger might appear at any moment. He had come
to watch for movement in his periphery and reacted sharply if someone approached him unexpectedly at
home or at work. In the office, if a colleague stepped beside his desk without first being noticed,
he would jolt and turn with visible alarm, embarrassed by how quickly his body responded. He also
became preoccupied with where he might get out if something happened. In conference rooms,
elevators, restaurants, and even at his own desk, he silently mapped exits before he felt
comfortable enough to settle. This habit extended into family outings as well, where he would scan
for doors and clear paths before sitting down, seeming unable to relax until he knew how quickly he
could leave. Unexpected touch had become especially difficult; if his wife reached for his arm from
behind or a coworker tapped his shoulder, he would react with a sharp startle that he could not
always hide.

Omar’s efforts to manage what had happened took the form of several private routines. He tried to
force himself into positive thinking, telling himself repeatedly that he should focus on what was
still good and not dwell on the quake. He made a point of pushing away anything that seemed to
remind him of physical vulnerability, including hospitals, ambulances, and medical examinations.
Even seeing an ambulance outside his building could tighten his chest and make him want to turn
away. He avoided routine medical visits when he could, and when he did need care he was tense and
watchful, wanting the interaction to end as quickly as possible.

Over time, his thoughts also took a darker turn. Omar would sit with the memory and ruminate about
revenge in the sense of wanting someone, somehow, to answer for what had happened, even though the
disaster itself had no assailant. The thoughts were less about action than about rage at the
unfairness of being trapped in an event that had left him so altered. Alongside that anger came a
harsher view of himself. He was certain that somebody else would not have gotten into this
situation, that another man would have handled it better, moved faster, or remained calmer. He
compared his former self to the person he felt he was now and concluded that he used to be happy,
but now was always miserable. He told his wife that nothing good could happen to him anymore, a
statement she had heard him repeat with increasing frequency and resignation.

By the time he sought treatment, Omar was exhausted by living in a state of constant readiness and
by the sense that his life had narrowed around what he no longer felt able to do. The change was
visible at home and at work: he was still functioning as a software engineer, but he was doing so
with persistent vigilance, careful placement near exits, and a tendency to flinch when interrupted
by sudden movement or touch. He came to therapy saying that he wanted relief from the moment the
memory seized him and from the certainty that his future held only more of the same. What frightened
him most was that the quake had not ended when the ground stopped shaking; it still returned without
permission, and he feared that if nothing changed, he would remain trapped in that same miserable
state indefinitely.
