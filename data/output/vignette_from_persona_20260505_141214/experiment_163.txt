Amina, a 47-year-old African American architect, first came to be seen after months of living with a
sense that her life had split into a before and after. The event she traced it back to was not a
natural disaster or a crime, but a brief, ordinary-looking moment that went disastrously wrong while
she was driving home from work. She had been behind the wheel in her usual route through her
neighborhood, thinking about revisions to a project and the errands she still needed to do, when she
turned at an intersection and struck a pedestrian who stepped into the road. The collision was
sudden and horrifying. In the aftermath, while people shouted and rushed toward the scene, Amina
found herself holding on to fragments more than a full sequence: the sharp taste in her mouth,
metallic and bitter, the sound of voices rising around her, and the sickening realization that she
had caused serious injury to another person. Even when she later tried to remember the incident
deliberately, the memory would not come to her in a smooth, organized way. Instead, it surfaced in
broken pieces, and when it did, the taste would return so vividly that she felt as if it were
happening again.

In the weeks that followed, Amina noticed that she could not summon the memory when she wanted to,
yet it forced its way in at unexpected times. A passing siren, a sudden honk, or even a cupboard
door slamming in her office kitchen could bring back an instant of the scene, and with it she would
sometimes hear the sounds from that day in her head, as though the shouts were playing just behind
her ears. She became jumpy at ordinary noises that never used to bother her. At work, where she
spent long hours at her desk reviewing plans, meeting with clients, and moving between construction
sites and the office, she began to react as if danger could appear at any moment. A dropped folder,
a phone vibrating on the table, or the clang of equipment from a nearby site would make her flinch
hard enough that colleagues noticed. She also found her attention slipping. During meetings she
would stare at blueprints and realize she had read the same line several times without taking it in.
She was embarrassed by how often she lost her place, as if part of her mind was still standing at
the intersection.

Amina made an effort to contain the experience in the ways that made sense to her. She told herself
that it could have been worse, that the other person had survived, and she repeated this privately
when the guilt became too intense. But she also dwelled on what she had done wrong, replaying the
seconds before the turn and searching for the moment she might have acted differently. At night she
would go through small mental routines, silently checking and rechecking things in an effort to keep
anything bad from happening again. She did this especially before driving, sitting for a moment with
her hands on the wheel and making herself think through routes and signals in a careful sequence, as
if the ritual itself might prevent another catastrophe. These habits gave her some brief relief, but
they also left her more preoccupied with the event and with what it might mean about her.

What troubled Amina most was the way she had come to see herself in relation to other people. She
believed someone else would not have gotten into the situation she had. The thought sat heavily on
her, not as a passing regret but as a judgment about her own character and competence. She began to
feel isolated and set apart from friends and even from her husband, who tried to be supportive but
could not fully understand why she seemed so distant. She stopped sharing the details of her
distress, partly because she did not want to burden others and partly because being around people
sometimes made her feel even more like an outsider, as though everyone else moved through the world
with an ease she had lost. At home she became quieter, and the version of herself that had once felt
organized, capable, and assured as an architect seemed increasingly unrecognizable to her. She would
look at herself in the mirror and feel that the person looking back was not the woman she had been
before the accident.

By the time she sought help, Amina was tired of living in this altered state and frightened by how
often she still felt on guard for danger in ordinary moments. She had come because she wanted the
constant jumping, the concentration problems, the intrusive fragments of sound and taste, and the
private rituals around driving to stop ruling her days. More than anything, she was afraid that the
event had permanently changed who she was, and she was hoping for some way to feel like herself
again without having to erase what had happened.
