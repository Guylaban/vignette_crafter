hurricane season that upended his sense of safety as the kind of event he had thought he would
weather like any other storm. When the warning came, he and his wife prepared as best they could,
but the disaster arrived with a force that made preparation feel useless. He recalled the house
filling with the sound of wind, the strain in the walls, and the moment the electricity failed,
leaving the rooms dark and unfamiliar. Water began to rise, and what he remembered most clearly was
how quickly the familiar shape of his neighborhood disappeared into noise, debris, and panic. In the
aftermath, he found himself watching everything around him with a kind of fixed intensity, as though
any ordinary scene might suddenly turn dangerous again. He had come to believe that the world was a
dangerous place, not in a vague philosophical way but in the practical sense that calm could change
without warning.

In the months that followed, Malik noticed that the memory did not stay in the past where he
expected it to belong. When he tried to talk about the hurricane, it came back to him like a film
that would play over and over, with the same images returning in the same harsh sequence. At times,
he would start describing the floodwater and then stop, because it felt as if he were right back in
the moment, hearing the wind, feeling the same pressure in his chest, and looking around as if the
event were happening again. He said the memory felt present rather than over, and that this was what
frightened him most. Even when he was sitting safely at home with his wife, or standing behind the
counter at the store where he worked, the scene could return with such force that he felt briefly
pulled out of the room. He did not describe this as a choice; it was simply how the memory arrived.

As time passed, Malik began to move through daily life with a constant watchfulness that was visible
in small, repeated actions. As soon as he entered any room or building, he scanned for the exits. In
the retail store where he worked, he automatically noted where the nearest door was before focusing
on customers or stock. If he was in the back room or in a crowded aisle, he made sure he knew how to
get out quickly. He stayed near the exit whenever he could, choosing a place that would let him
leave without delay. He often found himself checking the surroundings for signs of threat, not
because he believed something specific would happen each time, but because he felt he had to be
ready. He was superalert and watchful in a way that left little room for ease. By evening, this left
him mentally exhausted, though he still struggled to fall asleep because he felt he needed to stay
alert. Lying in bed, he would listen for sounds in the house and remain tense, as if sleep meant
lowering his guard too much.

Malik also described how he tried to manage the moments when the memories or alarm became too
strong. At work, if a sound, smell, or sudden movement reminded him of the hurricane, he would
distract himself with something else, turning his attention to shelves, prices, or the next task in
front of him. He used this in hopes of getting through the day without losing his composure. But the
effort to stay composed came at a cost. His wife noticed that he was less comfortable going places
that felt crowded or enclosed, and he admitted that he often preferred situations where he could
keep an eye on the exits. Over time, this narrowed how he moved through both his job and his
community. He no longer felt able to relax in the ordinary way he once had, and he had come to see
this as evidence that he had permanently changed for the worse.

By the time he sought help, Malik was no longer waiting for the fear to pass on its own. What
finally brought him in was the growing recognition that he was spending much of his day on guard,
scanning, watching, and bracing himself, and that even sleep no longer felt restful. He wanted to
understand why the hurricane still seemed to live so vividly in his mind and why ordinary places
kept feeling unsafe. At the same time, he was most afraid that if he let his attention slip,
something terrible would happen before he could react. He came to treatment hoping for some way to
feel less trapped by the memory and less certain that danger was always just a step away.
