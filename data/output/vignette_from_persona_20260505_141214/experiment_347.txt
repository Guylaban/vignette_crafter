he had taken many times after long days helping coordinate community outreach. In the rain, another
car had come out of nowhere and struck his vehicle with enough force to spin it and crush the side
where he was sitting. In the immediate chaos, he had noticed the hard, slick texture of the seatbelt
biting into his chest and the door panel scraping against him as he tried to orient himself. Later,
that same texture became oddly potent for him; even brushing against a similar seatbelt material in
the car or feeling a rough, synthetic surface under his hand could bring back the instant of being
trapped in the wreck. What stayed with Ravi most was not a narrative with clear words but a set of
stark images: the crumpled cars, headlights at strange angles, metal folded in on itself, and the
sight of another vehicle hit in just the wrong place. He would see a car damaged at an intersection
or in a parking lot and feel his stomach drop before he had time to tell himself he was safe.

In the weeks after the collision, Ravi found that unexpected loud sounds put him on edge in a way
that surprised him. A slamming door in the office, a bang from the street, or a cart being dropped
in the hallway could make him tense and scan the room as if something bad were about to happen. He
also noticed that sudden movement near him was difficult to tolerate. If someone approached quickly
from the side or a colleague moved abruptly at a meeting, Ravi would react as though he were in
danger, his body jerking before he had a chance to think. These moments were especially unsettling
because they came without warning, leaving him embarrassed and then more watchful. At work, where he
was often in shared spaces with volunteers and staff moving in and out, he began choosing seats with
a clear view of the door and felt more comfortable when people stayed at a distance. He did not
describe himself as fearful in the abstract; rather, he was sure that his body had learned to
anticipate impact, noise, and sudden force before his mind could intervene.

When he remembered the collision, Ravi did not experience it as a settled event in the past. He said
that recalling it brought back the same terror he felt at the time, and at moments it seemed to him
as if the accident were happening right then. These episodes were accompanied by vivid pictures
rather than a complete story: the rain on the windshield, the other car striking, the twisted metal,
and the sensation of being unable to get free quickly enough. Sometimes, while sitting in traffic or
hearing coworkers discuss a roadway incident, he would be flooded with the sense that he was back in
that moment, as if the present had dropped away. He would become quiet and fixed, his attention
narrowing to the imagined scene. He did not talk about these experiences readily with his wife or

him with a vigilance that left him tired by the end of the day. His wife had begun to remark that he
seemed less at ease on routine drives and more reluctant to discuss anything connected to the
accident. In his nonprofit role, where community events sometimes involved travel and coordination

kept him closer to the office and away from situations that might expose him to distressing
reminders.

By the time he sought help, Ravi was 61, still married, and increasingly troubled by the way
ordinary life kept bringing the collision back to him. The trigger of hearing talk about accidents
had become hard to avoid in a workplace that often dealt with community concerns and public

image or texture brought it back. What he hoped for was not to forget the event altogether, but to
be able to remember it without being thrown back into it, and to move through his work, his
marriage, and his daily routines without always bracing for the next shock.
