Malia, a 31-year-old married professor of Oceania background, sought treatment after nearly a year
of living with the aftermath of a life-threatening motor vehicle collision that had left her feeling
unlike herself in ways she could not ignore. Before the crash, she had understood her life as busy
but steady: teaching, preparing lectures, and sharing an ordinary domestic routine with her husband.
The collision happened on a day she had been driving home from work, when another car struck hers
with enough force to make the scene feel instantly chaotic and wrong. She remembered the violent
impact, the sudden loss of control, and the terrifying sense that her body and surroundings were no
longer responding as they should. In the weeks that followed, she found that her recollection of the
event did not settle into a single coherent story. When she tried to explain it to other people,
including her husband and later her doctor, she would lose the thread partway through and stop,
frustrated by the way the account slipped away from her. Some parts of the crash stood out with
painful clarity—the instant of impact, the sound, the sense of being hit—while other sections were
blank, leaving her with disconnected pieces that never quite fit together.

As time went on, certain moments began to bring the event back with unusual force. If coworkers
talked casually about accidents, traffic, or someone else’s close call, Malia would feel an abrupt
internal shift, as if the world had gone slightly distant and unreal. She described those moments as
beginning with a detached feeling that made her uneasy before she could even place why. Seeing cars
that had been hit in accidents had a similar effect. On her commute, a damaged vehicle at the
roadside could pull her attention so completely that she felt herself pulled back into the crash
scene, as though the sight were not simply something in the present but a doorway into what had
happened to her. These experiences were not just upsetting recollections; they carried the force of
being suddenly back there, and she found herself acting as if danger were once again immediate.

At night, Malia’s sleep was broken by a different kind of vigilance. She woke often, sometimes
without remembering what had stirred her, and checked whether she was safe. She would listen closely
in the dark, alert to anything that might suggest the car, the road, or the possibility that
something had gone wrong again. Even during the day she felt superalert and watchful, scanning her
surroundings in a way that made ordinary environments exhausting. In lecture halls and hallways at
the university, she noticed herself tracking sounds and movement rather than settling into the role
that once felt natural to her. The vigilance was especially difficult during her workday, because
the campus setting did not allow her to withdraw entirely; she had to stand at the front of
classrooms and remain present with students while part of her attention stayed fixed on whether
anything around her might signal threat.

Over time, Malia came to describe herself in increasingly bleak terms. She said that she used to be
a happy person but now felt miserable most of the time, a statement she made with an almost
disappointed disbelief, as though she were describing someone she no longer recognized. She also
spoke of a painful sense that she no longer belonged anywhere. This was not limited to one setting:
at home, she felt separate from her husband in a way she could not easily explain, and at work she
felt as though she occupied her role by habit rather than by any genuine sense of place. She also
said that nothing in her life felt meaningful anymore, including the career to which she had once
devoted so much energy. The work itself continued, but it no longer carried the same purpose for
her; days felt emptied out, as if the future that had once seemed open and organized now had no
clear shape.

By the time she first sought help, the accumulation of these experiences had become impossible to
dismiss. Malia was not asking for a quick return to how things had been, because she no longer
trusted that such a return was possible. What brought her in was the growing fear that the detached
moments, the night waking, and the sense of being on guard would keep narrowing her life until
nothing felt familiar or worth holding onto. What she wanted most was not simply relief from the
crash reminders, but some way to feel like herself again—to be able to describe what happened
without losing the story, to move through a day without constantly checking for danger, and to
believe that her life still had meaning.
