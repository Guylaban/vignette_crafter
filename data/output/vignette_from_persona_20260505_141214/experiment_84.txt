At 68, Darnell had built much of his life around movement. An African American dancer who had spent
decades performing, teaching, and keeping his body conditioned, he had always trusted rhythm,
timing, and the feel of a room. That changed after a life-threatening motor vehicle collision that
occurred on a routine drive home. He was traveling in his own lane when another car struck him hard
enough to send the vehicle spinning and stop traffic around him. In the aftermath, he remembered
flashes rather than a continuous sequence: the jolt of impact, the sound of metal, a glimpse of
faces, the confusion of being moved and spoken to, and then long stretches that remained blank. Even
soon afterward, when others asked him to explain what had happened, he found he could not tell a
clear story. The memory seemed to come in broken pieces, and when he tried to deliberately summon
it, the details would not stay in view. What was left felt like an incomplete picture, with missing
sections he could not fill in no matter how hard he tried.

In the weeks and months that followed, Darnell began to organize his days around avoiding reminders
and circumstances that left him feeling exposed. He went out less and less, telling himself that
staying home was simpler and safer than facing the unpredictability of the outside world. Social
contacts narrowed noticeably, especially with people he had once socialized with before the
accident. Invitations from fellow dancers, old colleagues, and friends who had known him in a more
outgoing period often sat unanswered. He did not want to be put in the position of talking about the
crash, and he also did not want to be seen as fragile by people who remembered him as capable and
physically assured. Even in therapy, he found himself steering away from anything that had been
discussed too directly, avoiding thinking about it afterward and sometimes coming away irritated
with himself for having opened the subject at all.

His sense of safety became tied to where he sat. In waiting rooms, restaurants, and rehearsal
spaces, Darnell sought out chairs against a wall so he could see the room. If he was seated where
people were behind him, he felt unsettled and watched the entrance, the exits, and anyone moving
quickly through the space. Unexpected or loud sounds kept him on guard. A sudden bang from a dropped
pan in the kitchen, a car horn outside his apartment, or the sharp clatter of equipment in a studio
would make him tense immediately, as if something dangerous were about to happen. Delays also became
hard to tolerate. If a ride took longer than it should, if someone was late, or if an expected call
did not come through, he assumed something bad was happening before there was any evidence of it. He
found himself scanning for the worst in ordinary pauses, unable to settle until the uncertainty was
resolved.

Darnell also came to doubt himself in ways that were deeply distressing to him. He had once relied
on quick judgment in performance and in daily life, but after the collision he began to fear that he
could not trust himself to do the right thing. That worry showed up in small decisions, from
navigating a crosswalk to planning an outing, and it left him second-guessing choices he would
previously have made automatically. More unsettling still was the sense that he could no longer
trust his own body. As a dancer, his body had been both instrument and identity, but now he saw it
as unreliable, unpredictable, and no longer fully under his control. Even ordinary bodily sensations
could make him feel vulnerable, as though the vehicle of his life had become less dependable than he
had ever imagined. Beneath that sat a deeper disappointment in the people and systems that should
have kept him safe. He had come to believe that the institutions involved failed him at the moment
he needed protection most, and that sense of being let down lingered whenever he thought about what
should have happened differently.

By the time he sought help, Darnell was still living with the same broken and vivid fragments of the
crash, the same difficulty making sense of it, and the same sharp unease in public places. The
changes had begun to interfere with the life he once led with ease: he was out less, seen less, and
less connected to the dance community that had long been central to him. He came to treatment not
because he expected the memories to disappear, but because he was tired of feeling on edge in his
own body and in ordinary rooms, and tired of shrinking his world to avoid what might unsettle him.
What he feared most was that he would never again trust himself, his body, or the world enough to
move through life the way he used to. What he hoped for was more modest but deeply important to him:
to make sense of what happened, to stop feeling so unsafe, and to return to some version of himself
that could sit in a room, go outside, and be with other people without constantly bracing for the
next thing to go wrong.
