much as a sequence of images: people ducking, bodies pressed low, a flash of movement, dust,
shattered sound, and the look on frightened faces. When he tries to describe it, he often says it
feels unreal, as if it must have happened to someone else, because it was too extreme to fit inside
an ordinary life and too violent to seem believable even to him. Yet the pictures remain vivid. He
sees the scene in fragments, and when he thinks back to it he sometimes hears the sounds again in
his head, as if they are replaying just behind his ears—the sharp, jarring noises of the attack and
the panic around him.

have done differently. He blamed himself again and again for not preventing it, for not noticing

sudden and no one could have controlled it, he kept circling back to his own part in it. The self-

accounting that never closed.
