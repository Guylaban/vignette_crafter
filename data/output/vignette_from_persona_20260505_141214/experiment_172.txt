adult life had become too heavy to keep arranging around himself. He was a North American Jewish

meetings, study, and the ordinary routines of a life built in safety far from the place where it had
begun. But the safety of his present life had never erased what had happened when he was a refugee.
He had fled as a younger man from a country where armed men had appeared suddenly, where shouting
meant danger before anyone had even understood the words, and where weapons were not abstract
symbols but the visible edge of control. He remembered the chaos of being moved along by force, the
hard certainty that if he did not stay alert he could be hurt, and the humiliation of realizing that
his own judgment had not protected him.

In the years after that escape, David learned to move through the world as if danger might still be
waiting just outside the frame. At first he told himself he was simply careful, but over time he
noticed how often he scanned for exits and settled into seats that let him see the room clearly. In
restaurants, in synagogues, at family gatherings, and even in meeting rooms at work, he chose the
place that felt most protected, usually with a wall behind him or a clear line to the door. If he
arrived somewhere and the only available seat felt exposed, he could remain tense through the entire
meal or meeting, aware that his body was braced before his mind had caught up. He found that he felt
safe only in a very limited number of places, and he organized his life accordingly. When he had to
concentrate on a sermon, a text, or a pastoral conversation, he could be pulled off track by the
smallest hint of raised voices from another room, and he would lose his place or have to re-read the
same lines several times before they made sense.

Certain moments still arrived in his body before he could prepare for them. Unexpected touch could
jolt him sharply, especially if someone came up behind him in the synagogue hallway and put a hand
on his shoulder without warning. He would flinch, his whole frame tightening before he could turn
and reassure himself it was only a friend or congregant. Shouting had the same effect. A burst of
loud voices on the street, or an argument breaking out nearby, could make him go still and
hyperalert, his attention narrowing to where the sound was coming from and what it meant. Weapons,
or even ordinary objects used as weapons in film, in news footage, or in a person’s hand in an
agitated moment, drew his attention with a fast, grim recognition. He did not need to see much to
feel his stomach tighten and to know that his mind had already moved back toward danger.

David had also become accustomed to speaking to himself harshly about the past. There were moments
when he told himself that he deserved to suffer because of what had happened, as if endurance itself
were a form of moral accounting. At other times, more quietly but just as painfully, he was certain
that somebody else would have stopped the event from happening. He returned to that thought often,
imagining that a stronger, wiser, or quicker man would have done something different and prevented
the worst of it. When he saw his own hands shaking or felt his pulse race in a harmless setting, he
interpreted it as proof that his body kept reacting as if he were still in danger even when he was
safe. That experience was deeply frustrating to him because, by temperament and profession, he
believed in steadiness and self-command. Instead, he found himself thinking that he could not rely
on himself, not when his body seemed to betray him so readily.

As the years passed, David developed habits that helped him get through the day but narrowed his
world. He distracted himself whenever painful memories pressed close, shifting into work, reading,
or practical tasks rather than staying with what he felt. He also kept emotional distance where he
could. Even in his relationship, where there was warmth and loyalty, he often held back from being
fully intimate, not because he lacked feeling but because closeness made him vulnerable in ways he
found hard to bear. He worried that if he allowed himself to be fully seen, the old fear and shame
would show too plainly. His partner noticed that he could be present and loving, yet sometimes
abruptly inward, as though he had stepped behind an invisible curtain.

By the time he sought help, David was no longer pretending these reactions were minor
inconveniences. He had begun to worry that the circle of places and situations that felt safe had
become too small, and that the effort of arranging his chair, monitoring voices, and suppressing the

home. What frightened him most was the prospect that he would continue living by avoidance and self-
reproach, and what he most hoped for was the chance to sit, listen, and touch the present without
being pulled so quickly back into the danger of the past.
