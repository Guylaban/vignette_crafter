Marianne had grown up in a small Midwestern town in a house where a caregiver’s anger could arrive
without warning and with it came blows she was too young to understand and too frightened to name.
As a child she learned to listen for the smallest shifts in a room, for the tone of footsteps, for
the sounds that meant someone was near and about to turn on her. She carried that vigilance into
adulthood in ways that later seemed, to her, less like habits than conditions for getting through
the day. She went on to build a life that looked steady from the outside: she worked for decades as
a forester, took pride in knowing the woods, and eventually divorced after a marriage that had never
fully reached her. Yet even in the open spaces of her work she found herself arranging her life
around danger as she understood it.

In her seventies, Marianne still rises early and goes out to the land she knows best, but she has
become careful about who is with her. Being alone with women has become especially unsettling. She
cannot explain it in tidy terms, only that a woman’s presence can make her body go rigid and her
mind begin searching for what might come next. At work, this has meant finding reasons to leave a
break room if a female colleague sits down beside her, or steering conversations toward logistics so
she does not have to stay seated across from one woman in a quiet office. Even family gatherings
have changed shape for her; if there is only one woman left in the kitchen with her, she will often
step outside or offer to fetch something from the truck. She tells herself she is being practical,
but the deeper truth is that she is trying to avoid the feeling that another person, especially a
woman, might suddenly turn into the kind of threat she knew as a child.

Sounds have also remained charged for her in a way that is hard for others to grasp. Certain related
sounds can catch her off guard and bring back the atmosphere of childhood so vividly that she feels
briefly removed from the present. The scrape of a chair in an otherwise quiet room, a thud from
another part of the house, a sharp sound that resembles movement in a hallway, can make her chest
tighten and her attention narrow. What unsettles her even more is silence that comes too abruptly or
becomes complete. In a building at work, when the machinery shuts down and the forest office falls
suddenly still, she notices it immediately and feels exposed, as though something is about to happen
in the gap. In those moments she listens harder, scanning the quiet as if it were a warning sign
rather than an absence. She has become known for keeping radios or a television on low in her
apartment, not because she wants company, but because she cannot tolerate the dead hush of a room
that seems to be holding its breath.

Over the years Marianne also began avoiding exercise, though she does not talk about it as a phobia
or a symptom. She simply says she does not see the point, and she has found more ways than one to
stay still. Walking for the sake of walking, stretching, anything that makes her body more
noticeable to herself, leaves her uneasy. She would rather remain occupied with tasks at the desk or
with small chores that keep her hands busy and her attention outward. In the same period she
developed another pattern that she describes with embarrassment: she spends money impulsively to
distract or comfort herself. After a difficult morning she may buy things she does not need,
stopping at stores on the way home from the forest or ordering items online late at night. The
purchases give her a brief sense of relief, a few minutes in which she can think about something
concrete instead of the familiar surge of fear and shame, though afterward she is left with bills
and regret.

Marianne’s life has also become organized around not being alone in a room or building. If she is in
her apartment and the neighbors are gone, she feels the walls closing in on her. If she has to lock
up the forestry office or be the last person left at a meeting, she hurries, checking the exits and
trying not to dwell on the emptiness around her. She will sometimes wait in her car rather than sit
by herself inside, even when the weather is poor. Over time these choices have narrowed her world
and made her more isolated, though she speaks of them as if they were simple common sense. At the
center of it all is a belief she has come to hold with grim certainty: that her life was destroyed
by what happened to her, that you can never really know who will harm you, and that in the end she
feels more like an object than a person, something acted upon rather than someone with a life of her
own.

She sought help after a stretch in which the reminders seemed to be crowding closer together and she
could no longer dismiss them as just part of getting older. She was tired of arranging her days
around empty rooms, unwanted silences, and the presence of women she could not trust herself to be
near. What brought her in was not a single crisis but the accumulation of years spent bracing for
harm, spending to soothe herself, and shrinking her life to avoid situations that left her feeling
trapped. At intake, Marianne said she did not know whether therapy could give her back anything of
what was taken, but she hoped, almost against expectation, that it might help her feel less like an
object moving through other people’s spaces and more like a person who still belongs to herself.
