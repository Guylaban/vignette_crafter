Robert, a 68-year-old North American man, had spent most of his adult life in schools, helping
students sort through crises and find steadier footing. He was married and, until recently, had
remained active in his work as a school counselor, a role that required him to be present, calm, and
available to other people’s distress. Yet when he began therapy, the story he told returned first
not to the school building but to the night he was assaulted years earlier, an event that still sat
in his mind as a series of sharp, disconnected images. He described it not as a continuous film but
as a few frozen moments: the approach of a man he did not know, the sudden violence, the brief
awareness of being overpowered, and then the next picture, each scene held in his mind like separate
photographs. When he tried to remember the assault in full, he said the terror came back with the
same force it had then, not as a distant recollection but as a bodily certainty that he was in
immediate danger again.

In the months that followed, Robert found himself repeatedly caught off guard by pieces of the
memory that seemed to arrive without warning and disappear just as quickly. One moment he might be
sorting papers at his desk; the next, a fragment of the assault would flash through his mind, vivid
and complete in itself, leaving him momentarily disoriented. He could not predict these intrusions,
and he learned to brace for them with a tension that never fully left him. Certain sensations made
them more likely to feel near and immediate. The smell of a hospital corridor was especially
unsettling. If he passed a clinic entrance after work or accompanied his wife to an appointment, the
antiseptic odor could make his stomach drop and bring him abruptly back to the helplessness of the
attack, as if his body recognized danger before his mind had time to orient itself. Alcohol smell
had a similar effect. A whiff of liquor on someone’s breath at a social gathering, or the stale
smell of alcohol near a bar in a restaurant, could make him go rigid, because the scent was tied in
his mind to the perpetrator and to the sense that he had been trapped with someone unpredictable.

Over time Robert began to structure his life around not being caught by those moments. He
deliberately tried to leave his body or blank out when something felt too close, describing a
familiar effort to step away from himself until the feeling passed. At times he would stare through
a conversation rather than into it, focusing on a distant object or the pattern on the carpet until
he felt more distant from what was happening around him. He also started avoiding other people in
ways that had practical consequences. His work as a school counselor had once depended on easy,
sustained contact with students, parents, and staff, but he began limiting the time he spent with
others and arranging his day to reduce unnecessary interaction. He found reasons to keep his office
door partially closed, shortened meetings, and skipped social events connected to the school. What
had once been one of the most relational parts of his life became a place where he moved carefully,
wanting to minimize the chance of being caught alone with another person’s emotional intensity.

This became even more pronounced in his marriage. Robert cared deeply for his wife, but he found
himself pulling back whenever she seemed to want more emotional closeness. The more she tried to
talk about feelings or pressed him to be more intimate, the more uneasy he became, as if being
emotionally close carried a risk he could not name except in terms of exposure and vulnerability. He
was not able to say that he feared her, and he did not want distance from her, but he had come to
believe that letting someone in too far could leave him with the same trapped feeling he had during
the assault. So he kept conversations practical, changed the subject when discussions turned
personal, and created a quiet, managed distance between them that his wife noticed, even if she did
not fully understand it.

By the time he sought help, Robert was exhausted by the effort of keeping himself composed and by
the way the memories continued to break in on him. He worried most about becoming increasingly cut
off from the people who mattered to him, especially his wife, and about whether he would be able to
remain effective in the school setting that had defined so much of his life. He came to treatment
hoping that the terror attached to the memory would not keep seizing him so completely, and that he
might be able to stay present with others without feeling compelled to retreat, blank out, or brace
himself for the next fragment of the past.
