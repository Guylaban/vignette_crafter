and had spent years taking pride in being steady, practical, and useful to others. Long before he
became a business owner, he had gone as a relief worker to a war zone, believing at the time that he
could help families who had already lost too much. He rarely talked about that period, but in the
first sessions he described one day that had never left him: he was working close enough to people
that he could feel bodies brushing past him in the crush of movement, and then the sharp crack of
gunfire cut through the air. The sound had been close and unmistakable, and the people around him
scattered in panic. In that moment, he had felt the physical nearness of danger in a way he had not
been able to forget. Since then, sudden gunshot noise in everyday life had become impossible for him
to dismiss. He found himself jolting at fireworks, at a car backfiring on the street, at a noise
from a television or video game if it sounded too much like a shot. Even in his own neighborhood, he
stayed alert to what might be happening around him, especially when he could not immediately tell
where a loud sound had come from.

Over time, he also came to dread moments that should have felt good. He said that positive emotions
had become oddly unsettling, as if feeling light or relaxed left him exposed. When something went
well at work, or when his wife tried to share a warm moment with him, he could feel a brief opening
of relief, followed by a sense of unease that made him pull back. He could not explain it in simple
terms, except to say that happiness felt confusing now, as though it did not fit the life he
expected to have after what he had seen. He sometimes noticed himself becoming tense in the middle
of celebrations or family gatherings, not because anything bad was happening then, but because the
feeling of ease itself seemed risky to him. He watched himself for signs that he was getting too
comfortable, and that watchfulness made it hard to stay present.

In the months after the war-zone work, Raúl found that his mind kept circling back to the question
of why the event had happened to him. He replayed scenes over and over, not always with a clear
answer in mind, but with the same exhausted attempt to make sense of being there when others had not
been. He was stuck on the meaning of it, wondering why that day, why that place, why he had been
close enough to hear the gunfire and feel people surge around him. He described this as a painful
loop of thoughts that he could not turn off, especially at night or when he was alone with no one to
distract him. The questions did not settle him; they left him tired and frustrated, as though the
event still demanded an explanation that had never come.

As the years passed, he began carrying a weapon more consistently, telling himself it was simply
being prepared. He worked in a business environment where he met customers, moved between his office
and the street, and often had to be physically close to people in cramped spaces, all of which made
him uneasy. He disliked feeling that others could come too near without warning, and he was more
comfortable when he could keep a bit of distance. The weapon gave him a sense of readiness, but it
also became part of a larger habit of staying on guard. At work, when a customer stepped too close
or raised a voice, Raúl became visibly tense and sometimes shifted away before he realized he had
done it. In his daily life he also found himself picking arguments or creating drama, especially at
home or among people he knew well. He said that stirring things up gave him something else to focus
on, a way to keep his mind from drifting back to the war zone. Unfortunately, the disputes left his
relationships strained. His wife had started to notice that he was quicker to challenge people,
quicker to escalate minor disagreements, and harder to calm once he felt provoked.

By the time he sought help, Raúl was carrying years of this pattern and feeling worn down by it. He
came because he was tired of being startled, tired of watching himself for danger, tired of his mind
returning to the same unanswered questions, and tired of the tension his behavior was causing in his
marriage and at work. What finally pushed him toward treatment was the sense that he was no longer
simply remembering the war zone; he was living as if its danger could return at any moment. He hoped
therapy might help him feel less on edge, less driven to arm himself and create conflict, and more
able to stay with the ordinary moments of his life without feeling that even comfort was something
he could not safely trust.
