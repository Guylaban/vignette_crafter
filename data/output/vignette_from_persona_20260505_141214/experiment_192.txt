adult life in Florida and prided herself on being steady, practical, and difficult to rattle. That
sense of herself was shaken one summer when a hurricane pushed water far beyond the usual boundaries
of her neighborhood. She had stayed through the early warnings because she was used to storms, but
as the hours passed the wind rose, the lights failed, and the water began to gather and spread in a
way that made the streets look unfamiliar. By the time she understood how quickly it was moving, she
was forced to leave in the middle of the night, listening to emergency vehicles and the crashing of
loose objects while the water climbed and flooded the area around her home. The scene was chaotic,
loud, and disorienting, and what stayed with Marisol was not only what she saw but the bodily terror
of it: the sickening surge in her stomach as she watched the water take over spaces that had always
felt solid and ordinary.

In the months that followed, Marisol found that anything connected to that night could throw her
back into distress with little warning. She became especially uneasy around water itself, avoiding
large bodies of water and feeling her chest tighten when she had to pass flooded drainage areas
after heavy rain. Even a glance at water gathering in a parking lot could stop her in her tracks. At
work, where she treated patients in a busy rehabilitation clinic, she noticed that the smell and
sensation of nausea were especially hard to tolerate. If a patient mentioned feeling sick, or if her
own stomach turned during a long session, she would abruptly lose her footing internally and have to
fight the urge to shut down. In those moments she sometimes deliberately tried to leave her body or
blank out, staring past whoever was speaking to her and waiting for the feeling to pass. She did not
describe this as dramatic; to her it felt like a necessary way to get through the moment without
becoming overwhelmed.

Marisol also found herself reacting strongly to sounds that startled her. Unexpected bangs in the
clinic—an equipment cart striking a wall, a door slamming, someone calling out sharply in the
hallway—put her on immediate alert. She would pause, listen, and scan the room before she could
return to what she was doing. At home, she slept poorly, often waking in the middle of the night and
struggling to fall back asleep. She said the nights were when her mind felt most exposed. Her
apartment, once a comfortable place, now felt like one of only a very limited number of places where
she could relax at all, and even there she remained watchful. She was more likely to stay inside
than to go out, especially when the weather looked unsettled, and over time this habit narrowed her
life in a way that had become hard to ignore. Friends invited her to dinners near the bay or walks
by the water, and she increasingly declined, offering practical excuses while feeling the strain of
her shrinking routine.

disaster could jolt her into an uncomfortable state of alert, not because the person in front of her

reports—and that comparison left her feeling that something essential had been taken from her.
