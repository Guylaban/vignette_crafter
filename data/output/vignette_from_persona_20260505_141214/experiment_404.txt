María was 69 years old when she first came to treatment, a married North American agricultural
worker who had spent most of her life in physically demanding jobs and in the routines of early
mornings, long shifts, and small circles of trusted people. She described the assault as the event
that split her life into a before and after. It had happened in a place that should have felt
ordinary and familiar, and what stayed with her was not only the violence itself but the suddenness
of being overpowered and the humiliating knowledge that someone had used her body against her will.
In the days that followed, she found herself jolted by a memory of the event coming to mind without
warning, so vivid and complete that it felt less like remembering than being pulled back into it. A
sound, a smell, or a stray thought could make the scene arrive all at once, and she would go still,
trying to get through the moment without letting anyone see how badly shaken she felt.

As the months passed, María began building her days around what seemed safest to her. She avoided
other people whenever she could, especially situations where she might have to sit close to
strangers or be caught in a conversation that drifted toward topics she did not want to face. She
noticed that certain conversation topics, especially anything that reminded her of vulnerability,
men’s behavior, or what had happened to “women who don’t watch themselves,” could leave her feeling
cornered and exposed. She also developed a narrow sense of where she could tolerate being. She said
she only felt safe in a very limited number of places, and that feeling governed much of her
movement. Even then, safety was fragile. On the bus to and from work in the fields, she was tense
from the moment she stepped on, choosing a seat where she could see the aisle and repeatedly looking
behind her to check whether someone was following her. If a man stood too close or if she heard
laughter behind her, she would stiffen and keep watch over her reflection in the window, scanning
for any sign that she should get off early. The bus was unavoidable on some days, and the ride
became one of the clearest places where her fear showed itself.

At work, María tried to keep herself occupied with tasks that required her hands and attention, but
she was aware of how much effort it took to stay composed. She had come to believe that she could
not rely on herself, not because she thought she was weak in a simple sense, but because she no
longer trusted her own ability to protect herself or to predict when fear would overtake her. Before
leaving home, she mentally rehearsed worst case scenarios to feel prepared: where she would stand,
who she would avoid, how she would escape if someone approached, what she would say if a
conversation turned in an unwelcome direction. These rehearsals did not reassure her so much as give
shape to her vigilance. She described them as something she had to do so she would not be caught
unready again. Even with that preparation, she often felt flooded by strong negative feelings such
as fear, horror, anger, guilt, and shame. The shame was especially hard for her to name. She spoke
of feeling set apart from others, as if something visible had changed in her that made her
permanently an outsider now. Around family, co-workers, and neighbors, she could be polite and
functional, but internally she felt separated from the ease and belonging other people seemed to
move through without effort.

To get through the hardest hours, María drank alcohol and sometimes took medication or used drugs,
saying she wanted “to quiet everything down” and make the evenings less sharp. She did not describe
the use as a celebration or a habit she enjoyed; it was more like a tool she reached for when her
mind would not settle and her body stayed on guard. Over time, the avoidance, the drinking, and the
constant checking narrowed her life further. She stopped lingering with others after work, stopped
joining conversations that might turn personal, and arranged her days to keep contact to a minimum.
By the time she sought help, she was tired of living as if danger could appear at any moment and
tired of feeling like a stranger in her own life. What finally brought her in was the sense that the
fear had become too persistent to manage alone. She hoped treatment might help her feel less trapped
in her limited safe places and less alone with the conviction that she could not trust herself,
while fearing most that if she opened the story too fully, the shame and panic would rush back with
the same force as before.
