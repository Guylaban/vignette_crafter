came for help he spoke about his life as if it had become divided into a before and after. The event
itself had happened years earlier, during an act of terrorism that shattered the ordinary rhythm of
a day he had expected to be routine. He did not dwell on every detail, but he described enough for
the scene to remain vivid: the sudden violence, the confusion of people trying to understand what
was happening, and the terrible sense that control had disappeared in an instant. What stayed with
him most was not only the danger itself but the feeling that he could not prevent it, could not
contain it, could not make the world behave as it should. In the aftermath, he found himself
returning mentally to that helpless moment, replaying the basic fact that bad things could break
through without warning and that he had been unable to stop them.

Over the following months, Arjun began to notice that night-time brought a particular kind of dread.
Waking in the dark was unsettling in a way he found hard to explain to others, even as a
psychologist used to talking about fear. A dark room could make him feel abruptly unmoored, as
though the room itself had become unsafe before he had fully surfaced into it. He described lying
still and listening, and when the house was quiet enough to make even small sounds feel amplified,
he would become tense and alert. Gunshot noises were especially potent. If he heard a sharp bang on
the street, a car backfiring, or a noise from outside at night, his body reacted before he could
think: he would flinch, freeze, and immediately scan for what had happened. The sound seemed to pull
him back toward the terror of the original attack, not as an abstract memory but as a bodily
certainty that danger was again near. At those moments he was convinced that the world was
dangerous, not merely in a general sense but in the practical way that meant safety could not be
assumed anywhere.

As the months went on, Arjun’s way of coping became increasingly restrictive. He began avoiding
anything that could be stressful, arranging his life to minimize demands, interruptions, and
situations that might stir up the sense of being overwhelmed. He was once someone who managed a full
clinical workload with confidence, but he started turning down professional commitments that felt
unpredictable and delegating more than he used to. At home, too, he became more guarded about what
he watched, read, or discussed, as if any extra strain might tip him into a state he did not trust.
His wife noticed that he seemed to disappear into himself during difficult conversations. Arjun said
that when things felt too intense, he would drift off into a world of his own, mentally stepping
away from the present until the pressure passed. He did not present this as a deliberate choice so
much as a place he slipped into when he felt cornered by what was happening around him.

He also began drinking alcohol and using medication or drugs in ways he did not like talking about
in detail, but he acknowledged that these substances had become part of how he got through evenings
and the hours after stressful days. He was not proud of it, and he spoke about it with a weary sense
of self-estrangement, as though even his own habits belonged to someone else now. That sense of
distance from himself had grown over time. He said the person he had become was unrecognisable to
him, not because everything about him had changed, but because the confidence and steadiness he once
associated with himself had been replaced by caution, avoidance, and a constant need to keep bad
things from reaching him. He found himself looking at his own reactions with a kind of dismay, as if
he were watching a stranger live his life.

By the time he sought help, the accumulation of these experiences had begun to shape his marriage
and his work in visible ways. The adjustments he made to avoid stress reduced the range of his days,
and his wife had started to see how often he was either bracing against the world or retreating from
it. He came to therapy not after a single dramatic crisis, but because he no longer wanted to keep
living as though danger were waiting everywhere, especially in the dark, in sudden noises, and in
any moment when he felt he lacked control. What brought him in was the fear that this narrowed, wary
version of life might become permanent. What he hoped for, though he said it carefully, was the
possibility of feeling like himself again and being able to move through the world without
constantly preparing for the next bad thing he was certain could not be stopped.
