Somchai, a 69-year-old South Eastern Asian American man, came to treatment after more than a year of
trying to keep himself busy enough that he would not have to think about the spring day when the
child he cared for almost died. Widowed and living alone now, he worked as a nanny for a family in
his neighborhood, a job that had once suited him because it gave his days structure and allowed him
to feel useful in a quiet, steady way. He had spent years caring for children with patience and
without complaint, and the family had trusted him with the boy every weekday while the parents
worked. That trust made the event feel even more shattering to Somchai. The child was suddenly
struck by a severe illness and became life-threateningly unwell before anyone expected it. Somchai
remembered the household changing minute by minute: the child’s condition worsening, the adults
speaking in urgent voices, the wait for emergency care stretching into what felt like an unbearable
silence. Even now, he could not tell the story in a clean sequence, as though the event had been
broken into fragments that refused to fit together. When he tried to describe it, he would start,
stop, and correct himself, saying that he could not tell what came first and what came next, because
the scene remained more like a series of vivid flashes than a finished memory.

What stayed with him was not a distant recollection but something that seemed to arrive in the
present whenever it surfaced. He said the memory felt “right there,” as if the room, the child’s
face, and the panic were happening again rather than being over. He could recall small, bodily
details with startling sharpness—the sound of urgent movement, the look of alarm in the adults’
faces, the helplessness of standing nearby while the child’s condition became critical. These
recollections were not experienced as ordinary memories of a difficult past; he described them as
more real than events from his daily life, as though the mind held onto that day with an intensity
that made everything else seem faint by comparison. Since then, he had tried hard not to let himself
think about it. When the image or the feeling came close, he pushed it away and filled his mind
immediately with anything available: chores, errands, television, counting tasks, checking and
rechecking what needed to be done next. He kept himself occupied all the time because stillness made
room for the scene to return. He also avoided medical care and any kind of psychological help for a
long while, partly because hospitals and doctors now felt tied to that crisis, and partly because he
did not trust himself to sit through a conversation about it without becoming overwhelmed by the
memory. Even scheduling an appointment had taken him months, and he delayed again and again before
finally agreeing to come.

Over time, Somchai began to feel that the man who existed before that day was gone. He spoke of
himself with sadness and a kind of disbelief, saying that the person he had become was
unrecognisable to him. He had once seen himself as calm, dependable, and open enough to be trusted
by others, but now he felt altered in a way that could not be reversed. What troubled him further
was how much trust itself had changed in his eyes. After seeing how quickly a normal day could turn
into a medical emergency, he became wary of what people and situations might conceal. He said people
were not what they seemed, and that feeling extended beyond the family he worked for into everyday
life, where he found himself cautious and unsettled around others who appeared calm on the surface.
The change was not just emotional but practical. At work, he continued caring for children, but he
often remained mentally elsewhere, filling any quiet moment so he would not have to face the memory
that could rise without warning. There were days when the smallest sign of illness in a child would
bring the event rushing back so vividly that he had to steady himself before he could continue.

By the time he sought help, Somchai was exhausted by the effort of keeping everything pushed down.
He came because the burden of remaining occupied, of avoiding medical settings, and of trying not to
think about the trauma had begun to feel like a life organized entirely around not remembering. He
did not say he expected to return to the person he had been; instead, he said he feared this was
permanent damage and that he would spend the rest of his life feeling changed for the worse. At the
same time, he hoped that talking might help him make some sense of the event that still arrived as
if it were happening now, and that he might find a way to carry it without having to outrun it every
hour of the day.
