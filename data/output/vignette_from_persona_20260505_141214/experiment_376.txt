described herself as living in a state of near-constant watchfulness that had been present ever
since the assault by a former romantic partner years earlier. She had moved through the days then as
if trying to keep her balance on uncertain ground, and even now she found herself scanning every
place she entered for signs of danger. Lan had been physically abused by a man she had trusted, and
the violence had taken place in the private setting of a relationship that should have felt safe.
What stayed with her most was not a single clear story she could tell from beginning to end, but a
series of disconnected images: a hand, a wall, a doorframe, the look on his face. When she tried to
remember the event, she said it was like looking at photographs laid out separately in her mind,
each one sharp and frozen but refusing to fit into a coherent sequence. The terror came back with
those images in the same raw form as before, so that even thinking about it could make her chest
tighten and her stomach drop.

In the months after the abuse, Lan noticed that she was no longer moving through ordinary spaces in
the same way. She began checking mirrors repeatedly, not out of vanity but because she needed to
know what was behind her and who might be approaching. In shops, parking lots, and hallways, her
eyes went immediately to exits, corners, and anything that could be used as a weapon. She would
notice bottles, pens, tools, pieces of metal, or heavy objects in any environment and register them
almost automatically as potential threats. Walking into a classroom for tutoring sessions, she found
herself scanning the room before a student even sat down, taking in chairs, sharp objects, and the
position of doors and windows. She described being superalert and watchful without choosing to be,
as though her mind and body had become permanently oriented toward danger. At times her body reacted
before she had fully registered why she felt tense: her shoulders tightened, her breathing changed,
and she would feel a sudden urge to move away or turn around before her thoughts had caught up.

This way of moving through the world also affected how she saw other people. Lan became increasingly
vigilant for dangerous people, and she found it hard to settle in crowded places or around strangers
whose intentions she could not immediately read. A man standing too close in a grocery line, an
unfamiliar person near her car, or someone speaking loudly in a parking lot could all set off the
same internal alarm. She had come to believe that you could never really know who might harm you,
and that conviction made every new encounter feel uncertain. She did not describe this as a passing
fear but as something that had changed how she understood the world and her place in it. She also
felt permanently like an outsider now, as though something had happened that separated her from
other people in a way she could not undo. Even in familiar company, there was a sense that she was
no longer fully part of things, that others moved through life with a kind of ease she could not
recover.

Lan’s work as a tutor had gradually become more difficult because it placed her in close contact
with new people in private or semi-private settings. She continued to teach because it mattered to
her and because she needed the income, but each new student required a silent assessment of the room
and the person in it. If a lesson ran late and the building was emptier than usual, she became more
uneasy. She often positioned herself near the door or chose seats where she could see the whole
room. She avoided lingering afterward, and she watched people’s hands and movements with a
seriousness that others sometimes misread as reserve. Over time, the effort of keeping herself alert
and appearing composed left her feeling worn down and increasingly separate from those around her,
as if she was performing normality from a distance.

What finally brought Lan to seek help was the sense that she could no longer keep managing this
level of fear on her own. The checking, the scanning, the startle in her body, and the way memories
could arrive in vivid, terror-filled fragments had become exhausting. She was most afraid that she
would never feel safe again, that her life had been permanently altered, and that one day she might
miss a sign of danger and be harmed before she could react. At the same time, she was hoping for
some relief from the constant alertness and for a way to live and work without feeling as though she
were always waiting for something terrible to happen.
