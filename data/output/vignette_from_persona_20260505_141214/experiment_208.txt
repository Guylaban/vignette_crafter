life when he was a civilian caught in a war zone, and he described that time as something that still
did not fully make sense to him. He would say, in a flat voice at first, that one minute he had been
going about ordinary errands and the next he was in a world where the sound of a bang could mean
death, where a smell in the air could suddenly bring back the sharp, choking atmosphere of danger,
and where there was no way to know what would happen next or how to stop it.

What he remembered most vividly was one particular episode in which an explosion occurred close
enough that it left his body braced and thrown into a position he had never quite forgotten. When
the memory came back now, he did not experience it as a calm picture from the past. He said it felt
as though his body returned to the same position it had been in then, tense and frozen in place,
with the same tightness in his muscles and the same helpless anticipation of what might follow.
Sometimes the recollection would arrive suddenly while he was at work, and he would be standing on a
ladder or bent over a wall, feeling for a moment as though he were back there again. He also spoke
of the grief that came with remembering, a grief that could overwhelm him without warning, making
him feel heavy and quiet for the rest of the day. At times he struggled to believe that something so
extreme had really happened to him at all, as though the story belonged to someone else’s life, not
his own.

In the years after he returned home, Imran tried to hold himself together by staying in motion. He
threw himself into painting work, taking extra jobs and keeping busy from morning into the evening
because, as he put it, “if I stop, I start thinking.” The thinking was often the same question, over
and over: why had this happened to him, what had it meant, and whether there had been any point to
surviving it. He could spend long stretches turning the event over in his mind, searching for an
explanation that never satisfied him. At the same time, he made a habit of avoiding news reports

not want “to let it into my head.” The effort to keep himself occupied and away from reminders
became part of his routine, though it also meant that he increasingly kept his world narrow and
controlled.

Certain moments in his present life still hit him hard and without warning. The smell of something
related to the war years could stop him in his tracks, bringing back the feeling of that time with a
force that was difficult to explain to others. He noticed it most when he was working in people’s
houses and encountered particular odors on the job, because a smell in a hallway or room could
suddenly make him feel he was back in the war zone, scanning the air and trying to understand
whether danger was near. Loud, explosive sounds were even harder. A slammed door, a dropped tool, or

him feel like himself. He still worked, but with less confidence and more strain, and there were
