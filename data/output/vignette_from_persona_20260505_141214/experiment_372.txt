Frank had been working late at the hotel he managed when the mugging happened, leaving through the
service entrance after a long evening of checking staffing and closing paperwork. It was dark, the
lobby noise had thinned, and he was walking to his car with his keys in hand when two men approached
from the side of the building. One moved too quickly for Frank to read his intentions, and what
stayed with him afterward was the sense that they looked suspicious before anything was even said.
He could smell alcohol on them before he understood the danger, a harsh, sour odor that seemed to
announce recklessness and trouble. When one of the men barked at him and the other stepped in close,
Frank was struck by the suddenness of it, by the feeling that the situation had shifted in an
instant from ordinary to unsafe. The episode ended with him being robbed, but he remembered the
humiliation of it most vividly: standing there as if reduced in front of strangers, unable to stop
what was happening and painfully aware of how exposed he had been.

In the weeks that followed, Frank found himself returning to that moment in fragments. At work, any
man lingering near the entrance, any guest who seemed watchful in the wrong way or moved with a
little too much purpose, could make him tense before he had time to think. He was especially
unsettled if someone appeared suspiciously near the service corridor or hovered by the parking area
after dark. Even if nothing happened, he would keep watching, scanning faces and hands, checking who
was coming and going, and mentally rehearsing where he could stand if trouble started. Loud noises
were another immediate jolt for him. A door slamming in the kitchen, a dropped tray, a car
backfiring in the lot, or the sharp bang of a suitcase hitting tile could send a spike of alarm
through him before he had sorted out what caused it. He described it as his body reacting first, his
mind catching up later.

At home, Frank had not been able to leave the fear behind at the hotel. He reported feeling on guard
even inside his own house, as if being indoors no longer fully separated him from danger. If a car
pulled up too fast outside, if voices rose on the street, or if he heard a sudden crash from another
room, he was instantly alert. His wife learned not to come up behind him without warning, because
when she touched his shoulder unexpectedly he reacted with visible alarm, jerking away and turning
sharply as though preparing for an attack. He hated that response and felt embarrassed by it, but it
happened quickly enough that he could not always stop it.

Over time, Frank came to believe that people could not be trusted. That belief sat beneath much of
what he did now. At the hotel, where his job required him to greet strangers, solve problems, and
keep the place running smoothly, he had become more guarded with guests and staff alike. He did not
think everyone meant harm, but he felt that assumptions of goodwill were naïve, and he watched for
signs that someone might be unstable, intoxicated, or planning something. The smell of alcohol in
particular still tightened his chest and brought back the memory of the men approaching him that
night. He noticed it in the lobby bar, on guests passing by, even in the hallway when a resident had
been drinking. Each time, he felt the old warning rise in him, and he would become more watchful
without announcing why.

Frank also spoke with painful consistency about feeling permanently humiliated by what had happened.
It was not only that he had been robbed; it was that, in his own mind, the event had marked him as
someone who could be cornered and made helpless. He said that he had spent most of his life being
capable and composed, the sort of man colleagues relied on, and the mugging had left him feeling
stripped of that image. He continued to work, but he found himself less comfortable in the hotel’s
public spaces and more eager to be behind the desk or in a back office where he could see the
entrances. The world, to him, no longer felt ordinary or broadly safe. He had come to see it as a
dangerous place where harm could arrive quickly and without warning, from people who looked normal
until they did not.

By the time he sought help, Frank was tired of living this way. He was not seeking reassurance that
the robbery had been “in the past”; what brought him in was the sense that he could not turn off the
alertness, even in his own home or in the workplace he had once moved through with ease. He wanted
to feel less on edge, less startled, and less ashamed of his own reactions. More than anything, he
hoped to stop feeling as though danger might be waiting at every entrance, every loud sound, and
every unfamiliar face.
