Asha was 44, married, and had spent most of her adult life working in retail in a busy store where
she was expected to stay on her feet, keep track of customers, and respond quickly when problems
came up. Before the event that brought her to treatment, she had been known at work as calm,
obliging, and hard to rattle. That changed after she witnessed a sudden, disturbing incident that
she has struggled to put in order ever since. Even now, when she tries to tell it, the story comes
out in fragments. She knows what happened was real, but the sequence slips away from her; she finds
herself moving from one image to another without being able to line them up properly. What stays
with her is the feeling of being overwhelmed at the time and the sense that everything happened too
fast for her to make sense of it.

In the weeks after the event, Asha noticed that her attention began to go constantly outward, as
though she needed to keep watch on every corner of whatever room she was in. In the store, she found
herself scanning the aisles, the entrance, and the faces of customers for any sign that something
was not right. She said she was “always looking,” even when nothing was happening, and that she
could not relax unless she knew where people were and what they were doing. The habit followed her
home as well. In public places she chose seats where she could see the door, and when she was with
others she tended to agree quickly with what they wanted, because going along with them felt safer
than expressing a preference. This people-pleasing was not how she had always been, but it became
her default way of getting through the day, especially when she felt uncertain about who around her
could be trusted.

Certain moments set off a sudden rush of distress. If someone in the store started crying, Asha
would feel a sharp jolt and have to turn away. The sound and sight of tears seemed to pull her
straight back toward the memory, not in a clear story but in a wave of feeling she could not
control. She also became uneasy when she felt dizzy or lightheaded, because that bodily sensation
reminded her of the physical helplessness she experienced during and after the trauma. Those moments
could make her feel as if she needed to sit down immediately, and she described a brief panic that
something terrible was about to happen again. Talking about the event was the hardest trigger of
all. Even before she finished a sentence, she would feel the pressure building in her chest and
throat, and there were times when she had to stop mid-sentence because the emotional force of the
memory became too much.

As time went on, Asha lived with a nearly constant sense of being on guard. She described herself as
superalert, watchful, and easily startled, as if her body had learned to stay braced for danger. In
the store, a sudden movement at the edge of her vision or an unexpected voice behind her made her
jump. She found herself turning quickly to check who was there, and she was embarrassed by how tense
she had become in ordinary situations. At home too, she remained keyed up, continually checking her
surroundings, as though missing a detail could put her or her family at risk. These habits became
tiring, but she felt unable to let them go. She believed that if she stopped watching closely, she
might be caught off guard in the same way she was before.

Asha also spent a great deal of time trying to understand why the event had happened to her. She
kept returning to it in her mind, asking herself what it meant and why she had been there to witness
it at all. She did not experience these thoughts as comforting; rather, they became another painful
place she could not leave. When she remembered the event, what came most strongly was grief. She
would be flooded with it so intensely that she had to stop what she was doing, sometimes putting
down her work or leaving a conversation because the feeling became overwhelming. At those times, the
memory itself felt disorganized and unbearable: the details would not settle into a clear order, but
the sorrow attached to them was unmistakable.

By the time she sought help, Asha was still working, but she was exhausted by how much effort it
took to keep herself composed at work and in public. Her marriage and daily routines had narrowed
around avoiding anything that might set off the memories or expose her to situations where she felt
unprepared. She came to treatment because the constant alertness, the startle, the dizzy spells, and
the overwhelming grief were no longer things she could simply push through. What she most hoped for
was some way to remember what happened without being overwhelmed by it, and what she most feared was
that if she let down her guard, something frightening would happen again before she could react.
