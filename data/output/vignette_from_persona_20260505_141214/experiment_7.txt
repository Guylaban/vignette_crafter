Minh was a 33-year-old South East Asian American man who had been married for four years and worked
as a physical therapist in a busy outpatient clinic. Before the accident, he had prided himself on
being steady and physically capable, the person others looked to when movement hurt or confidence
had been shaken. That sense of competence was upended one evening on the freeway when a sudden,
life-threatening crash left him trapped in a mangled vehicle with the force of the impact ringing
through his body. In the months after, he found that pieces of the event would rush back into his
mind without invitation. Certain moments arrived as unwanted thoughts while he was at work or
sitting at home, not as a deliberate remembering but as a sharp, unwelcome return of the scene. He
would be in the middle of demonstrating an exercise to a patient and suddenly be back in the crushed
metal, the smell of the car interior, the helpless knowledge that his body had been in danger. At
night, he sometimes woke abruptly from a nightmare with his heart pounding and a heavy residue of
the dream still clinging to him, so that for several seconds he was unsure whether he was still in
bed or back in the accident.

Loud noises became particularly difficult. A cart slamming in the clinic hallway, a dropped weight,
or a car backfiring outside could make him jerk hard enough that patients noticed. He described
feeling jumpy, as though his body reacted before he had time to think. Even in ordinary settings he
found himself constantly scanning for signs of threat. In the clinic, before he entered a room, he
would glance at exits, look over counters, and notice any objects that could be used as weapons or
felt dangerous in some undefined way. He did the same in parking lots, waiting rooms, and
restaurants, registering knives, metal tools, broken glass, anything that seemed potentially
hazardous. He was aware that he was watching for danger almost all the time, yet the habit felt
necessary to him. He had come to believe that you could never know who might harm you, and that his
own body could no longer be trusted to protect him if something happened again.

Over time, Minh developed a set of routines that gave him a temporary sense of control. He cleaned
and organized things repeatedly, especially at home after work, lining up objects on the counter,
wiping surfaces, and rearranging items until they felt “right.” He also mentally rehearsed worst-
case scenarios. Before driving to the clinic, before entering a crowded store, before taking a
patient into an exercise area, he would run through what he would do if something went wrong, almost
as if repeated rehearsal could keep him from being caught helpless again. These habits took up time
and attention, and his concentration began to suffer. At work, he occasionally lost his thread mid-
session, had to reread notes, or found himself staring at a chart while his mind drifted back to
scanning the room or imagining how quickly a situation could become dangerous. He remained committed
to his job, but the effort of staying focused now sat alongside the constant sense that he needed to
watch everything.

What distressed him most was the feeling that the accident had permanently changed his body. He was
not describing dramatic visible injury so much as a deep conviction that he was no longer fully
reliable physically. If his chest tightened, if his back ached after a long day, or if he felt even
slightly unsteady, he interpreted it as proof that his body had been permanently damaged by what
happened. This belief made ordinary fatigue feel ominous. He became cautious with movements he once
did automatically, and he sometimes paused before demonstrating techniques to patients, aware of the
irony that the man whose job was to restore function now doubted his own physical safety. His wife
noticed that he was quieter at home and that he spent more time cleaning or scanning the windows and
door than relaxing with her. He did not want to alarm her, so he kept much of his fear to himself,
but the strain was beginning to show in their evenings together.

Minh finally sought help when the combination of nocturnal waking, unwanted thoughts, loud-noise
reactions, and relentless scanning started to interfere more visibly with his work and marriage. He
said he was tired of feeling as if he had to stay on guard every waking moment, yet he was also
afraid that if he let himself stop, something terrible would happen and he would not be ready. What
he most hoped for was a way to feel safe again in his own body and in ordinary places without having
to spend so much of the day bracing for harm.
