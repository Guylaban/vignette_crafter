Amina was 28, a South Asian woman who had been married for four years and worked as a tailor,
spending long days bent over a sewing machine in a small shop near her home. Her trauma had occurred
during a period of detention in which she was tortured, and she spoke of it only in fragments, with
a flatness that made clear how much she had learned to keep contained. She described being held in a
room where she could not predict what would happen next, where her body was hurt and she was made to
feel trapped and watched. Even now, when she tried to tell the story, she focused first on the
setting: the door, the walls, the places she could not get to. What stayed with her most was the
certainty that escape had depended on someone else deciding to let her go.

In the months after she returned home, Amina found that ordinary places no longer felt ordinary. The
first thing she noticed was that whenever she entered a room or building, her eyes went straight to
the exits without her meaning them to. In the tailor shop she would stand at the doorway for a
moment before crossing the threshold, taking in the back door, the front gate, the narrow passage
behind the counter. At home she checked the courtyard gate and the room doors before settling down,
and in waiting rooms she sat with her body angled toward the nearest exit, as if positioning herself
could reduce the danger. She said she did this automatically, because not knowing where she could
get out felt unbearable. Her husband had noticed that she no longer entered crowded places easily,
and on days when she did manage to accompany him to a clinic or market, she remained alert in a way
that kept her attention fixed outward rather than on the people she was with.

Amina also described moments when, without warning, it felt as if the torture were happening again.
These episodes came with no ceremony: while bending over fabric, hearing a door close, or when
someone’s voice sharpened unexpectedly, she would suddenly feel pulled back into the terror with
such force that she had to pause and grip the table or the edge of a chair. During these spells she
said her body reacted before her mind could catch up, and for a few seconds she was no longer in the
shop or at home but back in the place where she had been hurt and could not move freely. Afterwards
she would feel shaken and ashamed that she could not simply “stay in the present,” as she put it,
but the experience itself was vivid and immediate, leaving her wary of anything that might bring it
on again.

Over time, Amina became increasingly watchful. She moved through her days as though she had to keep
something from happening by never letting herself become fully unaware. In the tailor shop she
listened for footsteps outside, watched customers’ faces for signs of impatience or anger, and kept
one part of her mind on the street and the door even while measuring cloth or pinning hems. At home,
she was on guard in the evenings and grew tense when unexpected visitors arrived. Her family noticed
that she seemed unable to settle, even during quiet moments, as if rest itself were risky. She had
also come to avoid anything that stirred strong feelings. Arguments, sad stories, and even
conversations that might lead her toward her own memories were carefully sidestepped. When her
husband tried to ask how she was really doing, she often changed the subject or said she was tired.
She did not frame this as unwillingness; she seemed to believe that strong emotion could overwhelm
her and leave her exposed in a way she could not manage.

For the same reason, Amina avoided relaxing. She no longer trusted the moments when the sewing work
was done and the shop grew quiet, because stillness left too much room for memories and sensations
to surface. At home she kept busy with small tasks rather than sitting on the bed or lying down. If
her husband suggested she rest, she would shake her head and say there was no need, though her body
looked exhausted. She had also avoided medical care, even when she was not feeling well, and had not
sought psychological help until recently. She said she had learned not to put herself in places
where she might be examined, questioned, or reminded of what had been done to her, and that asking
for help felt dangerously exposing.

By the time she came for help, Amina was no longer trying to pretend that things were manageable.
The constant scanning, the sudden moments of reliving, and the strain of keeping herself from
feeling too much had begun to affect her marriage and her work. She had started to miss parts of
conversations at the shop because she was watching the door, and she felt embarrassed by how often
she needed to orient herself before entering a room. What brought her in was the fear that she would
not be able to keep living this way forever. She hoped, tentatively, that she might learn to sit in
a room without immediately searching for the way out, and that she could trust that seeking help
would not put her in danger again.
