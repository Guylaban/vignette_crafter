steadiness under pressure, including years as a combat pilot. He came to treatment only after

history he had rarely spoken about: in a relationship years earlier, he had been physically abused
by a romantic partner, an experience he had kept largely to himself and carried in silence.

He recalled that the abuse was not an isolated incident but something that left him wary of ordinary
contact and of the person involved. At times, the smallest physical touch would make him stiffen
before he could stop himself. Even now, unexpected touch could bring an immediate jolt through his
body, as though he were bracing for harm. He was also unsettled by seeing the offender, and for a
long time after the relationship ended he went out of his way to avoid any chance of running into
that person or being in places where the encounter might happen. Alcohol smell was another potent
reminder for him. He associated it with the atmosphere surrounding the abuse, and if he caught the
scent on someone’s breath or in a room, he would feel abruptly on edge, pulled back into the same
sense of alarm he had felt then.

Robert spoke about how, over time, he had organized much of his life around not having to face
reminders of what happened. He said he had come to believe that he must never allow himself to feel
or express anger. When irritation rose in him, he pushed it down immediately, keeping his voice calm
and his expression blank. He described this not as a choice that felt natural, but as something he
had learned to do because anger felt dangerous and he did not trust what might happen if he let it
show. He also avoided anything that reminded him of the person who harmed him, including situations,
conversations, and even certain social settings where the memory felt too close. Intimacy was
especially difficult. Being emotionally close to others, or allowing himself to depend on someone,
felt to him like opening a door he could not close again. After his wife died, this reluctance
became even more visible in his daily life, as opportunities for companionship arose but he kept his
distance, telling himself that it was safer not to get too close.

In recent months, his threat awareness had become harder to set aside. At night he had difficulty

used to, noticing doors, movements, and the mood of a room almost automatically. He was aware that
this left him exhausted, but he also felt that staying on guard was necessary. When something
reminded him of the stressful experience, his body reacted strongly. A touch on the arm, the smell
of alcohol, or an unexpected glimpse of the offender could bring a rush of physical tension that he
experienced as immediate and intense, as though his body knew before his mind did that something was
wrong.

describe this with regret so much as resignation, as if he had long accepted that this was the price
of staying safe.

He sought help now because the symptoms had become impossible to manage alone and because he was

a way to sleep without having to stay on alert and to feel less ruled by the need to keep everyone
at a distance.
