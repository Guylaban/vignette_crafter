several months of feeling increasingly on edge in situations that used to feel routine. Her life had
changed abruptly after an encounter with police violence during a response call. What had begun as
another workday shifted into an event she still struggled to tell in a straight line: when she tried
to explain it, she would lose the thread partway through and stop, frustrated by how the pieces
would not stay in the right order. Even in session, she described the memory as though it were not
something finished and filed away, but something still happening to her in the present tense. She
could recall fragments with vivid force — the immediate sense of being under threat, the shock of
what was done to her, the physical unease in her body — yet the sequence never settled into a clear
before and after. The experience remained jumbled and unfinished in her mind, and that confusion
made it difficult for her to narrate without becoming overwhelmed.

In the months after the incident, Megan began noticing that stress itself could bring the whole
experience back with startling force. On busy shifts, when radios were blaring, requests were piling
up, and the pace became unrelenting, she would feel her body tighten and her attention narrow.
Alongside that, nausea became one of the clearest reminders of what had happened; when she felt sick
to her stomach, especially in tense moments, it would pull her back toward the event with an
intensity that surprised her. She had also become wary of being touched without warning. In the
field, when a colleague reached for her shoulder or touched her arm before speaking, she would react
with a visible alarm, jerking slightly as if expecting something bad to follow. She was embarrassed
by how immediate that reaction was, but she could not stop it. To her, unexpected touch now carried
the feeling of being caught off guard when she was least able to protect herself.

Megan’s daily life had gradually organized around keeping herself away from reminders of the person
who harmed her. She described this in practical terms rather than dramatic ones: she no longer
wanted to encounter faces, settings, or circumstances that made the perpetrator come to mind, and
she worked hard to steer around anything that might stir those associations. At work, that vigilance
became harder to avoid because police officers and uniforms were part of the environment around
emergency response. She found herself increasingly uneasy around authority figures and anyone in
uniform, including people she knew had no connection to the assault. There was a particular quality
to those interactions that made her body go tense before she had time to think. She noticed herself
becoming quieter, more guarded, and more eager to finish an exchange quickly. Over time, this
avoidance affected the way she moved through both work and public spaces, making ordinary encounters
feel loaded with risk.

She also described a persistent habit of suppressing anger, saying that she had learned never to
allow herself to feel or express it. That had become especially pronounced since the assault. Even
when she felt injured, cornered, or treated unfairly, she would clamp down on the feeling and
redirect herself toward calm competence. Friends and family saw her as composed, but she experienced
that composure as something tightly held. She did not trust anger to stay contained, and she worried
that once it surfaced, it would be harder to manage than she could tolerate. As the months passed,
that restraint became part of how she got through shifts, conversations, and the emotional strain of
being reminded of what had happened.

Her sense of danger remained active in daily life. Megan said she constantly scanned her
surroundings for signs that something was wrong, and she made a point of choosing where to sit based
on how safe or protected she felt. In restaurants, waiting rooms, and staff areas, she would take
stock of exits, sight lines, and what was behind her. At work, she preferred positions that let her
see a room clearly and avoid being boxed in. She was not able to relax into environments the way she
once had; instead, she stayed attentive to movement, posture, and proximity, as though readiness
might give her a better chance of avoiding another surprise. This way of being left her exhausted,
but it also felt necessary to her, a practical response to a world that had become less predictable.

By the time she sought help, Megan was discouraged by how long these reactions had lasted and by how
much they had narrowed her day-to-day life. The PCL-5 score reflected a level of distress that was
no longer easy to dismiss. What brought her in was not a single crisis, but the accumulation of
being unable to explain the event clearly, being pulled back into it by stress or nausea, flinching
at unexpected touch, and feeling herself tense around uniforms and authority. She wanted to
understand why the memory still felt so immediate and why her body kept acting as though danger were
close by. Most of all, she hoped for relief from the sense that she had to stay constantly on guard,
and she was frightened that if nothing changed, she would keep living as though the event had never
ended.
