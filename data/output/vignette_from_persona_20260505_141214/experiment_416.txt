instead made home feel unsafe. As a child, she was physically abused by a caregiver she had trusted,
and even in later life she could not tell the story in a smooth, ordinary way. When she tried, the
images came back in a series of still frames, sharp and literal, as if she were staring at
photographs laid out in front of her. She could see the position of a hand, the angle of a room, the
expression on a face that should have been protective. At other times she could recall that it had
happened, yet feel almost completely blank, as though the child it happened to belonged to someone
else. She often stopped mid-sentence because she knew what had occurred but could not find words
sturdy enough to describe it, and this loss of language left her frustrated and ashamed.

For much of her adult life, Eileen built a careful, structured existence. She trained herself into
routines and precision, and by the time she was a QA engineer, she was known for her meticulousness
and calm. Yet even in the workplace, uncertainty could make her feel suddenly exposed. When a
schedule changed without warning, when a supervisor gave an ambiguous instruction, or when she was
left waiting without clear information, she became tense and watchful, scanning faces and tone for
signs that something was about to go wrong. She was a woman who prided herself on competence, but in
moments of uncertainty she felt her body react before she had time to think. Her chest would
tighten, her shoulders would rise, and she would already be braced for harm before she could tell
herself that she was safe. Sudden movements had the same effect. If a colleague came up quickly from
behind her desk, if someone reached across a table too fast, or if a hand moved abruptly in
conversation, she was immediately on guard, alert in a way that startled even her.

Certain places and situations carried a particular charge. Funerals, cemeteries, and other grief-
related events were difficult for her not because of the grief alone, but because they brought back
the feeling of helpless exposure she associated with childhood. When she attended a funeral after
the death of a relative, she found herself watching the men in the room more than listening to the
service, especially if she ended up alone with one. Being alone with men had become one of the
clearest conditions for her fear, and she arranged her life around it whenever possible. In the
office, she chose group conversations over private meetings and made sure doors stayed open. At
social gatherings, she positioned herself near her husband or other women. Over time, this narrowed
her world in small but meaningful ways, and she noticed how often she was editing where she went,
who she stood beside, and how long she stayed.

The personal meaning she made of the past was heavy and self-condemning. Eileen had come to believe
that bad things could happen to her and that she could not stop them. What frightened her most was
not only remembering the abuse, but the conviction that she had failed in some way as a child, that
she should have done more to stop it, said more, fought harder, or somehow protected herself better.
She could not forgive herself for not having prevented what occurred, even though the truth of her
age and powerlessness was obvious when she spoke about it. Beneath that was a deeper wound: she felt
betrayed by someone she had trusted, and that betrayal still shaped the way she read people now. Her
husband remained a steady presence, but trust in general was not given freely. She assessed people
quickly, particularly men, and any abruptness or ambiguity could make her feel that the old danger
was close again.

By the time she sought help, Eileen was 73 and still working, though the effort of managing her
vigilance was wearing thin. She noticed that the strategies she had used for decades were becoming
harder to maintain as she moved through offices, funerals, and everyday interactions with less
certainty about what might happen next. She came to treatment not because she had found words for
what happened, but because she was tired of living as if danger might break in at any moment. What
she hoped for was not to erase the past, which she knew she could not do, but to feel less ruled by
the body’s instant alarm, less trapped by the old photographs in her mind, and less alone when she
found herself standing near men, or in a cemetery, or waiting in the uneasy space of not knowing
what would come next.
