pilot, married, and used to moving through airports with the practiced calm of a man who had spent

not just background noise but an immediate signal that something terrible could be about to unfold.

In the years that followed, Tane found that the memory did not sit comfortably in the past. When he
tried to think about it, he felt as though it belonged to someone else, as if he were describing
another man’s life rather than his own. That distance did not make it easier. If anything, it made
the memory feel stranger, harder to place, and when it surfaced it brought back the same terror he
had felt then, with none of the softening that time usually brings. He described how a sudden sound

not need to be trying to remember; the memory would arrive on its own.

through evenings with a kind of guarded patience, as if darkness made the world less trustworthy. He
would check the doors and look out into the yard, not because he believed something was definitely
there, but because being unprepared felt unbearable to him. When he entered a room or building, he

aircraft sounds with a tension that other people around him would not see. In airports and around

What finally brought Tane to seek help was the exhaustion of living this way for so long and the

constant vigilance, the intrusive returns of the memory, and the sense that he no longer recognized
himself had become harder to contain. He was afraid that if nothing changed, he would continue
shrinking his life around danger, always on guard, always bracing. What he hoped for was more modest
than forgetting. He wanted to feel safe again at night, to enter a room without immediately
searching for exits, and to be able to move through the world without every helicopter sound or
sudden movement pulling him back into terror.
