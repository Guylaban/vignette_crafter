Mai was 52 when she finally agreed to seek help, after months of living as if she had to keep one
part of herself on watch at all times. She was born in the United States to South Eastern Asian
parents, and had spent most of her adult life in the same quiet suburban town with her husband. She
worked as a librarian, a job she had once loved for its order and calm, though in recent months even
the hush of the building had become complicated for her. In the library, she found herself choosing
seats with unusual care, always looking first for the wall behind her, the clearest view of the
doors, the place where she could feel most protected. If a room fell suddenly silent, or if she
walked into a darker aisle after the overhead lights had been dimmed, her body would tense before
she could think. At those moments she became intensely watchful, scanning the space automatically,
checking who was near her and where she might go if something felt wrong.

The trauma had occurred years earlier during a powerful natural disaster that struck without warning
in the middle of the night. Mai had been asleep with her husband when the world outside seemed to
break apart all at once. What stayed with her was not only the violence of the event but the abrupt
darkness and the unnerving stillness that followed in between bursts of sound. In the confusion,
people around her were shouting, running, and trying to reach one another, and she later found that
anything reminding her of those close human links to the disaster could pull her back into it with
startling force. Hearing someone talk about families affected by the storm, or seeing people
connected in any way to the event, could make her chest tighten and her mind flash straight back to
that night. The recollection was not something she summoned carefully; it could seize her when she
least expected it, arriving full and immediate from a sound, a phrase, or a moment of silence around
her.

Over time, Mai began to dread the conditions that most resembled the disaster. Night-time was
particularly hard. Waking in the dark could send a jolt through her, as if she had been returned to
the instant when she could not see what was happening beyond the windows. She started avoiding being
in dark circumstances whenever she could, keeping lights on in the house longer than before and
hesitating before entering dim places. In the library, she sometimes asked for different tasks
rather than entering the lower-lit storage rooms, and she noticed that she spent more energy
arranging herself around safety than she used to. Even simple pauses in conversation could unsettle
her; a sudden complete silence in the house, or in a room at work, could bring the event back with
such force that she felt thrown into it rather than merely reminded of it. When that happened, she
would sometimes deliberately try to leave her body or blank out, wanting nothing more than to escape
the intensity of what had risen in her mind and senses. She described it as trying to disappear from
herself for a few moments, because facing the memory directly felt unbearable.

What troubled her most about remembering was the grief that came with it. When the memory forced its
way in, she did not only feel fear; she was also overwhelmed by sorrow for what had been lost in
that night and in the aftermath. She could be standing at the circulation desk, checking books in,
when a comment from a patron or the sudden quiet of the afternoon would bring the scene back without
warning, and she would have to fight to keep her face composed. The memory did not wait for her
permission. It intruded while she was working, while she was at home, and sometimes while she was
trying to rest, leaving her exhausted by the effort of containing it. She had also stopped
exercising, something she once used to clear her head, because moving her body too much seemed to
make her feel exposed and vulnerable in ways she could not tolerate. Her avoidance had gradually
narrowed her routine, and her husband noticed that she was less willing to go out after dark and
more inclined to sit quietly in places where she could monitor the room.

By the time she came for treatment, Mai was tired of living so narrowly, but she was also afraid of
what might happen if she let her guard down. She did not want to keep being superalert, constantly
scanning every room, but she was not yet sure how to feel safe otherwise. What finally brought her
in was the sense that the memory was taking over too often — that it could come rushing back without
warning in the library, at home, or in the dark, carrying the same grief and terror she had felt
before. She hoped for relief from the sudden intrusions and for a way to sleep through the night
without dreading the dark, while fearing most that if she stopped watching so carefully, something
terrible would happen again.
