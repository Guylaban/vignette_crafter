States and is now divorced, though she still carries herself with the measured composure of someone
who has spent years in a public, caregiving role. She serves as a pastor in her community, a
position that has long depended on her ability to sit with other people’s pain, speak calmly, and
remain steady in moments of distress. That steadiness was shattered years ago when she was taken and
held captive by a group of men who tortured her. When she tries to describe what happened, her
account comes in broken fragments: one scene is painfully clear, while the next is simply gone. She
remembers certain moments with a harsh precision—the feel of being restrained, the terror in a room
she could not leave—but other parts of the ordeal remain blank, as if her mind has sealed them off.
What returns most sharply are disconnected pieces that never fully join together, leaving her with
the unsettling sense that she knows enough to be haunted but not enough to make sense of it.

Since then, Asha has been unable to trust safety when it is offered to her. She can be sitting in
her church office, listening to a parishioner reassure her that she is secure, and still feel that
the words do not reach her. She has come to believe that her body recognizes danger before her mind
can catch up, and she describes this with a kind of weary certainty: her shoulders tense, her
stomach tightens, and her heart starts racing before she has even had time to decide whether there
is a real threat in the room. At church events, in crowded hallways, or while moving through parking
lots after evening meetings, she finds herself scanning faces and entrances, superalert for
dangerous people in a way that feels automatic and exhausting. Even when nothing is happening, she
remains on guard, as though the next moment could turn without warning.

Certain reminders pull her abruptly back. She has identified a specific taste from the period of
captivity that still unsettles her; if it appears in food, she can feel her chest tighten and her
attention narrow around the memory of that time. Because of this, she has taken to controlling what
she eats with unusual strictness, choosing and limiting foods with great care in an effort to feel
some sense of control over her body and surroundings. At home she can spend long periods planning
meals, avoiding anything that might carry that taste or even a faint resemblance to it. This has
narrowed her daily routines and made eating feel less like nourishment than a guarded ritual.

Talking about what happened is especially difficult. When the topic comes up, even in a church
context where she is expected to be compassionate and composed, she feels the urge to shut it down.
She has tried hard not to think about the trauma, pushing it away whenever it starts to surface, and
she worries that if she allows herself to go there she will not be able to handle it. Crying is
another potent trigger: when she sees someone cry, or when her own tears begin to rise unexpectedly,
she is thrown into an immediate state of distress. The sight and sound of crying seem to open a door
to the helplessness of the past, and she responds by tightening control over her expression,
sometimes leaving a room early or turning to practical tasks so she does not have to stay with the
feeling.

Asha’s sense of herself has been deeply shaken. She used to see pastoral care as something she could
do naturally and reliably, but now she fears she is no longer capable of doing the things that once
defined her. She has become preoccupied with the thought that simply thinking about the event will
overwhelm her, and this has made it harder for her to remain present in the reflective, emotionally
open work that her role requires. She can still perform many of the outward duties of ministry, but
she experiences them as thinner and more effortful than before, as if she is trying to speak from
behind a wall. In quieter moments, she has said with stark resignation that she has no future, a
belief that sits beside her ongoing efforts to keep going. It was the accumulating burden of these
experiences—the inability to trust safety, the strain of remaining watchful, the narrowing of her
eating, the fear of discussing what happened, and the sense that her life had become smaller than
the one she once knew—that eventually brought her to seek help. What she wants most is relief from
the constant vigilance and the hope that she might one day feel safe in her own body again, even
though part of her is afraid that the part of herself she lost to the torture may never return.
