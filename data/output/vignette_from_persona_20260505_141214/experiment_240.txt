Nia was 23 when she first came to treatment, a single African American woman working as an
environmental scientist, and she described her life as having split cleanly into “before” and
“after” the day her close loved one died unexpectedly. Before then, she had been building a routine
around fieldwork, lab notes, and the ordinary demands of her job; afterward, she found herself
moving through the same days with a sense that something essential had been taken and left
unresolved. The death had been sudden enough that she still struggled to put it into words without
feeling as if the scene were opening up around her again. When she tried to speak about it, she
often stopped partway through, pushing the memory away before she could fully recall it. She could
say what happened in outline, but not in a settled way; the details felt slippery, and when she
attempted to describe them, she would lose the thread and go quiet, as if the right words were just
beyond reach. What frightened her most was that the memory did not stay in the past. At times it
felt as if it were happening right then, in the room with her, immediate and unfinished rather than
over.

In the weeks after the death, Nia found herself repeatedly thinking that somebody else would have
stopped it from happening. She returned again and again to the moment of loss, turning it over in
her mind and arriving at the same painful conclusion: she should have done something differently.
Her self-blame was not abstract but repetitive and intimate, a private argument she seemed unable to
escape. She also came to believe that the event had happened to her because of the sort of person
she was, as though there was something in her character that made her vulnerable to this kind of
loss. That belief sat alongside a deeper sense of injustice. What had happened, in her view, was
deeply unfair, and nothing had been done about it. She spoke with anger about the absence of any
sense of repair, as if the world had simply moved on while she remained stuck with the consequences.

Nia’s body often reacted before her mind had time to catch up. At work, when a site visit ran longer
than expected or a colleague was late arriving to meet her, she would suddenly feel her chest
tighten and her stomach drop, already braced for something terrible before she had formed a clear
thought. In those moments she was certain that delay meant danger, that if something was taking
longer than expected it was because something bad was happening. The reaction felt instantaneous and
automatic, and it left her looking and listening for signs that confirmed her fear. Sleep became
difficult as well. She described trouble falling asleep and staying asleep, lying awake with her
mind pulled back toward the death and her body unable to settle. Even on nights when she managed to
doze off, she woke frequently and felt unrefreshed, as though she had spent the night on alert.

Over time, she began trying to manage the flood of feelings and memories in ways that gave temporary
relief but made her world smaller. She avoided television programmes, films, and reading material
that seemed similar to what had happened, especially anything that involved sudden death or loss.
She noticed herself changing the channel, scrolling past headlines, or putting a book down the
moment it reminded her of the event. At work, where her job required her to move between offices,
labs, and outdoor settings, she sometimes became tense if she had to wait for a response from a
teammate or for field equipment to be brought out, because the delay itself could set off that
immediate sense that something bad was underway. She also found that she sometimes picked arguments
or created drama to distract herself from the trauma. She could see, even as she did it, that she
was starting conflicts that had little to do with the people around her, but in the moment the
sharpness of the exchange gave her something else to focus on besides the death and the images that
kept forcing their way toward her.

By the time she sought help, Nia was exhausted by the combination of intrusive memory, persistent
blame, and the sense that her own body had become a warning system she could not switch off. She was
not seeking to forget her loved one, but she wanted the memory to feel like something that had
happened in the past rather than something that kept breaking into the present. What brought her in
was the fear that she could no longer keep living with the experience in this way, and the hope that
she might someday talk about it without losing the words or being pulled back into it.
