shifting, his mother’s face lit by lightning—and later he would say that his memory of the event was
mostly pictures, as if the night had been reduced to isolated frames. In the middle of it, he felt
terror so intense that even months later, remembering the storm could bring back the same fear in
his body, sudden and complete, as though the warning siren were sounding again.

In the days that followed, he found that certain sensory details could pull him back with startling
force. The smell of damp wood and mildew after the storm, especially in places where water had
seeped in, had lodged itself in his mind. He noticed it again when his workplace mop water was left
standing too long or when rain soaked the parking lot outside the restaurant, and the smell could
make him stop short and feel abruptly shaken, as if the night were returning around him. He began to
try hard not to think about the tornado at all, pushing away any image, sentence, or reminder that
might summon it. At the same time, he grew careful about stressful situations in general. He would
avoid anything that felt likely to unsettle him, whether that meant extra responsibilities at work,
difficult conversations with his manager, or even small conflicts with friends. Before almost any
interaction, he rehearsed what he was going to say, going over the words in his head until they felt
safe enough to use, because he had come to believe that being unprepared was itself a risk.

Jordan’s life had become increasingly narrow over the months after the disaster. He used to think of

storm and was miserable now, and that whatever had happened to him had changed him permanently for

him less social with the people around him, and he noticed that even casual plans felt easier to
