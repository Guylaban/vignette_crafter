Minh had spent most of his adult life working with his hands, moving between attics, crawl spaces,
and utility rooms as an HVAC technician, the kind of job that required him to notice the smell of
refrigerant, the feel of heat trapped in metal ducts, and the difference between a routine call and
a problem that could turn urgent. He had lived in the United States for years, was married, and was
known by coworkers as careful and dependable. That sense of himself changed on a job site when he
was exposed to a toxic chemical. The details of the exposure remained sharply fixed in his mind: the
confined work area, the sudden chemical smell that seemed to catch in his throat, and the immediate
realization that something was wrong in a way he did not know how to control. He remembered leaving
the space and still feeling as if the danger had followed him out with the air he was breathing.
Even later, when others told him he was safe and that the situation had been contained, Minh found
it impossible to take that in. Their reassurance sounded like words meant for someone else.

In the weeks that followed, Minh began to organize his life around the possibility of danger
returning without warning. He became preoccupied with doors and windows, checking and rechecking
them until he could no longer trust his own memory of whether they were secure. At home, before he
went to bed, he would move from room to room and then back again, testing locks and looking at
latches in a way that irritated his wife but made sense to him because, as he saw it, if he missed
something once, the consequences could be severe. Even in places that should have felt ordinary and
familiar, he remained on guard. When he was alone in a room or in a building, the feeling sharpened;
empty spaces seemed less restful than exposed. He would position himself near exits or keep the
television on so the silence would not press in on him. Being alone came to feel unsafe in a way he
could not easily explain, only endure.

He also began avoiding anything that might leave him feeling stressed or physically keyed up,
because any pressure seemed to make his internal alarm spike. He turned down extra work when he
could and tried to stay away from situations that might require him to think quickly or face
uncertainty. Even relaxation started to feel dangerous to him. Sitting still on the couch after
dinner, or trying to lie back and rest, was not calming; it made him feel too open, too unprepared,
as if letting his guard down invited something bad to happen. Instead, he stayed busy with small
tasks, keeping his hands occupied and his attention on practical matters. The moments when he tried
to stop and rest were the moments when his body seemed most insistent that danger could return.

What others saw as simple fatigue, Minh experienced as a body that never fully stood down. He
described himself as constantly tense and ready to react, as though he had to be prepared for the
next threat before it arrived. His muscles felt set for action, his attention always pulled toward
what might be wrong. At work, this made even familiar environments feel harder to tolerate. Entering
buildings for service calls, he watched doors more closely than before and noticed the arrangement
of exits before anything else. In his own home, he sometimes stood in the doorway for a moment
before entering a room, scanning it as if checking whether he had missed something important. He was
embarrassed by how much time he spent doing these things, but he could not persuade himself to stop.

Over time, the changes affected his life with his wife and his work routine. He became less willing
to stay anywhere alone for long, and he often tried to be near someone else when possible. His wife
noticed that he was more withdrawn in the evenings, not because he wanted distance from her, but
because he seemed locked into a pattern of checking, watching, and preparing. He no longer rested
easily in the house they shared, and he carried the same vigilance into the places where he worked.
Despite repeated reassurance from others that he was safe and that the exposure had passed, Minh
remained unconvinced. The chemical incident had become, in his mind, a reminder that a place could
seem ordinary and still suddenly turn dangerous.

By the time he sought help, the strain had become impossible to ignore. He was tired of living as if
he were braced for impact every hour of the day, exhausted by the checking, the tension, and the
effort of avoiding anything that might unsettle him. What brought him in was not a single dramatic
event, but the accumulation of months in which he could not relax, could not trust that he was safe,
and could not settle into being alone without feeling exposed. He hoped, cautiously, that therapy
might help him feel more like himself again, though what frightened him most was the possibility
that he would never be able to believe in safety the way he once had.
