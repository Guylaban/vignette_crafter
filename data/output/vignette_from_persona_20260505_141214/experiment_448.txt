Marisol was 34 when she first came to treatment, a married prison guard who had built her adult life
in the United States after leaving Latin America as a refugee years earlier. She described herself
as someone who had learned to keep moving and keep working, and for a long time that had included
taking on a job in a correctional facility, where uniforms, orders, and locked doors were part of
the daily rhythm. What she now talked about most, however, was a period before that life felt
settled, when she was fleeing with other people and became trapped in a frightening encounter
involving men in authority. She did not tell it as a dramatic story so much as a sequence of sharp,
remembered details: the smell of alcohol on someone who was giving commands, the hard tone of
instructions, and the sight of uniforms that made the situation feel instantly unsafe. Even now,
when she described it, she stayed close to the concrete fragments she remembered, as though those
details still arrived with the force of the original moment.

In the first months after the event, Marisol noticed that certain cues could bring her right back to
that helplessness. If she passed someone in uniform, whether at work or in a public place, her body
tightened before she had time to think. A command delivered in a firm voice could make her feel
exposed and cornered. She was particularly troubled by the smell of alcohol, which she encountered
in ordinary settings and which immediately made the past feel near. At work, the prison environment
repeatedly placed her in contact with authority figures and with people giving orders or
instructions, and she found herself bracing whenever a supervisor approached or whenever she had to
follow a direct command. The role that once felt manageable became more difficult to inhabit because
so much of it was built around the very cues that now felt loaded with danger. She said she often
chose where to sit based on how safe or protected she felt, scanning for the seat with the best wall
behind her or the clearest view of the room. In the mess area, in meetings, and even in her car
before going in, she weighed these choices without much conscious effort.

Marisol also described how, over time, she started avoiding people in authority or uniform outside
of work whenever she could. She would take a longer route if it meant not passing a cluster of
officers, and in public places she tried to keep her distance from security staff or anyone who
looked official. At home she often turned to loud music or the television, not because she wanted
noise for its own sake, but because silence left too much space for what she called “the event” to
come back. When the memory did return, it was not vague. She said it was very clear and detailed,
and that sometimes she would hear in her mind the same sounds she heard during the frightening
encounter: the voices, the sharp commands, the drunk tone that had stood out so painfully. Those
moments could become so overwhelming that she had to stop what she was doing, sit down, and wait for
the surge of feeling to pass. She experienced them as intrusions that arrived fully formed, with the
same sensory force as if they were happening again.

By the time she sought help, Marisol had become uneasy with her own reactions. She often reacted as
if she were in danger when nothing was actually wrong, and she disliked how quickly her body would
respond before she had a chance to reassure herself. Unexpected movement nearby could make her jerk
or freeze, especially in crowded hallways at work or when someone came up behind her without
warning. That startle response was particularly upsetting to her because it seemed to confirm the
sense that she was never fully settled in her surroundings. She spoke in a matter-of-fact way about
these moments, but also with embarrassment, as though she could not quite recognize herself in them.
She had come to believe that the world was a dangerous place and that people were not what they
seemed, a belief that felt especially strong whenever someone in a uniform spoke to her with
confidence or when a casual interaction suddenly reminded her of the past. She also said she felt
like she did not know herself anymore, and that this loss of familiar selfhood was almost as painful
as the memories themselves.

What finally brought Marisol into therapy was the accumulation of these experiences at work and at
home. She was tired of arranging her days around danger cues, tired of needing music or television
just to keep her mind from going back, and tired of feeling overwhelmed by memories she could not
control. More than anything, she wanted to understand why the smell of alcohol, a uniform, or a
simple order could still seize her so completely. She hoped treatment would help her feel less on
guard and more like herself again, and she was frank that what frightened her most was the
possibility that these reactions would keep shaping her life indefinitely.
