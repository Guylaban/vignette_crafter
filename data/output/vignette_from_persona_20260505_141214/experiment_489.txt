Marisol Nia, a 63-year-old project manager of Māori and Pacific Islander heritage, had always been
known at work as steady, organised, and hard to rattle. Her days were usually spent moving between
meetings, timelines, and calls, keeping track of details for other people when their own schedules
became tangled. That steadiness changed after a serious illness that had become life-threatening
before it was finally brought under control. The medical crisis had involved repeated examinations
and procedures, and what remained most vivid to Marisol was not a single moment but the frightening
sequence of being handled, monitored, and punctured by equipment she could not ignore. She recalled
the sight of needles, drips, and tubes with a grim, fixed clarity, and even now the mere appearance
of medical equipment could stop her in place. If a conversation at work drifted toward hospitals,
tests, or someone’s recent operation, she felt her attention snap shut and her body brace as if she
were back in that room.

Since the illness, Marisol had found that memories did not arrive as ordinary recollections. When
they came, they came all at once and with a force that made the past feel entirely present. She
would suddenly smell antiseptic, feel the chill of the room, and see the equipment beside her bed as
though it were still there. Along with the images came a strange bodily certainty: if she thought
about what had happened, her body seemed to settle into the same position it had been in during the
frightening event, as if she could feel again the helpless stillness of lying under scrutiny while
others worked around her. She hated how little she could shape the memory into words. When her
husband asked what she was remembering, or when a clinician asked her to describe the worst part,
she often faltered, aware only that something overwhelming had happened to her body and that
speaking about it felt impossible, fragmentary, almost disloyal to the immediacy of what she had
survived.

In the months that followed, Marisol became increasingly unsettled by anything that reminded her of
examinations or procedures. She would tense at routine appointments and feel a surge of dread if a
nurse mentioned blood work, scans, or a needle. At work, she began to avoid the small conference
room where a colleague had once casually described a hospital visit, because she did not want to
risk hearing more. She could still perform her role, but concentration had become noticeably more
difficult. She would reread the same line in a project brief, lose track of a budget conversation,
or find that a task she had managed easily before now required repeated effort to hold in mind. The
mental strain left her feeling frayed and short with people. She had become irritable in a way that
startled even her, snapping at her husband over minor things and feeling a hot anger she did not
recognise as her own. She noticed, too, that when someone came up behind her at home or at work and
touched her unexpectedly on the shoulder, she reacted with visible alarm, jerking away before she
could stop herself.

What distressed Marisol most was the meaning the illness had taken on in her own mind. She had come
to believe that her body could not be trusted in the way it once had been. Before the illness she
had relied on herself; now she felt there was a betrayal at the centre of her own skin, as though
her body had become unpredictable and unreliable. She was ashamed of what had happened to it and of
how exposed it had made her feel. At times she told herself that the illness had destroyed the life
she had carefully built, not in the sense that everything was gone, but in the sense that something
fundamental had been broken and could not be put back the way it had been. That belief sat heavily
with her, especially when she looked at her work calendar or tried to plan ahead and found herself
unconvinced that she could count on her own health long enough to follow through.

By the time she sought help, Marisol had reached a point where the reminders were no longer easy to
work around. A recent medical appointment had left her shaken for the rest of the day, and a
colleague’s passing remark about a family member’s surgery had brought on one of the same sharp,
full-body recollections that felt completely real across all her senses. She came to therapy hoping
for relief from the constant edge she felt around anything medical and from the shame and alarm that
had settled over her life. More than anything, she wanted to stop feeling as though her body might
fail her again at any moment, and she feared that without help she would continue shrinking her life
around hospitals, conversations, and procedures she could not bear to face.
